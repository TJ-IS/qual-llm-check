## Abstract

Based on a four-year longitudinal case study of a French multinational corporation (MNC) this research explains how a global enterprise system, initially designed to provide greater control by headquarters across its MNC sites, led to the emergence of unexpected practices – known as workarounds – in its Chinese subsidiaries. Drawing on concepts from activity theory our findings provide greater theoretical understanding of workarounds in three ways. First, users are involved in collectively constructing and implementing workaround practices rather than simply being passive users or reduced to the dichotomous framing of ‘accepting’ or ‘rejecting’ an information system. Workarounds are not simple individual user improvizations but are collectively developed and become unofficial local rules. Second, workarounds are part of learning which goes beyond the view of workarounds as deviant and noncompliant behavior. Third, learning activities are not restricted to users and their practices. Rather, as we demonstrate learning and evolution take place between the users, managers, processes and the system; that is, workarounds are not only a critical part of ‘getting work done’ but also an integral part of the institutionalization of an enterprise system.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

In this paper we investigate how users of information systems, and in particular, enterprise systems users, collec tively develop unexpected and improvised practices, also known as “workarounds” (Alter, 2014; Alvarez, 2008; Ignatiadis & Nandhakumar, 2009; Spierings, Kerr, & Houghton, 2017). Following recent interest in better understanding the post-adoption behavior of users of enterprise systems (Alter, 2014; Bagayogo, Lapointe, & Bassellier 2014; Germonprez, Hovorka, & Gal, 2011; Spierings et al., 2017; Van den Hooff & Hafkamp, 2017), we examin how workarounds emerge when multinational corporation (MNC) headquarters implement an enterprise system in their international subsidiaries (Robey, Ross, & Boudreau, 2002; van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016). Beyond the emergence of the workarounds, we also examine how they are formally adopted into the enterprise system and the organizational learning that precedes this process.

To support the quest for organizational effectiveness, MNCs implement centralized enterprise systems to man age people, processes and systems across different sites (Bannon & Bødker, 1997; Chen & Kamal, 2016; Devadoss & Pan, 2007; Whitaker, Ekman, & Thompson, 2017) and to maintain efficient and standardized processes and predict able worldwide outputs. The enterprise system is expected to provide a single communication and control system for interactions within and between communities of users. It is also expected to reduce the “distance” between th headquarters (HQ) and remote subsidiaries; whatever the local specificities of each remote site, users are expecte to use the same set of data, reports and business processes, mediated by the enterprise system (Westrup & Liu 2008). While the widespread use of such systems by MNCs in the last three decades (e.g. Davenport, 1998; Reimers, 2004) is a demonstration of the quest for control, the literature suggests that MNC-imposed templates often fail to match the specific processes and work cultures of subsidiary users (Soh, Kien, & Tay-Yap, 2000). As a response, users resort to manual adjustments enabling the enterprise system to function according to local norms (Markus & Tanis 2000; Soh et al., 2000). In this way, despite the initial intentions of HQ, the implementation of centralized “solutions” that do not fit with subsidiary norms often drives users to adopt practices that place greater reliance on workers’ dis cretion. (Sia, Tang, Soh, & Boh, 2002).

We address the following research question: “In the context of global enterprise systems, how do local workarounds emerge, and how are they enacted and formalized?” We draw on a longitudinal qualitative case study of a French MNC and its Chinese subsidiary. Our study is informed by activity theory (Engeström, 2001, 2010) to analyze and under stand the development and diffusion of workarounds by enterprise system users. This study follows research by th authors to study the use of enterprise system in MNCs. Our previous research studied the reactions of firms to the identification of workaround practices (Malaurent & Avison, 2016). The current paper aims to explain how workarounds emerge and are subsequently formalized into an enterprise system (or rejected), as well as the organi zational learning that precedes their formalization. Our findings demonstrate (1) how a contradiction between th local requirements of subsidiaries and the enterprise system may manifest in a range of misfits and frustrations; (2) how these lead to the emergence of workaround practices by users; and, (3) how the workarounds are either appropriated into the enterprise system, allowed to continue as insignificant practices, or prevented by the organization, Ir contrast to other theorizations (e.g. Azad & King, 2008; Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Choudrie & Zamani, 2016 Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006), our research suggests that workarounds should not be viewed solely as undesirabl resistance and non-compliance or as deviant behaviors, but rather as organized learning activities which involve th creation of new knowledge and new practices.

In the next section, we review the literature on workaround practices. We then outline our theoretical framing for their study. The research setting and data collection approach are then described. Following this, the analysi focuses on presenting three dimensions of workarounds and their associated learning activities. In the discussion, w summarize key findings and outline our central theoretical contributions, followed by a conclusion

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 | Positioning workarounds in the enterprise system and post-adoption behavior literature

How users enact technology comprises a central theme in the field of information systems (Bagayogo et al., 2014; Burton-Jones & Grange, 2012; Jasperson, Carter, & Zmud, 2005; Jones & Karsten, 2008). Rather than being dictate by organizational policies or managerial intent (Barki, Titah, & Boffo, 2007; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Leonardi, 2011; Orlikowski, 1992), many argue that enterprise systems are enacted through self-orchestrated interaction among users, technologies and institutional properties (Bagayogo et al., 2014; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Markus & Robey, 1988; Orlikowski, 1992). Previous research focused on what comes ex-post implementation (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Soh & Sia, 2004); with few exceptions though (e.g. Reimers, 2002) studies have not offered longitudinal accounts of how users workaround, adapt, and improvise around new technologies nor how these post-adoption practices may lead to system change, organizational learning, or new practices

Workarounds—often also referred to as adaptations, “feral-IS”, post-adoption design, shadow systems, and improvisations—are responses to structures inscribed in technology (Alter, 2014; Gasser, 1986)<sup>1</sup>. In the context of enterprise systems, workarounds occur for a variety of reasons: when processes are deemed too cumbersome; information is incomplete or not available; technology malfunctions; or situational constraints or anomalies make it difficult to perform work activities. They may also manifest from personal frustrations, when personal goals conflict wit organizational goals, or when it is deemed necessary to undermine processes or decision criteria mandated by corpo rate management (Alter, 2014; Burton-Jones & Grange, 2012). Beyond the boundaries of the organization they can also be driven by labor disagreements, industrial standards, suppliers and consultants, or government regulations (Alter, 2014; Soh & Sia, 2004). In some instances, they may also be acts of resistance (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005)

In the information systems literature, some work has focused on uses of enterprise systems that differ from those initially planned by designers (Soh & Sia, 2004), for instance, when an enterprise system is implemented in a top-down manner with little consideration for local work practices (Avison & Malaurent, 2007; Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Malaurent & Avison, 2015). Due to the lack of pliancy in the system users respond by creating workarounds to adjust the global enterprise system and its associated business processes into their preferred practices and frames of understanding (Alvarez, 2008; Azad & King, 2008; Azad & King, 2012; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Orlikowsk & Gash, 1994). In doing so, users often discover novel and unanticipated ways to make use of new enterprise systems or even to advance their own practices (Ciborra & Lanzara, 1994; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). This differs from the phenomenon of “customization” (Cox, Rutner, & Dick, 2012) where adjustments are top-down practices determined by the firm under a hierarchical system which agrees on the necessary and appropriate level of customization of the system. It also differs from enterprise system “personalization” (Fan & Poole, 2006) where analysts and programmers design systems to be personalized (in terms of users’ interfaces and functionalities) according to different criteria (i.e. the position of the users). While workarounds involve some bricolage and improvisation, they differ from the phenomenon of “frugal innovation” which typically occurs in the informal sector (see Radjou, Prabhu, & Ahuja, 2012). However, one major takeaway from this stream of literature is its recognition of local actors (e.g. citizens) as a reservoir of knowledge and skills for adapting technologies (Müller, 1980; Müller, 2010). For MNCs implementing enterprise systems globally it suggests that local users may have insights into and knowledge of how the system could be refined to suit local working practices while still conforming with overarching processes and demands

## 2.2 | Views and categorizations of workaround practices

The literature on workarounds typically falls into two dominant views. The first considers workarounds as a form of non-compliance and deviance. In this stream, much effort has been dedicated to investigating the factors that influ ence enterprise system adoption and the related dimensions of initial use (Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003). This has often limited understanding of post-adoption behavior as users either “accepting” or “rejecting” an enterprise system. As Bagayogo et al. (2014) have pointed out though, “use” is not sealed at the time of adoption, highlighting the need for research that takes a closer look at post-adoption IT use. The second predominating view acknowledges user involvement in a continuous development and innovation process which can include workaround practices (Alter, 2014; Van den Hooff & Hafkamp, 2017). It also acknowledges that users interpret, appropriate and use technology in diverse ways and that heterogeneous outcomes can often result at both the organizational and individual levels (Askenäs, 2004; Askenäs & Westelius, 2000; Choudrie & Zamani, 2016; Orlikowski, 1992). This shows how researchers can consider workarounds not only as a response to a new system but also as part of a process that could lead to its improvement (Malaurent & Avison, 2016; Novak, Brooks, Gadd, Anders, & Lorenzi, 2012) Alter (2014) explains workarounds as a process that moves from temporary improvised workarounds that lead to transformed, systematized practices overtime. He argues that greater theorization of workarounds could lead to insights into organizations, management, work practices, standards, and technology adoption on the basis that workarounds are entwined with organizational research topics and the implementation and use of technology. Both views of workarounds outlined here follow the growing recognition that workarounds can be essential or harmfu practices (Brooks, Oshri, & Ravishankar, 2018)

Proposed classification schemes for workarounds are based on their organizational contexts (e.g. Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006; Ignatiadis & Nandhakumar, 2009; Koopman & Hoffman, 2003; McGann, 2005). We opt instead for the established taxonomy suggested by Gasser (1986), who classified information system workarounds into thre distinct categories related to actions: data adjustments, procedural adjustments, and parallel system adjustments (see Table 1). While Gasser's framing occurred prior to the digital revolution, it has served as the basis for many of the other commonly used terms in the literature, as noted in column 2 of Table 1. Studying post-adoption behavior of users of enterprises systems, and information systems more broadly, necessi tates understanding the misalignment (Soh & Sia. 2004) that may exist between an artifact as designed and implemented by managers and users’ expectations, interpretations and enactment of it in their activities. As noted in Table 2, these studies and informing theories point to a range of perspectives on workarounds—from workarounds as an emergent process and part of organizational learning to disruptive and in tension with organiza tional efficiency. The origins of activity theory lie in Soviet cultural-historical psychology, founded by Vygotsky<sup>3</sup> during the 1920s–30s. Enduring from Vygotksy's work is the idea that interaction between the subject and object is mediated by tools and signs (Miettinen, 2009). In later years Vygotksy's work was internationalized and adopted by organiza tion studies as well as more technology-oriented fields such as human-computer interaction and information systems<sup>4</sup>. Engeström (1987, 1999) in particular, has developed activity theory by expanding on the concept of “activity system” and advancing a focus on collective activities and concern for how they develop and transform over time These developments have opened it up to use in the study of organizations (Jarzabkowski, 2003) and, more specifi cally, in the study of information systems and work practices (Forsgren & Byström, 2018; Simeonova, 2018; Slavov & Karanasios, 2018). Below we discuss how we draw on activity theory to answer our research question.

The concept of object-oriented activity contributes to our study of workarounds. This allows accommodation of technology and the activity into one framework (the activity system; see Figure 1), rather than treating them as separate. Kaptelinin and Nardi (2006, p. 5) argue that the appeal of the activity system lies in its move away from a techno-centric perspective; that is, “moving out from the computer as the focus of interest to understanding technology as part of the larger scope of human activities”. The object-oriented view of activity holds that an activity is composed of a subject or subjects (individuals, teams), acting upon an object (e.g. a project, artifact, person). This is distinct from most information systems studies which position individuals, groups, and organizations as their unit of analysis Objects differentiate one activity from another and act as the “the sense-maker” (Kaptelinin, 2005). At the same time, they are fluid and continuously reconstructed (Engeström, 2005) because activities do not exist in isolation but rather, are interwoven, combining and merging with other activities and dividing over time (Spinuzzi, 2008). In information systems research, the object is usually a technology under development (e.g. Karanasios & Allen, 2013) or a work activity, which is being enhanced/disrupted through a new information system (e.g. Allen, Brown, Karanasios, & Norman, 2013; Wiredu & Sørensen, 2006). In this way, the object provides a boundary and focus for the unit of analysis. Because of our focus here on operational-level activities, the objects of our analysis are work tasks e.g. finance reporting (we use the term “objective” to align with the general information systems literature). However, we have also zoomed out to understand the underlying reason for the workaround, context of the work activities, and other relevant connected activities. For instance, we found that the misfits that emerged between a new enterprise system and completion of a work activity led to subjects (users in the subsidiaries) from different functional units (and activities) forming a new “learning” activity to create workarounds. The process of acting on the objective then is mediated by tools (e.g. enterprise system) and governed by cultural-historical rules and norms; it is completed by a broader community of actors and through a division of labor (Karanasios, 2018).

The second contribution of activity theory that we draw upon is the notion of cultural-historical contradiction occurring within and between activity systems (Engeström, 2001). As contradictions arise or are revealed, the expose dynamics, inefficiencies, disturbances, and – importantly – opportunities for change and action (Blackler, 2009; Helle, 2000), precipitating the development of an activity. Contradictions are “historically accumulating struc tural tensions within and between activity systems” (Engeström. 2001, p. 137), as well as “disturbances and conflicts. but also innovative attempts to change the activity” (Engeström, 2001, p. 134). Analytically, contradictions manifest in the form of misfits, breakdowns, disputes and conflicts within and between elements of activity systems (Allen et al., 2013; Karanasios, 2018; Kuutti, 1996), for instance, between activities, people, collectives, technologies, practices and norms (Engeström, 1999).

We argue that this is also the case with workarounds: management and their objectives, motivations, priorities and views of labor and work are often in contradiction with those of workers. As a result, a schism or misfit arise between the information system implemented by mandate of top management (or headquarters) and users’ expecta tions, and thus frustration for users, disputes, or even rejection. In an attempt to reconcile this contradiction, activit subjects (users) adapt the system through workarounds (e.g. a parallel system or procedural adjustment) and relate practices in order to complete their activity. This then leads to an alternate way of completing the activity composed of workarounds. This is not straightforward, however. such workarounds can lead to maladaptive changes or aggra vate new contradictions that were not evident beforehand (Karanasios, 2018). The information systems literatur has shown this to be the case in terms of management views of workarounds as often undesirable (e.g. Azad & King 2008; Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006). This is in line with the activity theoretical view of the world whereby “equilibrium is an exception and tensions, disturbances and local innovations are the rule and th engine of change” (Cole & Engeström, 1993 p.8).

This framing allows examination of how contradictions trigger misfits between headquarters and users, how new technology is then “domesticated” by users, and how new practices emerge as part of the process of the use and institutionalization of an enterprise system over time. Figure 1 shows an activity system and presents examples of how actors might experience the contradiction between prescribed procedures (embedded in the enterprise system template) and local requirements (preceded by the text “in tension with”). While the examples are presented as occur ring within a node of the activity system, they can also exist across nodes and activities. In order to satisfy the activ ity objective (i.e. avoid failure and complete the work activity), it is necessary for the activity subjects to make sense of the situation and adapt their practices accordingly—that is, workarounds. In this way, changes are initiated and driven by actors in response to manifestations of contradiction. Through this, users of a new enterprise system break away from its constraints to make the activity congruent local prevailing norms.

## 4 | EMPIRICAL SETTING AND METHODS

## 4.1 | Research setting

The company we studied is a leading international organization in the energy sector and headquartered in France. It is established in more than 40 countries with 70,000 employees worldwide. Its business includes building infrastructure for transmission and distribution of energy.

The company sought to implement an enterprise system based on SAP R/3 technology across subsidiaries in order to create efficient and standardized processes worldwide. In 2004, the first implementation stage of th worldwide program took place in France. By early 2013, the company had implemented its enterprise system at 89 subsidiaries in 21 countries, with around 3,250 users. This represents about 80% of the company's subsidiaries This research focuses on its 38 Chinese subsidiaries (mostly joint ventures) with 670 users. In 2000, the company agreed on a set of several joint ventures (split between French [51%] and Chinese [49%] ownership) with the big gest electricity distribution company in China. China has a substantial energy market and increasing demand. Fo the MNC, setting up such joint ventures was the only way into the Chinese market at the time. In 2004, it had established in China five sales offices, one wholly owned factory, and four joint ventures, with a total of 820 employees. By mid-2006, a pilot of the Chinese enterprise system project took place in two subsidiaries, both located in the suburbs of Shanghai. With the exception of the local CEO and chief finance officer (CFO) al employees were Chinese.

The case study spans four vears of enterprise system usage at Chinese subsidiaries It provides an empirica account of how local users developed unexpected work practices to adapt the enterprise system solution in unexpected ways. Though the diffusion of enterprise systems in MNCs is common, there are few longitudinal studies and less that consider workaround practices in China. Throughout this case study, we sought a rich understanding of the varied user perspectives over an extended period of time. One of the authors had relatively unlimited access to the enterprise system solution in their role as “enterprise system internal consultant” at one of the Chinese join ventures.

## 4.2 | Data collection

This research takes an interpretivist case study approach (Walsham, 2006) to observe and collect data on work prac tices relating to the use of the enterprise system. Data collection took place over a four-year period. Multiple source of evidence were used, but predominantly interviews, observations and textual material. In total 57 interviews were undertaken. Site visits were conducted at the 38 subsidiary offices during visits to China twice a year. Each visit was on average, six weeks. A range of documents and reports were also analyzed. In line with Lave and Wenger's (1991 view on the value of in-depth field investigations of practitioners at work, we contend that empirical accounts of technology use in the workplace are necessary to improve our understanding of the dynamicity of enterprise system use. Our object of analysis was therefore everyday micro activities involving the enterprise system

## 4.2.1 | Interviews

Our main source of data is 57 open-ended and semi-structured interviews conducted with 49 participants, including functional users from different departments, and joint venture managers at both Chinese subsidiaries and the French HQ (see Table 3).

Interviews lasted between one and two hours, each guided initially by open-ended questions, then moving on to questions tailored to each participant. The first part included questions on the interviewee's history at the company and job description. This led to a more detailed discussion of the enterprise system functionalities used, and how th enterprise system solution was different from the previous legacy systems. Most of the questions focused on th learning phases of the enterprise system as well as the challenges raised by its use (see interview guide in Appendix 1).

All participants had between one and five years’ experience using the enterprise system. Interviews were conducted face to face and in English (the second language of most of the participants). Responses were audio-recorded where permission was granted (18 were not recorded). For the non-recorded interviews, extensive handwritter notes were taken. Most of the interviews were conducted at the work site, but eight interviews were conducted off site, in a more informal manner. There are difficulties associated with longitudinal investigation: granularity of data may vary over time, key participants leave the company, and organizational change can alter researcher access Despite these, we collected a continuous account of how users progressively adapted and adopted the system.

## 4.2.2 | Site visits

Two sites in the Shanghai region were visited regularly to observe the work of local enterprise system users. This included system monitoring related to accounting, management control, sales and distribution, materials manage ment and proiect system processes. These observations provided insightful and concrete information on how routine daily processes were performed. They helped capture potential differences between official processes designed b MNC headquarters and transcribed in the template, and the local practices in use at these remote subsidiaries. The observations raised questions about local practices and engendered informal conversations, offering the researcher contextual details that assisted interpretation of the formal interviews

## 4.2.3 | Archival documents

Meeting minutes, training documents and project reports were analyzed. We also had access to system data offering further insight into user actions.

Combined, these three forms of data (interviews, observation, documents) provided us with an empirical account on the same workaround practices from multiple perspectives. Moreover, the multiple periods of field research allowed for both intense episodes of data gathering as well as time spent focused on data analysis of data. Barle (1990) along with Van de Ven and Huber (1990) stress the usefulness of longitudinal research for bringing together multi-level analysis, perspectives, and data sources as it allows repeat observations to increase understanding of th research setting.

## 4.2.4 | Access to the field

Given a recent call for systematic reflexive accounts (Malaurent & Avison, 2017), we note that undertaking a fouryear longitudinal research project in China was a messy exercise, with many difficulties around access to the field especially as the legal status of the local Chinese subsidiaries evolved over time. As such, it was difficult to maintai privileged access to end users and the enterprise system. Concretely, this resulted in some periods of disconnection from the field (see Table 3). ## 5 | FINDINGS

Our empirical analysis elaborates on how the enterprise system led to user-created workarounds and how thos workarounds were formalized into work practices. We analyzed work practices with the following questions in mind: Why is something being done? What is being done? How is it being done? In total, 64 workarounds were identified Following Gasser's taxonomy (1986), 36 are classified as data adiustments. 19 as process adiustments, and 9 as par: allel system adjustments. In Table 4, we list indicative workaround practices observed in the Chinese subsidiaries selecting workarounds for each functional area covered by the enterprise system: Finance (FI), Management Contro (CO), Sales and Distribution (SD), and Material Management (MM). Columns 1–3 list the characteristics of the selected examples of business processes and categorize the associated workarounds as per Gasser (1986). In the following sections we outline three episodes. The first episode illustrated the high-level contradiction between the processes and functions embedded in the enterprise system and the requirements and expectations of local users, how this manifested in misfits and frustration, and in turn, led to users self-organizing into a community of practice. The second episode involved developing workaround practices to address the misalignment. The third episode involved the adoption and formalization of workaround practices into the enterprise system by the HQ team.

Episode: Encountering misfits and frustrations, and the emergence of self-organized learning

This episode was triggered by the contradiction between processes and functions embedded in the enterprise system by HQ and the requirements and expectations of local users. Manifestations of this contradiction are indicated in Table 4. Below we also show how it stimulated a self-organized learning process

Contradiction between local requirements and the enterprise system

Management expectations of the enterprise system were very high. While some staff were concerned about th fit of the new, system, most were expecting it to be a “magic box” (expression used by several participants) tha would perform operations automatically. However, as Chinese subsidiary users started to use the enterprise system they became frustrated and needed to make sense of how to accomplish their activities (column 4 in Table 4 pro vides examples). For instance, finance department users were frustrated by the complex and ambiguous data entr process when completing their activity:

For very simple operations such as data entry of a new customer, it takes me at least twice the time of what was needed with the legacy system. All the processes are much longer because they are much more detailed. But for me, most of these fields are useless. (Finance user 01)

Regularly users faced situations where they couldn't enter some data in the system as it was not compatible with our legal requirements. (Finance manager 01)

While some data fields were regarded as “useless”, users found that fields related to mandatory regulatory information were absent:

The worst thing is that some critical fields regarding Chinese tax calculations are missing which means that we have to double-enter data in both systems. (Finance user 02)

Other departments encountered similar difficulties. Users did not feel at ease with the system, even with the assistance of the external enterprise system functional consultants. The main concern was the lack of fit between the new enterprise system and local rules and norms

We are pushed by our managers to use the global system but even with the help of consultants, we cannot find out how to handle our business exceptions or get intermediary reports without using external spreadsheets. (Marketing user 03)

As a consequence, users continued using legacy systems in parallel to perform their work activities:

Then after go-live, we were still using both systems in parallel. All the missing information in the global system was managed through our legacy system and then loaded manually via spreadsheet files. It was extremely time-consuming, but we had no other choice to satisfy both daily business and MNC obligations (Production user 02)

Emergence of bottom-up, but organized, learning activity

The misfit and frustrations that the users experienced in completing their activities stimulated an unexpected parallel activity - that of learning and collaboration amongst local staff so as to domesticate the new system. Subjects moved outside their own activities to self-organize into a supra-activity with the objectives of learning and creatin workaround practices. This was spurred on by the unsatisfactory functional support provided by the HQ enterpris system team to address local concerns, and thus users took the lead by developing additional training sessions based on their own understanding of the system. Local staff started to feel more involved in the use and appropriation o the system as the users leading those training sessions knew their exact expectations and could anticipate thei questions. A collegial learning atmosphere rapidly developed:

As Finance Manager, I had no clear visibility on the content of these meetings, but I found it positive as the users seemed much more willing to learn and to train their colleagues. (Finance manager 01)

This local manager was also pleased that learning sessions were having positive consequences for staff motivation:

It was a great relief for me as some users became much more attracted to their job as they could train their own boss. (Finance manager 01)

The creation of this alternative and collaborative activity - a byproduct of the contradiction between HQ and Chi nese subsidiaries - took place without formal support at managerial level. Instead, it was motivated primarily by user desire to “get the job done” (expression used by a participant). In practical terms, this led to the creation of user groups and a knowledge database in which users shared tacit knowledge about the enterprise system and ways to work around the misfits and frustrations identified. An experienced user from the local project management offic explained how he used his previous experience with the system to lead the training:

I had already use SAP/PS in a previous company for almost three years. I had good knowledge and visibility of the functionalities and reports available in the system, and how to access them. This is why I rapidly became a trainer for my colleagues. (Project manager)

Groups of enterprise system users met regularly to discuss problems and share tips. Some users from the finance and production management departments created a mailing list and then an IBM Lotus Notes database to keep user aware of upcoming meetings and to share problems and user tips. These communication channels allowed users to rely on each other for assistance, with the basic objective to share useful knowledge:

Once we started to share our tips, I was amazed by how much we learned every day. Everyone was trying to bring his contribution to the group to thank the others for their previous help. (Finance user 03)

The contradictions arising from the introduction of the enterprise system manifested in local misfits and frustrations for users, and in the subsequent learning activity. In this episode, the learning activity fed into the work activi ties that were mediated by the enterprise system as the users progressed to enacting temporary resolutions– i.e workaround practices. Hence, we focus on the work activities that are subject to the workarounds.

## 5.1 | Workarounds emerge to address local needs

Our empirical investigation discovered three forms of workarounds: data adjustment, procedural adjustment, and parallel system adjustment (Gasser, 1986), as presented in Table 4. The learning activity involved users “trying things” to bridge the gap between the system and user expectations. This is evident in the quote below which shows users experimenting with different workarounds

We attended the training sessions by “Frank” as we were interested to get tips to see if they could apply them to their processes. Then we started to try things to make things faster. For example, I figured out how I could use some empty fields to track my sub-vendors. (Production user 01)

Here we discuss examples of the three kinds of workarounds detected.

## 5.1.1 | Data adjustment

In several situations, users needed to work around the formal enterprise system when entering data. For instance they entered data they knew were “inaccurate” or that did not reflect the data codification expected by the system as designed by HQ's experts so as to obtain “usable” results. For example, local accountants designed their own dat codification by adding miscellaneous sub-categories in order to track unexpected expenses of an on-going project For their perspective, the enterprise system's embedded codification for miscellaneous expenses payable did not provide sufficient granularity for local managers who were accustomed to making a distinction between different types of “miscellaneous” expenses. They also designed some new codes to account for the nature of such large accounts. Similarly, for payment terms and conditions, the accountants changed the initial codification prescribed through the global template to avoid blockage of the manufacturing process.

## 5.1.2 | Procedural adjustment

Modifying organizational procedures presented another way to work around formal computerized routines. However, the ability to work around established procedures depended on having the power, access and skills to create and exploit the capabilities of the enterprise system. Some procedures, such as “monthly closing”, were inflexible but for others, such as the “trading process”, local users found ways to overcome the misfit by using empty data fields for each enterprise system screen and then designing parallel procedures to fit their requirements. For example, within the trading process, Chinese subsidiary users regularly used a set of “fake” data to bypass the initial sale order and purchase steps that required some data entry.

Another instance of alternative practice came from the sales and distribution staff who reconfigured the bidding process by using unused data fields to capture information which was not initially accommodated in the global tem plate. Using data fields in such a manner became common practice for a number of departments

Once a purchase order is entered in the system you cannot modify the payment terms afterwards, which is awkward since the buyers do not always have this information or even worse, it could evolve over time depending on the relationship with each supplier. Then we used a field (initially empty) to put this information in the system. (Finance key user 02)

## 5.1.3 | Parallel system adjustment

A third type of workaround involved the use of parallel or backup systems, both manual and automated. For exam ple, it was found that users were obliged to calculate local tax within a third system (spreadsheet). Chinese finance regulations required some monthly reports (commonly called the “golden audit system”) that needed special dat treatment. based on the Chinese Generally Accepted Accounting Principles which are not in line with Internationa Financial Reporting Standards. This process could not be handled in the enterprise system as HQ were not aware of the specifics of each Chinese region's tax regulations. To meet this local requirement, each Chinese subsidiary had to maintain a parallel external accounting system to run at the month-end closing period.

## Episode: : Formalizing workarounds

In this episode, we move the focus away from Chinese subsidiary users to the activity of the HO team in charge of the enterprise system. How were the consequences of the workarounds addressed and by whom? When th practice of workarounds, and the extent to which users bypassed or adapted prescribed rules, was revealed to senior management at HQ there was some “shock” expressed. It made obvious to them that the objectives of the enterpris system were not being met, especially the quest for information transparency across the subsidiaries.

It is a great shock to see the spread of those workarounds in Chinese subsidiaries, but what certainly worries me most is that we cannot measure the impact of those obscure practices on our reporting system! Tell me how I am going to explain that to the executive committee. (HQ Global Enterprise System manager 01)

Following this initial reaction, HQ dedicated a team to achieving greater understanding of the extent of workaround practices, the rules and norms of the Chinese subsidiary, and the reasons for the workarounds. Their approach involved: (1) an in-depth analysis of each workaround in terms of its conseguences on the system (such as function: alities and data quality); (2) following that, a decision about whether to formalize the workaround or prevent it. Thi then led to (3) a new enterprise system user hierarchy, identifying champion users, key users, and end users. Thi was maintained on a yearly basis and aligned with current skills of local users as observed in the development of workaround practices. In the final stage, (4) the dedicated team designed a communication campaign to facilitate th integration of local Chinese procedures in the enterprise system.

In practice, 64 catalogued workarounds were scrutinized by the HQ team. A number of these practices were for malized (21 in total), while others were prevented through imposition of validation processes (28 in total). Yet other remained as workarounds (15 in total). In this way, the learning process expanded from user activities to a learning activity and up to the management team. Ultimately the workarounds were embedded in the enterprise system and became part of user activities (Foot, 2001). Over the four-year study new manifestations of the contradiction appeared, encouraging further learning processes. Regular examinations of workarounds by the HQ team mean that workarounds and formalization processes were continuously introduced. Thus, the contradiction between the MNC and its subsidiary identified in episode one was in a process of constant renegotiation, with each manifestation of misfit being a growth bud for learning (Foot, 2001). In this way the process was cumulative, rather than final

## 6 | DISCUSSION

Enterprise systems implemented across MNC headquarters and subsidiaries are typically embedded with predefined ways of working and prescribed sequences. As demonstrated by our four-year longitudinal study, the introduction of a global enterprise system at subsidiaries of an MNC may be subject to contradictory interests, pulling in different directions; between local practices and those formalized in the system, necessitating the creation of workaround practices. We have shown the development of alternative practices by enterprise system users in the Chinese sub sidiary to resolve the misfits and frustration that manifested. Our research goes beyond current theorizing of enter prise system workarounds by examining the micro-processes of their unfolding and by showing that workarounds are part of a collective learning process that involves the creation of shared knowledge and new practices. It thus addresses the suggestion that more research is needed on the what, why and how of workarounds (Azad & King 2017; Spierings et al., 2017).

The process of workaround emergence unfolded across three episodes that we conceptualized as contradiction, workarounds and formalization. Episode, commenced with the introduction of the global enterprise system and sub: sequent mismatch between MNC headquarters’ and users’ requirements. Users from Chinese subsidiaries realize that in many aspects, the global enterprise system was in contradiction to local practices. Subsequently, users began to self-organize as a community (Engeström, 2010) in order to collect and share knowledge about the enterprise sys tem and work towards making it fit with local work requirements (Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006). This phase lasted for about a year, during which users held regular meetings to share knowledge but also frustrations about the new system.

In Episode, users collectively created and introduced 64 documented workaround practices. These were not sim: ple improvisations; rather, they became unofficial rules governing local users’ work activity in tandem with the formal system. We classified these workarounds as data adjustments (36), procedural adjustments (19), and parallel system adjustments (9), meaning that most of these practices were done within the global enterprise system. Most were col lectively developed and allowed the users to collectively maintain congruence with their work objectives rather tha contradiction, and for necessary work actions to be completed. This phase of intensive workaround practices lasted for about two years.

In Episode , the learning activity moved beyond the collective of local users to encompass organizational processes and HQ managers, who responded to the inconsistencies between the enterprise system and workarounds in three important ways. First, workarounds considered as “critical” improvements through having a major positive impact on work practices, were formally implemented. Second, workarounds considered “harmless”, despite not conforming to company procedures, were permitted to continue. Third, workarounds considered a “hindrance” due to their affect on the accuracy of data but not the business workflow, were blocked through implementation of additional controls. This third phase of regulation and formalization lasted six months and led to the setup of ongoing monitoring processes, still in operation at the time of writing

Our research provides a new understanding and contribution to the literature on workarounds and on postadoption behavior of information system users. Our findings contribute to the view that these users are involved in constructing and implementing enterprise system practices. They are not passive users nor reducible to the dichotomous framing of either “accepting” or “rejecting” a system. This adds weight to the argument that the emergence and practice of workarounds should not be black-boxed. They should not be framed as undesirable, deviant or defensive behavior. Nor are they simply users’ non-compliant behavior with the organizational rules embedded in enterprise system or users’ responses to “user-hostile systems” (e.g. Koopman & Hoffman, 2003; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). Rather, our research supports the view that the contradiction between an enterprise system and local require ments should be viewed as the initiation of a learning process. Through this process enterprise system users in an activity may experience misalignment (e.g. between tools and activity objective). This demands a novel solution that may over time, lead to congruency between the users, processes, system and managers. For implementers of systems, this suggests that workaround practices should be seen as a logical and often necessary phase of adjustment and alignment between user needs and organizational demands. This matches with recent literature on organiza tional routines (Berente, Gal, & Yoo, 2010; Berente, Lyytinen, Yoo, & King, 2016), which suggests workarounds as organic manifestations of local practices. In our study, the contradiction manifested in work activity in a range of misfits and frustrations (Episode ). Users began to deviate from the newly prescribed norms, and this escalated into “col laborative envisioning and a deliberate collective change effort” (Engeström, 2001, p. 137) aimed at conceptualizing th work activity through workarounds. Clearly the users at the Chinese subsidiary proved to be knowledgeable agents with a capacity for reflecting and acting in ways other than those prescribed by headquarters. This follows the litera ture in recognizing that local actors, are highly knowledgeable and may, “know better" how technologies should be embedded in their specific context (Müller, 1980; Müller, 2010). This is evident from the high proportion of workarounds adopted (21) or allowed to continue by management (15), as opposed to those prevented (28). At the same time, the number of workarounds prevented does suggest that not all workarounds are desirable or aligne with the overall goals of the system; hence the need for organizational learning to identify which can be accommo dated and which should not. While we show links to the literature that views workarounds as an often-necessary phase of learning and development, caution is needed: some workarounds were actively blocked by managemen suggesting that they were not in line with organizational goals.

While we considered the micro-processes and “how” users form workarounds, our activity theory lens privileged a focus on collective activities. In our case, local users formed a community of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991) whic acted collectively and purposefully to tackle challenges, Our longitudinal account suggests that more attention shoulo be given to the role of communities of practice in developing and enacting workarounds (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Th learning that took place in our case study was not restricted to users and their practices. As demonstrated, learning and evolution can take place between users, managers, processes and, in terms of development, the system. Organi zational learning was initiated from the bottom up by users. So, in addition to showing that workarounds at task leve are a critical part of getting work done (Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006), we also demonstrate that they are an integra part of the institutionalization of an enterprise system (Azad & King, 2012).

While top-down rules may frustrate the users of enterprise systems, the subsequent workarounds are an essential part of learning and development (Engeström & Sannino, 2011). In MNCs where users, managers and designer of systems are distributed geographically this is particularly important. The congruency created through workaround may be temporary rather than long-term, with new contradictions emerging and new workarounds needed (Allen et al., 2013). Hence, learning activity, which was a byproduct of the implementation of the enterprise system, is a cycli cal rather than a linear process, as demonstrated in Figure 4. The mechanisms described in our discussion and repre sented in our model (Figure 4) demonstrate the relevance of activity theory concepts for analyzing and synthesizin workaround practices.

The model suggests that, in the context of a global enterprise system, the MNC agenda may be in contradiction to the local requirements of subsidiaries. Those contradictory agendas and subsequent practices are the roots of learning activities in which users engage collaboratively. As an output, the processes inscribed in the enterprise sys tem are modified by the use and spread of workaround practices. At the same time, we show how the organizatio and the enterprise system adapt to those evolving workarounds, with different levels of formalization. In our study the monitoring of on-going workarounds was necessary to avoid spiraling of adaptations.

This research makes a substantive contribution to practice by demonstrating that global enterprise systems, often considered a powerful normative tool, should be considered instead as a dynamic system. It is indeed the case that the assumed inflexibility of enterprise systems (Robey et al., 2002) can be circumvented through improvisation by non-experts. As enterprise systems for MNCs are expected to be deployed across institutional, cultural and organiza tional contexts, global templates purposefully omit specific local organizational requirements. These validation rules cannot be designed beforehand since they vary from site to site, dependent on local processes, norms and values Thus, as the localization stage of the enterprise system fails to fill specific gaps (Spierings et al., 2017), the subsidiary relies on bottom-up practices to enable the system to function in the local context. These practices can take different forms: data adjustments, process adjustments, or parallel system adjustments. Therefore, implementers of system should recognize workarounds as logical practices that are by nature unpredictable. Yet they are also an integral part of the enterprise system, addressing the contradictory expectations between subsidiary and HQ expectations

## 7 | CONCLUSION

We asked how workarounds emerge and become enacted in practice. We drew on a longitudinal study of a French MNC and the implementation of a global enterprise system in its Chinese subsidiary. We found that users were able to develop alternative practices in response to the contradiction emerging between local practices and the enterprise system as introduced. Building on the view of workarounds as part of a development process (e.g. Alter, 2014; Van den Hooff & Hafkamp, 2017), we provided a rich and unique portrayal of how workarounds unfolded in a communit of practice, later becoming embedded in formal practice, with some allowed to continue and others prevented Drawing on activity theory, we saw a learning activity unfold across three episodes. We conceptualized these as con tradictions, workarounds, and formalization.

Our findings provide deeper understanding of workarounds in three ways. First, users are involved in collective construction and implementation of enterprise system practices. They are not simply passive users or reducible to the dichotomous framing of either “accepting” or “rejecting” an enterprise system. Workarounds were not simple improvisations by individual users but were collectively developed and ultimately became unofficial local rules. Sec ond, workarounds are part of the learning process. This goes beyond the view of workarounds as deviant and non compliant behavior. Third, learning is not restricted to users and their practices. Rather, as demonstrated, learning and evolution take place between the users, managers, processes and the system; that is, workarounds are not onl a critical part of “getting work done” but an integral part of the institutionalization of an information system. Whil our empirical setting is a French MNC and its Chinese subsidiaries, we argue that our results and theoretical consid erations may be applicable to other MNC organizational settings and other contexts where the designers and man agers of enterprise systems are detached from users.

## AUTHOR BIOGRAPHIES

Julien Malaurent is an Associate Professor of Information Systems at ESSEC Business School (France). He uses qualitative approaches (in particular, case study and action research) to address issues related to the work practices of IS users in multi-cultural contexts.

Stan Karanasios is an Associate Professor in the College of Business at RMIT University in Australia and a visiting scholar at Leeds University Business School in the UK. His current research focuses on the interaction between new information and communication technologies and society and on the impact of digitalisation/digital transformation. His research is largely driven by activity theory. His research has been used to inform policy, drive organisational change and design systems. He has published in leading information systems and informa: tion management journals, including MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Journal, European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and Journal of the Association for Information Science and Technology. He has been awarded research grants from a range of government and industry sources. In addition to his research work he has undertaken a range of consulting work for industry and international bodies such as the United Nations.

## APPENDIX - INTERVIEW PROTOCOL

The sections and information listed below were used to structure the interviews. It does not mean that this protoco represents all questions asked; rather, it was used as an initial framework only, which varied according to the partici pants and their willingness to share information.

Part 1: General information about the participant

• Participant history at the company

• Job description

Part 2: Global enterprise system in comparison with the legacy systems

• Describe your daily tasks with the legacy systems.

• Did you have any previous experience with the global enterprise system prior to this one?

• What are/were your expectations regarding the global enterprise system?

Part 3: Daily use of the global enterprise system

• What does it change for your daily job?

• Do you see any gain in efficiency?

• How did you solve the initial difficulties met after its implementation?

• How do you exchange, communicate with your colleagues to learn how to use the global enterprise system?

The two interviews carried out with global enterprise system managers at the headquarters did not follow any particular protocol or framework.
