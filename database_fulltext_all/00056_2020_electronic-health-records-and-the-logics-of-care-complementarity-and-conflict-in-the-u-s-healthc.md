Abstract. The U.S. healthcare system has experienced rapid growth in the adoption and use of clinical health information technology, such as electronic health record (EHR) systems. In a field as complex and pluralistic as healthcare, the introduction of these sweeping information technology platforms is transforming the practices, roles, interdependencies and communication mechanisms that connect a heterogeneous mix of stakeholders. In this study, we assess the impact of EHR adoption and use on the practices of U.S. healthcare stakeholders and the institutional logics that they instantiate. We identify four prominent logics around the adoption and use of EHR systems—medical professionalism, private sector managerialism, technical design, and regulatory oversight. Our analysis reveals the ways in which practices enacted through EHR systems interact to engender a confluence of the institutional logics that mark the field. Specifically, we theorize a mechanism by which the reticulation, or intertwining, of practices mediated by an EHR system variously fosters complementarity and conflict between institutional logics based on the commensurability of the organizing principles invoked. This proposed framework offers a novel approach to the role of information system use in the institutional dynamics of the healthcare system. In addition, this research offers a range of practical insights regarding the ways in which the diverse institutional logics of the U.S. healthcare system may be impacted by the growth of EHR system use.

## Introduction

The continued rise in healthcare costs without commensurate improvements in health outcomes (GAO 2005, Sutherland et al. 2009) in the United States has led to a call for increased use of health information technology (HIT) to enhance efficiency, reduce errors, improve patient safety, and generally augment quality of care (Agarwal et al. 2010, Fichman et al. 2011). These arguments have taken root in recent years, with the U.S. healthcare system experiencing dramatic growth in HIT investment, most prominently focused on the proliferation of electronic health record (EHR) systems (Berner et al. 2005). In addition to the pursuit of practical benefits, this HIT investment boom is driven by governmental efforts to promote clinical HIT use. The Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act provided financial and regulatory incentives for EHR adoption and use, including a series of guidelines for escalating functionality and application dubbed meaningful use (Blumenthal and Tavenner 2010, Jones et al. 2014). The meaningful use program incorporates a range of incentive structures. Before 2016, the program offered providers a subsidy to offset the cost of EHR investment. In 2016, a “penalty” phase was introduced, with providers incurring reduced Medicare and Medicaid reimbursements for failing to meet meaningful use requirements. These incentives have had a significant effect. Federal administrators report that 85.9% of physicians have adopted some type of EHR system (ONCHIT 2019), up from roughly 50% in 2010. Hospitals have made even more progress, with 96% having a certified EHR system (Henry et al. 2016, ONCHIT 2018) up from 72% in 2011.

This dramatic rate of adoption is likely to have significant implications for the broader healthcare system. Healthcare in the United States is a remarkably complex and pluralistic field marked by constant interaction across a varied set of individuals and organizations (Blumenthal 2011, Ozdemir et al. 2011). In addition to the patients themselves, key stakeholder groups include multiple classes of health service providers (e.g., physicians, physician assistants, and nurses/nurse practitioners), clinical organizations (e.g., hospitals, testing laboratories, and long-term care facilities), payers (e.g., private insurance companies and Medicare and Medicaid programs), federal and state regulatory agencies, and credentialing entities (e.g., the Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations and the National Committee for Quality Assurance). Indeed, the fragmentation of health information across diverse systems and organizations is a primary factor undermining efficiency, impairing coordination of care, and driving up costs (Unertl et al. 2012, Gupta and Sharda 2013). As a result, the goals of interoperability and standardization are at the heart of the meaningful use program and the recent wave of EHR adoption (Kuperman 2011, Adler-Milstein et al. 2013).

Although the vision of information exchange is clear, the impact of clinical HIT adoption on the interactions of healthcare stakeholders remains ambiguous. The introduction of new information technology (IT) platforms with stringent certification requirements is likely to transform the practices, roles, interdependencies, and mechanisms of exchange across this heterogeneous mix of players. As this transformation takes shape, many questions remain regarding clinical HIT’s impact on service delivery, quality of care, and the systemic interactions of stakeholders. To explore these dynamics emerging in the U.S. healthcare system, we present an exploratory field study of diverse healthcare stakeholders around their current and planned EHR use as well as their broader outlook on clinical HIT. Specifically, we address the following research question. How has the rapid adoption of EHR technology influenced work routines and the interaction of stakeholder groups within the U.S. healthcare system?

In addressing this question, we adopt a practice perspective (Orlikowski 2000, Levina and Vaast 2006) and draw on the principle of institutional logics (Friedland and Alford 1991, Thornton et al. 2012)<sup>1</sup> to propose a theoretical mechanism for the interplay of institutional logics engendered by the adoption and use of new enterprise information systems (EISs), such as EHR. Specifically, we demonstrate how the reticulation, or intertwining, of practices mediated by an EHR system variously fosters complementarity and conflict between underlying institutional logics based on the commensurability of the organizing principles invoked. The proposed model extends our understanding of the role of institutional logics in information system (IS) adoption and use by clarifying a mechanism by which complementarity or conflict between logics emerges. In addition, this research offers a range of practical insights regarding the ways in which the diverse institutional logics of the U.S. healthcare system are likely to be impacted by the recent wave of EHR adoption.

In the next section, we offer an introduction to EHR technology as a special class of EIS. In the third section of the paper, we provide an overview of the institutional logics theory that emerged as a critical theoretical perspective during our analysis. This is followed by a discussion of our research methods, which center on the application of grounded theory methodology (GTM). We then present our key findings with regard to the role of EHR systems in conditioning key practices and the interplay of the institutional logics that mark the field. In our discussion section, we highlight the implications of our theoretical framework for research as well as the key practical implications of the study. We conclude with a reflection on the need for additional inquiry as a new healthcare system takes shape in the United States.

## Electronic Health Records

An EHR system is “a repository of patient data in digital form, stored and exchanged securely, and accessible by multiple authorized users” (Hayrinen et al. 2008). EHR systems have become a prominent area of interest in IS research, with several studies considering the factors driving adoption and innovation within the sector (Venkatesh et al. 2011, Ben-Zion et al. 2014, Yaraghi et al. 2014) and approaches to HIT investment (Reardon and Davidson 2007, Salge et al. 2015). Another stream of research focuses on the idiosyncratic data sharing and privacy concerns surrounding EHR systems and the factors impacting patients’ comfort with system use (Angst and Agarwal 2009, Ozdemir et al. 2011, Keil et al. 2018). As EHR adoption has reached critical mass, the IS community has increasingly focused on the ways in which EHRs influence organizational routines (Novak et al. 2012, Findikoglu and Watson-Manheim 2016, Burton-Jones and Volkoff 2017).

The impact of these resources on the ways of working within organizations underscores the fact that EHR systems are a form of EIS—a class of technology marked by its use by multiple functional roles/ units within an organization, the automation of workflow elements, and the incorporation of preconfigured usage and interaction patterns (Gosain 2004). For an EHR, this generally takes the form of a certified com mercial software product that (1) meets requirements for specific data entry and workflows and (2) provides integration of data and processes across a healthcare organization. An EHR supports the delivery of clinical care functions, such as recording diagnoses and treatment outcomes (Findikoglu and Watson-Manheim 2016). It also provides the platform for integrating clinical facets with coordination functions (for example, order entry) and administrative functions related to practice management and financial management. In pursuit of enhanced efficiency and organizational coordination, EHRs necessitate extensive redesign and standardization of organizational processes (Rai et al. 2015). Consequently, EHR use significantly conditions the nature of tasks, workflows, and by extension, individuals’ jobs in ways that are characteristic of EIS (Morris and Venkatesh 2010).

Given this impact on organizational routines, EIS technologies, such as her, have frequently been evaluated from a practice perspective, emphasizing the ways in which enterprise platforms are enacted in the lived practices of social actors (Boudreau and Robey 2005, Wagner et al. 2010). As the name implies, a practice perspective places a premium on how material artifacts are enacted and evolve in the practical experience of individuals rather than in an idealized procedural understanding (Orlikowski 2002, Levina and Vaast 2008). Thus, a given technology can be used by different groups of users with each group creating common work practices,<sup>2</sup> which are enacted through the technology. In the practice-oriented study of EIS, a persistent question centers on the degree to which enterprise technologies (or indeed, any IT artifacts) constrain the activities of individuals and thereby, the practices that they enact—the so-called structureagency debate (Mingers et al. 2013). Most of those adopting a practice lens argue against a deterministic view of IT’s influence, opting instead to emphasize the ways in which social actors reconstitute the structure of an EIS through adaptation and improvisation (Orlikowski 2002, Boudreau and Robey 2005). Others argue that the structures of EIS can indeed embed or inscribe the modes of enactment for various activities, establishing relevant constraints on the agency of social actors (Gosain 2004, Volkoff et al. 2007, de Vaujany et al. 2018).

In this analysis, we focus on the practical enactment of EHR technology in the daily routines of healthcare stakeholders. However, we similarly appreciate that EHR systems, which are premised on significant standardization of organizational action (as with EIS more broadly), both enable and constrain certain approaches to the enactment of practices (Gosain 2004, Findikoglu and Watson-Manheim 2016). As Orlikowski (2000) notes, “the recurrent use of a technology is not infinitely malleable. . .[T]he more a particular technological artifact is integrated into a larger system, network, or technological configuration, the narrower the range of alternative uses that may be crafted with it” (Orlikowski 2000, p. 409). As a form of EIS, EHR platforms represent just such an integration effort. Nevertheless, in keeping with a practice perspective, individuals and groups can still adapt and improvise in their use of these systems. Indeed, there is significant evidence of the potential for learning and innovation in EHR use (Strong et al. 2014).

## Institutional Logics

The U.S. healthcare system is made up of diverse organizations, professional categories, and individuals that interact to serve patients. This heterogeneous mix reflects an equally heterogeneous set of practices, values, and beliefs. The principle of an institutional logic can help us to explore these dynamic interactions, because the theory provides a perspective for evaluating the ways in which values, behavioral norms, and assumptions influence organizational and institutional change over time (Thornton et al. 2005).<sup>3</sup> Institutional theory posits that organizational structures and practices are influenced by socially constructed rules and myths guiding action in an institutional context (Meyer and Rowan 1977, DiMaggio and Powell 1983). In this tradition, institutions are understood as “ supraorganizational patterns of activity rooted in material practices and symbolic systems by which individuals and organizations produce and reproduce their material lives and render their experiences meaningful” (Thornton and Ocasio 2008, p. 101). Institutional logics, in turn, are the foundational ways of thinking and acting that characterize institutions (Friedland and Alford 1991)— “frameworks that incorporate the assumptions, beliefs, and rules through which individuals organize time and space, and which give meaning to their social reality” (Leca and Naccache 2006, p. 632). Although an institution represents a set of culturally and historically grounded social practices and behavioral patterns, an institutional logic establishes the rationale that undergirds such an institution (Jepperson 1991, Berente and Yoo 2012).

Much of the research on institutional logics focuses on competition for predominance between conflicting logics within a given institutional domain. As Dunn and Jones (2010) observe, “[s]cholars who have focused on changes in logics tend to conceptualize change as replacement, whereby a dominant logic that drives field-level practices is abandoned and another dominant logic takes its place” (Dunn and Jones 2010, p. 114). In a wide range of industries, researchers have explored conditions of conflict between (most often two) incompatible logics and the resulting contest for supremacy within the field. In healthcare, researchers have assessed the shifting prominence of competing logics within various national healthcare systems (Scott et al. 2000, Reay and Hinings 2005) as well as the implications of such conflict for systemic change initiatives (Currie and Guah 2007). Several of these healthcare studies (Jensen et al. 2009, Nigam and Ocasio 2010) highlight the conflict between a logic centered on a physician’s traditional role in guiding the care delivery (variously labeled “medical professionalism” or “the typified role of doctors”) and another logic emphasizing efficiency and cost reduction (variously labeled “business-like healthcare” or “private sector ethos”). This tension between the traditional perspective of physicians and the more recent emphasis on business outcomes in the healthcare sector has been a prominent focus of institutional logics research within the domain.

Several studies have explicitly focused on the issues confronting organizations that operate across multiple institutional domains, a situation referred to as institutional pluralism (Kraatz and Block 2008, Dunn and Jones 2010). Even in these environments, a sort of zero-sum game persists, where tensions between logics may result in open conflict, assimilation, or hybridization of one logic into another or outright shifts in the dominant logic of a domain. In this vision, equilibrium may exist for a period of time as a result of a “negotiated order” (Hallett and Ventresca 2006) or the general “ebb and flow” of competing logics (Berente and Yoo 2012), but the essential rivalry remains (Reay and Hinings 2009). Recently, scholars have questioned this antagonistic framing of interactions between institutional logics. Jarzabkowsk et al. (2013) contend that logics may either conflict with or complement one another, engendering institutional ambidexterity (that is, the ability to function across institutional domains) based on the diverse practices enacted by individual actors. Besharov and Smith (2014) similarly argue that distinct logics may exhibit compatibility, resulting in alignment (rather than mere coexistence) in organizational environments, where separate logics provide congruent prescriptions for action. In a longitudinal analysis of the pharmacy profession, Goodrick and Reay (2011) observe that conflicting and complementing logics can be grouped into “constellations”—that is, variable combinations of logics that influence a field at a given point in time. Finally, Smets et al. (2015) argue that organizations may maintain “conflicting yet complementary” logics by using various balancing mechanisms, which may themselves become institutionalized routines within an organization. However, as those authors observe “we know a lot about how [institutional logics] are kept apart, little about how they coexist, and virtually nothing about how they can positively feed off each other” (Smets et al. 2015, p. 933). This move toward exploring both conflict and complementarity between distinct logics has added a new richness to institutional analyses. However, the practical interplay between distinct logics in a field with a wide range of logics remains a pressing question.

This research seeks to address this concern through a consideration of the ways in which EHR system use influences the practices enacted within the U.S. healthcare system. As our overview of institutional logics suggests, the concept reflects significant parallels with a practice-oriented approach to EISs. Indeed, practices reflect the ways in which logics are made manifest in the world (Thornton et al. 2012). As Smets et al. (2012) observe, “[p]ractices are patterns of activities that are given thematic coherence by shared meanings and understandings. . .Practices in this sense are the material enactments of institutional logics” (Smets et al. 2012, pp. 879–880). Accordingly, the analysis of EHR’s impact on institutional logics within the U.S. healthcare system requires a consideration of the technology’s role in enabling or constraining various ways of working in healthcare settings.

## Research Methods

To explore the systemic impacts of EHR adoption, we present a field study of healthcare system participants from diverse stakeholder groups. Our data collection and analyses were conducted using GTM (Glaser and Strauss 1967, Corbin and Strauss 2015), relying on what is generally referred to as Straussian grounded theory procedures (Seidel and Urquhart 2013, Urquhart and Fernandez ´ 2013).

## Data Collection

The focus of our research shifted substantially during data collection and analysis. Initially, we set out to analyze IS governance issues within the healthcare sector in response to the clinical HIT adoption wave. The initial interviewing was conducted with HIT managers in hospitals and physicians’ practices or health services providers who had significant HIT responsibility. However, in preliminary data analyses (interspersed with data collection) (Birks et al. 2013), we identified several avenues for a deeper under standing of the dynamics reshaping the field. Specifically, our analytical memoing (primarily through written code notes and diagramming) suggested the prominence of diverse institutional forces and demands of multiple professional/organizational domains in our participants’ reflections. These included concerns over conflicting roles for private practitioners, demands of crossgroup coordination, and perceptions of change in care delivery flowing from EHR use. These observations led us to modify the theoretica sampling and data collection.

All interviews were structured around an interview protocol jointly developed by the researchers and designed to elicit responses regarding the professionals HIT experiences and service delivery practices, including day-to-day patterns of EHR use, approaches to IT investment and implementation, drivers for IT adoption, systems integration objectives, IT-based challenges, and approaches to collaboration with other entities. The interview protocol was augmented and modified as the focus of the study evolved. In addition to the protocol elements regarding EHR adoption and use, items relating to coordination between stakeholder groups (for example, health service pro viders and regulatory bodies as well as HIT managers and hospital administrators) were added, whereas some technically oriented questions concerning data governance, systems integration, and IT standards were removed. In all interviews, respondents were encouraged to express their thoughts on any topics that they felt were relevant regarding the adoption and use of HIT and information exchange in healthcare environments.

We sought participation from individuals in a variety of healthcare environments and roles, including HIT managers, health service providers (for example, physicians with diverse specialties, nurse-practitioners, and specialized service providers), healthcare payers, and information intermediaries (for example, regional health information organization (RHIO)<sup>5</sup> representatives). We did not seek participation from patients because of concerns about privacy and the protection of vulnerable populations. We believe that this was appropriate, because the majority of EHR adoption and use is organizationally based. A total of 30 interviews were conducted ranging in duration from 60 to 100 minutes. A summary of the interview respondents’ roles and labels is provided in the online appendix.

We used a “snowball sampling” technique, leveraging initial respondents to suggest other possible respondents for inclusion (Noy 2008). Participants in the first round of interviews were all located in the region of western New York state. As data collection expanded, we engaged participants from neighboring regions and states. Most of those interviewed were located in the northeastern quadrant of the United States. All but five of the interviews were conducted face to face with both researchers participating in the questioning. The remaining interviews were conducted via teleconference. Participants used a variety of distinct EHR platforms, although three vendor systems predominated in the discussions. Several of the respondents had experience with more than one EHR platform, and all participating providers and HIT managers had experience with a fully credentialed EHR when the interviews were conducted. In order to protect the confidentiality of the respondents, no quotes or statements from the interviews are attributed to specific individuals or organizations. A secondary data set of government documents highlighting the key federal initiatives around HIT adoption was collected and analyzed. These secondary sources were deemed relevant, because they embody the objectives, principles, and values underlying the effort to expand the use of IT in the U.S. healthcare system. The same data analysis techniques were used for both interview transcripts and secondary data sources.

## Data Analysis

All interviews were transcribed for formal data analysis. Interview transcripts and secondary data sources were coded using NVivo, a qualitative analysis application.

At the outset of open coding, the interview protocol served as the preliminary coding structure for the interview data. Additional codes were created as specific themes or recurring issues were identified. Most prominently, these centered on distinct roles with respect to systems use and challenges associated with HITbased information exchange. Although the initial ques tioning concerned HIT use in general, respondents quickly gravitated to EHR systems as the critical IT resources in their professional experiences. Accordingly, the coding of distinct practices that involved or relied on the use of the EHR technologies was a key aspect of the open coding phase. Themes around the concepts of intercommunity dynamics also emerged as prominent elements in the analysis. As a result, the interview protocol was modified to incorporate greater exploration of these emergent elements. The open coding process incorporated two distinct rounds of coding by both members of the research team. After each round, the researchers met to discuss their individua perspectives on the data and explore jointly the analyti cal memos created. These review sessions also included significant collective memoing with an emphasis on informal modeling to foster a shared conceptual perspective on the data set.

During axial coding, the wide variety of practices captured in open coding was organized into a series of practice categories, including those concerning the delivery of clinical care, the management of health care businesses (for example, hospitals and private practices), the management of IT resources, and coordination with regulators or other external entities (for example, RHIOs and credentialing bodies). In addition, at this stage, we considered a number of extant theories as relevant for comparison with the concepts emerging in the analysis, including the diverse literature on boundary spanning and a number of principles from institutional theory. We were careful not to force the existing theoretical structures onto our data set; rather, we used these theories to identify conceptual categories that might be relevant as axes for categorization of codes. A critical axis that emerged and represented a point of commonality between the data set and institutional logics theory was an emphasis on practices. Accordingly, distinct healthcare system practices emerged as a key unit of analysis and central basis for comparison. As noted above, this coding centered around an understanding of a practice as “the coordinated activities of individuals and groups in doing their ‘real work’ as it is informed by a particular organizational or group context” (Cook and Brown 1999, pp. 386–387), such as clinical care delivery or financial management. Consistent with an understanding of practices as comprising more granular units of activity (Schatzki 2005, Nicolini and Monteiro 2016), discrete activities or tasks associated with EHR system adoption and/or use were also coded in this process. Although activities can be executed by individual actors, practices are understood to be a social phenomenon, involving the engagement and interactions of multiple social actors. In our coding, discrete EHR adoption and use activities were related to higher-level practices based on the context of action and the objectives invoked. For example, an activity, such as data analysis, was related to multiple higher-level practices, including patient care delivery and population analysis.

With respect to the comparison with institutional logics theory, the literature presented a key challenge. Although preliminary coding suggested valuable parallels with the principle of institutional logics (for example, frequent reference to the distinct practices, values, assumptions, and organizing principles that guide action and EHR use), the institutional logics literature reflects at least two distinct conceptualizations of the relationship between practices and logics (i.e., practices as a component of the institutional logics themselves (Scott et al. 2000, Thornton et al. 2012) and practices as the instantiation of institutional logics (Jepperson 1991, Berente and Yoo 2012)). Through a series of coding and memoing sessions (for example, trial coding of the data using both framings independently), we determined that the framing of practices as the instantiation of logics (that is, logics providing the rationale for practices) reflected a stronger correspondence with our initial analyses.

A second challenge in the comparison of initial findings with extant institutional logics theory related to the diverse and loosely delineated ways in which institutional logics are captured or operationalized (Reay and Jones 2016, Zilber 2016), with researchers highlighting from four (Berente and Yoo 2012, Sauermann and Stephan 2012) to nine (Thornton and Ocasio 1999) dimensions of an institutional logic. Accordingly, we completed exploratory coding with a broad set of dimensions and subsequently eliminated dimensions (for example, focus of attention and strategy) based on redundancy with other dimensions or limited evidence of relevance to the data set. This resulted in a final set of six dimensions (Table 1). Thus, final operationalization of institutions and institutional logics was established based on our assessment of the structure that best reflected the comments of the respondents. Finally, in a selective coding phase, the dimensional categories established in axial coding were refined and consolidated to identify practices within the various healthcare domains considered as well as the institutional logics (and underlying dimensions) associated with those practices. With respect to the operationalization of institutional logics in this domain, the final dimensional structure adopted and its correspondence to the extant literature are provided in Table 1. As this process suggests, all interview transcripts and secondary data sources were iteratively coded as the final coding structure emerged.

## Practices and Institutional Logics Around EHR Adoption and Use

The respondents in our study focused much of their discussion on the ways in which EHR use has played out in their daily work routines. Consistent with prior EIS research (Gosain 2004, Volkoff et al. 2007, Berente and Yoo 2012), respondents emphasized their perceptions of how practices were altered or influenced through the use of EHR systems. Accordingly, we first highlight the prominent practices influenced by the adoption and use of EHR technology and the relationship of these practices to distinct institutiona logics. Practices are understood to be informed by particular organizational contexts, with the categories being largely differentiated by their salience to distinct stakeholder groups, such as health service providers, administrators, and HIT managers. However, a range of stakeholder groups are associated with each of the practice sets, and individual actors engage in multiple practices based on institutional roles that they play.

The category of practice most prominently affected by EHR system use was healthcare service delivery, meaning practices related to the provision of care to individual patients, including direct patient consul tation, charting of clinical data, medication management, diagnostic testing, and coordination of care between multiple service providers. Key stakeholders associated with these practices include diverse classes of health service providers (for example, physicians, nurses, and other clinicians) as well as various classes of diagnostic testing professionals. The perception of EHR’s impact among these health service providers focused on concerns over potential detrimental changes, particularly around the practices of patient consultation and charting.

The third set of practices around the adoption of EHR is those most focused on the EHR technology itself, namely HIT management, a category that includes systems implementation and maintenance (and enhancement when changes are deemed necessary), data management and integration (especially with health information exchanges, such as an RHIO), and management of relationships with EHR vendors. The key actors associated with this set of practices are HIT managers, information brokers, the software vendors, and private practitioners. In contrast to other sets of practices highlighted in the study, the practices of HIT management were not discernibly changed by EHR adoption, but the need for these practices was dramatically increased, because many healthcare organizations experienced the first significant IT investment in many years.

I think part of it [that is, motivation for EHR adoption] was a realization that just technology-wise in general we were way behind the times. The previous director had been here for 10 years. There were, for the most part, just two people in the IS organization for most of that time. —Elder Services IT Director 2

I contrast that scenario with my Practice Management System. I have had two upgrades in 28 years. The most recent was an upgrade for Y2K. —Health Service Provider 6

A final category of practices impacted by EHR adoption and use is regulatory compliance, which refers to practices to ensure compliance with requirements of state and federal regulators as well as credentialing bodies. Practices in this category include compliance verification, evaluation of healthcare organizations and individual service providers, population-level analysis of outcomes and trends, and EHR system certification. The central stakeholders associated with this category are healthcare administrators, regulators and public health agencies, credentialing entities, and once again, private practitioners. Because EHR adoption was largely driven by regulatory initiatives, such as meaningful use, it is not surprising that the practices related to regulatory compliance have been significantly impacted by rising EHR use. Indeed, given meaningful use requirements on data entry (Blumenthal and Tavenner 2010), these impacts ripple back from compliance reporting to patient encounters with a provider. In general, the use of EHR systems and associated information exchange has dramatically enhanced the speed and scale of reporting and analysis activities across all of these practices.

We’re going to have a dashboard within our [vendor] system to show our path towards meaningful use after we go live April. —Rural Hospital IT Director 3

[The hospital management is] even using the EHR to monitor now what antibiotic we use for what type of infection. Some of it’s used for regulatory compliance. Some of it’s used for internal clinical excellence. —Health Service Provider 5

[Regulators are] looking for, “You have X number of people over 65, and 90 percent of them got their flu shot. Show me that.” That kind of stuff. The overlap between the practice sets vis-a-vis \` stakeholder groups is noteworthy. None of the practice sets are unique to a single group of stakeholders, and a number of stakeholders (most notably, private practitioners and healthcare administrators) are associated with multiple sets of practices. Indeed, private practitioners have some engagement with each set of practices as they juggle the myriad demands of running a medical practice.

Although the practice categories cannot be isolated to individual stakeholder groups, our analysis suggests that they are associated with distinct institutional logics that give them meaning. This is consistent with institutional logics theory, where practices are understood to be instantiations of distinct institutional logics (Smets et al. 2012). More exactly, institutional logics and their associated practices have a bidirectional relationship, with a practice enacting a logic and the logic justifying the practice.

We identify four distinct institutional logics in our analysis—medical professionalism, private sector managerialism, technical design, and regulatory oversight. Although two of these logics, medical professionalism (Reay and Hinings 2009, Nigam and Ocasio 2010) and private sector managerialism<sup>6</sup> (Jensen et al. 2009, Harris and Holt 2013), have precedents in prior research on healthcare and HIT, the other two logics have not been previously explored. The four logics identified along with their composite dimensions are summarized in Table 2 and discussed below. In the online appendix, we provide exemplary statements from the data to illustrate all of the dimensions.

## Medical Professionalism

Consistent with extant research on institutional logics in healthcare, we see abundant evidence of a medica professionalism logic—a core focus on the role of the physician in guiding the delivery of care (Reay and Hinings 2005, Nigam and Ocasio 2010). Medical professionalism is associated with the practices of healthcare service delivery. In this logic, the autonomy of the physician in consultation with a patient is paramount—the professional judgment of a physician should determine the appropriate strategy for treatment, with determinations regarding the proper role of IT resources resting with the provider.

## Private Sector Managerialism

The U.S. healthcare system is composed of a range of interconnected for-profit and not-for-profit businesses, including both payer (for example, insurance companies and Medicaid and Medicare programs) and provider (for example, hospitals and private medical practices) entities. For each of these organizations, self-preservation through effective business management is a primary objective. In our analysis, the institutional logic of private sector managerialism is a core rationale underlying the practices of business management. This logic reflects a significant concern with financial performance (for example, cost control, revenue generation, and profitability), process improvement, and competitive positioning. Improved organizational performance is also a primary driver for participation in RHIOs because of the potential for efficiency and cost reduction.

## Technical Design

Reflecting our interest in the impacts of EHR adoption and the nature of our theoretical sampling, the third prominent logic is technical design.<sup>7</sup> Tied to the practices of HIT management, technical design emphasizes the quality of technical solutions and information system designs in the development, implementation, and maintenance of HIT infrastructure. Technical design relates to what Davidson (2002) describes as a frame of “IT capabilities and design.” Among our respondents, this logic is revealed in several related themes, including concerns for an entity’s status visa-vis the state of the art in IT use, access to speci\` fic IT functionality, the value of communications infrastructure, and the technical quality of vendor platforms. Not surprisingly, the logic was largely observed among respondents with IT management responsibilities.

## Institutional Reticulation: Practices and Logics

The institutional pluralism of U.S. healthcare raises questions regarding the ways in which diverse institutional logics interact and influence stakeholders within the field. Consistent with observations concerning earlier waves of EIS adoption (Gosain 2004, Wagner et al. 2004), all of the respondents in our study perceived that the clinical and administrative practices of healthcare have changed in significant ways through support for certain modes of enactment within EHR systems. Even as we acknowledge the potential for improvisation and emergence around EHR use (for example, the introduction of scribes in patient consultations), we contend that this rapid adoption of EHR technology represents an exogenous change to the U.S. healthcare system, with interactions between distinct practices and the institutional logics underlying them increasingly centered on EHR platforms. This unifying effect, however, means that the features and functionality of EHR systems are influencing the nature of logics themselves and the ways in which they interact.

Consistent with recent institutional logics research (Besharov and Smith 2014, Smets et al. 2015), our analysis suggests that the use of EHR systems simultaneously accentuates points of complementarity and conflict between logics. The concerns expressed by many respondents suggest differences in perspective around the design and use of EHR and the factors driving its adoption. Consider the following statements.

We’re being pushed, as in we don’t want to do this. We’re happy with our system. But we’re being pushed by the government, and the insurance companies, and the payers. We think it’s for them. It’s not for us, it’s for them. —Health Service Provider 1

Overall, it is very time consuming. We spend about 50% of our time in our monthly meeting talking about how to meet the meaningful use criteria. If we don’t, it will have a major financial impact on us. We used to be able to really discuss how we were treating our patients and delivering better care. We can’t do that anymore. We just focus on meeting the criteria and our attention to better care has fallen away. —Health Service Provider 4

Despite clear indications of conflict, points of alignment between distinct logics emerge around the benefits that EHR technology can bring to such critical facets as data analysis and support of clinical decision making.

[With data analysis] you start to hone in [on] who are the patients that are not going, and what are the barriers that are causing them not to go [that is, for preventive services]? So then from a continuous quality improvement perspective, the reality is that I would then go ahead and do some root cause analysis to [the reason]. . .What are those barriers? Then can I break those barriers? —Health Insurance Provider

I view integration as the IT piece of the continuum of care. So a patient goes from their doctor’s office to the hospital maybe for short term rehab. We want to make sure that their record and their information follows them the entire way. So that whoever is providing care to them has the most accurate and up-to-date information possible. —Rural Hospital IT Director 2

Given the simultaneous occurrence of conflict and complementarity even between the same two observed logics, the fundamental tension or alignment cannot be sought in the inherent characteristics of the logics themselves. What then is the mechanism by which such patterns of conflict or complementarity occur? What determines the degree to which distinct logics are brought into alignment within a given institutional field? Based on our analysis, we posit a mechanism by which complex patterns of interaction emerge.

We observe that practices are intertwined, or reticulated, through the enactment of common activities.<sup>8</sup> For example, in the use of EHR systems, codebased data capture is a lower-level activity required for the enactment of multiple distinct practices, including both charting in the provision of healthcare services and billing in the financial administration of healthcare organizations. Similarly, data analysis is an EHR use activity within several distinct practices, including healthcare provision (for example, physicians analyzing patient trends), business process management (for example, administrators assessing intake processes), and population-level outcome analysis (for example, regulatory entities evaluating public health trends). Because practices instantiate distinct logics, the reticulation of practices achieves the confluence of two or more institutional logics, drawing on the organizing principles that make up those logics. Yet, distinct logics may draw on related organizing principles.

Based on our analysis, the commensurability of organizing principles—that is, the degree to which they enable a common basis for comparison and evaluation (Espeland and Stevens 1998, Friedland 2009)—between distinct logics and with respect to a focal EHR adoption or use activity is critical to determining whether the associated logics can be brought into harmony or conversely, engender institutional conflict. In the example of code-based data capture, the logics of medical professionalism (as enacted in the practice of charting) and private sector managerialism (enacted in the practice of billing) draw on discrete and conflicting organizing principles. In the case of medical professionalism, health service providers engage in charting with an eye to patient care and the assurance of continuity of care across provider classes (that is, the organizing principle of “patient focus”). By contrast, from the logic of private sector managerialism, administrators or private practitioners engage in billing with an eye to revenue generation (that is, the organizing principle of “financial management”). It is in just such cases of incommensurability between organizing principles that we see evidence of conflict between logics, particularly when the enactment of practices conditioned by an EHR system is perceived to favor the organizing principles of one logic over another. With respect to the design of charting functionality, one physician observed the following.

This whole business about EHR helping with communication I think is a total fallacy. I think it really hinders communication. . .But it makes total sense, because when you design a system, you designed it with an end in mind. Well, the purpose of the systems is for insurance companies to be able to have the data so they can document a bill and pay accordingly. —Health Service Provider 2

In other cases, two distinct logics may draw on similar or consistent organizing principles to support a shared EHR use activity. In the case of data analysis, the logics of medical professionalism, private sector managerialism, and regulatory oversight can all be invoked around an organizing principle of continuous improvement. Although such continuous improvement may be applied to different underlying phenomena (patient outcomes, business performance, and public health outcomes, respectively), the core focus on improving results based on analysis of data is commonly held across the three logics. Indeed, when the organizing principles around shared EHR adoption/use activities are consistent, we see evidence of complementarity between distinct logics. In the context of data analysis, one physician highlighted the value that more widespread adoption of such functionality could provide.

Why not allow physicians to learn from the data? We enter stuff and it goes out into this world of analysis, and we don’t get any feedback on it. . .Why not make data available to physicians, the number of UTIs, and the best antibiotic treatments, and what’s working? I would love to see that, in the organization and in the communities—data to help us be smarter. —Health Service Provider 5

## A Framework for IT-Mediated Institutional Pluralism

Building on this analysis, we posit a mechanism (Hedstrom and Ylikoski¨ 2010, Illari and Williamson 2012) by which complementarity and conflict between distinct institutional logics emerge through the use of an IT artifact, such as an EHR system. As with other EISs (Gosain 2004, Volkoff et al. 2007), EHR systems are premised on a degree of standardization around the enactment of practices.

This conditioning of practices gives the EHR platform a central role in mediating the interaction of the observed practices. As we have argued, each practice is the enactment of a certain institutiona logic (Smets et al. 2012). However, distinct practices may share common activities, resulting in the confluence of those practices and the institutional logics that they instantiate. At a surface level, the common activities create points of interaction between different functions and roles within and across organizations (for example, health service providers and administrators). More fundamentally, because each of the practices is an instantiation of a distinct institutional logic (Smets et al. 2012), the interweaving of practices through shared activities results in the reticulation of institutional logics. In this way, the common activity—as it is enacted through the use of an IT artifact—is a mediating agent for the interaction of institutional logics. Based on the alignment (or lack thereof) between the organizing principles of the logics with respect to the common activity, distinct institutional logics are perceived to be complementarity or conflicting around that activity. Thus, when the organizing principles of two logics are consistent vis-a-vis a common activity, the logics are comple-\` mentary. In contrast, when the organizing principles of each logic are not consistent or commensurable, the logics conflict (Espeland and Stevens 1998, Friedland 2009).

The confluence of practices and logics through the enactment of common activities is key to the proposed mechanism. As noted earlier, a given institutional logic and its associated practices have a bidirectional relationship—the practice enacts the logic, and the logic gives meaning to or justifies the practice. Intertwining multiple practices and logics extends and changes these relationships based on complementarity or conflict. As outlined in Table 3, complementary logics have organizing principles that are shared or commensurable (Espeland and Stevens 1998, Friedland 2009). For example, medical professionalism, private sector managerialism, and regulatory oversight all share an organizing principle of continuous improvement in the common EHR use activity of data analysis. Although the focus of improvement varies across the practices and associated logics within which the activity is enacted (for example, a patient’s condition, effectiveness of a business process, and population-level health outcomes) the commonality of the organizing principles creates a basis for commensurability of those practices and logics (Friedland 2009).

To establish a generalized view of the proposed dynamic, consider two distinct practices, Practice A and Practice B (Figure 1). These practices instantiate distinct institutional logics: Institutional Logic 1 and Institutional Logic 2, respectively. Although instantiating separate logics, Practices A and B share a common lower-level activity (Activity α). That is, Activity α is part of both practices, creating a point of intersection between the two practices and their associated logics. In the scenario of complementarity, Activity α is supported by similar organizing principles in both practices, implying a point of perceived complementarity between the associated logics. In the second scenario, another common activity (Activity β) is similarly shared by two distinct practices (in this case, Practices C and D). However, in this case, Activity β is undergirded by distinct and incom mensurate organizing principles vis-a-vis those prac-\` tices. Consequently, the practices and their associated logics are perceived to be in conflict.

The designation of “points” of conflict and complementarity is significant. A focus on the intertwining of practices via common activities as the mechanism through which logics interact moves us away from a monolithic perspective of logics as inherently conflicting or complementary in nature. As noted in our analysis, specific institutional logics may be associated with multiple distinct practices in which enactment is at least partially influenced by the properties of an EHR system. Moving beyond the isolated case of two practices and their associated logics, the diverse practices conditioned by EHR or other EISs form a reticulated web around a diverse set of common system use activities. Because each practice is an instantiation of a logic, the secondary effect of common activities is the confluence of multiple institutional logics at several points within an institutionally pluralistic environment. Because the dynamics of conflict and complementarity occur at the level of relatively granular activities, various logics may be perceived to clash around one set of practices while aligning around others. We refer to this dynamic intertwining of multiple logics in networked patterns of conflict and complementarity as the reticulation of institutional logics.

The principle that the reticulation of logics within the institutional field of healthcare is founded on the intertwining of practices has some significant implications. By enabling and constraining the enactment of practices, an EHR fosters a certain perceived balance of logics within this reticulated web. With the use of these systems, organizational practices are transformed in an effort to match, adapt, or work around the functional properties of the EHR. Through social and habitual reinforcement, the routinization of practices imposes certain constraints on the ability of individuals or groups to modify or adapt practices further, reifying the perceived balance between logics supported by a given EHR platform. The reticulation of logics engenders significant institutional complexity, which presents challenges and inconsistencies for individuals and organizations enacting practices through these comparatively new EHR systems (Greenwood et al. 2011). Multiple factors, such as identity, organizational role, profession, field, organization structure, and governance, shape individual and organizational responses to the technology’s adoption and use. Encountering this institutional complexity, individuals and organizations exercise agency in determining how to achieve their objectives (Smets et al. 2012), but the potential for adaptation in EHR use is not unlimited. The deeper question that these observations engender is the impact that such changed practices and the resulting reticulation of institutional logics will have on the broader U.S. healthcare system.

## Discussion

The healthcare field is distinguished by the significance that it holds for every member of society. Eventually, each of us relies on the many stakeholder groups that make up the healthcare system to provide services that we need to enjoy a long and healthy life. Accordingly, all have a vested interest in the changes that are shaping the field through the rapid adoption of EHR and other clinical HIT systems. Based on our analysis, there are a number of theoretical and practical implications that warrant deeper consideration.

In this study, we propose a mechanism by which complex patterns of perceived complementarity and conflict between multiple institutional logics can emerge through the practices enacted using EHR platforms. Specifically, by enabling and constraining modes of enactment for various practices, EHR systems may simultaneously highlight distinct points of conflict and complementarity between logics based on the commensurability of the organizing principles undergirding those practices. By articulating a mechanism through which distinct logics may be brought into confluence around practices and shared system use activities, we believe that the model provides a means to move beyond the assumption of inherent conflict and/or complementarity between distinct logics. Furthermore, the framework reveals how the use of IS resources that both enable and constrain those practices can engender perceptions of complementarity and conflict.

We believe that the reticulation model warrants additional consideration both within and beyond the healthcare context to assess its value for mapping the interplay of institutional logics. In healthcare, application to other national healthcare systems might shed light on the degree to which the dynamics that we observe are unique to the United States, where individual and organizational identities may be more narrowly defined. Beyond healthcare, the reticulation model has implications for the broader category of EIS technologies and their potential to mediate the interaction of logics in an institutional field. The potential for networks of complementarity and conflict surrounding EIS use extends recent movements away from a binary treatment of the relationships between institutional logics (Besharov and Smith 2014, Smets et al. 2015). Even in the study of institutional pluralism (Kraatz and Block 2008, Dunn and Jones 2010), a zero-sum game perspective persists, where tensions between logics result in open conflict, assimilation of one logic into another, or shifts in the dominant logic of a field. Although this vision of competition has been augmented recently with a recognition that complementarities may exist between logics (Jarzabkowski et al. 2013, Besharov and Smith 2014), we believe that this study is among the first to explore how distinct logics may simultaneously complement and conflict with one another.

Even more broadly, the reticulation model provides a lens for evaluating the interplay of institutional logics through other IS tools that substantively frame the enactment of practices (for example, through extensive workflow automation and execution of decision rules). This category of IS includes such emergent technologies as autonomous systems and other artificial intelligence applications as well as blockchain platforms. Currently, prototype autonomous systems are being implemented with ethical reasoning and emotional processing facets (Arkin et al. 2012). As autonomous systems spread into less constrained contexts, questions regarding the foundational logics on which their ethical programming is developed will proliferate. Although a driverless train in an airport operates in a highly constrained environment, autonomous vehicles will be required to interpret diverse ethical values and make life or death decisions. Similarly, as automated financial portfolio management systems and blockchain infrastructure proliferate in financial services, these systems will raise significant questions concerning encoded fiduciary reasoning, trust, and privacy (Beck et al. 2017, Mulvey 2017). To the extent that these tools reflect distinct institutional logics (for example, in the case of autonomous vehicles, logics of safety, transportation, and enjoyment might all apply), the execution of practices via such systems will engender points of perceived conflict and complementarity. Exploring this interplay of logics—as well as the potential for unintended consequences—will be critical as broader and broader societal and organizational routines are executed with limited human intervention.

Our study also illustrates how a “conflicting-yetcomplementary” balance can exist within a broader network of multiple logics. The metaphor of reticulation underscores the value of a systems-oriented perspective on logics within an institutional field. Although much of the institutional analysis of logic in healthcare and other domains has focused on just two prominent logics, this study suggests the benefit of analyzing diverse interactions of multiple logics and the ways in which they may be leveraged in combination. We contend that the development of such a reticulated perspective on institutional logics is necessary in a field as complex as the U.S. healthcare system. By considering the networked interplay between logics, we seek a more nuanced understanding of the “field of forces” (Berente and Yoo 2012) at play in the use of EIS.

Finally, the study underscores the need for more theoretical exploration of the institutional shifts that EHR adoption may engender. As with shifts in institutional logics in earlier periods (Scott et al. 2000) and other national contexts (Currie and Spyridonidis 2016), our study suggests that efforts to create an ITenabled U.S. healthcare system may alter the prevalence of certain logics and organizational structures. Such alterations warrant continued focus as meaningfu use expands and evolves. Relevant questions include the following. How does the movement toward greater integration of health records across geographic ranges impact prevailing institutional logics? How will these changes impact organizational structures? What role do EHR vendors have in shaping the institutional logics of the field? To what degree do various EHR features impact the perceived “balance of power” among stakeholders through the conditioning of practices?

In the realm of practice, this research highlights critical considerations for various stakeholders, including HIT-adopting organizations, policy makers, and EHR vendors. In the wake of EHR adoption much social commentary has focused on the shortcomings or disadvantages of these systems, including concerns over physician burnout, declines in physician job satisfaction, and an increased potential for insurance fraud (Ries 2014, Farr 2016, Shanafelt et al 2017). Given these emerging concerns, our observations regarding points of conflict and complementarity in the use of EHR systems can inform efforts to foster consensus and confront challenges surrounding EHR use and public policy. Most fundamentally, our findings suggest that initiatives to redesign the U.S. healthcare system through the adoption and use of clinical HIT should be informed by the complex institutional dynamics surrounding such IT investment. Points of complementarity suggest opportunities to foster a common vision around clinical HIT. For example, the broad appeal of enhanced data exchange and analysis that we observed may represent common ground in promoting reform of the healthcare system. As policy makers and institutional actors consider new applications of clinical HIT, a persistent focus on facets where organizing principles align—for example, enabling the systemic analysis of health outcomes and augmenting judgment in care delivery—can serve to broaden support for such initiatives.

Conversely, points of conflict between logics can highlight facets that warrant additional attention in efforts to promote expanded use of clinical HIT. For example, with respect to health service providers concerns that EHRs impede the physician-patient relationship through information overload and the overlay of a data management orientation (observations that mirror extant research on EHR adoption (Shachak and Reis 2009, O’Malley et al. 2010)), stakeholders and policy makers may benefit from considering how EHR systems both enable and constrain healthcare practices and identifying design factors that can address sources of conflict. In what ways can organizations or EHR vendors refine their systems to support the organizing principles of both administrative entities (for example, payers, regulators, and administrators) and service providers (for example, physicians and nurses)? Because many health service providers perceive existing systems to favor the interests of administrative stakeholders, can platform interfaces or data structures be redesigned to recognize the information needs of providers in their interactions with patients? Ultimately, the degree to which EHR systems engender complementarities and conflict between institutional logics has important policy implications. If changes to practices associated with widespread EHR use may inadvertently augment some institutional logics at the expense of others, system stakeholders must consider whether the promotion of EHR adoption at the governmental level will have lasting impacts on the fundamental character of healthcare delivery in the United States.

## Limitations

Although we believe that this analysis provides a valuable exploration of the impact that EHR adoption and use have had on the practices and institutional logics in the U.S. healthcare system, a number of limitations should be acknowledged. Although the research aims for analytical rather than probabilistic generalizability (Eisenhart 2008), the degree to which our respondents are representative of the broader field is a valid question. We engaged in theoretical sampling, seeking participation from multiple stakeholder groups rather than narrowing the study to one class of respondents. However, we recognize that certain perspectives have been omitted from the analyses. Although we deliberately opted not to include patients in our respondent pool out of concern for privacy and the protection of vulnerable populations, we acknowledge that this limits the generalizability of our insights into all facets of healthcare. Furthermore, our findings pertain to the U.S. healthcare system only. We believe that this focus is a strength of the study, but we recognize that it may limit the generalizability of our theoretical arguments given the idiosyncrasies of national health systems.

## Conclusion

In this study, we have considered the rapid adoption of EHR technology within the evolving U.S. healthcare system with an emphasis on its implications for the interplay of institutional logics in the field. Specifically, we have focused on the impact of increased adoption of EHR technology on the practices and interactions of diverse healthcare stakeholders and the institutional logics that they instantiate. This research offers a number of significant contributions. We theorize a mechanism by which reticulation of the practices mediated by an EHR system variously fosters complementarity and conflict between institutional logics based on the commensurability of the organizing principles invoked. By articulating a mechanism that can engender the interaction of logics, this model offers a novel approach to the role of IS use in the institutional dynamics of the U.S. healthcare system. In addition, our findings reveal a wider range of observable institutional logics and more intricate interrelationships than have been previously explored in the healthcare context. Importantly, this reticulation of institutional logics in the healthcare system is accentuated by the EHR systems themselves, which function as a conduit for a broad range of practices and the institutional logics that they instantiate. Consequently, to achieve the promise of a more integrated and efficient healthcare system, healthcare stakeholders must grapple with the ways in which institutional logics and their interactions are likely to be impacted by ex panded EHR use.
