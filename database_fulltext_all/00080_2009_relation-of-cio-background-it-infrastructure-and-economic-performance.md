# Relation of CIO background, IT infrastructure, and economic performance

Marion G. Sobol <sup>a,</sup>\*, Gary Klein <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> E.L. Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, Dallas, TX 75275, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 16 August 2007 Received in revised form 25 September 2008 Accepted 9 May 2009 Available online 27 May 2009

Keywords: Chief information officer Information technology infrastructure Financial performance Leadership

## A B S T R A C T

The literature of the past three decades has not provided a consistent picture of payback for IT investment. Firm strategies and infrastructure play a part in determining return on investment and, in recent years, chief information officer (CIO) characteristics have been cited as essential factors in ensuring economic returns for IT investment. In our study we related CIO background and attitude toward IT investment to the objective measures of a firm’s performance. The financial measures tended to be higher when the CIO was from IT rather than general management, however, an IT manager in a firm that had a strategic orientation to IT rather than a utilitarian one was more likely to have higher financial performance. Thus, although a CIO should have a technological background, the CIO who has a strategic rather than utilitarian orientation is more likely to help in forming a highly profitable company. - 2009 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

Numerous facets of IT have been examined to determine their relationship to economic performance. Overall, the literature has shown a mixed picture with respect to the payback from IT investment [7,21]. Researchers have dissected and collected data in a variety of fashions including longitudinal and event studies, variations by industry, and resource relationships [5,8,18]. More conclusive results occurred when looking at performance issues through different lenses, such as infrastructure strategies, capabilities and processes, and firm strategies [12,19,22]. Recently, CIO leadership and performance has been considered a major area of importance, with promising directions and early results [10].

The role of a chief information officer (CIO) in major companies was introduced in the 1970s and CIO responsibilities grew through subsequent decades. In addition to knowledge of the technical function and the need for seamless service delivery, the CIO was expected to understand the business aspects of the organization, reduce costs, and add to the value of their firm’s IT resources [23]. CIOs were selected from different backgrounds than technology management to insure that they would have general management skills. But the role of the CIO still included the creation and management of the organization’s IT infrastructure, requiring a technology orientation. Over the years, the CIO has moved into the boardroom, becoming a key figure in pushing decisions, operational strategy, strategic planning, marketing and sales [6].

However, very little is known about the typical characteristics of the CIO and the directions taken by an organization. Our intent was to explore several relationships between elementary background characteristics of corporate CIOs, the IT orientation of the firm, and select financial measures of the firm’s performance. We hoped to determine whether elemental background characteristics of the CIO related to a strategic orientation of the firm’s IT or to more tactical IT infrastructure investment, and eventually to performance of the firm.

## 2. Background

For 30 years a globally strategic role for the CIO has been advocated and thus the CIO should help in setting organizational policies and strategies. This dual technical and business orientation and the imperative holistic, cross-functional view of the organization pose unique challenges. In addition, cross-cultural differences necessitate CIO knowledge and skill in handling global differences in international sectors of the organization. Thus, leadership and management skills are necessary.

Numerous studies on IT leadership populate the literature. In a seminal study, Enns et al. [9] examined whether CIO’s with greater or lesser technical orientation used different behaviors and ability to influence other top executives successfully. From a survey of 69 matched pairs of CIO’s and CEO’s, they found that the technical orientation of the CIO did not appear to influence behaviors in top peer contexts. However, the technology orientation of the target executive played equally an important role in determining which tactics were successful as did the management role played by the CIO [16]. No studies, however, have considered the effect of infrastructure investments on the economic performance of the company.

Investments in infrastructure have different economic impact, depending on whether the investment is transactional, informational, or strategic. There is good evidence that transactional IT pays off in terms of measures of profit such as return on assets (ROA) with an average return of 25–40% on each dollar of transactional information technology investment, mainly by cutting costs. No consistent impact had been found on financial measures of performance due to investment in informational IT. On the other hand, investments in strategic uses of IT, which are more common in the financial than manufacturing industries, have had positive impacts on ROA in the third year after investment. Firms investing in strategic uses of IT exhibited faster time to market, were able to charge premium prices, and had higher revenue per employee and higher revenue growth in finance. Strategic investment in technology was a high risk–high return part of the portfolio, making infrastructure the major task of the CIO.

Often the orientation of the CIO depends on his or her demographic surrogate (e.g., age) and whether the role in the firm is considered to require IT or general management skills. A study of more than 545 heads of IT found that while 71% said that their primary job experience had been IT oriented, 44% also had business operations experience in a non-IT field [17]. Forty two percent had been working in consulting, 25% in administration, 22% in engineering, 22% in finance or accounting, and 18% in sales. Their view of IT’s role in the organization varied. Seventy three percent felt that IT should proactively envision business possibilities and initiate technology while the rest felt that IT should support and enable predefined business initiatives. While papers have concentrated on the characteristics of the CIO and their relation to strategic performance or to innovation, few studies have attempted to relate these characteristics to the economic performance of the firm.

Leaving IT adoption to individual business units and dispensing with centralized control is now seldom advocated. There must be strategic alignment between IT capabilities and business strategies. Research on strategic leadership by CIO’s has followed seven general paths: the role of the CIO, the role of CEO in IT leadership, CIO effectiveness, the CIO/CEO inter-relationship, the CIO’s organizational impact, IT strategic alignment, and the CIO’s characteristics.

Upper Echelon theory has frequently been employed to explain the shaping of strategic choices within an organization: a CIO as a leader can shape policy on the deployment of infrastructure throughout the organization and can decide whether or not an organization emphasizes strategic or operational use of technology. When a strategic decision is required, the CIO must bring all cognitive resources (including knowledge and leadership style) to bear. We employed upper Echelon theory to predict general relations and act as a framework for exploring detailed relationships between the characteristics of the CIO, the policies they follow, and which result in better financial performance. Fig. 1 shows the relationships we investigated between CIO characteristics, orientation, infrastructure and objective financial performance.

First, we attempted to determine whether some of the characteristics of the CIO are statistically related to his or her strategic orientation towards IT, the IT infrastructure directions of the firm, or to the financial performance of the firm, as indicated by published financial measures. CIO characteristics that might influence decisions and performance (such as education and technical position within the firm) were included. Second, we consider relationships between the strategic orientation, infrastructure directions, and financial performance. The capabilities of a sound infrastructure were represented by several structural categories that collectively enable a firm to respond more fully to information needs and lead to improved financial performance [4]. The IT orientation of the firm was considered as either strategic, encouraging innovative approaches of IT to support long-term goals, or as a utilitarian, emphasizing rigid support of operations. Each relationship-link becomes a testable hypothesis, as discussed next.

## 3. Hypotheses

Upper Echelon theory considers that performance of an organization is dependent on the characteristics of its leadership. It may seem a trivial to argue that relationships such as educated and experienced executives are needed to ensure higher organizational performance. However, past studies have not been able to confirm this, possibly due to differences in industry or variables that could serve as controls or other antecedents. Essentially, the expectations of many studies were that a characteristic that strengthened a strategy was a strong predictor of success and that success varied as characteristics and positioning of the CIO differed [11]. Formally, we postulate:

Hypothesis 1 : There is no significant relation between CIO characteristics and financial performance.

Hypothesis 1 : There is a significant relation between CIO characteristics and financial performance.

According to upper Echelon theory, top executives shape an organization’s strategic choices and direction. For CIOs, this leads to consideration of the purpose of IT within the organization. Our expectation was that age, CIO position/orientation, and education would affect the intent of the technology deployed, whether focused more on efficiency or on strategic considerations. Previous studies have indeed found distinct differences between executive characteristics and the strategies that a firm pursues. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2 : There is no significant relation between CIO characteristics and the IT orientation of a firm.

Hypothesis 2 : There is a significant relation between CIO characteristics and the IT orientation of a firm.

When considering investments of an organization, we began by assuming that infrastructure investments were under the control of the CIO, that the power to set strategic investment directions was also at this level. In addition, we assumed that a CIO’s preference for various levels of infrastructure spending were associated with CIO characteristics (age, education, and technical position, etc.). Similar structures have held for CEOs and investment decisions [1]. Formally:

Hypothesis 3 : There is no significant relation between CIO characteristics and the organization’s IT infrastructure investments.

Hypothesis 3 : There is a significant relation between CIO characteristics and the organization’s IT infrastructure investments.

Performance of an organization is based on the strategic application of resources. Thus appropriately applied resources can lead to competitive advantage and improved performance. The IT infrastructure is designed to provide the means to implement essential information functions in a timely manner in order to exceed, or at least meet, the competition. Thus, we proposed:

Hypothesis 4 : IT infrastructure is not significantly related to objective measures of financial performance. Hypothesis 4<sub>a</sub>: IT infrastructure is significantly related to objective measures of financial performance.

The resource-based view anticipates intelligent deployment. ‘‘Senior executives make few choices more critical than deciding which IT investments will be needed for future strategic agility . . . those choices can significantly enable or impede business initiatives . . . managers must anticipate future strategic moves and make often-complex assessments about how the IT infrastructure must adapt to support the enterprise’’ [24]. Initiatives to support the strategic view should yield better performance than a simple utilitarian view that only supports existing operations. This argues for our final hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5<sub>0</sub>: There is no significant relation between IT orientation of the firm and financial performance.

Hypothesis 5a: There is a significant relation between IT orientation of the firm and financial performance.

## 4. Methodology

A questionnaire was constructed to gather self-reported information from CIOs on their backgrounds, perceived status, extent of implementation of 25 infrastructure components, and strategic orientation of IT in the firm (strategic or utilitarian). Financial data was obtained from financial data sets and matched to the firms.

The questionnaires were mailed to Chief Information Officers in the U.S. They were selected because respondents needed an overall picture of IT investments and their strategic orientation to provide the answers and show their position within the firm hierarchy. Addresses and CIO names were obtained from the Compact Disclosure database. One thousand instruments were mailed. A postage paid envelope was enclosed with each questionnaire. Respondents were assured that their responses would be kept confidential.

One hundred and twenty-three questionnaires were returned; of these, 92 had significant financial data for 3 years prior to the survey on the COMPUSTAT database, thereby providing an objective measure of economic performance, productivity, marketing power and knowledge management ability. The subjects selfreported gender, age, education, and whether their position was considered technical or general management. Though there may be a bias toward inflated self ratings on some items, the anonymous nature of the instrument does not allow for any checking. Measures were collected of strategic orientation and infrastructure services from the CIO’s view. Table 1 shows demographics of the respondents. Early respondents were compared to late respondents to check for late or non-response bias. No significant differences were found lessening concern for this. In addition, demographic features were similar to those in previous studies, suggesting external validity of the final sample [2,3,15].

The seven items in Table 2 measured two poles of orientation—strategic, represented by the even numbered items, and utilitarian, measured by the odd numbered items. A utilitarian focus leans toward using IT as a cost saving device while strategic considers IT as a tool needed to gain competitive advantage through strategic alignment. No deviations were made from Weill and Broadbent’s model. Orientation was then computed as simple averages of the odd items (Utilitarian) and even items (Strategic).

## 4.2. IT infrastructure services

The specific IT infrastructure services list used in our study was also that developed by Weill and Broadbent. The factor structure was examined to provide descriptive factors of IT infrastructure for this data set. Table 3 shows all the items; each was scored using a five-point Likert-type scale. All are presented so that the greater the score, the greater the extent of the service that would be provided by the firm’s IT infrastructure. A greater number of services in a firm indicated greater firm-wide IT infrastructure capability. This measure was used as opposed to IT investment dollars as it reduced the effect of erratic investments and deployment times, and it indicated breadth of services provided.

A principal components analysis (PCA) was conducted on the items in Table 3 to determine if a reduced structure provides a more parsimonious framework for analysis. An initial PCA indicated five factors were likely to be present in the data using an Eigenvalue greater than 1 rule. A total of 85% of the variance was explained by the five factors. Furthermore, Kaiser’s measure of sampling adequacy (MSA) was 0.72 (>0.70 recommended); this indicated an adequate sample size. A subsequent factor analysis with varimax rotation resulted in the factor loadings.

## 4.3. Business performance

We used several measures of firm performance, as listed in Table 4. The data was taken from COMPUSTAT tapes for the 3 years leading up to the year of our survey. An average was computed of the 3 years to help reduce random fluctuations. To determine patterns in the data, a PCA was conducted on the averages. An initial result indicated four Eigenvalues greater than 1. A varimax rotated PCA with four factors resulted in the structure shown in Table 4; we labeled these size characteristics (SIZE), financial performance measures (PERF), market power indicators (MAR-KET), and knowledge management metrics (KNOW). A total of 64% of the variance was explained by these factors. Furthermore, Kaiser’s measure of sampling adequacy (MSA) was 0.85 (which is greater than the 0.70 normally recommended for acceptance) indicating sufficiently large sample size. Each data item was normalized to N(0, 1) and averaged within the factors.

SIZE is a factor indicating the overall magnitude of the firm, including measures of market value, expenditures, and income. Financial PERFormance measures are common ratios used to express the overall health of an organization, containing return and turnover ratios. MARKET power indicates the market share and ability to react to change, based on the number of employees and their sales productivity. The KNOWledge factor is based on ratios that represent value placed on the organization by the free market: the market value to replacement cost ratios that represent organizations receiving returns on knowledge capital, avoiding diminishing returns.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Hypothesis 1

To determine if significant differences of CIO characteristics are significantly related to financial performance measures, ANOVA tests were conducted using the financial characteristics data as the dependent variable and the characteristic data as the independent variables. Table 5 shows the significant relations found. SIZE, MARKET, and PERF all indicated significant relationships to POSITION, where technical positions had higher values than general management ones. GENDER was found to have a significant effect on SIZE and MARKET with males serving in larger organizations with greater market impact. Thus, there is ample evidence to accept the alternate hypothesis that a relationship does exist between CIO characteristics and financial performance.

The POSITION of the CIO, whether a general or technical manager, seems to indicate that the speculation of previous researchers is correct, where a technical focus is related positively to performance of the firm; even though the magnitude is quite small it is statistically significant. More prominent are the relationships with MARKET and SIZE, indicating that larger firms with greater market share are staffed by CIOs with a technical emphasis. This could also be due to hiring practices, where more successful firms may seek out CIOs that have an IT background. In turn, the relationship of gender to the SIZE and MARKET showed that women were likely to be CIO’s of smaller and less market dominant companies. This is generally true for women CEO’s as compared to male CEO’s [13].

## 5.2. Hypothesis 2

To determine whether differences in characteristics of CIOs were significantly related to the firm’s IT orientation, individual ANOVA tests were conducted with the two orientations as dependent variables and the CIO characteristic data as the independent variables. The only significant relation found was between the POSITION of the CIO and the utilitarian orientation: the mean for technical was higher than for general management. Thus, we could not conclude that any characteristics related to the strategic orientation, but that CIOs considered to have an IT position were more commonly found in organizations that emphasized a utilitarian perspective.

The effect of strategic orientation is beneficial. Recent articles have advocated the addition of a chief innovation officer who would be more involved in business strategies [20]. Interviews with CIO’s at leading global organizations indicated a shift of the CIO from a tactical, operational to a strategic management level [14]. It seems that the ideal qualifications of a CIO are changing as IT becomes central to business planning. Interestingly, when we contrast this with the average stay of a CIO (4.2 years), this suggests that few CIO’s will see the fruition of their plans.

## 5.3. Hypothesis 3

To determine whether differences in CIO characteristics were significantly related to infrastructure, ANOVA tests were conducted with the infrastructure variables as the dependent variable and the CIO characteristic data as the independent variable. Table 6 shows the significant relations. Four of the infrastructure variables (COMM, SUPPORT, MGMT, DATA) were found to be significantly related to POSITION such that a technical management orientation resulted in higher values for these four variables. No other significant relationships were found. Thus, there is evidence to accept the alternate hypothesis that a relationship does exist between CIO characteristics and the IT infrastructure. If we look at the position of the CIO we find that when the CIO was technically oriented the company had a significantly higher focus on these infrastructure elements. There was, however, no relationship between POSITION and number of applications. Applications may depend more on the demands of the users rather than the position of the CIO.

The IT infrastructure depended on many factors, including contextual ones, especially when dealing with IT infrastructure investments. The mix of IT technologies in a firm varied. The activity of each CIO depends on desired performance which depends on a successful IT investment mix. Having a greater infrastructure emphasis when the CIO has a technical IT focus should be expected.

## 5.4. Hypothesis 4

To determine whether significant differences of infrastructure significantly impact financial performance, regressions were run for each infrastructure variable with financial performance items as the independent variables. Table 7 shows the significant relations found from the significant regression coefficients. All five of the infrastructure factors were significantly related to at least one of the performance measures. Thus, there was evidence to confirm the alternate hypothesis (that a relationship does exist between the IT infrastructure and financial outcomes); the greater the infrastructure adoption the higher the financial measures. SIZE and MARKET play the most frequent role in these relationships. One might expect that some infrastructure components become more important as the organization and its market dominance grows. Large organizations invest in such resources in order to be responsive to change. Also, increased emphasis on applications results in increased performance. However, only high adoption of applications correlated with standard financial performance metrics, such as ROA and ROI. This pattern follows early economic studies. Knowledge management ability, an outside measure of financial management, was significantly and positively related to MGMT support services.

## 5.5. Hypothesis 5

To determine whether differences of CIO orientation were significantly related to financial performance, regressions were run for each infrastructure variable against the financial performance items as independent variables. Table 8 shows the significant relations found. Both orientation measures were significantly related to at least one financial performance measure, showing evidence for the alternate hypothesis (that a relationship exists between the IT orientation and financial characteristics). For a strategic orientation, there was a strong, positive relation to PERF. For the utilitarian orientation, however, there was a strong negative relation to PERF and a weaker one to KNOW indicating that a pure utilitarian view can be detrimental while a strategic view can be beneficial. Thus, overall strategic orientation of the firm’s IT is associated with better economic performance than is a utilitarian orientation.

## 6. Conclusions

The objective financial measures of a firm tend to be higher when the position of the CIO is oriented toward IT rather than general management. This is independent of organization size as well as market power. Maybe more profitable firms are more likely to hire more specialists or to hire those with training for particular jobs. They seem to be more likely to hire from outside the firm than to promote ‘‘good managers’’ from other departments.

A CIO with a more technical management orientation was associated with a more utilitarian view of IT by the firm. This could be due to shaping of the IT infrastructure by a technically oriented CIO or a history of hiring to meet organizational objectives. When infrastructure characteristics were studied for CIO’s whose positions were characterized as IT vs. general management, in four out of five categories, the IT manager’s companies had higher infrastructure investments. By utilizing CIOs with a more technical orientation, firms may specifically give a signal to ‘‘build up infrastructure’’ although the converse could also hold from a staffing perspective.

As we have seen, all the five types of infrastructure services were positively related to at least one financial measure. CIO’s with a technical position can perform well in facilitating desired financial outcomes. Interestingly enough, only application support is highly correlated with performance measures such as ROI, ROA, and fixed asset turnover. These services are demanded by the user departments so that the joint work of functional area departments and IT departmentsiscrucialtothefiscalratios.Thispointstoaneedtohave more CIOs serve with functional area heads on planning committees.

With respect to IT orientation, the position of IT vs. general manager related positively to financial and support measures. Additionally, an IT manager in a firm that takes a strategic rather than utilitarian orientation is much more likely to be in a company of high financial performance. This tends to argue for staffing the CIO post with individuals who can make the connection between technology and the strategic direction of the organization, suggests a need for more business education in strategic management for IT executives, and provides an argument for including the CIO in top staff councils so that they are aware of the strategic thrust of the company. This cross-representation would ensure the alignment of IT and organizational strategy and thus align IT advances with new strategic policies. A good match of IT direction and the strategic goals of the organization should lead to success.

Our study was rich in data that combined results of an extensive questionnaire with financial data from COMPUSTAT. We found no relation between technical position of the CIO and the number of IT applications. From this we surmised that most of the impetus for the applications came from business units rather than from inside the IT department. If the applications are useful across the firm, they can be included in firm-wide infrastructure and other business managers can consider using the applications; thus their cost is shared by many units. This provides a new role for the technically oriented CIO.
