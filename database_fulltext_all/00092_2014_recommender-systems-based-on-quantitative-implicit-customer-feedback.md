## a b s t r a c t

Due to the abundant variety of products offered by e-commerce companies and online service providers, recommender systems become an increasingly important decision aid for customers. In this paper we focus on quantitative implicit customer feedback like sales and play records data. We extend the current state-of-the-art method for recommendations based on matrix factorization under a normal distribution assumption by allowing for different distributions which are more suitable to model this kind of data. In particular, we use the Poisson, the inverse Gaussian and the gamma distribution as extensions. The experimental evaluation with three realworld data sets shows the improved performance of our approach and we demonstrate the merit of using various distributions depending on the respective data set.

## 1. Introduction

Strained by the challenges caused by the global economy, companies worldwide realize that their success depends on “getting closer to the customer”. Customer-oriented decision support systems should, therefore, consider information about everything related to customer actions in order to enhance the entire customer experience. Recommender systems are a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of personalized web-based decision support systems that analyze data about customers and products to help customers <sup>fi</sup>nding items of interest [7,17]. According to the similar de<sup>fi</sup>nition in [23], “Recommender systems are decision-support systems; they should support the user in making a decision as to which (of many) items to select, purchase, download, and so forth”. Basically, the recommendation process can be seen as a decision problem for which a model representing the user's preferences is built. In this framework provided by [10], a user in a recommender system corresponds to the decision maker in a decision process and the goal is to learn the decision maker's preferences. So, in this way decision support systems and in particular recommender systems “try to assist the decision maker and user, respectively, throughout the decisionmaking process” [10]. In our context, this decision-making process consists of either the purchase of a product or the consumption of an item like playing a song. And more precisely, we attempt to predict the extent of preference by forecasting the quantity of purchase or consumption.

Recommender systems are widely used in e-commerce to aid customers in this decision-making process, convert browsers into buyers, increase cross-sell opportunities, and build customer loyalty [20]. It has been estimated that investments in recommenders bring in returns of 10 to 30%[8]. Collaborative Filtering (CF) is a popular category of recommender systems that is commonly implemented in major e-commerce systems, such as Amazon [11]. CF recommender systems analyze the interdependencies among customers or products, in order to accurately predict those products that the customers will prefer in the future. The only required information for this analysis is the past behavior of customers. There are two types of information about past behavior:

• The <sup>fi</sup>rst type is the way customers rate products, which is referred to as explicit feedback, since customers explicitly quantify their interest in products, by providing a rating in a numeric scale; for instance 1–5 stars or −1/+1 representing thumbs up/down.

• The second type is based on the transactions that customers make, which is referred to as implicit feedback and is inferred from customer actions, e.g., purchasing and browsing.

## 1.1. Contribution and outline

In this paper, we propose a novel method for CF recommender systems suitable for quantitative implicit feedback. Our approach extends the popular method of matrix factorization [9] by allowing for data with different kinds of distributions. This is a major contribution compared to the state-of-the-art matrix factorization, which is based on the assumption of a normal distribution, which is designed for explicit feedback. One advantage of considering implicit feedback instead of explicit feedback is that the latter may be scarce or even entirely unavailable, since customers are in general reluctant to express their preferences in such a way [5]. In contrast, implicit feedback is abundant, since it represents streaming data, such as click-throughs, viewing times, and scrolling actions. The option of handling count data arising from implicit feedback by thresholding was <sup>fi</sup>rstly described in [5]. However, this results in binary data, since the only distinction made is whether a count value is below an arbitrarily chosen threshold or not. For instance, if a customer purchased a product or not. In contrast, our method directly operates on implicit feedback given by count data, therefore allowing the use of the whole quantitative information. For instance, how frequently a customer purchased a product. Our work develops matrix factorization algorithms that are utilizing alternative probability distributions, namely the Poisson, the inverse Gaussian and the gamma distribution, which can better model quantitative data and their unique characteristics, such as a heavier tail in the distribution of quantitative implicit feedback caused by extremely popular items. While there has been work in the literature addressing possible extensions of the standard matrix factorization method to handle frequency data [3,12,4], this has been derived for different application settings and focuses on a single model based on a <sup>fi</sup>xed distributional assumption. In contrast, we show the possibility and the necessity to make use of various distributions for count data depending on the characteristics of the data set at hand. The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The following section reports the related work and in Subsection 2.3 we provide a more detailed comparison to the previous approaches for handling frequency data. Next, we describe the examined problem in Section 3. This is followed by Section 4 in which we present our proposed method. After this, we experimentally evaluate the accuracy of the proposed method using three real-world data sets. Hereby we show the superiority of the proposed approach compared to the state-of-the-art matrix factorization approach and demonstrate the need to make use of different distributions. This part is divided into Section 5 in which we describe the experimental setup and Section 6 in which we provide the results of our experimental evaluation. The paper is <sup>fi</sup>nally completed with the discussion in Section 7 and the conclusion as well as an outline of future work in Section 8.

## 2. Related work

In Section 2.1 we <sup>fi</sup>rst describe popular types of recommender systems before we focus on the particular category our method belongs to in Section 2.2. Afterwards, in Section 2.3, we discuss in more detail the relation of our work with existing approaches in the literature dealing with quantitative implicit feedback.

## 2.1. Memory-based, content-based and hybrid recommender systems

Memory-based Collaborative Filtering (CF) recommender systems [19] generate predictions based on preference data (e.g., ratings) of similar users. They have attracted a lot of interest and researches have been improving its performance continually. Users of memory-based CF systems receive recommendations mainly by memory-based (a.k.a., nearest-neighbor) algorithms, which can be either user-based or itembased:

• User-based CF <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fi</sup>nds for users a rating pattern similar to the one of the active user, and then uses the ratings of the corresponding users to perform the predictions of the ratings of the active user for speci<sup>fi</sup>c items, by calculating the weighted average of the ratings of similar users for the same item

• Item-based CF generates predictions by <sup>fi</sup>rst detecting a set of similar items.

Memory-based CF, due to its simplicity and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, has attracted popularity in e-commerce applications. For example, Amazon's recommendation system has been reported to use item-based CF [11].

Content-based (CB) recommender systems [15] typically perform predictions by utilizing content features of items. In particular, based on content features, similarity functions can detect the most related items, following the assumption that items that are similar in content will be rated similarly. The main advantage of CB recommender systems is that their performance is not based on the existence of preference data, and thus they are suitable in the case of the so called “cold-start problem”. However, pure CB recommender systems ignore information about user preferences, in case it is available, which can lead to overspecialization and a low level of personalization. CB recommender systems are used in several real-world applications. Pandora Radio is a popular example that recommends music with similar characteristics to that of a song provided by the user as an initial seed. Several movie recommendation sites also use CB approaches, such as: “Rotten Tomatoes”, “Internet Movie Database”, “Jinni”, “Rovi Corporation”, and “See This Next”.

As mentioned previously, memory-based CF and CB recommender systems have advantages and disadvantages. The important difference between them is that CF systems are based mainly on preference data, whereas CB systems are based on the content of items. For this reason, previous research has focused on the combination of memory-based CF and CB, which led to the so called hybrid recommender systems [1]. A prominent hybrid method is the Content-boosted Collaborative Filtering (CBCF) [13], which learns a content-based model over the training data to generate ratings for unrated items. This process results in a dense rating matrix, because of the predictions made for all empty entries in the original rating matrix (i.e., cells without any given rating). The derived matrix is then used by a memory-based CF recommender system.

## 2.2. Model-based collaborative filtering

Model-based CF systems differ from the previously mentioned categories, because they develop a prediction model-based on patterns detected in the training data. Several techniques have been used for this purpose, such as Bayesian networks, clustering models, and probabilistic latent semantic analysis. [22]. Matrix factorization is a technique that has demonstrated its ability to build accurate prediction models for model-based CF systems [9]. Matrix factorization generates recommendations based on latent features that determine users' preferences. A state-of-the-art matrix factorization method is the Probabilistic Matrix Factorization (PMF) [18], which can be seen as part of a general matrix factorization framework described in [21]. We will discuss this method below in Sections 4.1 and 4.2. Another related method that uses matrix factorization for implicit feedback is [16]. In this work the problem of ranking is addressed by considering suitable loss functions, while in our work we focus on the different distributions of quantitative implicit feedback themselves.

## 2.3. Comparison and positioning of our work

The use of implicit feedback for recommender systems in order to overcome the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of gathering explicit feedback has already been proposed in early work [14]. Studies have shown the value of implicit feedback. For instance, in an analysis performed in [6], explicit feedback is compared with quantitative implicit feedback, whereby the latter is from the same kind as in one of our applications, namely the number of times a music track is played. In this work it is concluded that despite their different characteristics, both types of feedback lead to similar performance when user-based CF is applied.

Table 1 provides a comparison of the presented approaches in recommender systems, by summarizing their advantages and disadvantages. It also reports (last column) whether the existing approaches are taking into account quantitative implicit feedback.

As a main conclusion from Table 1, model-based CF systems present the best accuracy of recommendations compared to the remaining approaches. Recent advances in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of recommender systems have demonstrated the superiority of model-based approaches, which are nowadays considered as state-of-the-art methods [17]. For this reason, our proposed approach is model-based. However, existing model-based CF approaches are only partly capable of suitably dealing with quantitative implicit feedback and have not been applied to song play count and sales data which are the applications in the present paper. Chen et al. [3] deals with an advertisement problem setting, Ma et al. [12] with web site recommendations and Cheng et al. [4] addresses locationbased social networks. All three approaches extend the standard matrix factorization model by using a variant of the GaP-model developed in [2] for document data. Particularly, they assume a Poisson distribution of the frequency data. In our method, we also make use of the Poisson distribution as one extension, but we demonstrate that this is not always the best distributional choice, especially in case of count data which exhibits heavier tails. This is the reason why we also extend the standard method to the case of an inverse Gaussian and a gamma distribution. In our proposed method, all three distributional extensions can be seen as special cases of a general maximum likelihood approach, which is not based on a <sup>fi</sup>xed distributional assumption as in [3,12,4]. Furthermore, our Poisson extension differs from the ones previously proposed in the literature. While [3,12,4] assume gamma priors, we instead use $L ^ { 2 } .$ -norm-based regularization of the rows of the factor matrices and employ customer and item biases in our model. The main reason we do this is due to the fact that in [9] it is shown that the incorporation of biases yields improvements of the prediction accuracy in case of the standard matrix factorization method which assumes a normal distribution.

## 3. Problem description

## 3.1. Definitions

In the following we will consider a user–item matrix X ∈ $\mathbb { N } _ { 0 } ^ { n \times m }$ whose n rows correspond to customers and whose m columns correspond to available products. The entries of this matrix X are count data and correspond to the quantitative implicit feedback given by customers about products. These entries can represent the number of times each product was bought by each customer in an e-commerce setting. Alternatively, for example in an online music provider setting these entries can also represent how often each user listened to each available song. We identify every customer by its row index $i \in \{ 1 , . . . , n \}$ and every product by its column index $j \in \{ 1 , . . . , m \}$ . This means that $X _ { i j }$ stands for the quantity sold of product j to customer i, or in the second case, the number of times user i listened to song j. The value $X _ { i j }$ can be understood as an implicit rating. While customer i did not provide an explicit rating, we expect that the higher the quantity $X _ { i j } ,$ the more customer i likes product j. This is at least the case if j is a consumer good which is commonly bought or used more than once. This constellation is important since in practice explicit ratings are often not available or dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to obtain, because extra work of customers is required as an input. The number of sales or play counts on the other hand is recorded naturally in databases and is easily available for companies. Note that furthermore one could also incorporate additional information like demographic data about the customers or category information about the products and store it in additional matrices. Here, however, we just focus on sales and play count data itself which are easiest to obtain in practice.

## 3.2. Problem

Now we can formulate our problem. Most of today's e-commerce companies and online service providers have a large amount of products they offer. This implies that customers typically can only acquire a small fraction of the available products. Therefore the user–item matrix X will be sparse, meaning that most of its entries are zero. Due to the large variety of products, if customer i did not acquire produc $: j ,$ the reason for this will most likely not be a known disinterest in this product, but rather the lack of awareness of the existence of this product. This means that in general we do not interpret $X _ { i j } = 0$ as disinterest but as a missing value. Only if we already know that this value is likely due to disinterest, e.g., because of a previous unsuccessful recommendation of this product, we should consider this and do not treat it as a missing value. Our goal is now to predict the value of $X _ { i j }$ when customer i becomes aware of product j. For this purpose, we can use the data about the previous transactions of this customer and that of all the other customers. We will describe our proposed method to do this in the next section. Apart from the sparsity of the matrix, a major challenge for the prediction is the skewness of sales or play records data. The state-ofthe-art method for explicit ratings assumes a normal distribution for the latter, which is often a reasonable assumption in practice. However, if we are dealing with implicit ratings based on count data, this assumption is not appropriate in many cases where we are in fact dealing with data which has a positive skew. This means that low counts appear more often than high ones, but large counts can still appear with lower probability because there is no certain rating range restriction like 1–5 stars. Since the count value distribution in Fig. 1 is not symmetric and exhibits a heavier tail, we can heuristically infer that the normal distribution is not likely to provide good results. Therefore it is necessary to use some other distribution than the normal distribution, which <sup>fi</sup>ts better to the given data. Due to the fact that we are dealing with count data, it is plausible that for example a Poisson distribution assumption will yield a better result. In the next sections we discuss the details of this and demonstrate in our experiments in Section 6 that it is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to use different distributions depending on the tail of the respective data set.

## 4. Proposed method

## 4.1. Matrix factorization basic

We now give a brief summary of the current state-of-the-art method in matrix factorization, which is based on a normal distribution assumption. The probabilistic foundation of this method goes back to [18]. Here, we present a version of this based on [9], which takes biases into account. This method is particularly successful in applications where explicit ratings are available, the Net<sup>fl</sup>ix Million Dollar Prize Challenge being an important example where it has been used. The basic idea in this model is that customers and products exhibit “latent features”. One of these features could be, for example, the ex tent of preference of a customer for a certain product property like a particular ingredient or a long best-before date for food or a certain genre for music. And the corresponding feature of the product describes to what extent it possesses this property. However, no prior assumptions on the type of the fea tures are necessary and they are learned by the algorithm itself. Suppose that there exist k ℕ such latent features and that we store all of them for every customer in a matrix $U \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \bar { n } \times k }$ and for every product in a matrix $V \in \mathbb { R } ^ { m \times k }$ <sup>k</sup>. The interaction between customer i and product j is then modeled by the do

product of both feature vectors, namely $U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } = \sum _ { l = 1 } ^ { k } U _ { i l } V _ { j l } ,$ where $U _ { i . }$ is the i-th row of U, $V _ { j . }$ is the j-th row of V and $V ^ { T }$ denotes the transpose of matrix V. The value of $U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T }$ can be small or large, depending on how much product j satis<sup>fi</sup>es the needs of customer i, described by the latent features. In most applications however, the expected rating of product j by customer i will not solely depend on such an interaction, but a part of it can be explained by intrinsic characteristics of the customer and the product. Some customers tend to rate products higher or are more active than others and some products are top sellers among a large part of the whole customer base. Biases are introduced to account for this fact. We denote by μ the overall mean value of all ratings, by $b _ { U _ { i } } \in \mathbb { R }$ the bias of customer i (i.e., the deviation between the overall rating mean and the average rating of custome i) and by $b _ { V _ { j . } }$ ∈ℝ the bias of product j. The resulting optimization problem in our quantitative implicit rating case then reads as follows:

$$
\underset {U, V, b _ {U}, b _ {V}} {\arg \min} \sum_ {(i, j): X _ {i j} > 0} \left(\left(X _ {i j} - U _ {i.} V _ {j.} ^ {T} - \mu - b _ {U _ {i.}} - b _ {V _ {j.}}\right) ^ {2} + \lambda \left(\sum_ {l = 1} ^ {k} \left(\left(U _ {i l}\right) ^ {2} + \left(V _ {j l}\right) ^ {2}\right) + \left(b _ {U _ {i.}}\right) ^ {2} + \left(b _ {V _ {j.}}\right) ^ {2}\right)\right).\tag{1}
$$

Here, $\lambda > 0$ is a constant which adjusts the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the regularization term with the purpose to prevent over<sup>fi</sup>tting. The summation with re spect to the pairs i and j is done over all observed implicit rating count values $X _ { i j }$ which are nonzero, since we treat zeros as missing values. Note that here the latent feature matrices of the customers U and of the products V as well as the vectors $b _ { U }$ of the n customer biases and $b _ { V }$ of the m product biases are the model parameters which have to be estimated. A commonly used technique to solve this is stochastic gradient descent, which we de scribe below for our method.

## 4.2. Extensions for count data

Now we want to derive extensions of the currently used matrix factorization method that are more suitable for count data like sales of products or play records of songs for each customer. In [21] it is shown that the standard method, which assumes a normal distribution, can be extended to a more general matrix factorization framework to which many common methods belong. In this framework, the distribution does not need to be normal, but can be an arbitrary member of the natural exponential family and the variety of the comprised models can be described equivalently by a few modeling choices. In this setting, one can obtain a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed estimation method which corresponds to the minimization of a certain Bregman divergence depending on the distribution, In the following, we will use the Poisson, the inverse Gaussian and the gamma distribution, which are also members of the natura exponential family. However, we do not follow this general framework proposed by [21] to derive our algorithms, but instead we use maximum like lihood estimation, And we also take into account biases similar to the case of a normal distribution in [9]. The main reason we do this is the greater <sup>fl</sup>exibility and generality of the maximum likelihood approach. In particular, future work can include distributions which are not from the natural ex ponential family, like power law distributions. In this case the abovementioned framework does not apply, but corresponding estimation methods can still be derived by maximum likelihood. In the next two sections we show the results of matrix factorization in case of a Poisson, an inverse Gaussian and a gamma distribution assumption. In different contexts, all three distributions are commonly used to model count data

## 4.3. Matrix factorization under a Poisson distribution assumption

While in the standard method it is assumed that every rating $X _ { i j }$ of a product j by a customer i is distributed normally with mean parameter $U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { j . } }$ , we now assume that every count outcome $X _ { i j }$ of a product j by a customer i is Poisson distributed with natural parameter $U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + l o g \Big ( \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { j . } } \Big )$ (this corresponds to a parameter value of exp $\Big ( U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + l o g \Big ( \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { j . } } \Big ) \Big )$ in the commonly used parameterization of the Poisson distribution), where we set μ to be the overall training count data median instead of the mean. Furthermore, we assume that the $X _ { i j }$ are drawn independently and therefore their joint density factorizes into the product of the marginal densities. For the estimation of the parameters, we minimize the negative log-likelihood function, because the resulting sums are easier to handle. This is equivalent to the maximization of the likelihood function, since on the one hand the natural logarithm is a strictly monotonically increasing function and thus the maxima remain the same, and on the other hand the maximization of a function is equivalent to the minimization of the negative of the func tion. With regularization the minimization of the corresponding negative log-likelihood function results in the following optimization prob. lem in place of Eq.(1):

$$
\underset {U, V, b _ {U}, b _ {V}} {\arg \min} \sum_ {(i, j): X _ {i j} > 0} \left(\log \left(X _ {i j}!\right) + \left(\mu + b _ {U _ {i.}} + b _ {V _ {j.}}\right) e x p \left(U _ {i.} V _ {j.} ^ {T}\right) - \left(U _ {i.} V _ {j.} ^ {T} + \log \left(\mu + b _ {U _ {i.}} + b _ {V _ {j.}}\right)\right) X _ {i j} + \lambda \left(\sum_ {l = 1} ^ {k} \left(\left(U _ {i l}\right) ^ {2} + \left(V _ {j l}\right) ^ {2}\right) + \left(b _ {U _ {i.}}\right) ^ {2} + \left(b _ {V _ {j.}}\right) ^ {2}\right)\right).\tag{2}
$$

To <sup>fi</sup>nd an approximate solution for this numerically, we use stochastic/mini batch gradient descent with momentum. Standard gradient descent relies on the fact that the negative of a gradient of a multivariate differentiable function points in the direction of steepest descent which suggests a good search direction for a minimum. Stochastic/mini batch gradient descents are extensions of standard gradient descent appropriate for big data sets. Here, one only loops successively in each step $s \in \mathbb N$ over one of some randomly chosen disjoint subsets $B _ { s }$ whose union is the full training index set $\{ ( i , j ) | X _ { i j } > 0 \}$ instead of looping over all the training data indices in one step and repeats this procedure after one full pass through the training data. An additional momentum term is used to increase the speed of convergence and this procedure is referred to as the “heavy-ball method”, which requires the determination of a suitable learning rate $\varepsilon > 0$ and momentum coef<sup>fi</sup>cient $\nu > 0$ which vary from problem to problem. After taking partial derivatives with respect to all target parameters of the objective function in Eq. (2), hereby we obtain the following update equations, which are repeated in every step s of the algorithm:

$$
U _ {i.} ^ {(s)} := U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} - \varepsilon \sum_ {j: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(\left(\mu + b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) e x p \left(U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T}\right) - X _ {i j}\right) + 2 \lambda U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \left(U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} - U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 2)}\right)\tag{3}
$$

$$
V _ {j.} ^ {(s)} := V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)} - \varepsilon \sum_ {i: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(\left(\mu + b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) e x p \left(U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T}\right) - X _ {i j}\right) + 2 \lambda V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \left(V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)} - V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 2)}\right)\tag{4}
$$

$$
b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s)} := b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)} - \varepsilon \sum_ {j: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(\exp \left(U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T}\right) - \frac {1}{\mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)}} X _ {i j} + 2 \lambda b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \left(b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)} - b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 2)}\right)\tag{5}
$$

$$
b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s)} := b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)} - \varepsilon \sum_ {i: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(\exp \left(U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T}\right) - \frac {1}{\mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)}} X _ {i j} + 2 \lambda b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \left(b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)} - b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 2)}\right).\tag{6}
$$

Here, for every customer i the summation is done over all corresponding products j such that $( i , j )$ lies in the current batch subset $B _ { s }$ and analogously for every product j over all corresponding customers i for which (i, j) is the element of that batch subset. The algorithm starts with initial values $U _ { i . } ^ { ( 0 ) } , b _ { U _ { i . } } ^ { ( 0 ) }$ for every customer i and $V _ { j . } ^ { ( 0 ) } , b _ { V _ { j . } } ^ { ( 0 ) }$ for every product j and is repeated for a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large number of steps until the approximation is good enough. In practice, we obtained good results by choosing the initial values $b _ { U _ { i } } ^ { ( 0 ) }$ and $b _ { V _ { j . } } ^ { ( 0 ) }$ as the differences between the median training data count values of customer i and product j, respectively, and the overall median μ, and all the feature vectors $U _ { i . } ^ { ( 0 ) } , V _ { j . } ^ { ( 0 ) }$ as some random perturbations around zero. Once we have completed the estimation and obtained all the <sup>fi</sup>nal parameter values $U _ { i . } , V _ { j . } , b _ { U _ { i } } , b _ { V _ { j } }$ , we can generate the prediction of the number of times product j is acquired by customer i by $\hat { X } _ { i j } = \left( \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { j . } } \right) e x p \Bigl ( U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } \Bigr )$ , since this is the expected value of a Poisson distributed random variable with natural parameter $U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + l o g \Big ( \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { j . } } \Big )$ . Subsequently, for each customer i the products j with the highest predicted value $\hat { X } _ { i j }$ are the most appropriate ones to recommend to him.

## 4.4. Matrix factorization under an inverse Gaussian distribution assumption

We now state a similar result under the assumption of an inverse Gaussian distribution instead of a Poisson distribution. While the Poisson distribution is the more common one to model count data, the inverse Gaussian distribution can work better in cases where the data has a bit heavier tails than the Poisson distribution exhibits. In this case we assume with the same notation as before that every implicit rating $X _ { i j }$ of a product j by a customer i follows an inverse Gaussian distribution with mean parameter $U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { \perp } }$ , where μ again is the overall training data median. Using negative log-likelihood minimization with regularization like in the Poisson case, we obtain the following optimization problem:

$$
\underset {U, V, b _ {U}, b _ {V}} {\text {arg   min}} \left(- \sum_ {(i, j): X _ {i j} > 0} \left(\log \left(\sqrt {\frac {\alpha}{2 \pi (X _ {i j}) ^ {3}}}\right) - \frac {\alpha (X _ {i j} - U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} - \mu - b _ {U _ {i .}} - b _ {V _ {j .}}) ^ {2}}{2 (U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}) ^ {2} X _ {i j}} - \lambda \left(\sum_ {l = 1} ^ {k} ((U _ {i l}) ^ {2} + (V _ {j l}) ^ {2}) + (b _ {U _ {i .}}) ^ {2} + (b _ {V _ {j .}}) ^ {2}\right)\right)\right).\tag{7}
$$

Here, $\alpha > 0$ is an additional distributional parameter which determines the shape of the inverse Gaussian distribution. But α does not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the expected value of the inverse Gaussian distribution and its speci<sup>fi</sup>c value does not affect the solution of the optimization problem (7) and can be held constant, since the <sup>fi</sup>rst term in the sum appearing in Eq. (7) is independent of the target parameters $U , V , b _ { U } , b _ { V }$ and α is only a multiplicative factor of the second term. Below we see that α can be chosen arbitrarily by choosing the learning rate ε and the regularization parameter λ accordingly. In anal ogy to the case of a Poisson distribution, the following update equations for the inverse Gaussian case can be derived:

$$
U _ {i.} ^ {(s)} := U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} + \varepsilon \sum_ {j: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(\alpha V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(\frac {X _ {i j}}{\left(U _ {i .} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j .} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {j .}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {3}} - \frac {1}{\left(U _ {i .} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j .} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {j .}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {2}}\right) - 2 \lambda U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \Big (U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} - U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 2)} \Big)\tag{8}
$$

$$
V _ {j.} ^ {(s)} := V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)} + \varepsilon \sum_ {i: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(\alpha U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(\frac {X _ {i j}}{\left(U _ {i .} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j .} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {j .}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {3}} - \frac {1}{\left(U _ {i .} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j .} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {j .}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {2}}\right) - 2 \lambda V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \Big (V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)} - V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 2)} \Big)\tag{9}
$$

$$
b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s)} := b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)} + \varepsilon \sum_ {j: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(\alpha \left(\frac {X _ {i j}}{\left(U _ {i .} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j .} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {j .}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {3}} - \frac {1}{\left(U _ {i .} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j .} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {j .}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {2}}\right) - 2 \lambda b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \Big (b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)} - b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 2)} \Big)\tag{10}
$$

$$
b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s)} := b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)} + \varepsilon \sum_ {i: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(\alpha \left(\frac {X _ {i j}}{\left(U _ {i .} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j .} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {j .}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {3}} - \frac {1}{\left(U _ {i .} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(V _ {j .} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} ^ {(s - 1)} + b _ {V _ {j .}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) ^ {2}}\right) - 2 \lambda b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \Big (b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)} - b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 2)} \Big).\tag{11}
$$

The initial values can also be chosen in the same way as in the Poisson case. Note that in the equations above we could eliminate α by setting $\varepsilon : = { \widetilde { \varepsilon } } / { \alpha }$ and $\lambda : = \widetilde { \lambda }$ α with new values of the learning rate ε and the regularization parameter λ. Therefore, the concrete choice of α does not affect the algorithm and can be described equivalently by respective choices of ε and λ. After the parameter estimation, the predictions can be generated by ${ \hat { X } } _ { i j } =$ $U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { j . } }$ , since this is already the expected value of an inverse Gaussian distribution with mean parameter $U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { j . } }$

## 4.5. Matrix factorization under a gamma distribution assumption

Finally, we derive an analogous result under the assumption of a gamma distribution. Like the inverse Gaussian distribution the gamma distribu tion is more suitable to model count data with slightly heavier tails than the Poisson distribution and is used more often in practice than the inverse Gaussian distribution. Similar as before, we now assume that every quantitative implicit rating $X _ { i j }$ of a product j by a customer i follows a gamma dis tribution with scale parameter $\frac { U _ { i } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + \mu + b _ { U _ { i } } + b _ { V _ { j . } } } { \kappa }$ , whereby μ once again is the overall training data median and κ denotes the additional shape parameter. This expression for the scale parameter is due to the fact that the expected value of a gamma distribution with scale parameter θ and shape paramete κ is given by $\theta ^ { \cdot } \kappa S 0$ by this approach after parameter estimation the expected value is given by $U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { j } }$ . Negative log-likelihood minimization with regularization now leads to the following optimization problem:

$$
\underset {U, V, b _ {U}, b _ {V}} {\text {arg   min}} \left(- \sum_ {(i, j): X _ {i j} > 0} \left(- \log (\Gamma (\kappa)) - \kappa \log \left(\frac {U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}}{\kappa}\right) + (\kappa - 1) \log \left(X _ {i j}\right) - \frac {\kappa X _ {i j}}{U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}} - \lambda \left(\sum_ {l = 1} ^ {k} \left(\left(U _ {i l}\right) ^ {2} + \left(V _ {j l}\right) ^ {2}\right) + \left(b _ {U _ {i .}}\right) ^ {2} + \left(b _ {V _ {j .}}\right) ^ {2}\right)\right)\right).\tag{12}
$$

Here, Γ denotes the gamma function. Analogously as for the previous distributions, the corresponding stochastic/mini batch gradient descent up dates can now be derived as follows:

$$
U _ {i.} ^ {(s)} := U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} + \varepsilon \sum_ {j: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(\kappa V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(- \frac {1}{U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}} + \frac {X _ {i j}}{\left(U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}\right) ^ {2}}\right) - 2 \lambda U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \Big (U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} - U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 2)} \Big)\tag{13}
$$

$$
V _ {j.} ^ {(s)} := V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)} + \varepsilon \sum_ {i: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(\kappa U _ {i.} ^ {(s - 1)} \left(- \frac {1}{U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}} + \frac {X _ {i j}}{\left(U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}\right) ^ {2}}\right) - 2 \lambda V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \left(V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 1)} - V _ {j.} ^ {(s - 2)}\right)\tag{14}
$$

$$
b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s)} := b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)} + \varepsilon \sum_ {j: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(\kappa \left(- \frac {1}{U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}} + \frac {X _ {i j}}{\left(U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}\right) ^ {2}}\right) - 2 \lambda b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \left(b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 1)} - b _ {U _ {i.}} ^ {(s - 2)}\right)\tag{15}
$$

$$
b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s)} := b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)} + \varepsilon \sum_ {i: (i, j) \in B _ {s}} \left(\kappa \left(- \frac {1}{U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}} + \frac {X _ {i j}}{\left(U _ {i .} V _ {j .} ^ {T} + \mu + b _ {U _ {i .}} + b _ {V _ {j .}}\right) ^ {2}}\right) - 2 \lambda b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) + \nu \Big (b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 1)} - b _ {V _ {j.}} ^ {(s - 2)} \Big).\tag{16}
$$

After estimating parameters in the same way as before, we can generate our predictions by $\hat { X } _ { i j } = U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { j } }$ , since this is the expected value of a gamma distribution with scale parameter $\frac { U _ { i . } V _ { j . } ^ { T } + \mu + b _ { U _ { i . } } + b _ { V _ { j . } } } { \kappa }$ :, as noted above.

## 5. Experimental setup

For the experimental evaluation of the proposed method, we make use of three different data sets. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one is a random sample of the commonly available “Echo Nest Taste Pro<sup>fi</sup>le Subset”. This data set consists of play count records of songs by users along with the corresponding user and song IDs. It can be assumed that the higher a play count, the more a user likes the respective song. We created the random sample of this data set with the property that only play count values between 3 and 100 are included. The purpose of this is to clean the data set from noise and outliers. A low play count can occur because a user is unaware which kind of song is behind a link and also by channel zapping. A very high play count, on the other hand, is likely due to some automated mechanism like scripts and Web crawlers. Furthermore, in the resulting sample we <sup>fi</sup>ltered out all users and songs for which at most 5 play count records exist. This is a widely used preprocessing technique in the area of recommender systems, since users and items for which only few observations exist do not provide a gain for the collaborative effect. These users are the less active and thus less valuable ones and the songs the less popular ones. These less active users are addressed better by content-based approaches or recommendations based on the overall most popular items. After applying the described preprocessing, we obtained a random sample consisting of 71,428 (user, song, play count)-triplets with 9503 unique users and 5892 unique songs. A histogram of the frequency of the different play count values is shown in Fig, 1 The number of users depending on the number of observations per user, meaning the number of different songs for each user, and the number of songs depending on the observations per song are shown in Fig. 2. As we can see, very active users with a high number of observations are rare, popular songs with a high number of corresponding users occur more often. In both cases a relatively low number of observations still dominate. The second data set is the publicly available Ta-Feng data set coming from a Chinese supermarket, which consists of sales records containing a customer and a product ID together with the purchase quantity of the product. We have aggregated this data set by the user and product IDs to obtain the total quantity that each customer purchased of each product. The third data set was obtained from an e-commerce company selling baby products. It is based on purchases made during a time period of three years and four months.<sup>1</sup> Consistently, for these data sets we applied the same <sup>fi</sup>ltering as for the Taste Pro<sup>fi</sup>le sample data set. In these settings the reasoning behind this is that by discarding a very small quantity, products like furniture items are excluded for which a higher quantity of purchases naturally does not occur and therefore a large quantity and liking correlation do not apply. On the other hand, too large quantities are excluded as well, since they can be caused by purchases of commercial vendors which do not have a certain preference but buy a large variety of products without a common pattern for resale only. For the Ta-Feng data set, the <sup>fi</sup>ltering yielded a total number of 26,626 (customer, product, quantity)-triplets with 3187 unique customers and 2187 unique products and the purchase quantity histogram shown in Fig. 3. For the E-Commerce data set, on the other hand, this <sup>fi</sup>ltering resulted in a total number of 222,691 (customer, product, quantity)-triplets and 19,368 unique customers as well as 4754 unique products. The corresponding purchase quantity histogram is depicted in Fig. 4. In contrast, the corresponding Fig. 6 for the E-Commerce data set shows that the number of popular items with many observations is larger in this case.

This procedure is common practice in machine learning and consists of searching through the joint parameter space with re<sup>fi</sup>nements in regions where good values were obtained. Two frequently used error measures for performance evaluation of the predictive quality in recommender systems are root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE). If we denote the number of all customer–product pairs $( i , j )$ in the validation index set by N and the predicted value of every $X _ { i j }$ by $\hat { X } _ { i j }$ , these measures are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows:

$$
\operatorname{RMSE} \left(\hat {X}\right) := \sqrt {\frac {1}{N} \sum_ {(i , j) \in \mathcal {V}} \left(X _ {i j} - \hat {X} _ {i j}\right) ^ {2}} \quad \text { and } \quad \operatorname{MAE} \left(\hat {X}\right) := \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {(i, j) \in \mathcal {V}} \left| X _ {i j} - \hat {X} _ {i j} \right|.
$$

Here, the summation is done over all customer–product pairs in the validation set. While RMSE is a good choice in cases where the rating range is small, like 1–5 stars, it is not suitable for our case of count data. Because in this case the values can vary in several orders of magnitude, the squaring in the RMSE de<sup>fi</sup>nition would imply that only small relative changes in the values can lead to large errors. Thus, we use MAE to assess the predictive quality of models, since this measure is more robust in this sense.

This suggests that the customers and the assortment of the products of Ta-Feng are inherently less diverse and can already be explained by a low number of latent features.

## 7. Discussion

In the previous section we showed that it is worthwhile to consider different distributions when dealing with quantitative implicit feedback. Since hereby prediction accuracy is improved, our proposed method offers a variety of immediate business advantages while being easily applied, since no explicit customer feedback is required. Firstly, customer satisfaction and loyalty increase because of the sophisticated and personalized product recommendations generated by this method, which help customers in their decision-making. Since customers thereby spend less time searching for suitable products, it increases their trust towards the recommender system, which results in more loyalty as well. The higher accuracy of the recommendations causes more customers to rely on them, which in turn implies that more information about the customers can be captured, further improving the accuracy of the method. So a positive reinforcement can be observed here which also occurs because an increase in sales of a product results in an increase of the corresponding recommended products. Secondly, in e-commerce settings cross-selling increases signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly by the application of the proposed algorithm. The more accurate the recommendations, the more likely the customers buy additional products which results in higher average order values. Thirdly, retailers have the possibility to combine marketing policies with inventory control rules as the recommendations are based on sales data instead of explicit ratings in this case. Thus, the recommendation method can directly take into account and handle special circumstances like the promotion of overstocked products or the omission of articles out of stock. ## 8. Conclusion and future work

In this paper we have proposed a novel algorithm for recommendations based on quantitative implicit customer feedback. Our method uses matrix factorization under different distributional assumptions. In particular, we have used the Poisson, the inverse Gaussian and the gamma distribution for our extensions. Unlike the current state-ofthe-art method in matrix factorization, which assumes a normal distribution, our method is more suitable for count data, including widely available sales records and play count data. The information on how often every customer purchased each product or clicked each song is all that is needed to apply this method. We have veri<sup>fi</sup>ed the improved predictive power for three different real-world data sets, showing the business value for companies in practice. Hereby, we have also pointed out the need to use different distributions depending on the given data set and its characteristics, in particular its tail. Future work focuses on formulating and elaborating our problem in a generalized fashion and includes the use of additional distributions to capture further forms of count data, like power law distributions. Also, temporal dependencies can be taken into account by the model. Furthermore, additional data sources could be included, like category information about products or demographic data about customers as well as explicit feedback from customers. Apart from this, a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis based on predicted sales <sup>fi</sup>gures could be performed to assess the economic gain of the recommendations generated by the algorithm in more detail. Finally, since our method is based on <sup>fi</sup>nding latent features of customers and products, these can be examined accurately and future work can include a combination of this method with cluster analysis in order to perform a sophisticated customer segmentation.
