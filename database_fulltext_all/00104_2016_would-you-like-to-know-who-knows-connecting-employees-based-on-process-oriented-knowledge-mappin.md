## a b s t r a c t

Employees' knowledge as the guarantor of companies' success is an important asset of the enterprises' value. However, this knowledge is often not optimally used or even visible and available for employees and management within companies. This intangible knowledge leads to continuously reinvented wheels within organisations and employees misspend time learning processes in a cumbersome way on their own. To overcome this problem, we present a new approach aimed at connecting employees socially. The main novelty is the use of the organisation's business processes as basis for connecting employees by indicating their process-related areas of expertise. The goal is to enable enduring sharing and distribution of knowledge within an organisation to support decision making in process execution. Our evaluation results from two financial services companies show that employees perceive such a network as very helpful. Our approach leads to easier access, better search results and more opportunities to connect knowledge with other employees.

## 1. Introduction

Employee knowledge is essential to the prosperity of an organisation [71]. However, time-consuming searches by employees for missing information, or the redundant development of already existing knowledge, are a common problem [1]. Non-existent documents, missing contact persons or incomplete information are some examples. To execute daily work correctly, the required knowledge documentation is often inefficient [2]. Thus, tacit knowledge built on individual experience and intuition is not considered properly, and consequently this knowledge is often not available to other employees [3]. Thus, an organisation's efficiency suffers due to a non-optimal usage of resources [4].

To provide employees with such knowledge, business process related knowledge plays a major role. Business processes are crucial to an organisation's success [5] and can be considered as the DNA of an organisation. This genetic information describes how employees, machines and information systems are interconnected to produce services and goods [6]. Therefore, business processes are the core of an organisation's value chain, and empower employees to execute the business model. Thus, questions arising in daily work situations are mainly referring to knowledge being used in executing processes.

Knowledge can generally be separated into explicit and implicit knowledge [7]. In contrast to explicit knowledge, which can be documented in numerous forms, e.g. as databases and process models [8], tacit knowledge is not documented and needs to be developed or accomplished by employees [9]. While some approaches try to support employees with process (un)related explicit knowledge [e.g. 10,11], the majority of knowledge still remains implicit and is difficult to maintain and distribute in an explicit form [12]. Tacit knowledge is individual-specific behaviour and practical know-how [3]. This kind of knowledge often guides the daily work of employees without being consciously perceived [3]. In comparison to explicit knowledge, tacit knowledge is difficult to transfer from one employee to another due to personal characteristics and context sensitivity [13,14]. Additionally, especially the aspect of ensuring an effective knowledge transfer in a given social setting of a company requires intensified research efforts to understand and support these mechanisms better [15]. A preferable method to present knowledge to employees is the concept of knowledge maps [16] visualising knowledge connections [17]. Consequently, the following research question arises: How can employees be provided with adequate knowledge maps in the execution of their business processes?

By analysing prior research we identify requirements regarding such a knowledge map and develop a process-oriented social knowledge system. The system enables employees to connect with each other with regard to business-relevant knowledge. In particular, the exchange of tacit knowledge is encouraged and stimulated, accompanied by facilitated

## 2. Research methodology

The research methodology applied follows a design science approach, based on Venable [18] and Hevner et al. [19]. Design science provides a procedure for creating new approaches (also called artefacts), such as frameworks and methods. It is also used in the domain of developing knowledge mapping solutions [e.g. 20]. The development of the approach presented follows the framework of Venable [18]. In line with Venable, we follow the design science research cycle and conduct theory building, and subsequently develop a solution technology and evaluate the developed approach.

“Theory building” should occur as a precursor for the “solution technology invention” and induce as a result a “‘Utility Theory” or hypothesis, which defines an approach to decreasing or solving a problem [18]. The objective of the proposed process-based social knowledge system is a solution that allows connecting employees and their knowledge with regard to business processes. Overall, for theoretical reasoning social exchange theory is used to identify relevant prior research as knowledge exchange is seen as interaction between persons which is interdependent but dependent on other persons [12]. We conduct a literature review following the approach of Rowley and Slack [21]. “Knowledge map” was used as the search term within Ebsco Business Source Premier using the integrated search feature, which includes ABI/INFORM Complete, Business Source Premier, Emerald ManagementXtra 150, JSTOR, PsycARTICLES, ScienceDirect and Springerlink. The search results were analysed by two researchers independently and articles were selected based on title and abstract first and if relevant identified by reading the text second. From an analysis of this literature, the requirements for an approach to answer the research question are derived, which form our utility theory.

Based on theory building, a solution technology has to be developed. Our process-based social knowledge map is designed according to the identified design requirements and we describe the developed information system. The system is implemented in a department of a large German bank and a second German bank to conduct a naturalistic evaluation. Naturalistic evaluation is described by Venable [18] as evaluation in the field, i.e. how an artefact works in a real environment. Such an evaluation can cover case studies, survey studies, field studies and action research. Contrary, artificial evaluation is done with an exemplary dataset or environment, e.g. computer simulations, role playing simulations and experiments. As the proposed process-based social knowledge map is mainly addressing the needs of employees, we use a quantitative survey to question employees based on research regarding the evaluation of information systems. The details of this evaluation are described in the Measures and procedure section. To ensure reliable answers, we ask users of the first bank to compare our new approach with the existing knowledge system, which is not targeting the defined requirements. Within the second bank, we gather data two years after introduction and compare the results with the first bank. We compare the results of the introduction phase of the first bank with results of continuous usage without the introduction phase to ensure an independent comparison.

## 3. Knowledge in business processes

Within a business process, an input is transformed into an output (process result) in various sub-processes by the necessary resources, for instance employees, machines and information systems [22]. The transformation is performed on process instances, e.g. a car, an insurance claim or a house-building permission [23]. The resources of a process are connected by the possible links between the sub-processes involved. Every process instance passes the net of sub-processes on an individual path, i.e. different paths of process instances through the net are possible [24]. The execution of business processes across organisational units is an important characteristic, thus, the necessary knowledge for the production of goods and services should be connected in a process-oriented way and not to focus on functional-oriented organisational units [25]. Applying the idea that business processes are the basis for the production of goods and services within an organisation, the management of process-oriented knowledge becomes a key factor for success [23]. If process-oriented knowledge is not captured, stored and applied, an organisation is likely to fail [26].

Knowledge can be separated into explicit and tacit knowledge [13]. Explicit knowledge is codified, i.e. independent from a certain person, and can be transferred easily most of the time [7]. Typically, explicit knowledge is covering declarative knowledge (“know what”), i.e. facts [27]. Contrary, tacit knowledge is associated with individuals and covers mainly declarative knowledge (“know how”) [13,14]. Tacit knowledge is “typically acquired on the job or in the situation where it is used” [28,p. 28]. As such it refers to individual-specific know-how and behaviour [3] that, however, can partly be explicated using knowledge elicitation techniques and is then termed as implicit knowledge [29]. Such implicit knowledge refers to implicit rule-based knowledge (easy to explicate and codify by individuals) and know-how (highly contextual and thus not codified) whereas tacit know-how (difficult to verbalise) and deep tacit (underlying beliefs) cannot be explicated by individuals [30].

Based on this characterisation of explicit, tacit and implicit knowledge, three knowledge forms can be identified in the context of business processes, [8]:

1. Explicit knowledge in the form of process documentation shows how resources should be combined for the production of goods and services in the organisation [23]. The relevant process-steps, their relationship and the assignment of employees, machines and IT systems are captured.

2. Explicit knowledge as a result of monitoring and analysing process execution [31]. These facts cover how efficient a process has been over time in the past. This knowledge is mainly necessary for the management of an organisation in order to achieve the targeted process performance.

3. Procedural knowledge regarding processes, that contains tacit knowledge of employees executing the process [32]. It is a combination of cognitive processes and factual knowledge and regulates how an individual completes tasks and solves problems. It refers mainly to tacit know-how and deep tacit, but covers also implicit know-how in the context of process steps that is not codified.

Moreover, process related knowledge is vulnerable as a result of job changes and terminations within an organisation. This leads to vanishing tacit knowledge and the resultant unnecessary recapturing effort. To avoid this squandering of resources, organisations can try to make tacit knowledge explicit to a certain degree [33]. The visualisation of business processes empowers employees to understand their activities, business processes and information systems [34]. Nevertheless, several problems can occur during the attempt to explicate tacit knowledge [13]. Explicit knowledge persists in the abstract through the limited possibility of documentation to visualise reality and the intangibility of process execution. Because of the rapidly changing environment of organisations, a high degree of effort is essential to keep the explicit knowledge up to date. A continuous validity-checking and updating procedure is necessary, but usually not done sustainably due to the high resource involvement. Furthermore, implicit knowledge is hard to grasp requiring a high effort of knowledge elicitation techniques and also being not able to cover knowledge fully from employees. If some parts might be captured it has to be retained in explicit knowledge sources, i.e. updated continuously. The know-how and experience of employees grow from a day-to-day routine and cannot be achieved in the short term.

Consequently, relying only on explicit knowledge is not adequate for acquiring the essential implicit knowledge of employees. This perception is substantiated by the social exchange theory, which describes the connection between employees in companies [12]. The social relationships with alternating support between employees in companies lead to better results than explicating knowledge and consuming this documented knowledge [35]. However, the connections between employees rely on the behaviour of employees to consider colleagues as supports and not as rivals [36].

Such a connection of knowledge on the level of business processes can be regarded as independent from the type of organisation with regard to handling knowledge. Courtney [37] describes five types of organisations (Leibniz, Locke, Kant, Hegel and Singer) showing different characteristics in decision-making style, knowledge perspective/mode, knowledge creation process and supporting information technology. Knowledge generation ranges from a linear and internal formal procedure (Leibniz, Locke) over a discursive, thus more open to different external influences, and analytical (Hegel, Kant) to a procedure that considers every possible knowledge source and type as relevant that should continuously be absorbed (Singer). While each type has different procedures to generate and distribute knowledge, every knowledge incorporation will be reflected in the business processes of an organisation whether these are more or less formal [38]. Additionally, knowledge regarding process execution will remain explicit and tacit independently from the described organisational types as not every information regarding process execution can be made explicit.

## 4. Related work

Our search for related literature on approaches to provide knowledge for employees revealed 182 articles in the chosen databases. The list of articles was scanned according to title and abstract, resulting in 14 articles. Other hits related mainly either to cognitive knowledge maps of individuals, to context-specific knowledge maps of a nonorganisational domain or to analysing the learning process of specific topics using knowledge maps. The remaining 14 articles focus on providing a knowledge overview in the organisational context. The purpose of such knowledge maps is to develop a visual representation of knowledge carriers, stocks, sources, structures and applications within an organisation [39]. Existing approaches can be structured as follows:

▪ Visualising explicit knowledge: Keane et al. [10] mainly focus on process maps in which explicit knowledge in the form of process execution data and management and intranet databases is integrated. Tacit knowledge is only considered insofar as it should be put into the databases by employees and thus linked to the process maps. A different approach is presented by Yoo et al. [40], who propose knowledge maps with differentiating process and knowledge (information) flows. The idea is to highlight the differences between both as information flows can be different from process flows. Another type of knowledge maps is focusing on incorporating documents independently from business processes, e.g. Dang et al. [20] in the domain of scientific articles and patents.

▪ Explication of tacit knowledge: Approaches in this field attempt to explicate tacit knowledge in general. The mapping of tacit knowledge in so-called knowledge maps is promoted by Howard [41] and focuses on cause-and-effect diagrams in order to visualise knowledge relations. Further development of this approach by Wexler [42] includes different roles such as map designers, users and managers, with their heterogeneous needs.

▪ Explication of process-oriented tacit knowledge: Such approaches concentrate on collecting information about the business processes to cover the essential knowledge. Tacit knowledge is then explicated and knowledge maps are created that do not contain the processes anymore. Additionally, explicated tacit knowledge can no longer be traced back to the knowledge carriers. According to Tuggle and Goldfinger [11], an attempt is made to externalise the tacit knowledge of the processes concerning employees, detailed process steps and communication on the basis of a repository of business processes. The approach of Kim et al. [43] uses a process map as a starting point to gather and externalise explicit and tacit knowledge. For the purpose of process improvement (applying the methodology Business Reengineering), Rao et al. [16] create knowledge maps based on available documentations and expert interview information which contain the information of process maps. The single purpose of these maps is to identify process improvements in a Business Reengineering project which are derived by the project leader through assigning priority characteristics.

▪ Implementation of knowledge maps in organisations: Vail [44] makes a proposal to create a basic condition for implementing knowledge maps that close the gap between knowledge of the functional organisation's employees and the supporting IT. Eppler [17] concentrates on the details of an implementation in an intranet with a fivestep method. To identify the knowledge-intensive tasks, the valuechain and business processes need to be analysed and experts interviewed. In the domain of software maintenance, Lee and Fink [45] highlight based on an empirical investigation that personal perceptions (usefulness, ease of use, compatibility) and subjective norms (peer influence, culture and semantics) have the most important influence on the adoption of knowledge maps. However, organisational management still has to encourage the adoption with communication, incentives, supporting facilities and a responsible management champion.

▪ Technical level of knowledge maps: Kang et al. [46] present an approach for the technical realisation of knowledge maps on the basis of workflow-management systems. However, the tacit knowledge is not considered in this concept, only the explicit knowledge within processes is in focus. Hao et al. [2] substantiate this general idea by treating a knowledge map as a mathematical graph with the aim of identifying important knowledge. Using documents, semantic similarities of the contained text fragments are calculated and displayed as knots. Knots with a high number of connections indicate more important knowledge. Irani et al. [47] use the technique of knowledge mapping in order to assess generic knowledge flows within organisations. The purpose is to discover where information systems support the acquisition and diffusion of knowledge within an organisation. Lin and Hsueh [48] describe a knowledge map management system suitable for every type of generated knowledge map containing explicit knowledge. Thus, text is indexed and keywords are extracted which are then weighted. These keywords should then be classified and clustered to be presentable for users. Similarly, Nevo and Wand [49] postulate the positive effects of using technology-enhanced solutions to retain explicit as well as tacit knowledge. The authors base their considerations on the transactive memory approach that conceptualises an underlying system for supporting the exchange of knowledge between individuals' memories. Such a meta-memory should classify knowledge according to the subject (employees), the retainer (e.g. knowledge regarding a customer) and the meta-knowledge (specific knowledge how to deal with a customer). While the authors try to describe explicit meta-knowledge of tacit knowledge, the connection of such knowledge from a context perspective is not addressed. None of the four approaches refer to process-related knowledge but try to find

structures in an inductive way within explicit knowledge. Thus, the approaches are not suitable for mapping concrete tacit knowledge in company processes.

▪ Social level of knowledge maps: An important aspect of the introduction of knowledge maps is the intention of employees to participate voluntarily [50]. Typical barriers preventing the acceptance of a knowledge map system can be seen e.g. in a missing usability, instability of the system, imposition of IT and fears that an organisation is not able to create a knowledge sharing culture as reported by Damodaran and Olphert [51] using a case study design. Stewart and Osei-Bryson [50] show with empirical evidence regarding help desk solutions show that intrinsic motivation, organisational commitment, social inclusion, perceived ease of use, extrinsic motivation, conducive organisational climate, knowledge self-efficacy, low knowledge-sharing costs and top management support have a positive influence on the intention of employees to contribute their knowledge. The design of a knowledge map should take these aspects into account to ensure a high acceptance.

Concerning their individual purpose, approaches are reasonable, but a concept for mapping tacit and explicit knowledge referring to business processes is missing. The existing approaches regarding the explication of tacit knowledge require an immense effort to implement a knowledge map, complicated by the necessity of continuous updates. Additionally, the approaches address the organisational management due to the abstract overview focus not taking the continuous use by employees into account. For the purpose of providing process-related knowledge to and for employees in an organisation in line with the social exchange theory and that knowledge should be process related [52, 53] as well as addressing potential barriers for acceptance [51], the following requirements regarding the design of a solution can be derived: (1) The approach has to be structured clearly with regard to business processes. (2) Connect various sources of explicit and tacit knowledge. Regarding the usage of a solution requirements are as follows: (1) The approach has to provide the relevant knowledge and (2) has to be usable in the day-to-day routine. (3) The approach has to be accepted by employees to ensure integration into the day-to-day routine. (4) The approach has to be able to be kept up to date without a large effort to lead to a continuous use. These requirements are only partly addressed by existing approaches, but will be taken into account by the processbased social knowledge system.

## 5. Process-based social knowledge system

The tacit knowledge of an organisation should not be transferred from the minds of its employees into a knowledge map. The purpose is, rather, to indicate which employee is the knowledge owner for certain processes. Thus, we adopt Davenport's general understanding with regard to gathering tacit knowledge [53,p. 72]: “A knowledge map – whether it is an actual map, a knowledge ‘Yellow Pages,’ or a cleverly constructed database – points to knowledge but doesn't contain it. It is a guide, not a repository.” Therefore, we are not trying to explicate tacit knowledge, accompanied by the advantage of avoiding the immense transformation effort and the issue of not covering ongoing changes. In contrast, we propose an internal social network system that is closely linked to the processes of an organisation and does not contain the tacit knowledge itself. The idea is to motivate employees to indicate their areas of expertise and to claim expert status for other employees.

We apply the four primary classification principles for knowledge maps from Eppler [54,p. 68]: (1) Which knowledge-management purpose do I want to achieve with the map? — The “why?” of the map. (2) What kind of content about knowledge do I want to represent in the map? — The “what?” of the map. (3) Who should use the map in which context or situation and at what level? — The “for whom?” and “when?” of the map. (4) Which graphic form should be used and who can create the map in what way? — The “how?” and “who?” of the map.

While these principles can be used to sort existing knowledge maps, they can also be applied to develop a new type of knowledge map as the one proposed in this article. We address the aspects with our processbased social knowledge system by picking up the design principles and usage requirements. The development of the proposed system is conducted independently from a specific industry context as the described problems are supposed to be evident in any industry.

## 5.1. The “why”

The purpose of the process-based social knowledge system is to provide easy access for receiving an overview of available knowledge sources for process-execution related questions. The system illustrates the explicit and tacit knowledge sources for organisational processes while the knowledge itself is not contained. Tacit knowledge is not explicated following the idea of the transactive memory approach [55] to design the process-based social knowledge system [49]. Thus, the tool includes information regarding knowledge origin, that is, which employees possess or which databases and documents contain required knowledge on processes.

The system can be searched quickly and systematically for information to indicate where potentially necessary process knowledge can be found. The random search for information can be improved and employees will be less dependent on their individual informal networks. Using the social knowledge system, employees can see their connection to other employees in the indicated areas of expertise, and extend or start up their own social process-based knowledge network. The idea is to motivate employees to indicate their areas of expertise and to claim expert status for other employees.

The system targets every type of organisation with regard to differ ent approaches of knowledge management [37] as described in Section 3. The processes covered are not on a detailed level (see Section 5.1 for a description) and as such this explicit knowledge needs to be extended by tacit knowledge from employees when performing a task.

Summarising the purposes of knowledge management, the four aspired benefits of the process-based social knowledge map for business processes and their experts are: enabling employees to connect with each other; increasing the quality of search results; reducing the time needed for the search process; and motivating employees to provide and share knowledge.

## 5.2. The “what”

Using the criteria of Mansingh et al. [33] for building knowledge maps, the first step is to build an ontology. In our case, the ontology focuses on the processes as these provide the basis for assigning relevant knowledge in an organisation. The processes represent the relevant schema of an organisation from our perspective and their connections are depicted in a process model [56]. Thus, the starting point for the introduction of our social knowledge system lies in the organisation's business processes. Processes define the business-related connections between employees [57]. The second step is the identification of processes. Nevertheless, recording processes in detail is not the objective of this step, as only the processes and their main activities are relevant for the purpose of the social knowledge system. The focus is not on modelling processes in detail with incorporating sequential or nonsequential as well as standardised and individual information. Only the processes and their main activities are relevant for the purpose of the social knowledge system that are included in the main process architecture. A process architecture describes the basic structure of an organisation and the main connections between its processes and subprocesses [58,59]. If a significant number of processes are mapped, the illustration should comprise multiple levels. In this way, core processes

As well as the reference to explicit knowledge in the form of documented process models, knowledge databases and documents, tacit knowledge is assigned by indication of the process experts or knowledge owners of the processes. The map provides an overview of where to find explicit and tacit knowledge about the department's processes. Explicit knowledge is not imported, but knowledge in existing databases is linked to the map. Necessary assignments take place by the users when indicating that they are responsible for a process. These users are then questioned whether any known documents are of help regarding a process step and a dropdown menu of existing databases that are linked to the map in general is opened. Users can select relevant documents if available.

The knowledge map does not require a transformation of tacit into explicit knowledge. Instead, the focus is on creating a social knowledge system that simplifies the identification of knowledge owners and therefore enables employees to connect with each other in business processes.

The third step according to Mansingh et al. [33] is the extraction of instances of a process which in our case is covered within the usage of the system. An instance is created if an employee is seeking knowledge and contacting an employee regarding a sub-process. The system can record how often users searched for knowledge and provide this information to other users and managers.

## 5.3. The “for whom” and “when”

Target groups of the process-based social knowledge system are employees and managers. The primary function of the map is to serve as a knowledge directory, that is, the software provides a search function with a focus on the department's processes. The search result enables employees to find specific processes containing the related process information and documentation. Within the process information, process experts and knowledge owners are indicated for specific processes. This enables employees to connect with these colleagues to share and exchange tacit knowledge. This possibility helps to expand employees' social networks within the department and even helps new employees to set up their own networks. No further rules for communication were established as the assigned employees are responsible for providing information to colleagues as part of their job.

Managers can use the system to get an overview of the sources of explicit and tacit knowledge. Knowledge loss is often not discovered and only noticeable after a delay, when problems occur in the relevant processes. Then employees from other departments who possess process knowledge from a former position can help short term. If an employee leaves the organisation, it will be easier to discover in which processes knowledge is being lost. Proactive management of tacit knowledge can be implemented. For this purpose, managers can evaluate existing knowledge to discover how many individuals in the organisation own knowledge about the particular processes and activities. Thus, knowledge transfer can be arranged for activities that are known only by one employee.

## 5.4. The “how” and “who”

The introduction of the process-based knowledge system has to be supported by top management who need to be convinced with the described functionalities for managers. They should support the introduction to create organisational commitment while the design features of the map target social inclusion and conducive organisational commitment among employees and managers.

The system itself consists of a dynamic user front-end, a process architecture structure, an archive function of links and an instant search function. The knowledge-map builder facilitates the import of links to personal user pages, documents, images and web pages. The viewer version of the software enables employees to utilise the generated knowledge map without an editing or transformation function. Employees are provided with the map containing the processes initially and have to assign themselves to micro-processes for which they possess knowledge. Options offered are related to working currently in a micro process and having worked in the past. The level of expertise in each category should be determined [49] and can be chosen on a scale from low (1) over medium (2) to high (3). For each micro-process in which they are no longer working, a maximum timespan should be defined for which employees are displayed on the knowledge map. The aim is to ensure a mapping of experts that can provide useful and up-to-date knowledge for the employees who are inquiring.

To guarantee integration into the day-to-day business the viewer version of the software-based tool guarantees easy and direct access, whereas the knowledge-map builder is only available for a restricted number of employees to build and keep the map up to date. Codification and mapping are two complementary prerequisites for a successful searching and locating process, therefore basic technology is necessary for knowledge management and especially knowledge mapping [44]. The codification and mapping are retained from the existing process map. The graphical visualisation of the process-based social knowledge map is mind-map oriented and shows the connection of sub-processes ordered by arrows, as shown in the example in Fig. 1.

The map in the example is based on eight main process areas in the first level. clustered in products such as Securities Institutional. Securities Retail and Securities Investment Banking. The second level separates the groups into the product clusters such as physical securities or collateral management. The third level contains the processes itself. The knowledge map display utilises the whole window without downsizing through navigation boxes. The user is enabled to customise the quantity of levels and the items on each level. In addition, when the user navigates through the process structure by selecting a new item (process), the map rearranges automatically and moves the new selected item to the centre of the screen. The tool indicates via “mouse over” the process experts who are ordered by level of competence. The ranking of the level of competence is derived from self-rating by employees for each process step they claim knowledge for. Scores are calculated with a value of 2 for working in the process and 1 working previously (up to one year) multiplied with the level of expertise (i.e. 6: Currently working, High; 4: Currently working, Medium; 3: Previously working, High; 2: Currently working, Low as well as previously working, Medium; 1: Previously working, Low). If employees have the same score regarding a process step, order will be alphabetical. If users click on a selected expert picture (or a small preview of their contact details is visible), the expert profile is faded in with contact details and organisational level. It is important to mention that the user pages are not part of the tool; they are integrated via hyperlink and can be used within the tool. If further navigation within the organisational structure is necessary, the profile pops up in a new window.

For each of these scores, the respective score is multiplied with the distance to the process step found (e.g. two steps further on results in “2”) as well as with the level of detail in the process map (e.g. both process steps being connected in the same process results in “3” whereas if the link is via one level above – second level – then the value is “2”). The idea behind this ranking is that the more far away the process step which has a hit is away from the process steps with expertise from the search person, the more unlikely it is that it is of relevancy regarding the search objective.

## 6. Measures and procedure

## 6.1. Categories for evaluating the usage of the knowledge system

The dominant model to measure information systems (IS) success is from Delone and McLean [62,63]. First proposed in 1992, it was updated in 2003 to integrate empirical evidence and suggestions from other authors and to adapt to the internet age. Success of an IS is structured in three pillars, of which pillar one influences pillar two and pillar two influences pillar three. The two constructs in pillar one are system and information quality. Pillar two consists of use and user satisfaction, which are not only influenced by the first pillar but also interrelated. In the third pillar, individual impact is influenced by both constructs of pillar 2 and also influences organisational impact. This model was updated with the construct service quality, which also has an influence on use and user satisfaction. In addition individual and organisational impact is grouped into net benefits that are not only influenced by use and user satisfaction but also influence both. In the context of decision making, Larcker and Lessig [64] differentiate between perceived usefulness by whether provided data has to be processed further on and how helpful the information about the respective decision is. This separation of the retrieved information is in line with the two constructs of system quality and information quality. In the context of process-oriented knowledge, an employee needs information about process execution. Thus, clarity and accuracy of information is essential for a system providing such information.

When evaluating an IS, general models are helpful to structure the relevant constructs to be measured, but they have to be customised for the context and purpose of an IS [62]. In the domain of enterprise systems, Sedera and Gable [65] developed a comprehensive IS success measurement instrument. The authors found the constructs' system quality, information quality, individual impact and organisational impact to be relevant. While an enterprise system broadly covers functional aspects in a company, our knowledge-management system serves a specific function within a company. However, the application area (company IS) and the purpose (business-related information provision) are similar. Regarding the domain of knowledge maps, Wang et al. [66] analyse the impact of ease of use and perceived usefulness on the attitude to and behaviour intention of using a knowledge map system. This conceptualisation is based on the technology acceptance model from Davis [67], which serves predicting and explaining IS usage. These two constructs are in line with Sedera and Gable [65] but leave out a more detailed conceptualisation of the perceived usefulness. Doll and Torkzadeh [68] developed a measurement scale for system quality that differentiates into content, accuracy, format, ease of use and timeliness. According to the items of the constructs, the five constructs can be grouped into three perspectives. While content can be seen as an indicator for information quality, system quality can be seen from two perspectives. Accuracy, format and timeliness refer more to the speci c functionality of the system, while ease of use can be assigned to usability of the system in general.

Adapting the measurement instrument from Sedera and Gable [65] to our context and taking into account the idea of separating system quality into general usability and search functionality, we derive the following categories:

▪ General usability (system quality): This category covers ease of use of the knowledge system.

▪ Search functionality (system quality): Within this category we evaluate ease of learning, system features and system accuracy of the search process.

▪ Results quality (information quality): We evaluate the retrieved information on availability, conciseness, relevance and understandability.

▪ Social networking benefit (individual impact): Encouragement of individual users to exchange knowledge is measured by individual productivity.

Additionally, we cover the category “Intensity of usage.” This category evaluates how intensely the system is used over a certain time period. It also compares the usage of more than one system to derive whether the participants' perception of the knowledge systems is on a similar level.

The evaluation criteria cover the defined usage requirements as follows: (1) Provision of relevant knowledge is addressed by the category “Results quality” as users evaluate whether adequate results are provided. (2) Usability in day-to-day routine is addressed by the category “General Usability” as users evaluate how easy they can use the system in their daily work. (3) Acceptance by employees is addressed by the category “Intensity of usage” as this category refers to whether employees use the system or not. (4) Continuous usage is addressed by the category “Intensity of usage” in case of a long-term analysis as it reflects whether users accept the system over time.

## 6.2. Questionnaire

The main demographics regarding age and working experience in the company are scaled in intervals. Additionally, employees are asked about their hierarchical level (managerial position vs. employees without managerial function) and their main task in the job (processing, project work, specialist task, managerial task and advisory). To cover potential motivation for using the map, we also included questions regarding process ownership (yes/no) and the personal social network (5-point Likert scale).

The introduction of knowledge management systems comes along with potential barriers [51]. Barriers for using the knowledge map can be seen in the areas of having no interest at all (Stage 1), having potential interest but no knowledge that it exists (Stage 2), having both and struggling with the installation (Stage 3) and stopping use after testing it (Stage 4). The questionnaire can be found in the Appendix. The proposed knowledge map has several characteristics from a user point of view. To ensure a reliable evaluation, it should be compared with existing possibilities. Thus, items refer to the existing system as well as to the new proposed knowledge map. The categories (see appendix) applying context-relevant variables from Sedera and Gable [65], including the subsequent items (5-point Likert scale), are contained in the questionnaire (applying a reflective modelling structure).

## 6.3. Procedure

The system is evaluated in two banks. The process-based knowledgemanagement system was implemented in the financial transaction department of a large German bank (Bank A). A knowledge-management system was already installed in this department limited to the presentation of explicit knowledge using a process-oriented structure. To reach a high acceptance within the department, the management team and process owners were part of the identification process. Overall, 832 processes could be mapped with one or more process experts. Employees' contact information is provided via a link to the existing company directory. Participants received emails containing an invitation to use the new system, an explanation and installation instructions with five weeks to test the knowledge map.

Within the second bank (Bank B), a prior system does not exist as we ask users two years after introduction. Participants have the knowledge system with over 600 processes continuously in use.

To ensure a high participation of employees (taking the criteria of Stewart and Osei-Bryson [50] into account), we addressed organisational issues in addition to aspects incorporated in the design of the processbased social knowledge system. We acquired top management support and thus an overall organisational commitment could be assured. In both banks, employees were open to a new solution providing a conducive organisational climate.

## 7. Evaluation results regarding the usage of the knowledge system

## 7.1. Descriptives

Both banks can be best characterised as Kantian systems following the types of organisations described in Section 3. Financial service companies in Germany are characterised by similar types of organisations mainly focused on analytical and functional designs to the nature of financial markets and products [69]. Decision-making is open but incorporates formal hierarchical procedures and organisational units focus on their functional aspects. Knowledge is created by financial models on data from existing and potential customers to identify financial products and manage asset portfolios. Nevertheless, next to analytical systems, data warehouses and groupware tools are used heavily.

Within Bank A, a total of 1000 employees were invited, of whom 153 (15.3%) participated in the questionnaire. None of the employees were stakeholders in the introduction of the knowledge map. Regarding Bank B, 500 employees were targeted of which 62 (12.4%) responded, all of them users. Demographics (showing no significant differences between both banks) show that the majority of participants are between 30 and 49 (30–39: 33.6%; 40–49: 34.4%), 16.0% are between 50 and 59, 15.3% are between 20 and 29 and 0.7% are over 60 years old. 14.4% of the participants are managers.

The majority of participants without a managerial position has working experience of 10 to 19 years in the company (46.6%), 17.6% have worked for up to 3 years in the company, 18.3% for 4 to 6 years, 3.1% for 7 to 9 years, and 14.4% for over 20 years. Participants work mainly in processing (47.3%) and special task jobs (37.4%). The others spend most of their working time on project work (13.7%) and advisory (1.5%). Of the participants, 15.3% have ownership of at least one process. The personal social network of participants without a managerial position is almost average with 3.17 (SD: 0.921).

Managers are older than their subordinates on average, i.e. 27.3% are between 30 and 39 years, 54.5% are between 40 and 49 as well as 18.2% are between 50 and 59. In line with the higher age, managers have a higher working experience with 45.5% between 10 and 19 years and 54.5% over 20 years. Managers in both banks tend to maintain in the banks for a longer time as the working experience with the bank is 0 to 3 years for 9.1%, 4 to 6 years for 18.2%, 10 to 19 years for 50.0% and over 20 years for 22.7%. The working time of managers is predominantly dedicated to managerial tasks (90.9%) while 4.5% are spent for project tasks as well as 4.5% for special task jobs. 50% of the managers have process ownership in addition to their hierarchical position. The personal social network of managers is a bit higher than for employees (M: 3.86; SD: 0.744).

Out of 153 employees from Bank A, 65 (42.5%) were users of the knowledge map. The 88 participants who did not use the knowledge map were asked about the barriers. Of the 88, 10.2% reported having no interest at all in such a map, 36.4% did not know about the introduction and 15.9% knew about it but did not install it as they found the installation too complicated. Other barriers for not installing it were having no need so far (13.6%) or having no time (8.0%). Some participants installed the map but did not use it as it was too complicated (10.2%) or their relevant content was missing (3.4%). The remaining 2.7% reported other barriers which cannot be put into one of the categories.

## 7.3. Evaluation of the usage of the knowledge system

To confirm the validity of the concept for evaluating the perceived usefulness of process-oriented knowledge-management systems, we conduct a confirmatory factor analysis. A main-axis factor analysis with varimax rotation is applied fulfilling the reliability criteria of Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (0.921) and Bartlett's test of sphericity (χ(300) = 2937.53 (p b .001)). Results confirm the measurement model. The reliability of measuring the categories with the described items is also confirmed by Cronbach's Alpha values for each category (General usability 0.899, Search functionality 0.960, Results quality 0.902, Social networking benefit 0.946, Intensity of usage 0.926).

Within Bank A, the usage of the existing and proposed knowledge systems is not statistically different (T(106) = −0.515, ns). Participants used both systems rarely to occasionally (existing system 2.27, new system 2.36) within the five week period. Thus, the answers regarding the four evaluation categories can be compared without a bias. Within Bank B, participants show a significantly higher usage of 4.11 indicating that the system has been established $\mathrm { F } ( 2 , 1 6 7 ) = 7 7 . 8 8 6 , \mathrm { p } < . 0 0 1$ . The results of an ANOVA to compare the three results show that the new system is rated significantly better in every category except for the general usability. The results regarding the general usability indicate no difference between the three measures (Games-Howell post-hoc test, ns). Comparing the search functionality, the new system is significantly better in Bank A (Games-Howell post-hoc test, p b .001) and results of Bank B regarding the new system are better than the existing system in Bank A (Games-Howell post-hoc test, p b .01). The results of the new system in Bank B are also better (Games-Howell post-hoc test, p b .05) while there is no difference between implementations of the new system (Games-Howell post-hoc test, ns).

## 8. Discussion

Retrieving information about required knowledge in process execution is not necessary every day. Employees are normally quite familiar with the necessary knowledge to perform their daily tasks. Additional knowledge is typically required when working in a new process, when executing a non-day-to-day process (e.g. once a year), in the case of an exception, and where there is a change in external process information. Thus, employees should not use a knowledge-management system every day. This assumed behaviour is in line with the observed usage rate of the two knowledge systems.

Employees using one or both of the knowledge-management systems find the general usability of both systems slightly acceptable. General usability is rated higher for the existing system, but not statistically significant. It should be considered that users are expected to be more familiar with the existing system which is not a valid argument taking the results of Bank A into account. Nevertheless, this category is less important in comparison to the other three categories, which focus more on the specific functions. Here, we achieve improvement rates between 22% and 24%.

In addition, taking the working experience with the bank (in both banks) into account, it is surprising that there is no difference observed. Although employees work for a relatively long time with the bank, they still see a valuable contribution in the implemented process-based social knowledge system. Personal social knowledge nets seem to be limited even if employees stay in a bank for a longer time and thus a tool providing an overview is still be regarded as valuable.

Despite the promising results, the level of acceptance leaves room for improvement in comparison to the maximum achievable rating especially regarding search functionality, results quality and social networking benefit. It could be that the evaluation results are improved by more employees participating in the knowledge exchange and being more familiar with the new knowledge-management system. The verification of results with data two years after introduction provides evidence that there is no improvement over time. One reason for this result could be an adapted expectation requiring more functions and thus influencing evaluation results in a negative way [70].

Regarding the necessity of the developed process-based social knowledge system, the percentage of employees having no interest at all is comparably low. Thus, following the results of Wang et al. [66] from the domain of virtual communities, a high participation can be expected if the system is promoted and applied over a longer period of time. Major opportunities to turn non-users into users can be seen in informing them more and providing easier installation possibilities. Almost 25% indicate no need or time, which has to be seen in the light of the five-week evaluation period. Within Bank B, the majority of employees use the new system while the part of the questionnaire on reasons for non-usage could not be distributed due to organisational privacy reasons. Thus, we can expect that the system is used broadly but draw no conclusions on reasons for non-usage in the long-term.

The results for users vs. non-users show that employees working in processing have less interest in using the knowledge-management system. It can be assumed that such jobs are more standardised in comparison to special tasks, managerial tasks and advisory jobs as well as project work. Thus, less unknown information regarding task execution is needed. In addition, demographics indicate that younger employees are more likely to use a new system than older employees. It could be that the latter group have an established social network and do not require a social knowledge system. However, as having no need at all is not a major argument for non-usage but installation issues are, it would be beneficial to make the installation easier.

The knowledge system addresses both design principles as it integrates business processes and allows for the connection of tacit and explicit knowledge sources. Existing approaches referenced are extended as follows:

▪ Visualising explicit knowledge: Approaches such as that of Keane et al. [10] are extended as tacit knowledge is integrated in addition to explicit knowledge. As we do not put the knowledge into the maps, we do not differentiate between process-related and information flowrelated knowledge.

▪ Explication of tacit knowledge: Contrary to approaches such as Wexler's [42], our system proposes the use of a directory instead of a repository. Both can be used in coexistence, but a company's main focus should be on supporting the exchange of tacit knowledge in the first place. Thus, our system delivers the possibility of having an overview of and managing tacit knowledge.

▪ Explication of process-oriented tacit knowledge: Approaches such as that of Kim et al. [43] are extended by using a dynamic approach for the connection of processes and knowledge. Our system highlights ongoing updating by employees. Thus, centralisation should be avoided to cover the relevant tacit knowledge at particular points in time. Rather, employees are encouraged to do this continuously on their own. Starting from process maps, explicit knowledge (from documents and ISs) as well as tacit knowledge (generated by expert interviews) is gathered and externalised.

▪ Implementation of knowledge maps in organisations: The approaches of Vail [44] and Eppler [17] are extended by clarifying their generic steps to implement a knowledge map in the given context. Furthermore, their general ideas are applied to a knowledge map focusing on business processes. Moreover, results from Lee and Fink [45] in the domain of software maintenance are applied and extended in the domain of knowledge exchange in business processes.

▪ Technical level of knowledge maps: We extend the approach of Kang et al. [46] by describing the integration of the tacit knowledge directory with existing IT systems. A major difference to Irani et al. [47] is that we clearly point out process-related knowledge as an object of interest. These knowledge streams are connected with regard to tacit and explicit knowledge. Furthermore, while Nevo and Wand [49] describe a general idea of using the transactive memory approach, we clearly outline that processes should be the major criteria. Additionally, the purpose of our approach is not just to identify knowledge streams, but to provide accessibility to this knowledge for employees in their daily work and to foster social connections between employees.

▪ Social level of knowledge maps: The design of our system aims at a high organisational acceptance by targeting especially usability and the evaluation results show a positive indication that however can be improved. Additionally, we include top management support with advantages for managers and organisational commitment with the focus on social connections. While relevant aspects of Stewart and Osei-Bryson [50] have been included we did not incorporate intrinsic and extrinsic motivation leaving room for improvement when analysed in future work. Finally, our results of the evaluation did not point at systematic barriers regarding the system as identified by Damodaran and Olphert [51], but that could be different with other case studies.

Despite the observed benefits of the process-based social knowledge system, some limitations have to be considered as well. Firstly, the system does not cover areas of knowledge that are not directly connected to a process. This can be, for instance, how a documentation tool is properly used. However, the system allows for a knowledge network between employees for important business processes. Such a network allows for improved systematic knowledge exchange. Secondly, the introduction of a social knowledge system implies a significant effort for an organisation. If the existing processes have not yet been recorded sufficiently, this will have to be done first. In our evaluation, information about the processes and activities could be transferred from current process models. Thirdly, as the system does not lead to an externalisation of knowledge, it can be seen as a directory rather than a repository. Therefore, a loss of knowledge when employees leave the company cannot be avoided. Fourthly, the evaluation is limited to two case studies in the financial services industry although the proposed knowledge map is developed independently from a specific industrial context. The choice of the two case studies aims to increase rigour but there might be differences to other organisations and industries that have to be addressed in future research. Fifth, we did not analyse the motivation of employees to use the proposed process-based system. We addressed the reasons why employees were non-users, but the identification of detailed reasons for non-participation was not in the focus. However, an organisation could face difficulties with an introduction and thus the model of Stewart and Osei-Bryson [50] would be helpful in addressing such issues.

## 9. Conclusion

Using the conceptualisation presented, a sustainable implementation of a process-oriented social knowledge system could be set up within the two chosen organisations. Here, the system provides an overview of sources of explicit and tacit knowledge for process execution. It allows social connections between employees with regard to the knowledge needed in their daily work routine.

Practical implications are that knowledge exchange in companies should be process oriented and a system such as the one described should be used to support employees adequately. To be successful it is important to accompany the introduction with sufficient communication. Employees should receive guidance with the installation process or the software should be distributed with an automatic roll out to employees' computers. Further trainings regarding such a system are not necessary as the results have shown. Finally, our results also demonstrate that the critical aspect of a continuous usage to foster an ongoing knowledge exchange can be achieved with our proposed approach.

Theoretical implications are that social exchange is necessary in business driven knowledge exchange. It is not sufficient to provide explicit knowledge independent from the type of knowledge. Knowledge should be process oriented, i.e. the business process paradigm is also relevant in this context.

The next steps in research should concentrate on overcoming the limitations discussed. First, further analysis is required of how adequate details on the levels of business processes can be identified. Further research has to aim at finding a balance between the coverage of necessary details and reduction of complexity to ensure model use. Second, the type of knowledge that is not connected to business processes has to be identified. Once the characteristics are known, analyses should be undertaken of whether and how such non-process-related knowledge can be integrated into the proposed system. Third, the connection of the directory with the repositories has to be analysed. A mechanism should be developed allowing the identification of suitable tacit knowledge that can be transferred into explicit forms. Thus, the loss of knowledge when employees leave the company is intended to be reduced. Fourth, research should concentrate on improving the processbased social knowledge system to achieve higher evaluation scores. This analysis should be focused on broadening the scope towards the organisational perspective. Fifth, the evaluation should be broadened to other case organisations including other industries. Sixth, reasons for understanding and enhancing reasons for participation in the social knowledge system should be addressed in further detail.
