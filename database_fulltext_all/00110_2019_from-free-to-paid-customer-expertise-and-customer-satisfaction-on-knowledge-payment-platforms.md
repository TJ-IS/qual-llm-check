# From free to paid: Customer expertise and customer satisfaction on knowledge payment platforms<sup>☆</sup>

Jin Zhang<sup>a</sup>, Jilong Zhang<sup>a</sup>, Mingyue Zhang<sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> School of Business, Renmin University of China, Beijing 100872, China

<sup>b</sup> School of Business and Management, Shanghai International Studies University, Shanghai 201620, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Knowledge payment Customer expertise Customer satisfaction Price Customer segmentation

## A B S T R A C T

In this study, we investigate what factors are influential to customer satisfaction of paid knowledge, especially among diferent customer segments, by integrating user activities on both free and paid platforms. Considering the complexity of knowledge acquisition, we first propose a novel measurement of “customer expertise” based on text mining, as a criterion for customer segmentation. Drawing upon the value-percept diversity theory, we then postulate a conceptual model proposing that customers with diferent expertise would react diferently to the price of knowledge and historical knowledge-consuming transactions, in terms of customer satisfaction. We test the model empirically through the hierarchical OLS regression with data collected from Zhihu and Zhihu Live. Distinguishing expert and novice customers, we have findings that (1) expert customers are less sensitive to price; (2) historical price positively influences the satisfaction of novice customers, but negatively for expert customers; (3) expert customers are less influenced by historical satisfaction, which have important implications for market targeting and knowledge pricing strategy.

## 1. Introduction

Capitalizing on the popularity, some free knowledge sharing plat forms recently have attempted to charge their users for accessing some elaborate knowledge [1], which has fractured users into two groups: knowledge providers and knowledge customers. For example, <ce:italic>Zhihu</ce:italic> which used to be a free question & answer community, launched a pay-for-listening service, namely, <ce:italic>Zhihu Live</ce:italic> soon after, through which users can join live talks on specific topics given by experts for an entrance fee. The knowledge monetization industry significantly encourages professionals to provide more domain knowledge and helps customers to get high quality knowledge as a result [2]. By utilizing the benefit of large number of accumulated users on free knowledge sharing platforms, the knowledge payment mode has gained a big success with its estimated economy scale over 30 billion CNY in 2017 [1].

The core of maintaining the prosperity of a knowledge payment platform is to stimulate its members to spontaneously provide knowledge with high quality and thus to guarantee high satisfaction of knowledge customers, which remains neglected and obscure in existing literature yet. Limited researches about knowledge payment platforms mostly focus on maximizing the profit or sales of each Live, which emphasizes the pre-purchase behavior of customers [1,3-5]. However, due to the intangibility and inaccessibility of information goods before purchasing, customers have to take high perceived risk with limited pre-purchase knowledge [6], which further afects customer satisfaction. Therefore, besides the pre-purchase behavior, the post-purchasing satisfaction of knowledge customers also plays an important role for revealing valuable content and dedicated knowledge providers in knowledge consumption. This leaves a gap in our understanding of what factors influence customer satisfaction of knowledge consump tion. Knowledge is quite diferent from tangible goods in the following ways: (1) knowledge quality is hard to guarantee without traditional gate-keeping on the side of knowledge production; (2) As a result of (1), knowledge quality can not be easily evaluated before consuming [7]; (3) the perception of knowledge quality is related to the backgrounds and expertise levels of customers, and is considerably subjective [8]. These raise challenges when exploring the influential factors of customer satisfaction of knowledge consumption.

Factors explored from paid knowledge payment platforms provide basic clues for this research issue. Among the limited information on knowledge payment platforms, the price of knowledge is usually regarded as a significant indicator of a buyer's cost [3,4], but rarely a potential signal of knowledge quality [9]. Generally, customer satisfaction would be lower with higher price and lower quality from the cost-benefit theory view [10]. Moreover, from a dynamic perspective, customers' past knowledge consumption behavior may also play a role towards their current satisfaction since they tend to be “sticky” to some internal reference points [11], such as historical prices and historical satisfaction.

However, lacking customer depiction, those factors seem insuficient to explain the individual diference in satisfaction, which requires comprehensive customer profiles with rich data. To cope with that, we explored the data from a free knowledge sharing platform (Zhihu) and a paid knowledge payment platform (Zhihu Live) in this study, which share the same user base from free to paid. Those data on free knowledge platforms imply the interest and expertise of each customer, which contribute to explaining individual heterogeneity in perceived quality of purchased knowledge and thus customer satisfac tion. Accordingly, this motivates our research questions: On knowledge payment platforms, how knowledge price and customer historical consumption behavior influence customer satisfaction about the paid knowledge? Do such influences difer among customers with diferent levels of knowledge expertise? How to objectively measure customer expertise towards specific knowledge?

Our study focuses on the role of expertise in customer satisfaction on knowledge payment platforms, aiming to answer the above research questions. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has been devoted to this specific research topic. Therefore, we propose a textmining method to measure customer expertise towards specific knowledge by utilizing the switching process of customers from free to paid knowledge platforms. Secondly, we build three hypotheses about customer satisfaction, moderated by customer expertise. The model is then empirically tested using the hierarchical OLS regression with data of Zhihu and Zhihu Live. Our findings reveal that (1) customers with higher level knowledge expertise are less sensitive to price; (2) historical price has positive influence on satisfaction of novice customers, but negative influence on satisfaction of expert customers; (3) customers with higher level knowledge expertise are less influenced by historical satisfaction.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Online knowledge sharing

Online knowledge sharing refers to the voluntary behavior of sharing knowledge for free on knowledge sharing platforms. Existing studies can be broadly divided into two streams: behavioral research and recommendation research. In behavioral research, scholars majorly focused on the motivation and the influencing factors of knowledge sharing behavior [12], including individual cognition [13,14], interpersonal interaction [15,16], and organizational context [17]. Specifically, from the perspective of social exchange, researchers [12,14] explained what motivated individuals to share. Other studies discussed the role of environment based on social capital theory [15,18] and organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) [17]. However, these studies paid little attention to the knowledge content itself, let alone the difference between knowledge content and its impact on customer behavior. The recommendation research can be categorized as answer retrieval and expert recommendation. where researchers conducted text mining to extract semantic information lurking in questions or answers. Answer retrieval is to achieve related answers to a given question [19], while expert recommendation aims to suggest potential responders based on semantic association between responders and questions [20].

## 2.2. Online knowledge consumption

Online knowledge consumption can be driven by either knowledge providers (expert users) or knowledge customers (general users). For the provider-driven mode, expert users design knowledge product/ service themselves, such as lectures and tutorials, for one-to-many promotion. For the customer-driven mode, customers raise questions with rewards, and then invite professionals or celebrities to give one-toone consultation. Most of existing literature focuses on the providerdriven mode [1-3,5,21], in which this study can be also positioned.

In the provider-driven mode, some studies focused on revenue maximization for a single provider or knowledge product, considering sales [1], customers' willingness-to-pay (WTP) [5] and payment deci sion [4]. Some other researchers investigated the influence of trust [21] or monetary incentive [2] on free knowledge contribution. However, none of these studies put efort on customer satisfaction to paid knowledge, which was found to further afect repeat sales, word of mouth, customer loyalty, and overall firm performance in other domains [22]. Customer satisfaction reflects the perceived knowledge benefit gained by customers on knowledge payment platforms, which further promotes repurchase and encourages high-qualified knowledge products from providers. Thus, the gap of customer satisfaction of online knowledge consumption highly motivates our study to explore its influencing factors for diferent customer segments.

## 2.3. Customer expertise

Customer expertise can be defined as the ability of customers to perform product/service-related tasks successfully and their understanding of knowledge about various attributes in a product/service category [8]. Expertise was generally discussed in technical or highly customized service, such as finance [23], DIY retailing [8] and legal service [24]. The impact of customer expertise on perceived technical/ functional service quality has been explored [23], which further influences trust [23], loyalty [8] and satisfaction [8,24]. Similar to studies on customer expertise, other studies on online shopping revealed the moderate efect of customer product knowledge on satisfaction [25] and purchase decision-making process [26]. However, the influence of customer expertise in knowledge consumption scenario has rarely been studied. For the measurement of customer expertise, most behavioral studies mainly depended on survey data (self-reported expertise [8,24], or several simple questions [23,26]). Instead, we measured it through text mining based on the historical knowledge activities of each user in this study. Such measurement is inspired by expert finding studies in knowledge management [27,28]. The task of finding experts is defined as follows: given a collection of crawled documents, a list of expert candidates and a set of topics. find the experts for each of these topics. The most popular approach for expert finding is called the candidatebased approach which estimates the expertise scores by textual similarities between the topics and candidates' profiles. For instance, Balog et al. [27] first created a textual representation of the individuals' knowledge according to the documents with which they were associated, and then defined expertise as its similarity with the textual representation of the given topic. Ha-Thuc et al. [28] computed the expertise score for a (member, skill) pair (m, s) using the inner product of the latent factor vectors $s _ { m s } = x _ { m } ^ { T } y _ { s } ,$ , where $x _ { m }$ represents the member and y represents the skill. And they finally got a vector representing the expertise scores for diferent skills $( x _ { m } ^ { T } y _ { s 1 } , x _ { m } ^ { T } y _ { s 2 } , . . . , x _ { m } ^ { T } y _ { s n } )$

## 3.1. Customer expertise

Knowledge is a special case of service with its own unique characteristics. The intangibility and non-standardization of service lead to the uncertainty in customer satisfaction [29], which is further amplified by the nature of “knowledge”. For general service, such as delivery service, there are relatively predictable outcomes and clear rules to evaluate, while knowledge seems more dificult to evaluate. For paid knowledge, the variance of perceived quality is attributed to customer expertise, such that individual heterogeneity in expertise may form diferent performance assessments [24,30]. For example, common folks are always confused with impressionist paintings by Vincent van Gogh, which is regarded as treasure by artists, owing to the varying perceived quality.

The learning efectiveness of paid knowledge is also largely di vergent with diferent expertise levels for the same knowledge. On knowledge payment platforms, online learners are easier to get confused due to the lack of face-to-face interaction. The expertise reflects previous knowledge accumulation on certain field, which leads to better understanding of similar knowledge. Therefore, expert customers tend to acquire more knowledge with better understanding of knowledge attributes. In this study, customer expertise can be extracted from free knowledge sharing platforms since there is much more abundant behavior information about customers' knowledge interest and demand.

## 3.2. Current price

Existing literature has revealed both positive [9] and negative roles of price [1,4,5] for online knowledge consumption. According to the cost-benefit theory [31], a rational individual will perform a certain activity only if the marginal benefits from doing so outweigh the marginal costs [10]. Intuitively, price is regarded as the economic cost for customers to access the knowledge [1]. With the same benefit but more cost, customer satisfaction will decrease [10]. Therefore, current price is negatively associated with the customer satisfaction.

Meanwhile, the theory of the value-percept diversity model predicts that satisfaction is a response to a cognitive-evaluative process [10]. It indicates that the value perception is based on mapping the price with individuals' subjective perception of quality. When the price of service/ product remains the same but the perceived quality increases, customers will feel more satisfied. For the same knowledge, novice customers may fail to realize or understand the sophisticated knowledge due to limited quality perception [24], while expert customers can gain additional fulfillment, which ofsets the corresponding cost to some extent. Therefore, we argue the moderate role of expertise in the negative relationship between current price and customer satisfaction, with the hypothesis:

H1. On online knowledge payment platforms, current price will be less negatively influential on customer satisfaction for individuals with higher level knowledge expertise, compared to those with lower level expertise.

## 3.3. Historical price

Historical price represents the deal prices of previous transactions. According to the view of Helson's adaptation level theory, individual would possess a corresponding average adaptation level based on stimuli in prior experience [32]. Therefore, historical price, as an explicit stimulus, would form the adaptation level and serve as a foundation when estimating further stimuli. Several researchers have also suggested some form of past prices as an appropriate price standard, such as Mayhew and Winer [33], Kalwani et al. [34] and Lichtenstein and Bearden [35]. This indicates that historical price would serve as reference price [36], which can be defined as “an internal standard against which observed prices are compared” [37]. In this regard, customers would compare the historical price of similar knowledge to the current encounter, which further influences their purchase choices [38]. Apparently, higher historical price leads to the perception of relatively lower current price since price perception is rather relative than absolute [39]. This further improves satisfaction because of lower perceived cost, in line with the cost-benefit theory.

Given that historical price has a positive impact on customer satisfaction through the formation of reference price, the magnitude of its impact may be moderated by the customer's expertise to the specific knowledge. Prior literature has found that there are distinguishing features of expert decision making [40]. Experts are more able to select information on the grounds of its relevance to the task at hand [41] and show a much stronger preference for efectual logic in decision making. Novices, on the other hand, use less complex decision rules and evaluate problems more on the “perceptual level” of the input information [40]. Regarding product evaluation task, expert customers with a great deal of product knowledge are able to reliably estimate the value of an item on the basis of its features [42]. Novice customers are likely to use extrinsic cues, such as price or brand name, to a greater extent than experts, due to their inability to analyze intrinsic cues [43]. This suggests that the environmental factors including price and historical prices may have diferent influences on expert and novice customers. More specifically, Biswas and Sherrell [43] examined the impact of reference prices on customers' final judgment and found that product knowledge played an important role. Customers having familiarity and knowledge of the product category are not likely to use reference prices to shift their product evaluation [44,45]. In our context, customers with high expertise of the paid knowledge are capable assessing the value or worth of a particular knowledge on the basis of its content, thus the reference price formed by historical prices would has less impact. Hence, we hypothesize:

H2a. On online knowledge payment platform, historical price will beless positively (or even negatively) influential on customer satisfaction for individuals withhigher level knowledge expertise, compared to those with lower level expertise.

## 3.4. Historical satisfaction

Koufteros et al. [46] distinguished the current transaction-specific encounter satisfaction and historical satisfaction and studied their relationships. Particularly, historical satisfaction is an evaluation of cumulative or summary satisfaction relating to all transactions prior to the current one, which better predicts customers' intentions and behavior [22,47]. Scholars have long recognized the cumulative effect of long term experiences with the product/service on customer judgment [48]. For example, customers with positive previous experiences tend to be more forgiving and tolerant [49]. The current encounter satisfaction is derived not only from the current experience, but also from historical satisfaction since all human interactions or evaluations develop and accumulate over time [47]. This is consistent with the adaptation theory [32] that historical satisfaction is viewed as the adaptation anchor for the consumer's evaluations of the current encounter. Hence, previous studies argue that historical satisfaction has main positive efects as an antecedent to encounter satisfaction by modeling it in an adaptation theory framework [46].

Consider next the moderating efects of expertise, in other words, whether customers' expertise to the specific knowledge interacts with historical satisfaction to determine the encounter satisfaction. As mentioned above, experts and novices have distinguishing features when making decisions, including product evaluation tasks [40,42]. Compared to novice customers, expert ones are sensitive to the current quality of knowledge, which leads to fair judgment based on the present experience. Novice customers with limited ability to distinguish knowledge quality are more afected by stereotype [40]. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2b. On online knowledge payment platform, historical satisfaction will beless positively influential on customer satisfaction for individuals withhigher level knowledge expertise, compared to those with lower level expertise.

## 4. Research context

The research was conducted on Zhihu with a free knowledge sharing service Zhihu and a paid knowledge service Zhihu Live. In Zhihu Live, there are two distinguishing roles, namely, speaker (knowledge provider) and customer (knowledge consumer). Speakers are encouraged to launch lectures (Lives) through multiple media like texts, images, video and audio. To access these Lives, customers should pay certain entrance fees, and then are able to leave comments with scores (1 to 5 stars), which are regarded as an explicit indicator of customer satisfaction. The vast majority of speakers and customers are also active on the free knowledge sharing platform (Zhihu), where they can publish original articles and answers to share knowledge or vote up others' articles. All the textual information can well reflect the knowledge domain or interest of certain users.

## 5. Research method

## 5.1. Data collection

As of July 2018, there had been 4010 Lives from 1756 speakers on Zhihu Live. We obtained the complete Live list, containing more than 270,000 customers and over 500,000 reviews. From this list, we managed to find a representative sample of customers through the system sampling method on reviews, i.e., choosing the first review in every 10 reviews. Then, the corresponding reviewers were extracted as the raw customer sample consisted of 41,071 members.

To reduce the bias from infrequent customers, we focused on frequent customers who had reviewed over 5 Lives. With rich experience, these customers were more likely to provide reasonable scores. Finally, the dataset consisted of 8538 frequent customers. We collected their complete activity history between their first day on the site (when they registered) and July 2018 from both free and paid platforms. Consequently, 100,780 reviews on 3911 Lives on Zhihu Live were reserved. We also collected the speakers' activity history of those lives on Zhihu (1687 speakers). ## 5.2. Customer expertise

On knowledge payment platforms, customer expertise means how expert a customer is with the knowledge (Live) s/he pays. It has been indicated that expertise crucially depends on the topic [27,28], which means that “expertise” towards diferent knowledge can be diferent for the same customer. Thus, for the measurement of customer expertise, we draw on the ideas of previous studies [27,28] measuring expertise with the similarity between a person and content/skill, to define expertise for each customer-Live pair by text mining. However, the knowledge in Live is far less explicit: (1) the title or description of Live is too short to provide suficient information; (2) the content of Live involving audio, video, pictures and slides, is complex to model jointly and not available before the start of Live. Thus, to overcome the problems above, an alternative method is used in this study to represent the knowledge of Live with the knowledge distribution of its speaker. For most speakers, it is very common that what they talk in Live is related to those they share on free platforms. For example, an influencer of IT on free platforms would probably provide Live about IT to his/her followers, rather than Lives about fashion or film. Given the same speaker, we assume the high correlation between a speaker's free knowledge distribution and the knowledge in Live. Based on this assumption, we could measure expertise with the similarity between a customer-speaker free knowledge distribution pair instead of a customer-Live pair. Although reasonable in most cases, it should be clearly pointed out that such assumption might raise potential issues for speakers disseminating little knowledge on free platforms or totally diferent knowledge in Live on paid platforms. In such case, the correlation between a speaker's free knowledge distribution and the knowledge in Live will become weak, which could be seen as the limitations of this study.

In this study, we calculated customer-Live expertise with the similarity of the knowledge distributions of the customer and the speaker generated by doc2vec model. Concretely, for each user, we firstly collected all the answers and articles s/he created or voted up, and then joined them end to end as the user's characteristic document, as shown in Fig. 2.

Then, we applied the doc2vec model to generate a vector of each user, visualized in Fig. 3. Doc2vec is an efective Neural Network-based unsupervised learning algorithm to learn vector representations for variable-length pieces of texts [50]. Doc2vec can be seen as an improved version of word2vec [51]. In word2vec, words are mapped into a numerical high-dimensional vector space by fully incorporating contextual and semantic information. Similar to word2vec, doc2vec could also transfer texts into high-dimensional vectors, which achieved out standing performance on text processing tasks [50]. Specifically, there were 10,208 characteristic documents from 10,208 users (1687 speakers and 8538 frequent customers) in the dataset. As a result, a 10,208 × 200 matrix was generated as the knowledge distribution matrix where 10,208 refers to the number of users and 200 refers to the dimension in doc2vec model.

Finally, the expertise of customer (C ) towards a Live by speaker (S ) was measured as the similarity of knowledge distribution (KD) between $C _ { i }$ and $S _ { j } ,$ , which equals the cosine metric between $K D _ { C _ { i } }$ and $K D _ { S j }$ as Eq. (1).

$$

The distinctiveness and advantage of the proposed expertise measurement is to mitigate the “cold start” problem in the knowledge payment platform by linking the customer's activities from free to paid platforms. Generally, the knowledge payment platform is evolved from the free knowledge sharing platform by capitalizing the popularity, and customer won’t make a purchase until they have fully experienced the free content. Hence, as a beginner customer for the paid platform (e.g., Zhihu Live), s/he has probably accumulated some activities such as reading or commenting on the free platform (e.g., Zhihu). We learn his/ her knowledge distribution from those activities in free platform with doc2vec model. Then an expertise score can be calculated as the similarity of knowledge distribution between the customer and the knowledge provider.

We conducted a user study to demonstrate the rationality of the measurement. We firstly chose three diferent topics, namely web frontend development, anime and musical composition, from Zhihu Live platform. Taking anime for example, we selected 10 most-rated Lives. For each Live, we ranked all its customers with their expertise, choosing the top 10% customers as the high-expertise segment (H) and bottom 10% as the low-expertise segment (L). We then invited 3 raters who were expert in anime according to their self-reports to rate the “expertise” of customers in both H and L segments. We expected that the “expertise” levels for H segment reported by the raters would be sig nificantly higher than those for L segment. If so, it could be concluded that the proposed measurement performed efective to distinguish different expertise levels. Concretely, since all the contents that the customer created or voted up were displayed in his/her homepage, we then asked the 3 raters to browse the homepage of each customer in both H and L segments. All the homepages were randomly assigned to each rater. After spending suficient time reading the homepage, the 3 raters were asked to provide answers to the following questions on a sevenpoint Likert scale.

Suppose that the customer purchased a Live about anime (Insert Live name here). (Q1) Is the customer able to understand the knowledge in the Live? (Q2) Is the customer able to evaluate the qualification of the Live? (Q3) How much is the expertise of the customer on anime?

The result of the user study is shown in Table 2. Taking anime for example, there are 18 customers in segment H and 19 customers in segment L.(Some customers closed their accounts, which leads to mismatch in sizes between segments H and L.) High-expertise customers are believed to be able to understand (4.93 > 3.33, t = 3.1356) and evaluate (4.93 > 3.32, t = 3.2199) the Live better than low-expertise customers, and are believed as more expert (4.24 > 2.58, t = 3.0952) in the domain of anime. Therefore, the proposed measurement consists with human's evaluation of expertise. Similarly, the results of the other two topics (i.e., web front-end development and musical composition) also show similar conclusions, supporting the rationality of the proposed measurement.

## 5.3. Main variable and summary statistics

## 5.3.1. Dependent variable

This study mainly explores the influential factors of customer satisfaction on knowledge consumption for customers with diferent degrees of expertise. In Zhihu Live, customers can rate the Lives after their participation, along with review texts. Numerical ratings on goods and services are deemed to well reflect the customer's overall satisfaction [52,53]. Therefore, numerical ratings were used to measure the individual satisfaction of each customer.

## 5.3.2. Independent variable and control variable

The independent variables include customer expertise, current Live price, customer historical price and historical satisfaction.

5.3.2.1. Customer expertise. To acquire a relative rather than absolute value, these expertise scores were further normalized in the final dataset. To eliminate the potential influence of outliers, all the standardized scores larger than 3 were set to 3, and scores lower than −3 were set to −3. Therefore, we finally got a standardized expertise score in [−3, 3] for each customer-Live pair.

5.3.2.2. Current Live price. Current price is directly displayed to customers in every Live page. The distribution of prices of all the 3911 Lives is shown in Table 3. The prices of over 90% Lives are between CNY 5 to CNY 40, and more than 99% Lives are between CNY 5 and CNY 100.

5.3.2.3. Historical price. Since the perception of the current Live price is rather relative than absolute [39], for frequent customers on Zhihu Live, they would unavoidably compare the current Live with other Lives s/he has participated. Therefore, for every customer, we calculated the average price of all the Lives s/he had reviewed, as the reference price of the customer, denoted as H\_price.

5.3.2.4. Historical satisfaction. To measure the historical satisfaction, we collected all the evaluation ratings provided by each customer in previous transactions. Considering the consistency among each rating, all the ratings given by the same customer would fluctuate around his/ her rating baseline. Therefore, we measured the historical satisfaction by the average rating of all the Lives reviewed by every customer, denoted as H\_sat.

## 5.4. Model specification

We used the hierarchical OLS regression analysis to test our hy potheses. For better interpretability, all the variables were scaled to zstandardized values $( z ( x ) = ( x - \bar { x } ) / s d ( x ) )$ . In Model 1, we only considered control variables. In Model 2, we tested the main efects of independent variables, current price, historical price, historical satisfaction, and customer expertise. As the primary focus of this study, we further tested the interaction terms of expertise and other three variables in Model 3 as follows:

$$

## 6.3. Robustness check

To validate our results, we conducted further experiments with diferent parameter settings of the text-mining method, alternative measurement of expertise and diferent text representation methods.

## 6.3.1. Dimension of doc2vec model

A potential caveat regarding our model estimation is that the expertise is calculated as the cosine of two doc2vec characteristic vectors with a given dimension level (i.e., 200). However, the dimension of doc2vec may have an impact on the measurement. To dispel the worry, we also tested other settings of dimension, including 10, 50 and 100. Table 7 shows the correlation matrix of these expertise values where doc2vec10 means doc2vec model with 10 dimensions. According to Table 7, expertise values calculated from vectors of diferent dimensions are highly correlated $( r > 0 . 7 )$ , which preliminarily suggests the consistency of expertise with diferent dimension.

We further tested again Model 3 (for main efects and interaction efects) with these new expertise values and the results are shown in Table 8. For all the main efect and interaction efect of the independent variables, the direction and significance of coeficients are independent of the dimension $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 5 )$ , which indicates that the dimension of doc2vec has no impact on our conclusions.

## 6.3.2. Alternative measurement of expertise

As a robustness check, we constructed an alternative measure of customer expertise and found consistent results. In previous models, we used all textual information of users (i.e., customers and speakers) from free knowledge sharing platforms to represent his/her knowledge ex pertise. This included the answers and articles s/he voted up or created. However, there is a slight diference between “vote up” and “create” activities. “Vote up” means users agree with the content written by others, while “create” reflects content written by themselves. Hence, it is worth considering that the characteristic document of each user could be built in a single perspective, i.e. “vote up” or “create” separately.

Secondly, we emphasize the role of customer expertise in online knowledge consumption, which contributes to customer segmentation research. In existing literature of knowledge consumption, scholars mainly explore tag-based segmentation of knowledge provider (e.g., real name authentication [4] and gender [1]) and knowledge content (e.g., topic [1]), while there are rare criteria for customer segmentation. Given the challenge in knowledge quality perception, we draw our attention to customer heterogeneity. Specifically, we extend our vision to the rich user behavior data on free knowledge sharing platforms, which contributes to depict the interest and specialty of users. We further distinguish customers with diferent levels of expertise towards various knowledge.

Thirdly, we contribute to value-percept diversity theory and adap tion level theory for revealing the diferent influence mechanisms under varying expertise levels. In our research, we extend the psychological theories to online knowledge payment context and found that expert customers percept the knowledge quality diferently compared to novice customers, as well as diferent adaption levels.

Fourthly, our study clarifies the influence of price and historical transactions on customer satisfaction in online knowledge consumption. For example, although researchers have found the dual role of current price as both economic cost and quality indicator, it remains unclear which role takes efect under what situation. We investigated the interaction efects between expertise and these main factors, and find opposite directions for the efects with expert and novice customers, which provide us with a comprehensive view to understand the mechanism of customer satisfaction to paid knowledge.

For the methodological implication, our measurement overcomes the subjective bias and the problem of limited scale of participants in empirical studies with self-reports. Besides, as the traditional measurement is usually targeted at certain product/category, it is impossible to measure the customer expertise towards knowledge of various fields simultaneously. Given the diversity of customers and knowledge purchased, the proposed measurement could measure the customer expertise separately with the behavioral similarity of knowledge customers and knowledge providers, which is specific to a certain knowledge domain.

## 7.2. Practical implications

Knowledge sharing platforms become to gain much more profit by developing derivative paid knowledge service. For sustainable development, both knowledge providers and platforms should guarantee customers' satisfaction to the paid knowledge, which requires selling the right knowledge to the right customers at the right price. Emphasizing the customer expertise as a new segmentation criterion, our research distinguishes diferent reaction to price and historical experience between expert customers and novice customers. Such difference would lead to opposite marketing decision to traditional strategies.

Firstly, traditional pricing strategies tend to reduce price to attract more customers, since they enjoy the free knowledge and are unwilling to pay high price. Given the same discount, the absolute reductions are larger for expensive Lives. However, do customers of expensive Lives really need such reductions? For most customers seeking advanced knowledge in expensive Lives, they are probably quite expert in that domain. According to our findings, they are actually less sensitive to price. Therefore, the large reduction in expensive Live is indeed unnecessary for expert customers, which provides a chance for knowledge providers and platforms to expand their profits.

Secondly, traditional treatment for customer with strong purchasing power could benefit from our research findings. Supposing a customer with high historical price, traditionally s/he may be recommended with Lives of his/her expert domain. According to our findings, the historical price would largely improve his/her satisfaction for Lives of other do mains, compared to his/her expert domain. Therefore, Lives of diversified domains are better choices to take full advantages of his/her purchasing power. Besides, expert customers who have adapted to both high price and high quality should be provided with qualified Live, while kept away from low-quality Lives with low prices.

Thirdly, solution for consumer dissatisfaction may be re-considered. Suppose a critical customer who always gives worst ratings. Traditionally, platforms may doubt that wrong domains are identified for the customer and continue trying more diversified domains (even the inexpert domains of the customer). However, such diversification would amplify the negative impact of low historical satisfaction. Instead of widely trying, platforms should return to the free knowledge to find the expert domain of the customer, and stick to his/her expert domains.

Fifthly, our study also sheds lights on the recommendation strategies. Currently, platforms may recommend Lives to customers according to their interest instead of expertise. However, our proposed measurement of customer expertise provides new insights that plat forms can monitor customer expertise on free platforms, and then make the appropriate recommendations. For example, for customers with low expertise in movie, they should be firstly recommended for cheap Lives to ensure their satisfaction. Then, platforms can enrich their knowledge by recommending movie-related answers on free Q&A platforms or inviting them to join the discussion on movie-related topics. After they have accumulated enough knowledge, more sophisticated and expensive movie-related Lives could be recommended.

Finally, the proposed measurement of expertise also mitigates the “cold start” problem in knowledge payment platforms. Generally, knowledge payment platforms evolve from free knowledge sharing platforms by capitalizing the popularity, and users won’t make a purchase until they have fully experienced the free contents. Hence, for a beginner user on paid platform (e.g., Zhihu Live), there are probably some activity records such as reading or commenting on a free platform (e.g., Zhihu). We could learn his/her knowledge distribution from those activities with the doc2vec model. Then an expertise score can be cal culated and could be used as valid information before his/her purchase.

## 8. Conclusion and limitation

This study mainly investigates the moderate role of customer expertise in exploring the influential factors of customer satisfaction of paid knowledge. Specifically, we first propose a novel measurement of customer expertise based on the text-mining method, considering user migration from free to paid knowledge platforms and incorporating user activity information on these two platforms. By analyzing the data collected from Zhihu and Zhihu Live, we find that with higher expertise, the satisfaction of customers is less sensitive to price. Besides, expert customers with high average price of historical transactions are more dificult to satisfy in following transactions, while novice customers with high historical price are easier to satisfy. Meanwhile, the satisfaction of expert customers is less dependent on historical satisfaction.

The current study can be extended in several directions. Firstly, some customers may purchase Lives without leaving reviews and ratings. A possible way to validate our study is to conduct laboratory experiments in similar situation, getting a random sample of customers and accessing their true perception of satisfaction. Secondly, we represented the knowledge of Live with the speaker's knowledge distribution for the measurement of customer expertise. This measurement can be further enhanced by incorporating the content of Live, such as audio, video, pictures and slides. Such multimedia content can be modeled jointly with an advanced deep learning framework, which is worth a separate study. Thirdly, the social network among users can be further incorporated to the model in future work. Finally, building a predictive model of the personalized ratings and generating knowledge recommendations would also be an interesting future direction.
