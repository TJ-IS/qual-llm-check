## a b s t r a c t

This paper examines how information costs and congruence in the objectives of collaborators in production jointly affect the equilibrium number of managers and employees in an economy where individuals with different endowments of skills make occupational choices. We use the ratio of employees per manager, i.e. the average span of control (ASC) in the equilibrium, as a summary of how production is organized in the economy. The theory predicts that generalized trust, favoring congruence in objectives, will imply higher ASC in the equilibrium, while the intensity of use of information and communication technologies that lowers information costs has an undetermined effect on the equilibrium span of control. The empirical test of the hypotheses, with data from the Spanish regions, con<sup>fi</sup>rms that social capital-trust has a positive effect on the average span of control, while the sign of the effect of information–communication capital varies with the kind of information assets: communications, software or hardware.

## 1. Introduction

Social scientists have been aware for many years of the relevance of information and trust in overcoming the limits of organization, increasing production ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and contributing to economic progress [6]. Empirical research <sup>fi</sup>nds that the productivity of individual <sup>fi</sup>rms [15,40] and of the whole economy [37,49,64], is higher in environments of generalized trust. The contribution of information and communication technologies (ICT) to productivity growth, on their own and in combination with organization capital, is also well documented [12,13,20,22,45].<sup>2</sup> Furthermore, advances in ICT offer practically unlimited technological possibilities for the creation and expansion of technology-supported virtual markets, but the economic viability of these ventures strongly depends on the parallel development of trust-building mechanisms [7,9,36, for a survey].

Although research broadly supports Arrow's [6] vision on trust and information as “lubricators” of hierarchies and markets, a close examination of the joint in<sup>fl</sup>uence of trust and information in the structure and functioning of hierarchies, as organizational solutions for the production of goods and services, remains unexplored.<sup>3</sup> This paper examines the endogenous formation of hierarchies in economies where individuals with different endowments of skills, make occupational choices of either working as managers (occupying high hierarchical positions) or as employees (being employed in low-level positions). Studying the size distribution of hierarchies in the context of occupational choice models is relevant because the distribution of sizes is matched with the allocation of skilled individuals in management and operational job positions, with implications for productivity and productivity growth over time.

In Lucas [41] and Rosen's [57] occupational choice models, individuals are endowed with general skills to use in their job position. Managers make decisions on the strategy of the business and coordinate and supervise direct employees for proper execution; employees perform operational tasks. The joint production technology shows scale economies of skills in management positions because higher skills imply better management decisions, affecting the productivity of all employees under the direction of the manager; the supervision function, however, shows decreasing returns. In Garicano [28] and Garicano and Rossi-Hansberg [29] individuals differ in cognitive skills and managers help employees in solving operational problems. The limits to the size of the hierarchy come from the limited time available for coordination and supervision that a manager allocates to employees on a one-to-one basis [57], or from communication costs that limit the consultation of managers by lower-level employees [28]. The two limits are broadly referred to as “organizational diseconomies”.

The market equilibrium from occupational choices is summarized in the variable average span of control (ASC), calculated as the ratio between the number of individuals that choose to work as employees and the number of managers, in the equilibrium. The ASC depends on the parameters of the model capturing information costs, degree of goal congruence, intensity of product market competition and dispersion in the distribution of skills. The main focus of our paper is on how the use of ICT and the level of generalized trust interact in determining information costs and the goal congruence of collaborating agents and, <sup>fi</sup>nally, in determining the equilibrium ASC. The analysis is completed with other theoretical determinants of the size of hierarchies, such as competition and the distribution of skills in the population. The theory predicts greater ASC in environments of greater trust, more intense product market competition, and more dispersion in the distribution of skills. However, the predictions from intensity in use of ICT on ASC are more ambiguous, as they depend on whether the ICT asset lowers communication costs (i.e. mobile phones) or lowers the information acquisition costs, as for example the ERP [29].

The hypotheses on determinants of ASC are tested with data from 18 Spanish regions (Autonomous communities AACC) during the period 1984–2005. The main explanatory variables are: i) Social capital index for each AACC and year [45], as a measure of the level of trust; ii) the stock of ICT assets, also for each AACC and year (IVIE-BBVA Foundation data base), separated into three classes, software, communication, and hardware; and iii) certain proxies of market competition and distribution of skills variables. We <sup>fi</sup>nd a positive effect of social capital on ASC, as expected if trust contributes to goal congruence. The intensity of each ICT capital affects the ASC with a different sign: negative for the software capital (which is consistent with the prediction from the theory if software assets lower information acquisition costs), and positive for the communication capital (lower communication costs). The empirical analysis also <sup>fi</sup>nds cross-product effects between social capital and ICT assets on the ASC, pointing to the existence of complementarities between trust and information related variables.

Our paper contributes to the literature on the determinants of the internal organization of <sup>fi</sup>rms and its implications for productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, in several ways. First, we offer a comprehensive explanation of how variables such as economy-wide social capital and the stock of ICT assets affect the internal organization of <sup>fi</sup>rms, the size distribution of management hierarchies, and the total output produced in the economy. Research has documented the general trend towards more delegation of decision power to lower levels of the hierarchy [13,47], and towards the <sup>fl</sup>attening of hierarchies [50,55] in modern <sup>fi</sup>rms. But the net effect from these changes in the trade-off between centralization and decentralization and in the size of the <sup>fi</sup>rms, is more ambiguous than might be expected [63] so empirical research should help to disentangle such ambiguity. Moreover, existing research explains the observed heterogeneity in management practices and organization of work across <sup>fi</sup>rms and countries [14], but does not explain the differences in equilibrium solutions for an economy as a whole, as we do in this paper.

Second, this paper focuses on differences in the organization of production with all variables simultaneously: trust, ICT assets, competition, and distribution of skills. Empirical research with <sup>fi</sup>rm-level data has examined how trust [15,36] and ICT assets [16] affect organization decisions, as well as the size and the productivity of <sup>fi</sup>rms. But these analyses are carried out with one set of variables at a time, while we consider the whole set of variables and their interactions (complementarities). Third, modeling ASC, as representative of the organization solution for the production of goods and services in the economy, is new in the literature.

There have been papers interested in explaining differences in the size of <sup>fi</sup>rms [21,38,39,42], but occupational choice models explain ASC, not the size of the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

In the rest of the paper, Section 1 presents the theoretical models of hierarchies and market equilibrium solutions from occupational choices, and the formulation of the hypothesis. Section 2 contains a description of the data sources and the variables used in the analysis, together with the descriptive statistics. Section 3 shows the results from the estimation of the econometric models. The <sup>fi</sup>nal section contains a discussion of our results and conclusions.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

A hierarchy is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a collection of nodes and connecting lines ordered as an inverted tree with one or more levels. The study of hierarchies in the social sciences reveals at least three kinds: information, authority, and skills hierarchies.

In information-hierarchies the nodes are individuals/job positions and the connecting lines are communication channels. There is full congruence of objectives (the group is a “team” organization in the language of Marschak and Radner [44]) and the information <sup>fl</sup>ows through the communication channels responding to coordination needs. The organization design sets the number of hierarchical levels and the span of control in each of them, jointly with the decision on the amount of centralization (sending information to one point to make more informed decisions) or decentralization (making decisions at each point based on the limited available information) in decisions. The trade-off between the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of more informed decisions and information costs are the main determinants of these design decisions.<sup>4</sup>

The authority-hierarchy responds to motivation needs when collaboration takes place under null or limited goal congruence, with or without additional coordination needs. The “power” of the higher levels of the hierarchy over the lower levels is in the form of: giving orders on what to do [26,62]; supervising the quality and quantity of effort contributions [3]; setting up incentives [51,54]; and holding residual decision rights under incomplete contracts [2]. Information costs continue to play an important role in design decisions within authority-hierarchies, for example in the measurement of variables that will be part of the complete contract between managers and employees.<sup>5</sup> But other costs are possible, for example those resulting from decisions on the allocation of ownership rights when contracts are incomplete.

The skills-hierarchy is based on the division of labor in production among individuals with different skills. There are two kinds of job positions, management and operational jobs. Managers decide what to produce, where to sell, how to organize production and so on. The quality of these decisions will affect the productivity of the majority of individuals in the organization. Managers also are concerned with the diligent implementation of these decisions by employees performing operational tasks. Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency considerations justify placing more skilled individuals in management positions, while the less skilled will perform operational tasks. Scale economies in skills would justify concentrating most of the operational jobs and staff under the direction of the most skilled individuals, but there are trade-offs to be considered; for example, organizational diseconomies of size from supervision and coordination activities by managers. Models differ in the kind of skills considered, general skills that can be applied either to one function or the other [41,57], or cognitive problem-solving skills [28].

In this section, we solve for the equilibrium ASC and its determinants in economies where individuals differ in their endowment of skills and make occupational choices as to whether to become managers or emplovees. The theoretical results will be the basis for the hypotheses to be tested in the empirical section. We present a detailed closed-form solution of the organization problem because prior research has not highlighted the role of organizational variables in the equilibrium, and because we extend the model to the case where producer organizations have market power. We close this theory section with a brief comparison of predictions on average ASC from transaction costs economics and predictions from occupational choice models.

2.1. Market equilibrium in an economy with occupational choices and skills-hierarchy

## 2.1.1. The joint production of managers and employees

The total number of individuals in the economy is normalized to one and they are distributed in the range of general skills $[ e _ { m } , e _ { M } ]$ according to the cumulative density function H(e). An individual with a given level of skill will decide to perform operational functions as an employee, or become an entrepreneur–manager and perform decision-making and supervision tasks. Under constant returns to scale and the optimal allocation of the entrepreneur's one unit of working time in the supervision of each employee, Rosen [57] shows that the output of joint production Q, from the combination of employees who supply an amount of operational skills A, and a manager of skills $e ,$ will be equal to:

$$
Q = g (e) A f (e / A)\tag{1}
$$

Where $f ( )$ is increasing and concave in $e / A .$ The joint production of managers and employees involves three activities, direction, supervision, and operations, tied together by the production function. The direction function involves discrete choices on the business strategy with decisions such as what goods to produce, in what varieties and volume, and how to produce them; then such decisions are implemented under the manager's supervision at the operational level. Indivisibilities inherent in the direction function are represented analytically as a total factor productivity improvement $g ( e )$ . Better strategic decisions will have a positive impact on the productivity of the whole organization (the decision as to what good to produce is largely independent of scale, applying equally well to a very large production unit as to a very small one), i.e. the production function exhibits scale economies of ability. Supervision, on the other hand, must be performed on a worker per worker basis. The “concavity” of the function f( ) is an indicator of the intensity of supervision time in the joint output from the collaboration between the entrepreneur and the employees.

## 2.1.2. Equilibrium average span of control

Let $G ( Q ) { = } G ( g ( e ) A f ( e / A ) )$ be the revenue function, increasing with output Q from selling the product to the market. If the market price per unit of skill is w, an individual with skills e working as a manager will earn a pro<sup>fi</sup>t

$$
\Pi (A ^ {*} (w, e), e) = M a x _ {A} G (g (e) A f (e / A)) - w A\tag{2}
$$

Where $A ^ { * } ( w , e )$ is the pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing level of skills. From the properties of the functions $g ( e ) , f ( e / A )$ and $G ( \mathbf { \theta } )$ , the pro<sup>fi</sup>t function will be increasing and convex in skills e. The same individual with skill e can earn a salary $W =$ we as a salaried employee, and there will be a level of skills $e ^ { * }$ for which pro<sup>fi</sup>t and salary will coincide, so the individual will be indifferent between working as an entrepreneur and working as a salaried employee:

$$
\Pi \left(A ^ {*} (w, e ^ {*}), e ^ {*}\right) = w e ^ {*}\tag{3}
$$

Let $e ^ { * } ( w )$ be the solution to Eq. (3). From the properties of the pro<sup>fi</sup>t function, those who have skills above $( \mathsf { b e l o w } ) e ^ { * } ( w )$ will prefer to work as managers (employees) because they earn higher income. The market equilibrium requires that, in addition to Eq. (3), supply of skills be equal to demand of skills. The supply equal to demand condition for skills is given by:

$$
\int_ {e ^ {*}} ^ {e _ {M}} A ^ {*} (w (e ^ {*}), e) d H (e) = \int_ {e _ {m}} ^ {e ^ {*}} e d H (e)\tag{4}
$$

Eqs. (3) and (4) solve for the level of skills $e ^ { * }$ and price of skills $w ^ { * }$ in the equilibrium solution. The equilibrium average span of control (ASC) will be equal to the ratio of all employees divided by the number of managers<sup>6</sup>:

$$

$$

We solve for the close solution when $g ( e ) = e$ and $f ( e / A ) = ( e / A ) ^ { \beta }$ so that $Q = e A ( e / A ) ^ { \beta } = e ^ { 1 + \beta } A ^ { 1 - \beta }$ (Eq. (1)) and skills are uniformly distributed in the range $[ e _ { m } , e _ { M } ] .$ The parameter β captures the degree of organizational diseconomies. A value of $\beta = 0$ implies that the contribution to output from a marginal increase in management skill e is equal to A, the total number of operating skills used in production. If $\beta = 1$ then the marginal contribution to output from a marginal increase in e is equal to 2e, the same as would result if the manager worked as self-employed without employees, and the scale economies of skills would be lost.

Output is sold in a market with a price $p ( Q ) = Q ^ { \frac { - 1 } { \eta } }$ where $\eta > 1$ is the price elasticity of demand. Therefore, the revenues function is $G ( Q ) { \overset { - } { = } } p ( Q ) Q = { \overset { - } { Q } } { \overset { - } { \eta } }$ increasing with $Q$ when $\eta > 1$ . Straightforward calculations give the following result for the level of skills at which supply is equal to demand:

$$
\frac {e ^ {*}}{e _ {M}} = \left(\frac {2 (1 - \beta) \mu}{1 + 2 \mu}\right) ^ {\frac {1 - (1 - \beta) \mu}{1 + 2 \beta \mu}}\tag{6}
$$

Where $\mu { = } ( \eta { - } 1 ) / \eta$ . Individuals with relative skills $e / e _ { M }$ higher than $e ^ { * } / e _ { M }$ will choose to work as managers and those with relative skills $e / e _ { M }$ lower than $e ^ { * } / e _ { M }$ will be employees. The equilibrium solution also implies that the span of control and the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts of managers will increase more than proportionally with skills, so the model explains the asymmetric distribution of size of <sup>fi</sup>rms and salaries, and the positive correlation between the two variables, observed in many economies [53].

The average span of control (ASC) in the equilibrium is the ratio between the number of employees and the number of managers. That is, from Eq. (5),

$$
A S C = \frac {e ^ {*} - e _ {m}}{e _ {M} - e ^ {*}} = \frac {\left. ^ {e ^ {*}} \right| _ {e _ {M}} - \left. ^ {e _ {m}} \right| _ {e _ {M}}}{1 - \left. ^ {e ^ {*}} \right| _ {e _ {M}}}\tag{7}
$$

Where $e ^ { * } / e _ { M }$ is given by Eq. (6).

Result-1. The equilibrium average span of control (ASC) of the economy decreases with the organizational diseconomies parameter $\beta$ and with the compression of skills $e _ { m } / e _ { M }$ , and it increases with the price elasticity of demand $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { \acute { n } } }$

This result follows immediately from Eq. (7) taking into account Eq. (6) and $\mu { = } ( \eta { - } 1 ) / \eta$

As the parameter β increases from zero, the organizational diseconomies from the supervision and coordination activities performed by the manager on a worker by worker basis are more pronounced. Consequently, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from having more operational skills and employees under the direction of a more skilled manager will decrease. In the equilibrium, there will be more managers and the ratio of employees per manager will decrease.

More price-elastic demand for the output implies lower pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins, so the minimum level of skills beyond which an individual will choose to occupy a management job (because she or he earns a pro<sup>fi</sup>t at least equal to the salary as employee) increases. Under more price-competition, the number of managers in the equilibrium is reduced and the average size of <sup>fi</sup>rms increases as well as total output produced. Conversely, lower price elasticity of demand in absolute terms, i.e. less product market competition, implies more <sup>fi</sup>rms of smaller size and lower total output produced in the equilibrium solution.

The term $e _ { m } / e _ { M }$ in Eq. (7) is the ratio between the lower and the upper bound of skills in the population. Higher values of this ratio mean that the relative difference between high and low levels of skill is small, in other words the distribution of skills is compressed. On the other hand, lower values of the ratio imply a greater gap between high and low levels of skill. According to the model, more skills compression (dispersion) reduces (increases) the ASC in the equilibrium.

## 2.2. Empirical hypothesis on determinants of average span of control

Result-1 provides empirically testable predictions in terms of parameters of the model that are not directly observable. However, organizational diseconomies are the result of a complex mixture of trust and information cost-related variables, so the hypotheses suitable for empirical analysis require further elaboration.

## 2.2.1. Trust and average span of control

In Rosen's [57] model, organizational diseconomies are the consequence of the allocation of the manager's time to the supervision of the operational employees. Higher values of the parameter β imply relatively more time dedicated to each employee and, for a limited total time available, a lower number of employees per manager. The value of β will itself be endogenous as managers will decide on the internal organization of the joint production, balancing the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of more delegation of decision power to lower level employees. Any factor that enhances the relative bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of more delegation and less direct supervision will lower the value of the parameter β and from Result-1 it will increase the equilibrium ASC. We <sup>fi</sup>rst focus on the level of generalized trust in the economy, where production and exchange takes place, as a determinant of delegation of decision power and lower supervision intensity.

Research provides several explanations for why more delegation of decision power is expected in atmospheres of higher trust. One of these is the argument that trust is associated with greater congruence of preferences in principal–agent relationships [2,25]; when the interests of the manager and employees are aligned with the collective interest of maximizing total wealth created, the organization becomes a team [44]. In this situation there are no con<sup>fl</sup>icts of interest and therefore motivation problems disappear, so the only organizational problem remaining is coordination.

Another explanation has to do with the argument of reputationbased trust in repeated interactions [11,15]. Individuals will honor trust when the short-term gains from abuse of that trust are lower than the present value of future gains – assuming the trustworthy reputation is maintained. In repeated interactions, the prisoners' dilemma type of inef<sup>fi</sup>cient non-cooperative behavior in self-managed groups (that Alchian and Demsetz [3] use to justify the shift towards hierarchical forms of organization) can turn into cooperative behavior. At the macro economic level, the generalized trust that supports cooperative behavior in prisoners' dilemma type situations is viewed as a collective asset called social capital. Higher levels of social capital make individual behavior more sensitive to collective interests [28,52] and for this reason implicit contracts are more widely used.

On the empirical side, Bloom, Sadum and van Reenen [15], from cross section <sup>fi</sup>rm level data, that top management delegation of decision power is more frequent in <sup>fi</sup>rms operating in regions with greater levels of generalized trust. La Porta et al. [36] <sup>fi</sup>nd a positive association between the level of generalized trust and the importance of large <sup>fi</sup>rms in the economy.

We then formulate the following hypothesis on trust as determinant of ASC:

Hypothesis 1. The average span of control (ASC) of an economy increases with the level of generalized trust.

## 2.2.2. ICT and average span of control

Technological developments that affect the costs of producing, communicating and processing information will affect the organization of production activities, since markets and hierarchies are viewed as organizational responses to information overloads. The skills-hierarchy of Lucas [41] and Rosen [57] incorporates features of the information-hierarchy for solving coordination problems, and features of the authority-hierarchy for solving motivation problems. And technology-mediated information costs are key determinants of the size of the hierarchy from cognitive skills [28].

However, the theoretical predictions on how ICT affects the average size of the hierarchy are ambiguous. One reason for this ambiguity is that ICT, which facilitates communication by lowering the costs of sending and processing information, affects vertical as well as horizontal communication. Technological developments that reduce the loss of control [23,43,61] in vertical communication will justify adding hierarchical levels for the same level of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in coordination, increasing the size of hierarchies. But, if advances in ICT, especially of the networking type, facilitate communication among workers and managers at the same hierarchical level, then intermediate level managers may have access to richer data to be analyzed and shared with peer managers [46]. If more horizontal coordination replaces vertical coordination, the hierarchy may have more levels but the span of control at each level is reduced with an undetermined net effect in the overall size of the hierarchy.

Garicano and Rossi-Hansberg [29] solve for the market equilibrium from occupational choices when individuals who differ in cognitive skills collaborate in operational and problem-solving activities. Employees perform operational functions but can also spend time in problem-solving activities. When problems are too complex for their cognitive skills, operating employees can consult more skilled managers for help. Individuals make occupational choices in management or operational job positions in similar terms as in the model above. The ASC of the knowledge hierarchy in the equilibrium solution depends on two parameters that vary with the ICT technology, the communication cost and the information acquisition costs.

Advances in ICT that lower communication costs (for example the Internet and mobile phones) will imply more consultation of managers helping operational employees in problem solving, i.e. there will be more centralization of decisions. The equilibrium hierarchy will have more levels and the span of control in each level will also increase. Then, Garicano and Rossi-Hansberg [29] predict that lower communication costs increase the average size of the hierarchies in equilibrium. On the other hand, advances in technologies that facilitate information acquisition (for example of the ERP type) will increase the knowledge of operating employees and reduce consultation. The model predicts that, as a result of lower information acquisition costs and more information available within a hierarchical level, the optimal hierarchy will have higher span of control at each level, but a lower number of hierarchical levels. Therefore, the effect of lower information acquisition costs on the ASC or average size of hierarchies is ambiguous. Bloom et al. [16] con-<sup>fi</sup>rm with <sup>fi</sup>rm level data that communication and information acquisition technologies can have different effects on delegation of decision power and on the size of <sup>fi</sup>rms.

The empirical examination of the predictions from Garicano and Rossi-Hansberg [29] requires having data on variables that are proxy for differences in communication and information acquisition costs across economies. The proxy variables include the intensity of use of three different ICT assets, communication, software, and hardware. We expect that higher intensity in the use of communication capital will imply lower communication costs and, according to the theoretical predictions [29], higher ASC. For software and hardware assets, whether they are dedicated to communication or to information acquisition services and, therefore, whether they lower one kind of information cost or the other is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to anticipate. For this reason, the formulation of the hypothesis on how their intensity in use of software and hardware assets affect the ASC, will contemplate the two possible signs.

## Hypothesis 2

a) Higher intensity of use of communications assets will be associated with higher ASC of the economy.

b.1) Higher intensity of use of software and hardware assets will be associated with smaller ASC (software and hardware assets lower information acquisition costs).

b.2) Higher intensity of use of software and Hardware assets will be associated with larger ASC (software and Hardware assets lower coordination costs).

## 2.2.3. Interactions between trust and ICT

The potential interactions between trust and ICT in determining the structure and functioning of hierarchies remain unexplored at the theoretical level. The theory behind the cognitive skills hierarchy does not consider the other functions of hierarchies, coordination and motivation. If the knowledge hierarchy includes a situation where organization members pursue their own interests which con<sup>fl</sup>ict with those of the organization, then it can be expected that, in order to save on communication costs, there will be more involvement of lower level workers in problem solving (delegation of decision power) in environments of high trust, than in environments of low trust. In other words, the increment in ASC resulting from lower communication costs will be smaller in environments of high trust than in environments of low trust, because there will be less consultation for problem solving in organizations that operate in the former environment than in those operating in the latter.

With technologies that lower information acquisition costs, the effect is expected to be the opposite since the lower costs of delegation in environments of high trust will increase the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from more intense use of information acquisition technologies. With all this in mind, we formulate the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the intensity of use of communication, software, and hardware assets in the ASC, will be lower in absolute terms in environments of higher trust.

2.2.4. Product market competition, skill compression and average span of control

The predictions on how product market competition and compression of skills affect the equilibrium ASC follows directly from Result-1. The term $e ^ { * } / e _ { M }$ in Eq. (7) increases with μ (from Eq. (6)) and μ in turn increases with the absolute value of the price elasticity of demand η. In markets with high price elasticity of demand, <sup>fi</sup>rms have less market power, equilibrium prices are closer to marginal costs, and the equilibrium pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing output is higher than in markets with low price elasticity of demand. More competition and less market power will reduce pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins and will increase the ASC in the equilibrium<sup>8</sup>:

Hypothesis 4. The ASC will be higher in economies with more intense competition in product markets.

According to Eq. (7), the equilibrium ASC depends on the ratio between the lower and upper bounds of the uniform distribution of skills in the population $\boldsymbol { e } _ { m } / e _ { M } .$ When skills concentrate in a small range of values (skill compression), the equilibrium ASC will be smaller than in situations where skills are dispersed through a wider range of values. The explanation of this result has to do with the scale economies of skills in management positions: when the upper bound of skills is relatively high, then these highly-skilled managers will direct the work of proportionally more employees.

Hypothesis 5. The ASC will decrease with skill compression in the working population.

## 2.3. Markets and hierarchies under transaction costs

Occupational choice models consider the contracting between managers and employees for joint production, but they do not consider the contracting among hierarchies (<sup>fi</sup>rms); for example the contracting between a <sup>fi</sup>rm and its input suppliers. Transaction costs economics [72] has examined the specialization between <sup>fi</sup>rms and markets, comparing the costs of contracting among <sup>fi</sup>rms (marketing costs) and the costs of contracting inside the <sup>fi</sup>rm (management costs) [26]. Higher organizational diseconomies, for example, will be associated with higher management costs so, from transaction costs economics, as organization diseconomies increase, all the rest equal one would expect fewer transactions within <sup>fi</sup>rms and more between <sup>fi</sup>rms, reducing the size of <sup>fi</sup>rm-hierarchies. Transaction cost economics may offer an explanation of the average span of control, our dependent variable, different from the explanations from occupational choice models. We close this theory section with a brief comparison of the two theories in the context of our research.

In this paper we focus on information costs and trusts as determinants of the average size of the hierarchies (span of control). Since both markets and hierarchies respond to ef<sup>fi</sup>cient management of information, higher trust and lower information costs will affect <sup>fi</sup>rms and markets solutions in the same direction, i.e. it is expected that they will lower both, management and information costs. From the transaction costs theory, higher trust and lower information costs are likely to improve the overall ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in production and exchange, but their effects on how production will be organized, more marketcontrolled transactions or more hierarchically-controlled transactions, is likely to be neutral. In the occupational choice theory, information and trust affect the size of hierarchies for given transaction costs because they reduce organizational diseconomies and, when this happens, the hierarchy increases in size to take advantage of the scale economies of skills in management positions.

Markets and hierarchies can also be compared in terms of the indicators of performance used to set the terms of the exchange. In general, markets rely more on output-based indicators of performance, while hierarchies rely more on input-based indicators [51]. Under this view, advances in ICT that facilitate output-based performance measures will favor relatively more market-like solutions, while advances that facilitate input measurements will increase the value of hierarchical solutions. Baker and Hubbard [10] document that the development of a special piece of ICT, facilitating the monitoring of truck drivers by the management of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, favored vertical integration of transportation <sup>fi</sup>rms by hiring independent truck drivers as employees. So, from transaction cost economics, it may also happen that the sign of the effect of intensity of use of ICT assets on the size of ASC can vary, depending on the asset type. With the information available, it is impossible to know whether communication, software or hardware assets facilitate output based or input based indicators of performance. Therefore, with the information available it will be impossible to test if the differences in span of control are affected by information technologies that change the comparative advantage of measuring input based or measuring output based indicators of performance.

## 3. Data, descriptive statistics and empirical model

## 3.1. Data sources and variables

We test Hypotheses 1 to 5 with annual data from the 18 Spanish autonomous communities (AACC) in the period 1984–2005.

## 3.1.1. Dependent variable

The Spanish Economically Active Population Survey (Encuesta de Población Activa – EPA), elaborated by the Spanish National Statistics Institute (INE), provides data on the number of individuals occupied in each of the following four occupational groups: Employers (selfemployed individuals hiring employees); Own account self-employed (self-employed individuals who do not hire employees); Managers (salaried employees in top management positions) and Employees (the rest of salaried employees).<sup>9</sup> The dependent variable is Average span of control (ASC) = Employees/(employers+managers). Employers and managers perform similar functions, so we add the two into one variable of management services.<sup>10</sup>

## 3.1.2. Main explanatory variables

The explanatory variable Social capital index (SCI), for each AACC and year, is the index of social capital elaborated by the IVIE (Instituto

Valenciano de Investigaciones Económicas) available at http://www. ivie.es/banco/ksocial.php. A detailed explanation of the foundations and methodology of this SCI appears in Pérez-García et al. [49].<sup>11</sup> The methodology used to obtain the IVIE index of social capital builds from two main references, the theoretical paper of Glaeser, Laibson and Sacerdote [31] and the OECD framework for the measurement of economic capital. Glaeser, Laibson and Sacerdote [31] de<sup>fi</sup>ne an individual's social capital as the set of social characteristics that enable the individual to obtain market and non-market rents from interactions with others. The individual social capital at a given moment of time is determined according to a perpetual inventory model, where the stock in time t is equal to the stock in t−1 net of depreciation, plus the investment <sup>fl</sup>ow in the time interval. The amount invested takes into account the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the decisions, which very much depend on the resulting opportunities for social interaction in production and trade activities, and on the opportunity cost of the time spent building such opportunities. The aggregate stock of social capital of an economy is different than the sum of individuals' endowments of social capital and includes the resulting externalities from group memberships.

Glaeser, Laibson and Sacerdote [31] do not provide empirical estimates of social capital. The important contribution of Perez et al. [49] is in obtaining such estimates with a rigorous selection of proxy variables and using a methodology that follows closely the OECD's perpetual inventory method, valid for any kind of capital. For example, they use bank loans over GDP as the proxy of the degree of connectivity of individual networks; the marginal cost of investment in social capital varies inversely with the percentage of working age population with secondary education; and the unemployment rate of the economy is the proxy variable for the depreciation rate of the social capital stock.<sup>12</sup>

The explanatory variable ICT capital is obtained from the data base Capital Stock of the Spanish Economy, also elaborated by the IVIE-BBVA Foundation (available at http://www.fbbva.es/TLFU/ microsites/stock09/fbbva\_stock08\_i32.html). The calculation of capital stock follows the methodology of the EU-KLEMS project. Capital assets include Housing and Productive assets but this paper considers only the Productive assets. These assets are grouped into ICT and non-ICT productive assets. ICT assets include: Software, communication and hardware (including of<sup>fi</sup>ce machinery). The explanatory variable is the intensity of use of the respective asset, i.e. the stock of software, communication, and hardware assets, for each AACC at the end of the year, divided by the GDP of the corresponding AACC and year. This way we obtain the explanatory variables: ICT capital software/GDP, ICT capital communications/GDP, ICT capital hardware/GDP.

Proxy measures of the demand price-elasticity, representative of all products and services sold in the economy (i.e. proxies of market power) are conditioned by data availability. The competitive conditions in each AACC and year are summarized in three variables: size of the regional economy, sector specialization, and openness. The size of the economy will correlate with larger product markets, more <sup>fi</sup>rms competing in these markets, closer substitutes and higher absolute price elasticity of demand. The size of the interior market is measured by the variable GDP at constant prices for each AACC and year.

The barriers to entry, product differentiation, imports and exports, will differ between sectors, for instance manufacturing and construction. Differences in specialization of each regional economy are captured by the proportions of individuals occupied in Manufacturing and energy, in Construction, in Services, and in Primary (Agriculture Cattle and Fishery) industries. In consequence, we construct four Economic specialization variables: Manufacturing and energy, Construction, Services, and Primary, where each variable is measured by the ratio occupied individuals divided by all occupied individuals. Data for each AACC and year come from the National Accounts of the Spanish Economy (INE). We expect more intense competition in Manufacturing and Service sectors than in the Construction and Primary industries.

Spain is a member of the Euro-zone. The monetary union is likely to increase the openness of the Spanish economy for trade and foreign investment, as well as for <sup>fi</sup>nancial integration. The variable Euro that takes the value of 1 for years 1999 to 2005 and zero otherwise, will compare the ASC in the years before and after the creation of the Euro zone.

The proxy variable of skills in this paper is the years of formal education of the working population. The EPA statistics provide information on the highest educational degree of working individuals and from such information it is possible to estimate the average years of formal education of individuals in each occupational group. The Skills' ratio variable, $e _ { m } / e _ { M }$ is approximated by the ratio between average years of formal education of the occupational group with lower average years of formal education (the own account self-employed according to the EPA data), and average years of formal education of the occupational group with higher average (managers), for each AACC and year.<sup>13</sup>

## 3.1.3. Control variables

These variables account for sources of cross section and over time variations in the ASC that may be correlated with the main explanatory variables. The variable intensity of productive capital different from ITC, capital not-ICT/GDP, calculated as the ratio of the stock of productive assets different from ITC over the GDP for each AACC and year, is expected to reduce the number of employer–managers in the equilibrium because it has a positive effect on the wages of employees and reduces the incentives to become an employer–manager [41].

Differences in human capital across regions and over time are controlled by the average years of formal education and average age of the individuals in each occupational group: Education and Age. The model predicts no effect of these variables on ASC.

Socio-economic and cultural factors that may condition the willingness to work and the incentives to accept a job, across AACC and over time, are controlled by the variables: Unemployment rate (UR); the proportion who want to work out of the total working age population, Participation rate (PR); and the proportion of public employees over all occupied people, Rate of public employees (RPE). The data again come from EPA.

The macro-economic conditions of the Spanish economy, common to all regions but changing over time, are accounted for by two explanatory variables:, the percentage year-change in real GDP for the whole Spanish economy, GDP growth; and the real interest rate calculated as the difference between the year average interbank interest rate and the rate of change in the consumer price index (in<sup>fl</sup>ation rate), RIBIR.

## 4.2. Other estimations and robustness

In the exposition above, individuals working as salaried managers, and self-employed with employees (employers), are viewed as homogeneous management inputs in the production process. However, Table 4 reports more years of formal education, on average, for the group of managers than for the group of employers, so managers are likely to be endowed with more skills than employers, implying that the same total number of managers and employers can result in a different number of managerial skills, depending on the proportion of managers in each total. If years of formal education are correlated with skills, then in economies with a higher proportion of managers relative to employers, the upper tail of the distribution of skills will concentrate a higher proportion of employees than in economies with more employers and fewer employees. Therefore, the proportion of managers relative to employers is expected to be positively correlated with ASC.

We estimate model (8) again, this time with the additional explanatory variable ratio Managers/employers (values of the variable can be obtained from Table 1). The results of the estimation are shown in the <sup>fi</sup>rst column of Table 6. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the variable Managers/employers is positive and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Therefore, salaried-managers and entrepreneur–owners are not perfect substitutes as suppliers of managerial inputs. Relatively more professional managers and fewer employers imply more skilled individuals in the upper tail of the distribution of skills, more employees working in larger hierarchies (probably multilevel ones), and higher ASC in the equilibrium.

The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of Social capital and of the ICT capital variables remain practically unchanged, compared with their values in the <sup>fi</sup>rst column of Table 5. However, the inclusion of the new variable in the model lowers the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the variables economic specialization and non-ICT capital intensity, indicating that the explanatory variable ratio of managers over employers moderates the effects of the non-ICT capital intensity and specialization of the economy variables in the ASC. In other words, the ratio of professional managers over employers in the economy is higher in the manufacturing and energy industries than in the other sectors, and in industries with higher capital intensive production technologies (probably industries with more complex operational tasks).

Employers, managers and employees organize their work around hierarchies, while the other occupational group, the own account selfemployed, work outside hierarchical organizations. It can be reasonably expected that the conditions that favor hierarchical organization of production will reduce the economic activities outside the hierarchies. This conjecture can be formulated by saying that economic, organizational and technical factors that contribute to (go against) the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of hierarchical organization of production, will at the same time contribute to a contraction (expansion) in the relative number of own account self-employed in the economy.

To test the conjecture, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the variable rate of own account self-employed, OASER = Own account self-employed/(employers+own account self-employed+managers+employees) and explain the values of the new variable for each AACC and year as a function of the same explanatory variables used in the model of determinants of ASC (the explanatory variables in the <sup>fi</sup>rst column of Table 5). The estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the functional relationship are presented in the second column of Table 6. The comparison of the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cients in Table 6 with those shown in Table 5 con<sup>fi</sup>rms that, in most of the cases, when a given explanatory variable has a positive association with the ASC variable, then the same variable has a negative or non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant association with the rate of own account self-employed.

## 4.2.1. Robustness

Eq. (8) was estimated with different sets of control variables. First, we limit the control variables to dummy variables for each AACC and time periods. The estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the Social capital, software, and hardware capital intensity variables keep their sign and statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, while the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the Communication capital intensity variable and of the variables used as proxies of market competition keep their sign but reduce their statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. Second, the non-ICT capital intensity variable is removed from the list of explanatory variables, considering that ICT and non-ICT productive assets are correlated. The basic results remain unchanged except the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the intensity of communication ICT capital, which increases in value and statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. Third, we substitute the three ICT intensity capital variables as explanatory of ASC, by a single variable constructed as the sum of all ICT capital divided by the GDP; the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for the intensity of total ICT capital is not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

## 5. Conclusion

Economic progress depends very much on ef<sup>fi</sup>cient specialization, collaboration, and exchange of skilled persons. Information costs and incongruence of objectives of collaborating parties, will limit the ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency of hierarchies of managers and employees as a solution to the organizational problems in joint production. Trust and ICT capital, as they contribute to higher goal congruence and lower information costs, will expand the limits of hierarchical organizations and increase productivity [6,59]. This paper models the role of trust and information in hierarchies where management positions are occupied by individuals of higher skills, while the less skilled occupy operational jobs. We solve for the average span of control (ASC) of the economy in the market equilibrium, and formulate several hypotheses that are tested with Spanish data. These hypotheses refer to economy-wide conditions that determine the relative advantage of hierarchical organization for ef<sup>fi</sup>cient production, and include, in addition to the generalized level trust and the intensity of ICT capital, the degree of productmarket competition and the dispersion in the distribution of skills among the population.

The empirical analysis supports the predictions from the model that higher trust, more product-market competition, and more skill dispersion create a favorable environment for the expansion of hierarchies and higher ASC in the market equilibrium. The relationship between ICT assets and the scope and depth of hierarchies is more complex, as advanced by the theory of hierarchies with individuals who have different cognitive skills [29], and supported by qualitative empirical evidence [63]. We <sup>fi</sup>nd supporting evidence that the contribution of ICT capital to the comparative advantage of hierarchies is different, depending on the kind of asset considered, positive in the case of communication ICT capital, and negative in the case of software ICT capital. The empirical evidence also supports the general hypothesis that factors contributing to a comparative advantage of hierarchical organizations will imply less economic activity conducted outside hierarchies, in our case lower relative numbers of own account self-employment in the economy.

These results have implications for the determinants of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient resource allocation of an economy, and for growth in productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency over time. Social contexts with higher generalized levels of trust will take more advantage of the scale economies of ability by concentrating larger amounts of resources, including employees, under the direction of more able entrepreneurs as managers. These bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from scale economies of ability are in addition to the social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of higher generalized trust itself, due to the lower transaction of relational contracts compared with those of explicit contracts. Social contexts with higher social capital that facilitate ef<sup>fi</sup>cient decentralization of decisions and lower organizational diseconomies (in what Coase [26] referred to as “management costs”) will contribute to higher ASC of those who choose to be entrepreneurs in the economy, and will increase the gains from specialization between entrepreneurs and salaried employees.

The importance of the internal organization of <sup>fi</sup>rms as a comparative advantage may not be the same across industries and over time.

Research shows that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of decentralization of decisionmaking increase with the use of information technology, and that decentralization and ICT together imply high productivity gains [15,20,22]. Therefore, economies with higher social capital will be in a better position to invest and grow in activities that are relatively intensive in ICT-related assets. These <sup>fi</sup>rms will have stronger incentives to make ICT investment more productive, combining the investment with more decentralized organization designs. In countries open to foreign competition, the social context has clear implications for the competitive position of <sup>fi</sup>rms and of the economy as a whole. In closed economies, all <sup>fi</sup>rms face the same social context and therefore this context will not be an advantage/disadvantage for any particular <sup>fi</sup>rm. In economies open to external competition, the <sup>fi</sup>rms in the social–economic context that facilitate the adoption of more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective organizational design will have a comparative advantage, relative to those operating in economies endowed with lower social capital.

The theoretical part of the paper could be extended into a more general model of hierarchical organizations, combining the features of the three types of functions attributed to hierarchies in the literature: coordination, supervision, and problem solving. This extension will provide a richer perspective on the interaction between trust and information in shaping the span of control and the depth of hierarchical organizations. The empirical part of the paper could be extended by testing the predictions with data from other countries, and/or with <sup>fi</sup>rm- level data. So far, empirical analysis with micro data looks at trust and ICT separately, and it would be of interest to integrate the two factors in the same empirical model. Data on vertical integration of <sup>fi</sup>rms and on ITC assets that improve input or output based indicators of performance would also be of interest in comparing predictions from transaction cost economics and from occupational choices, on average span of control. Future theoretical and empirical analysis should also contribute to explaining the joint determination of the degree of professional management, economic specialization, and capital intensity in production technology, observed in the empirical results of our paper.
