# ON THE DIFFERENCES BETWEEN VIEW-BASED AND PURCHASE-BASED RECOMMENDER SYSTEMS<sup>1</sup>

Jing Peng and Chen Liang Department of Operations and Information Management, School of Business, University of Connecticut Storrs, CT, U.S.A. {jing.peng@uconn.edu} {chenliang@uconn.edu}

E-commerce platforms often use collaborative filtering (CF) algorithms to recommend products to consumers. What recommendations consumers receive and how they respond to the recommendations largely depend on the design of CF algorithms. However, the extant empirical research on recommender systems has primarily focused on how the presence of recommendations affects product demand, without considering the underlying algorithm design. Leveraging a field experiment on a major e-commerce platform, we examine the differential impact of two widely used CF designs: view-also-view (VAV) and purchase-also-purchase (PAP). We found several striking differences between the impact of these two designs on individual products. First, VAV is about seven times more effective in generating additional product views than PAP but only about twice as effective in generating sales due to a lower conversion rate. Second, VAV is more effective in increasing views for more expensive products, whereas PAP is more effective in increasing the sales of cheaper products. Third, VAV is less effective in increasing the views but more effective in increasing the sales of products with higher purchase incidence rates (PIRs). Finally, when aggregated over all products with the same levels of price or PIRs, VAV dominates PAP in generating views and the difference is more striking for products with higher prices or lower PIRs. Interestingly, PAP is more effective than VAV in increasing the sales of products with low prices or moderate PIRs, though VAV generates more sales than PAP overall. Our findings suggest that platforms may benefit from employing different CF designs for different types of products.

Keywords: Collaborative filtering, substitute, complement, price, purchase incidence rate, cross-sell, up-sell

## Introduction

The rise of algorithmic recommender systems has reshaped the world of e-commerce. Almost all major e-commerce platforms (e.g., Amazon and Taobao) provide algorithmic recommendations to consumers visiting their websites or mobile apps. Recommender systems play a crucial role in helping consumers discover products to purchase. It is estimated that 30%-35% of sales on Amazon come from algorithmic recommendations (Kumar & Hosanagar, 2019; Smith & Linden, 2017).

E-commerce platforms typically use collaborative filtering (CF) to identify products that are relevant to consumers, which was pioneered by Amazon in the mid-1990s (Linden et al.,

2003). CF computes the relevance of two products based on how consumers on the platform interact with these two products rather than the attributes of products (e.g., price, category, and color). Two common types of interaction data used to calculate product relevance are consumers’ view and purchase activities. CF algorithms trained with the view data are often referred to as “people (customers) who viewed this item also viewed” (view-also-view, VAV), while those trained with the purchase data are often framed as “people who purchased this item also purchased” (purchase-alsopurchase, PAP) or “customers who bought this item also bought.”<sup>2</sup> Owing to the difference in the types of data used to compute product relevance, the products recommended by VAV and PAP are radically different. Co-view usually occurs among substitutes, whereas co-purchase mostly happens among complements (Lin et al., 2017; Smith & Linden, 2017;

Zhang & Bockstedt, 2020). Accordingly, the products recommended by VAV and PAP tend to be substitutes and complements, respectively.

Despite the prevalence of VAV and PAP in the industry, there is little understanding of when each CF design works the best. Most extant empirical studies on CF recommender systems have focused on how CF affects demand. Their analyses have typically involved only one type of CF design (Hosanagar et al., 2014; Lee & Hosanagar, 2021; Li et al., 2021; Oestreicher-Singer & Sundararajan, 2012), and the potential differences between different CF designs trained with different types of data have been largely ignored. A few studies (Lee & Hosanagar, 2019; Lin et al., 2017) have considered two types of CF designs (i.e., VAV and PAP) but their primary objectives were not to investigate the differential impact of these two designs. Due to a lack of research that dives into the differences between VAV and PAP, the strengths and weaknesses of these two designs and how their effectiveness varies across products are far from clear.

The extent to which VAV and PAP influence the views and sales of individual products likely depends on the characteristics of the products. For example, when a purchase involves high expenditures, consumers have a strong incentive to extensively search and explore substitutes for their current needs (Schmidt & Spreng, 1996), indicating that VAV recommendations for more expensive products are more likely to be viewed. Meanwhile, when deciding whether or not to purchase the complements recommended by PAP, price also plays an important role, as unplanned purchases of complements are price sensitive (Iyer et al., 2020). In addition, consumers exploring products with a low purchase incidence rate (PIR),<sup>3</sup> such as home decor and jewelry, are more likely to be in a deliberative mindset (Büttner et al., 2013; Fujita et al., 2007; Gollwitzer et al., 1990), making them more likely to explore the substitutes recommended by VAV. Conversely, when shopping for products with a high PIR (e.g., household supplies and baby products), consumers tend to be in an implemental mindset characterized by action orientation, goal commitment, and the willingness to make choices (Chandran & Morwitz, 2005; Gollwitzer et al., 1990). Therefore, they are more disposed to purchase complements that are related to their purchase goals. These arguments suggest that the impact of VAV and PAP can vary across products with different levels of price and PIRs.

To shed light on the differences between VAV and PAP, this study aims to answer the following research questions: (1) Is VAV or PAP more effective than the other in increasing product views or sales? (2) How do the effects of VAV and PAP on views and sales vary across products with different levels of price? (3) How do the effects of VAV and PAP on views and sales vary across products with different levels of PIRs?

We answer the above research questions using archival data from a field experiment on a large e-commerce platform offering a wide array of products from many different categories. The field experiment used a between-subject design and was randomized at the user (customer) level. Users were randomly assigned into one of the following three groups when they arrived at the website: (1) the control group, in which users were not offered any product recommendations; (2) the VAV group, in which users received VAV recommendations when browsing a product; and (3) the PAP group, in which users received PAP recommendations when browsing a product. Based on the view and purchase activities of users in the three groups, we investigate how VAV and PAP affect the views and sales of individual products and how their effects vary across products with different levels of price and PIRs.

We report several intriguing differences between the impact of VAV and PAP on individual products. First, VAV is about seven times more effective than PAP in generating additional product views and twice as effective in generating additional product sales. Second, the positive effect of VAV on product views is stronger for expensive products, whereas the positive effect of PAP on product sales is stronger for cheap products. Third, VAV is less effective in increasing views but more effective in increasing the sales of products with higher PIRs, and PAP is more effective in increasing the views of products with higher PIRs. Finally, when aggregated over all products with the same levels of price or PIRs, we find that VAV continues to dominate PAP in generating additional views and the difference is more striking for products with higher price or lower PIRs. Interestingly, PAP generates more sales than VAV for products with low prices or moderate PIRs, despite the fact that VAV leads to more sales than PAP overall. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of VAV and PAP is both product dependent and performance-metric dependent.

Our study makes several important contributions to the literature. Although it is well-known that CF can shift demand across products, the important role of CF design has been overlooked. We fill this research gap by examining the differences between two of the most widely used CF designs (i.e., VAV and PAP). We find that VAV is more effective in generating views than PAP but less effective in converting them into purchases. In addition, we further reveal the strengths and weaknesses of VAV and PAP by characterizing the conditions under which they are more effective. We find that the relative strength of VAV and PAP varies considerably across products with different levels of price and PIRs, suggesting that platforms may benefit from adopting different CF designs for different types of products. Finally, our work deepens the understanding of recommender systems as a potential marketing tool for cross-selling and upselling. We find that VAV is effective in creating leads for upselling but the conversion rate is low. On the other hand, PAP is particularly effective in cross-selling cheap products. These findings suggest that different CF designs may be used for different marketing purposes.

## Literature Review

In this section, we review related empirical work studying the impact of CF recommender systems, which can be broadly divided into three streams. The first stream investigates how recommender systems influence consumers’ purchase patterns. Hosanagar et al. (2014) found that recommender systems increase the similarity among consumers’ purchases and the increase can be explained by both the volume effect (i.e., consumers purchase more and hence are more likely to have overlaps) and the product-mix effect (i.e., consumers buy a more similar mix of products). Lee and Hosanagar (2019) showed that CF reduces the aggregate sales diversity across a wide range of product categories, owing to the fact that popular products benefit more from CF than niche products do. Li et al. (2021) found that CF increases consumers’ purchase probabilities and basket values. Their further analysis shows that this finding primarily results from the increase in the breadth, rather than depth, of the consideration set.

The second stream examines the economic value of recommendation links. Oestreicher-Singer et al. (2013) argued that the value of a product depends not only on its intrinsic value but also on the value it receives (generates) from incoming (outgoing) recommendation links. They showed that the value of best sellers may be overestimated because, compared to low sellers, they extract more value from incoming links and yet contribute less value via outgoing links. Kumar and Hosanagar (2019) found that the net impact of incoming and outgoing recommendation links on product demand is positive on average, and they further showed that the positive effect is driven by the relevance of recommendations, not by the increased exposure. Further, prior work has explored the association between the characteristics of the product networks formed by recommendation links and product demand (Lin et al., 2017, Oestreicher-Singer & Sundararajan, 2012).

The third stream studies how the impact of recommender systems on product demand varies across products with different characteristics. Lee and Hosanagar (2021) showed that the positive effect of PAP on product views is stronger for utilitarian, experience, and high-rating products, whereas its effect on conversion rate is stronger for hedonic and lowrating products. Their general explanation for such differences is that consumers value different factors at the exploration and purchase stages. Relatedly, Wan et al. (2020) found that recommendations can help consumers discover and purchase lower-priced products and the effect is stronger for product categories with higher price dispersion and larger proportions of niche products.

Despite the growing interest in the economic impact of CF recommender systems, the important role of algorithm design has been largely ignored in the literature because prior studies have typically focused on only one CF design. While a few studies have considered both VAV and PAP (Lee & Hosanagar, 2019; Lin et al., 2017), their research focuses are not on examining the differential causal effects of these two designs on product demand. Our study advances the literature by studying the differences between these two types of most widely used CF designs and how their effects vary across products with different characteristics.

## Hypotheses

Recommender systems create a recommendation network that allows consumers to jump from one product to another product. The recommendation network can affect a product’s traffic from indirect visitors (i.e., consumers who begin their sessions by viewing other products and then view the target product) but not the product’s traffic from direct visitors (i.e., consumers who visit the target product directly). Therefore, our hypotheses focus on how VAV and PAP affect the views and sales of a product from indirect visitors. We refer to the views (sales) from indirect visitors as indirect views (sales). The idea of separating direct and indirect views (sales) is similar to the notion of differentiating the revenue of a product from self-generated visits versus the recommendation network (Oestreicher-Singer et al., 2013).

## VAV Versus PAP

VAV tends to recommend substitutes that serve the same purpose or share similar functionalities with the focal product browsed by a consumer. By recommending substitutes, VAV facilitates consumers’ expansion and exploration of their consideration sets for their needs. In contrast, PAP often recommends complements that are used in the same context or consumed together with the focal product (Smith & Linden, 2017). In doing so, PAP can help consumers visualize the usage context and remind them of other products they may need in that context (Sarantopoulos et al., 2019). To find the products that best match their purchase goals within their budgets, consumers often need to spend sufficient time collecting, exploring, and evaluating the information of multiple products (Li et al., 2020). Since substitutes are generally better aligned with consumers’ original purchase goals than complements, they are more likely to attract consumers’ attention. As a result, consumers are more likely to click on the recommendations generated by VAV than those generated by PAP. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1a: Compared to PAP, VAV is more effective in increasing the indirect views of a product.

The impact of VAV and PAP on the sales of a product may be different from their impact on the views of a product. Given that VAV tends to recommend substitutes, it may only shift consumers’ demand from one product to a competing product without increasing the overall demand. As such, VAV may not increase the sales of an average product. However, by recommending complements, PAP can remind consumers of other needed products they would have otherwise missed, which can increase the sales of an average product. Moreover, the presence of complements can help consumers visualize the consumption context and make more impulse or unplanned purchases on complements (Janiszewski, 1998; Sarantopoulos et al., 2019). Therefore, we propose that:

H1b: Compared to VAV, PAP is more effective in increasing the indirect sales of a product.

## Moderating Effect of Price

Price plays a crucial role in consumers’ decision-making processes. Consumers looking for more expensive products tend to engage in more extensive information search because the cost of making wrong decisions is higher (Schmidt & Spreng, 1996). Therefore, for more expensive products, the advantage of VAV in facilitating consumers’ expansion and exploration of their consideration sets becomes more valuable to consumers. Consequently, they are more likely to click on VAV recommendations when evaluating expensive products, rendering VAV more effective in increasing the views of such products.

Moreover, when skimming through the different substitutes recommended by VAV, consumers are more likely to view expensive substitutes than cheap substitutes. Compared with cheap substitutes, expensive substitutes usually offer more advanced features. Even though expensive substitutes may exceed consumers’ budgets, exploring the advanced features of these substitutes can allow consumers to better understand the pros and cons of the products in their existing consideration set and help them make a more informed decision (Dhar & Sherman, 1996). We thus posit that:

H2a: VAV leads to larger increases in the indirect views of more expensive products.

Price may also influence how consumers respond to the complements recommended by PAP. As explained earlier, PAP can potentially lead to more impulse purchases on the recommended complements. The impact of PAP on impulse purchases is unlikely to be the same for cheap and expensive products, as impulse purchases are rather price sensitive (Iyer et al., 2020). Consumers tend to feel guilty about overspending on impulse purchases of products that are not bought for their current needs (Rook, 1987). To avoid post-purchase regret due to overspending, consumers tend to allocate more cognitive resources and engage in more deliberation when purchase decisions involve high expenditures, rendering impulse purchases less likely for expensive complements. Conversely, since cheap complements are less likely to lead to significant overspending, consumers may just act on their impulses and purchase the recommended complements. Thus, we propose:

H2b: PAP leads to larger increases in the indirect sales of cheaper products.

## Moderating Effect of PIR

The PIR of a product may also moderate the impact of VAV and PAP. Products with a high PIR often are items that need to be bought on a routine basis, while products with a low PIR tend to be items that are purchased discretionally by consumers. When shopping for products with a high PIR, consumers tend to focus on their purchase goals, activating an implemental mindset (Büttner et al., 2013; Gollwitzer et al., 1990). They are likely to make purchase plans (Chandran & Morwitz, 2005) and take action to facilitate the attainment of their purchase goals (Büttner et al., 2013; Wyer, 2018). In contrast, when exploring products with a low PIR, consumers are usually undecided about exactly what to buy and tend to be in a deliberative mindset (Büttner et al., 2013; Fujita et al., 2007; Gollwitzer et al., 1990). As a result, they are more open to new information that can help them weigh the pros and cons of the potential purchase (Fujita et al., 2007). Different mindsets while shopping for products with different PIRs can influence how consumers respond to VAV and PAP recommendations.

Moderating effect of PIR on VAV: When shopping for products with a high PIR, consumers are more likely to activate an implemental mindset and make purchase plans beforehand (Büttner et al., 2013; Xu & Wyer, 2007). In such cases, consumers’ consideration sets are likely to be prespecified and consumers are inclined to directly search for products they plan to buy without considering potential substitutes (Novak et al., 2003). As a result, the substitutes recommended by VAV are more likely to be ignored by consumers. Conversely, when searching for products with a low PIR, consumers tend to activate a deliberative mindset (Büttner et al., 2013; Xu & Wyer, 2007) and are motivated to extensively explore and compare substitutes (Hoffman & Novak, 1996). In the deliberative purchase process, consumers are more open to new information (Fujita et al., 2007) and more likely to be affected by environmental stimuli or product cues (Janiszewski, 1998), such as the substitutes recommended by VAV. These arguments suggest that the VAV recommendations for products with a lower PIR are more likely to be viewed by consumers. Consequently, VAV recommendations may generate more indirect views for products with a lower PIR as a whole. We hypothesize this moderating effect as follows:

H3a: VAV leads to larger increases in the indirect views of products with a lower PIR.

Although we expect VAV to be more effective in generating views for products with a lower PIR, the same need not be true for sales. For products with a low PIR, consumers tend to engage in exploratory information seeking without specific purchase plans (Büttner et al., 2013; Moe, 2003), resulting in a low likelihood of affirmative purchase decisions. Conversely, when visiting products with a high PIR, consumers tend to have specific purchase goals and be committed to purchasing the right products to attain their goals (Xu & Wyer, 2007). As a result, consumers are more likely to purchase the recommended substitutes of products with a high PIR, leading to higher overall indirect sales for products with a high PIR. Thus, we posit that:

H3b: VAV leads to larger increases in the indirect sales of products with a higher PIR.

Moderating effect of PIR on PAP: Given that PAP tends to recommend complements, how the PIR moderates the effects of PAP on the indirect views and sales of products largely depends on how consumers respond to the recommended complements. When consumers are shopping for products with a high PIR, their decision-making processes tend to be more goal-oriented (Büttner et al., 2013; Xu & Wyer, 2007). Consequently, they are more likely to view and purchase complements that can provide additional utility when used together with the focal products they are currently considering (Dhar et al., 2007). On the other hand, when visiting products with a low PIR, consumers are often in a deliberative mindset focusing on information gathering (Büttner et al., 2013; Moe, 2003). Given that consumers likely have not decided to pursue the purchase goal, it is difficult for them to visualize the specific usage context and appreciate the value of the recommended complements (Sarantopoulos et al., 2019). Accordingly, they are less likely to view or purchase the complements recommended by PAP. Based on these arguments, products with a high PIR, as a whole, are expected to benefit more from PAP, in terms of both views and purchases. Thus, we propose the following two hypotheses:

H3c: PAP leads to larger increases in the indirect views of products with a higher PIR.

H3d: PAP leads to larger increases in the indirect sales of products with a higher PIR.

## Experiment and Data

## Experiment

We tested our hypotheses using archival data from a field experiment on a major e-commerce platform in North America that sells products in a wide variety of categories, such as appliances, books, clothing, electronics, groceries, and office products. In the experiment, over one million users were randomly assigned to three groups: control (77%), VAV (11%), and PAP (12%). In the control group, users did not receive any recommendations when visiting a product on the platform. In the VAV (PAP) group, users received up to six recommendations generated by the VAV (PAP) algorithm when browsing a product page. A third-party analytics company specializing in A/B testing operationalized this experiment.<sup>4</sup> The experiment lasted for two weeks in August 2013 and the treatment assignment for the same user remained unchanged over time.

Users’ clickstream and purchase data in the experimental period were tracked and the clickstream data were sessionized by the third-party company. Table 1 summarizes the numbers of users, sessions, products, view activities, and purchase activities observed in each group. As 14% of users had more than one session, the number of sessions was larger than the number of users. To avoid the carryover effect, we focused on the first session of each user, though the results remain similar when all sessions of each user are retained.

## Identification Strategy

We analyzed the experimental data at the product-group level. Specifically, we tested our hypotheses by comparing the total indirect views and sales of each product in the three experimental groups (i.e., control, VAV, and PAP). As the numbers of users assigned to the three groups were different, we normalized the views and sales of a product in each group by the group size. Because users were randomly assigned into the three groups, the changes in a product’s normalized views and sales in the VAV (PAP) group relative to the control group can be attributed to the impact of providing VAV (PAP) recommendations.

A potential threat to the validity of our analysis at the productgroup level is that not all products listed on the platform appeared in the experiment and whether a product appeared in the experiment might have been influenced by the treatment. Specifically, due to the concentration bias of CF, the products appearing in the experiment are more likely to be popular products. A tempting solution to eliminate the influence of the treatment would be to focus on the sample of products that appeared in the control group. However, this seemingly plausible solution could lead to another type of selection bias—sampling by outcome. Specifically, the products appearing in the control group were viewed at least once in the control group but not necessarily in the VAV or PAP group.

To avoid both types of sampling bias, our analysis focuses on the set of products viewed by control users in the first five days (the results are similar using other cutoffs, such as the first four or six days, as well) and then compares their views and sales across the three experimental groups in the remaining nine days of the experiment period. In the first five days, 309,584 users in the control group viewed a total of 55,265 products. This sample of products represents what users would normally view on the platform in the absence of recommendations. Because we only considered the first session of each user, the control users in the first five days had no overlap with the control users in the last nine days, ensuring that our product sample was not in favor of any group.

## Product Price and Category

Two pieces of information critical to our hypotheses testing are the price and category of each product. Nevertheless, the data we obtained only include limited information about products, such as the SKU numbers and several tags assigned to each product. The tags describe the products using a vocabulary more flexible than category names, such as “apparel & footwear,” “movies,” “consumables,” and “great value.” There were 3,620 unique tags in total and 68% of our product sample was tagged, with 5\~8 tags each. The price of a product was recorded when it was purchased, resulting in 22% of products with price information.

To obtain further information about the products, we searched the products on the platform website using SKU numbers. The search was successful for 39% of products and we scraped the price and category information of those products. The other products were not found, likely because their SKU numbers were not indexed by the platform or they had already been discontinued by the time we scraped the data in April 2016. For products whose prices were not recorded in purchase data, we used scraped prices when available, resulting in a total of 50% of products with price information. This imputation strategy may result in measurement errors as product prices may change over time. However, this was likely not a major issue for our study, as such price changes would be quite small in most cases (see Appendix C for details) and were independent of the treatment.

On the focal platform, the products were organized into 16 level one categories (e.g., “electronics” and “jewelry & watches”) and 157 level two categories (e.g., “mobile phones” and “earrings”). We focused on level two categories unless otherwise specified. While the category information was only available for 39% of products that could be scraped from the website, the tag information was available for 68% of products. Since tags can be viewed as descriptions of products in a higher dimensional space than categories, we trained a machine learning model to predict the categories with tags. In our fivefold cross-validation, the average prediction accuracy was 93%, which is extremely high for a prediction problem with 157 classes.

We also used the tags to predict whether a product was expensive or not, based on whether the price was above \$100 (Throughout the paper, we use the symbol “\$” to refer to the local currency, rather than U.S. dollars.) We did not use a regression approach as we did not expect tags to be informative enough to predict the exact price. We trained the binary price prediction model using fivefold cross-validation, and the prediction model achieved an accuracy of 92%. After the prediction, category (price) information was available for 77% (76%) of products. Please refer to Appendix D for more details on price and category prediction.

## Variables

Dependent variable: CF can increase the views (sales) of a product both by increasing the number of users viewing (purchasing) a product and by increasing the view frequency (purchase quantity) per user. Since our hypotheses are primarily concerned with how CF influences the number of users viewing/purchasing a product, rather than the view frequency or purchase quantity of users, we counted the multiple views and multiple units of purchase by the same user only once when calculating the views and sales of a product in each experimental group. Given that recommendations were only expected to influence the indirect views and sales of a product, our main dependent variables are the indirect views and sales of a product in each experimental group but we also considered direct views and sales for a sanity check.

Treatment variables: The focal variables in our analysis are two indicator variables for the treatment condition (VAP or PAP) of an experimental group. The first indicator variable VAV takes a value of 1 if an observation is for the VAV group and 0 otherwise. The second indicator variable PAP takes a value of 1 if an observation is for the PAP group and 0 if not. We examined the moderating effects of two productlevel variables on each treatment variable.

Price: We primarily focused on a binary measure of price (i.e., high\_price), which is 1 if a product is priced above \$100. We prefer this binary measure over the continuous price because the latter is available for fewer products. However, our findings are similar using both price measures.

PIR: In the marketing literature, purchase incidence refers to the occurrence of a purchase within a product category (Bucklin & Gupta, 1992; Seetharaman et al., 2005). It focuses on whether a consumer makes a purchase to fulfill his/her need, rather than which brand or specific alternative in the product category is purchased. Thus, we defined the

PIR of a product as the average probability of all consumers visiting the product purchasing a product in the same category. We computed the PIRs of products based on the view and purchase activities of control users in the first five days, which ensured that this measure was not influenced by the treatment. Realizing that the so-calculated PIRs may be unreliable for unpopular products with sparse view and purchase activities, we also calculated PIRs at the category level, as given by the percentage of users visiting a category who ended up purchasing at least one product in the category. We used the category-level measure for a robustness check and found consistent results.

## Results

Since price and PIR are both predetermined variables, their interaction effects with the two randomized treatment variables can be consistently estimated in a linear regression model (Nizalova & Murtazashvili, 2016). Thus, the interaction effects also have causal interpretations.

## Main Effects of VAV and PAP

We considered four dependent variables (i.e., direct views, indirect views, direct sales, and indirect sales) when estimating the main effects of VAV and PAP using the regression model specified in Equation (1). The results are reported in Table 4.

The coefficients of VAV and PAP in Columns 1 and 3 show that neither PAP nor VAV had a significant effect on the direct views or sales of products, which confirms our argument that recommendations are unlikely to influence the direct views or sales of a product. This finding lends empirical support to our decision to focus on indirect views and sales.

The coefficients of VAV and PAP in Column 2 suggest that, on average, both VAV and PAP can increase indirect product views. The effect size of VAV is 7.2 times larger than that of PAP and the difference between them is statistically significant $( \beta _ { 2 } - \beta _ { 1 } = 0 . 2 8 9 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . This finding supports H1a and demonstrates that VAV is much more effective than PAP in increasing indirect product views, likely because the substitutes recommended by VAV are more relevant to consumers’ needs (Zhang & Bockstedt, 2020).

## Moderating Effects of Price and PIR

We estimated the moderating effects of price and PIR by interacting them with the two treatment dummies both separately and jointly, as summarized in Table 5. We found that VAV is more effective in increasing the indirect views of expensive products, while PAP is more effective in increasing the indirect sales of cheap products. These two findings lend support to H2a and H2b, respectively. For expensive products, users are more motivated to conduct an extensive search to avoid poor decisions (Schmidt & Spreng, 1996), rendering them more likely to explore the substitutes recommended by VAV. On the other hand, PAP tends to recommend complements, and users are thus more likely to purchase cheap complements for combinational usage, which explains why PAP is more effective in increasing the sales of cheap products.

Consistent with H3a and H3c, the effectiveness of VAV in increasing indirect views decreased with PIR, whereas the effectiveness of PAP in increasing indirect views increased with PIR. The opposite moderating effects suggest that consumers may explore VAV and PAP recommendations for different reasons. In Columns 4 and 6, the interaction VAV\*PIR is significantly positive, whereas the interaction PAP\*PIR is not statistically significant. These results lend support to H3b, but not H3d. It is noteworthy that VAV was less effective in increasing indirect views but more effective in increasing the indirect sales of products with a higher PIR. The likely reason is that the indirect views generated by VAV are more likely to convert to sales when consumers are shopping for products with a high PIR.

## Aggregate Impact of VAV and PAP

The impact of VAV and PAP on the views and sales of individual products is of great interest to producers and sellers. However, platform operators may be more concerned with the aggregate impact of VAV and PAP on all products of a certain type. The aggregate impact of VAV and PAP depends on the distribution of products on the platform, in addition to their impact on individual products. To investigate the aggregate impact of VAV and PAP on products with different levels of price and PIRs, similar to Oestreicher-Singer et al. (2013), we split the products into five percentile bins in both dimensions. We cut price into five bins, with each bin including 20% of products. Since the product level PIR was dominated by zeros (86% of values are zeros due to sparse purchasing activities), we used category level PIR to split the 157 product categories into five bins, each with roughly the same number of products. Figure 1 (2) shows the number of indirect views, the conversion rate of indirect views, and the number of indirect sales in each of the five price (PIR) bins for each experimental group.<sup>5</sup>

Several intriguing findings emerge from Figure 1. First, VAV is more effective in increasing indirect views than PAP and the advantage is more pronounced for expensive products. A plausible explanation is that consumers are more incentivized to consider the substitutes recommended by VAV for larger expenditures. Second, the indirect views in the VAV group have lower conversion rates than those in the PAP group and the gap is wider for cheaper products. For products under \$6, the conversion rate in the PAP (VAV) group is 29% higher (19% lower) than that in the control group.<sup>6</sup> As a result, it is not surprising that PAP leads to more indirect sales than VAV for these products. This finding suggests that consumers respond positively (negatively) to cheap complements (substitutes) recommended by PAP (VAV). Third, the indirect sales in all three groups exhibit a V-shape relationship with price. Taking the control group as an example, the reason for this is that products between \$11 and \$20 received 1.5\~1.8 times higher indirect views but had 1.8\~3.6 times lower conversion rates than products below \$11, and they had 1.8\~4.0 times higher conversion rates but received 1.9\~4.6 times less indirect views than products over \$20. This finding highlights the challenge for midrange-priced products to extract value from recommendation networks.

Figure 2 on the five PIR bins also reveals a number of interesting findings. First, VAV dominates PAP in increasing indirect views regardless of PIR. The advantage of VAV is larger in the first three bins than in the last two bins, likely because the purchase of products with lower PIRs required more deliberation, leading consumers to consider and explore more substitutes. Second, the indirect views in the PAP (VAV) group generally have higher (lower) conversion rates than those in the control group and the difference is more pronounced for products with higher PIRs. This finding suggests that PAP recommendations, if viewed, are much more likely to lead to conversion than VAV recommendations, especially for products with high

PIRs. Third, VAV leads to more indirect sales than PAP for products with low and high PIRs, whereas PAP outperforms VAV for products with moderate levels of PIRs.

When aggregated over the entire product sample, the number of indirect views in the VAV (PAP) group is 29% (4%) higher than that in the control group. The conversion rates of the indirect views in the control, VAV, and PAP groups are 1.29%, 0.99%, and 1.43%, respectively, indicating that the conversion rate of the VAV (PAP) group is 23% lower (11% higher) than that of the control group. Regarding indirect sales, the VAV (PAP) group is 14% (7%) higher than the control group.

In a nutshell, the aggregate-level analysis not only corroborates our findings at the product level but also provides further evidence that VAV and PAP each have their own strengths and weaknesses. Platforms may benefit from employing different CF designs for products with different levels of price and PIRs.

## Summary and Robustness Checks

Table 7 summarizes our findings at the product level, the user level, and the aggregate level, which provides a comprehensive view of the differential impact of VAV and PAP on products, consumers, and the platform.

We conducted a series of additional analyses for our main analysis at the product level. First, to account for the fact that views and sales are count variables, we reestimated the effects of the two CF designs using a Poisson model with product-level fixed effects. Second, we considered an alternative product sampling strategy by splitting the control group into two random subsets and the results are consistent. Third, we consider alternative measures for price and PIR and the results are highly consistent. Fourth, we showed that our findings are unlikely to be driven by the missing data issue or measurement errors using multiple subsample analyses. Finally, we conducted an analysis in which we allowed the error terms of all products in the same category to be arbitrarily correlated and our results continued to hold. The details for these robustness checks are reported in Appendix F.

## Conclusion

Despite the popularity of VAV and PAP in e-commerce platforms, little is known about when these two types of CF designs are most effective. To provide guidelines on when to use these two designs, we investigated how their effectiveness varies with product characteristics (i.e., price and PIR) and performance metrics (i.e., views vs. sales).

Our analysis of a large-scale field experiment documents several interesting findings about the differences between VAV and PAP, which have important implications for platforms, producers, and sellers. First, VAV is about seven times more effective than PAP in generating additional views for an average product, but only about twice as effective in generating additional sales, due to a lower conversion rate. E-commerce platforms should determine which design to use based on the problems they are facing. If the bottleneck of sales is a low volume of views, platforms could deploy VAV to facilitate consumers’ exploration of products. If the bottleneck is a low conversion rate, PAP may be a better fit.

Second, price plays an important moderating role in the impact of VAV and PAP on product demand. VAV is more effective in generating additional views for more expensive products, but the conversion rate of these views is low. To realize the full potential of VAV in upselling, platforms could prioritize products with high conversion rates when displaying VAV recommendations. Meanwhile, PAP is more effective in increasing the indirect sales of cheaper products, due to the increase in both views and conversion rate. This finding suggests that PAP can be particularly effective in cross-selling cheap complements such as accessories.

Third, the impacts of VAV and PAP vary across products with different levels of PIRs. The advantage of VAV over PAP for increasing views is more pronounced for products with lower PIRs, whereas the advantage of PAP over VAV for increasing the conversion rate is greater for products with higher PIRs. For increasing aggregate sales, VAV is more effective for products with high and low PIRs, but PAP is more effective for those with moderate PIRs. These nuanced findings suggest that whether VAV or PAP works better depends not only on the PIRs of products but also on the performance metric of interest (views or sales).

Our work has several limitations that point to potential directions for future research. First, our study only considered one ecommerce platform and one widely used open source algorithmic implementation for VAV and PAP. Future research could investigate whether and how the effects of VAV and PAP vary across e-commerce platforms with different product compositions and/or algorithmic implementations. Second, we did not have impression data on which products were recommended to users and hence do not know which views or purchases can be attributed to recommendations. When impression data become available, researchers could provide deeper insights into the mechanisms by investigating how consumers respond to VAV versus PAP recommendations. Third, our argumentation for the hypotheses and explanations for the empirical results is based on the common belief that VAV tends to recommend substitutes and PAP tends to recommend complements (Lin et al., 2017; Zhang & Bockstedt, 2020). Researchers with access to impression data could obtain a more diagnostic understanding of the differences between VAV and PAP by quantifying the extent to which VAV (PAP) recommendations are substitutes (complements). Fourth, this paper focuses on the absolute price of products, whereas the impact of CF designs may also be influenced by the relative price of the recommended products compared to the focal product. Investigating the differential moderating effects of the absolute and relative prices would be a promising future direction. Lastly, since the experiment only lasted for two weeks, the long-term effects of VAV and PAP are unknown. Future research could investigate how their effects evolve over time.

## Appendix D

## Price and Category Prediction

Considering that the categories may be linearly separatable in the higher dimensional tag space (i.e., a certain combination of tags may represent a category), we used an SVM model with a linear kernel to predict product categories. The model for this multiclass prediction problem was trained using a “one-against-one” approach. That is, we train a binary classifier for each possible two-category pair (a total of 157\*156/2 binary classifiers) and then make the final category prediction based on the votes of all trained binary classifiers. We implemented the SVM model using the e1071 package that is available at https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=e1071. We tuned the SVM model using fivefold cross-validation and found that the model performance was not sensitive to hyperparameters. In our cross-validation, the average prediction accuracy was 93%, which is extremely high for a prediction problem with 157 classes (note that the accuracy of random guess i less than 1%). After the prediction, the category information was available for 77% of products.

In a similar vein, we also predicted the price levels of products using the tags assigned to products. While the tags were not informative enough to predict the exact prices, they were useful in predicting the rough price level of a product. Thus, we used the tags to predict whether the price of a product was above \$100. We chose \$100 as the threshold because the platform seemed to have followed the practice of psychological pricing to price most products under a round number. As shown in Appendix C, among the products with price information, only 11% of products had a price higher than \$100. We trained the binary price prediction model using fivefold cross-validation and Table D1 reports the confusion matrix. The confusion matrix is well-behaved with diagonal values dominating the off-diagonal values. Overall, the binary price prediction model achieved an accuracy of 92%.

In addition to predicting the price of a product as low or high, we also considered classifying price into three discrete levels: low (less than or equal to \$10), medium (\$10-100, right inclusive), and high (more than \$100). ## Percentage Change at Aggregate Level

In the main paper, we report how the indirect views, conversion rate, and sales vary by price and PIR for each of the three experimental groups (see Figures 1 and 2). To better understand the economic significance of the impact of providing recommendations, we calculated the percentage changes of the VAV and PAP groups compared to the control group at each level of price and PIR for each of the three metrics.

Figure E1 summarizes the percentage changes at various levels of price. The deployment of VAV increases the indirect views by 21%-46% relative to a world without recommendations (i.e., the control group) and the percentage increase is generally larger for more expensive products. Conversely, the deployment of PAP increases the indirect views by 5%-16% and the percentage increase is larger for cheaper products. Given that consumers were encouraged to view more than they normally would in the VAV and PAP groups, the conversion rates would be expected to decrease. We found that the decrease in conversion rate was much larger in the VAV group than in the PAP group. Notably, for products under \$6, the conversion rate of indirect views in the PAP group is 29% higher than that in the control group. This finding suggests that consumers find the cheap products recommended by PAP to be more relevant than the other products they would normally visit after visiting the first product.

Figure E2 summarizes the percentage changes at various levels of PIRs. VAV dominates PAP in terms of increasing indirect views at all levels of PIRs but substantially underperforms PAP in terms of conversion rate. As a result, PAP turns out to be more effective than VAV in increasing indirect sales for products with moderate levels of PIRs.

It should be noted that the aggregate level findings, unlike the individual product level findings, are sensitive to the distribution of products and may not generalize to other platforms with different product compositions.

## Alternative Product Sampling Strategy

In the main analysis, we used the activities of control users in the first five days of the experimental period to select our product sample and calculate the PIRs, and then compare the views and sales of the product sample across three experimental groups in the last nine days. Although this strategy can avoid selection bias in product sampling, a caveat is that we were only able to use the data from the last nine days to estimate the treatment effects.

To estimate the average and heterogeneous treatment effects of VAV and PAP in the entire experimental period, we considered an alternative produc sampling strategy. Specifically, we randomly sampled half of the users from the control group and used their activities during the entire experimental period to select the product sample and calculate the PIRs. Then, we compared the views and sales of products between the two treatment groups and the remaining half of the control group during the entire experimental period. The alternative strategy resulted in a new product sample with 62,002 products, which represents what users on the platform would normally view in the experimental period in the absence of product recommendations. Table F2 summarizes the regression results on this new product sample. The findings are similar to those from the main analysis. While this alternative product sampling strategy allowed us to use all data on treated users, a potential threat is that the resulting PIR measure is not predetermined and the estimated moderating effects of PIR may not be consistent (Nizalova & Murtazashvili, 2016).

## Correlated Error Terms

On e-commerce platforms, the demand for related products, such as complements and substitutes, is usually correlated. As a result, the error terms for these products may be correlated. Although the correlation among error terms does not bias the point estimates of a linear regression model estimated with ordinary least squares, it can bias the standard errors (Greene, 2012, p. 258-259). To account for the potential correlation among the error terms of related products, we conducted a robustness check in which we report robust standard errors clustered by categories. In doing so, we allowed the error terms for all products under the same categories to be arbitrarily correlated. This flexibility may have led to overly large standard errors because there may not necessarily be correlated error terms among all products within the same category. Table F7 reports the results of this robustness check. Despite the likely overestimated standard errors, all significant coefficients in Tables 4 and 5 continue to be significant at least at the 10% significance level in Table F7, except for the coefficient of PAP in Column 2.
