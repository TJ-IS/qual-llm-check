# COMMERCIALIZING SOCIAL MEDIA? HOW SHOWROOMS ON SOCIAL MEDIA FAN PAGES INFLUENCE CUSTOMER BEHAVIOR<sup>1</sup>

Jaehwuen Jung Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {jaejung@temple.edu}

Shuting (Ada) Wang Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, City University of New York New York, NY, U.S.A. {shuting.wang@baruch.cuny.edu}

Sunil Wattal Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {swattal@temple.edu}

While marketing on social media fan pages has received widespread research attention, few studies have investigated the impact of adding a showroom to a social media fan page. Showrooms on social media fan pages are unique in that they can amplify the conflicts between businesses’ commercial purposes (selling) and customers’ expectations (socializing) on social media, making it unclear how they might influence customer behavior. In this study, we open this black box by using data from a leading fashion retailer. We found that adding a showroom to a fan page has both positive implications, in that it leads to more user engagement and purchases, and negative implications, in that it leads users to “unfollow” the retailer’s social media fan page. We further found that such impact is moderated by customer willingness to disclose private information. Specifically, the positive (negative) implication is significantly greater (smaller) for customers who are willing to disclose their private information to the retailer on social media. Mechanism-level analyses suggest that adding a showroom to a fan page can increase customer purchases both directly and indirectly by facilitating their engagement with the fan page and that customer willingness to disclose private information negatively moderates the mediation effect of user engagement on purchase behavior. In addition, results from an online experiment indicate that such showrooms can increase unfollowing by undermining users’ social perception of the fan page and raising users’ privacy concerns. Our findings suggest that even when firms see a significant increase in user purchase and activities after adding a showroom on their fan pages, they should carefully consider the potential risk of driving away customers and strategically target users who are less privacy sensitive.

Keywords: Social media, fan page, showroom, privacy, purchase, engagement, unfollow

## Introduction

Given that customers are increasingly engaging with retailers on social media, businesses have begun to leverage social media platforms as an important marketing channel. Many traditional retailers that heavily rely on their brick-and-mortar stores, especially small and medium-sized ones that cannot afford their own websites, use social media fan pages (i.e., business pages on Facebook, Pinterest, and WeChat) as an extension of traditional mass media by posting brand stories, updates, and promotions to engage customers (Ashley and Tuten, 2015;

Stelzner, 2011). A recent industry report shows that more than 80 million small and medium-sized businesses have established fan pages for marketing purposes on Facebook <sup>2</sup> and utilize these fan pages to build an online presence and interact with customers (Algharabat et al., 2020). Interestingly, some retailers take further steps to explicitly commercialize their fan pages by adding a showroom section that offers a catalog of all the products they sell (e.g., product pictures, descriptions, realtime inventories, and prices; see Figure 1 for an example of a fan page and a fan page showroom section). Such showroom features were especially appealing during the COVID-19 pandemic when offline traffic to businesses was substantially reduced because the showrooms enabled businesses to set up free online storefronts on major social networks and increase their online presence at no tangible cost. Notably, from January to March 2021 Facebook Shops reported an average of 250 million monthly active users worldwide.<sup>3</sup>

Intuitively, offering a showroom on social media fan pages would seem like a win-win proposition for both retailers and social media users. For retailers, the showroom serves as an additional channel to market products, increases the online exposure of detailed sales information, and offers customers another reason to engage with their fan pages on social media. For social media users, the showroom allows users convenient access to product and purchase information without leaving the social media platform. Yet, despite these potential benefits, many retail giants, such as Gap, J.C. Penney, Nordstrom, and GameStop, closed their showroom sections on Facebook within one year of opening them. <sup>4</sup> Such counterintuitive moves suggest that more research is needed on the effect of adding showrooms on social media fan pages.

The impact of adding a showroom to a social media fan page may be more complicated than it appears. First, showrooms on fan pages are different from other online showroom websites, as they target social media users who have unique expectations (socializing) and behaviors (social engagement and unfollowing) in comparison with general customers or internet users. Such uniqueness may lead to unintended consequences. Second, unlike other advertisements/posts on social media that focus on generally promoting a brand, product, or service, they resemble an online catalog that displays products with detailed information, such as pictures of products, descriptions, inventories, real-time prices, and discounts. As a result, adding a showroom to a fan page can amplify retailers’ commercial purpose in a way that may annoy social media users or trigger privacy concerns (Palos-Sanchez et al., 2019; Wattal et al., 2012).

In this study, we examine how adding a showroom to social media fan pages may influence customer behavior in terms of customers’ engagement (e.g., views, shares, likes, and comments) with the fan page, purchases, and the propensity to unfollow the fan page. Further, motivated by the role that privacy concerns may play in moderating users’ responses to marketing efforts (Wattal et al., 2012), we also explore the heterogeneous impacts of the showroom across social media users who are willing (versus not willing) to reveal their private information (e.g., age, gender, education, and occupation) to retailers on social media. To examine these questions, we used data from a traditional fashion retailer and leveraged a propensity score-matching method, along with difference-indifferences analyses and falsification tests. We also supplemented our analyses by collecting additional data from a quasi-experimental setup in which we compared customers in the city where the showroom section was launched with those in another city where the showroom section was not available. We conducted robustness checks and an Amazon Mechanical Turk experiment to support the theoretical arguments.

Our study has several notable findings. First, we found that adding a showroom on social media fan pages has both positive and negative implications. Specifically, while customers’ adoption (viewing) of the showroom increases their engagement (as measured by the number of activities) with the fan page, the number of purchases, and the amount of spending (by 63%, 2.8%, and 23.5%, respectively), it also significantly increases customers’ propensity to unfollow the fan page (by 24.4%). Second, we found that customers’ willingness to disclose private information moderates the impacts mentioned above. Specifically, we found that customers who are willing (versus not willing) to disclose private information are more likely to engage and make purchases while being less likely to unfollow after showroom adoption. Third, the results of our mechanism analyses suggest that when firms add a showroom to a fan page, it undermines users’ initial social perception of the fan page and, as a result, raises more privacy concerns. Meanwhile, customers’ adoption of the showroom enhances the number of purchases directly and indirectly through the mediation effects of customer engagement with the fan page, and customers’ willingness to disclose private information negatively moderates the direct and indirect relationships (e.g., meditated moderation). That is, if a customer is not willing to disclose private information, both the marketing stimuli in the showroom section and the engagement with the fan page following adoption of the showroom will become less effective in increasing customer purchases. Our findings underscore the potential risks of leveraging social media fan pages for salesoriented activities.

Our study contributes to the extant literature in at least three ways. First, while prior literature has studied the business value of social media and fan pages for retailers (Aral et al., 2013; Gu & Ye, 2014; Hutter et al., 2013; Rishika et al., 2013), this study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to examine the impact of fan page showrooms, a feature that has been widely adopted by leading social media platforms and retailers but has received little attention from academia. Second, while prior work has investigated the impact of showrooms on customer behavior (Bar-Gill & Reichman, 2017; Gallino & Moreno, 2014; Wiesel et al., 2011), our study contributes to the existing literature on omnichannel retailing by empirically investigating the impact of showrooms in the novel context of social media, where customers have unique behaviors and expectations. Third, while existing studies have focused on how retailers successfully leverage social media to improve customer relationships and increase purchases (Aral et al., 2013; Hutter et al., 2013; Rishika et al., 2013), our study is one of the first to examine the negative implications (i.e., customers’ decision to unfollow the fan page) of social media marketing.

## Literature Review

This research relates to the studies on the impact of showrooming in the retailing industry (Gensler et al., 2017; Gu & Tayi, 2016; Kuksov & Liao, 2018) and the literature on the business value of social media (Aral et al., 2013; Hutter et al., 2013; Rishika et al., 2013).

Prior studies have investigated how showrooming influences product sales (Bell et al., 2018; Gao & Su, 2017; Jing, 2018) with mixed findings. Showrooming was initially defined as a phenomenon in which customers examine products in a physical store and then make a purchase online, often from a competing retailer at a lower price (Daunt et al., 2017). The concept of showrooming has drawn considerable attention from both academia and industry, as traditional brick-andmortar retailers (e.g., Macy’s, Best Buy, and Target) are facing the major challenge of becoming a free offline showroom for their online competitors (Jing, 2018). Some scholars have observed that showrooming could also start from the online setting (often called webrooming), as customers may search for certain product information on the internet and then physically inspect and buy products in offline stores (Arora et al., 2017). The impact of online showrooming on the sales of traditional retailers is mixed and context specific, depending on how the online display influences customers’ visits or exploration in the offline channel (Gao & Su, 2017; Herhausen et al., 2015).

Previous studies have also investigated how retailers marketing efforts on social media (e.g., posts on social media and online communities) influence customer engagement, purchasing, and relationships, although the findings are often mixed and competing (Duffett, 2017; Zarrella, 2009). One stream suggests that social media is a promising place for marketers to promote short-term sales and engagement. For example, retailers can use social media fan pages as an information-display medium, passively waiting for interested customers to visit (Goh et al., 2013; Hutter et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2015; Rishika et al., 2013; Stephen & Galak, 2012). Meanwhile, retailers can also leverage fan pages as a marketing channel to promote sales, actively reaching out to potential customers and initiating interactions by posting messages on their fan pages (Cvijikj & Michahelles, 2013; Luarn et al., 2015; Miller & Tucker, 2013). Another stream suggests that retailers should be cautious when leveraging fan pages for marketing purposes, as commercial activities on social media may annoy users and drive potential customers away (Fournier & Avery, 2011; Goldstein et al., 2014; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010). Relevant studies propose that if retailers marketing efforts do not align with customer expectations, such efforts will lead to unfollowing and long-term loss in sales (Wang et al., 2020).

Given the mixed results from the relevant studies and the uniqueness of fan page showrooms, the impact of adding a showroom on social media fan pages cannot be easily indicated by the prior literature. First, fan page showrooms are different from traditional online showroom websites. While fan page showrooms resemble online showrooming websites to some degree, fan page showrooms have a unique customer base (social media users), customer expectations (socializing), and customer behaviors (social engagement and unfollowing) specific to social media platforms. Due to such uniqueness, fan page showrooms may (1) trigger customers’ privacy concerns on social media platforms (Palos-Sanchez et al., 2019; Wattal et al., 2012) and (2) influence customers’ engagement (e.g., likes, shares, and comments) with the fan page as well as unfollowing. Second, fan page showrooms are also different from other existing marketing efforts on fan pages in that they resemble an online catalog displaying products with detailed information (e.g., product pictures, descriptions, real-time prices, and discounts). In contrast, other types of social media advertisements either focus on promoting a specific product/service or general branding. In other words, the showroom format is more rigid and direct than other types of social media presence. Due to such differences, adding a showroom to fan pages could amplify retailers’ commercial purpose in a way that could be more clearly recognized by social media users. This invasion of customers’ social networks with blunt business incentives may decrease users’ social perception and trigger privacy concerns (Palos-Sanchez et al., 2019; Wattal et al., 2012). Third, existing studies about online showrooming or retailers’ marketing efforts on social media indicate a possibility of both a positive and a negative effect of the fan page showroom on online engagement and sales (Fournier & Avery, 2011;

Goldstein et al., 2014; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Wang et al., 2020). Therefore, an empirical exploration of how adding a showroom to a fan page may influence customer behavior is warranted, especially given that many retail giants (e.g., Gap, J.C. Penney, Nordstrom, and GameStop) closed their showroom sections on Facebook within one year of opening them. These counterintuitive practices from the industry underline the potential of unknown or unexpected risks of adding a showroom to a fan page. To fill this gap in the literature, we explore the ramifications of fan page showrooms in customer behavior on social media.

## Hypotheses

## Fan Page Showrooms and Customer Engagement

How do fan page showrooms influence customer engagement with retailers’ fan pages? To answer this question, we relied on the literature on product exposure and the theory of information economics (Chang & Wildt, 1994; Eldredge & Systematics, 1986; Hayek, 1945; Stigler, 1961).

Studies on product exposure suggest that customers compare purchase alternatives and select the product that best fits their needs in terms of price, quality, shopping experience, etc. (Goldman & Johansson, 1978). When customers are exposed to a product, they will be motivated to search for more information about the product and potential purchasing alternatives (Chang & Wildt, 1994; Detlor et al., 2003; Kim & Lee, 2008). Fan pages were initially designed for retailers to post brand-level updates, announcements, events, and promotions, rather than to systematically list products and prices. When a retailer introduces a showroom section that displays a catalog of product images, descriptions, inventories, and prices, these stimuli could potentially entice more interest in the products, especially from customers who have already displayed interest in the brand by visiting the retailer’s fan page (De Vries et al., 2012). The increased purchasing interest will motivate customers to search for more information about the displayed products beyond the showroom section.

Information economics theories suggest that customers prefer to search for more information on a convenient channel (Birchler & Bütler, 1999), as searching for information requires a considerable amount of time and cognitive effort (Stigler, 1961). As a result, if the showroom section motivates customers to search for more product-related information, they would naturally expand their search by exploring the fan page where the showroom section is embedded. For example, interested customers can view posts on the retailer’s fan page to see updates about the brand and promotions, read online discussions and feedback about product performance from other customers, and directly ask questions and interact with other users. All such activities naturally lead to more engagement with the fan page.

The above discussion indicates that fan page showrooms may increase product exposure and motivate customers to search for more product information by exploring and engaging with the retailer’s fan page. To measure the effect of the showroom, we used customers’ adoption of the showroom (i.e., whether a customer has ever clicked the showroom) as an indicator of their usage. Accordingly, we propose our first hypothesis:

H1: Customer adoption of showrooms on retailers’ social media fan pages is positively associated with customers’ engagement with fan pages.

## Fan Page Showrooms and Customer Purchase

Our theorization above suggests there may be positive implications of the showroom section for retailers, e.g., more engagement on the fan page. In this section, we discuss another positive implication—increased purchases.

The prior literature suggests that fan page showrooms can increase purchases by raising product awareness and reducing the search cost of customer shopping (Eechambadi, 1994). In contrast to the original use of fan pages (e.g., general marketing), the showroom section displays detailed product information that directly aims at sales (e.g., pictures, real-time prices, and product availability). Such detailed product information may increase product awareness and thus purchase intentions by informing customers about new products, describing the advantages of specific products, and matching products with potential buyers (Hui et al., 2013; Inman & Winer, 1998). Additionally, the showroom section could also reduce customers’ search costs by allowing customers to check product information without leaving the social media fan pages, thus reducing the possibility that customers will visit online/offline stores only to find the desired products unavailable. The lowered search cost and decreased uncertainty of product availability could motivate more customers to visit the brand’s online/offline stores to inspect products and complete purchases (San-Martín et al., 2017).

The social media literature also suggests that fan page showrooms may act as a social actor that customers can engage with, leading to more customer engagement and thus more purchases due to stronger brand preferences in purchase decisions (Heller Baird & Parasnis, 2011; Kim & Ko, 2012;

Sashi, 2012). When customers engage with fan pages, they often seek product information, express their opinions about the brand, or interact with other users (Heller Baird et al., 2011). Our theorizing of H1 suggests that the showroom section may motivate customers to engage more on the fan page. With increased engagement, customers will have greater opportunities to view brand stories, socialize with other customers who are also interested in the same brand, and share posts within their own networks. Such engagement often creates a favorable customer experience with the focal brand and solidifies brand preference, leading to more purchases (Dhar & Jha, 2014).

The above discussion suggests that fan page showrooms may increase customer purchases by increasing online product awareness, lowering search costs, and facilitating customer engagement with the brand. Therefore, we propose:

H2: Customer adoption of the showrooms on retailers’ social media fan pages is positively associated with customers’ purchases from the retailers.

## Fan Page Showrooms and Customer Unfollowing

Despite the positive implications discussed above, i.e., more engagement and purchases, there are also potential drawbacks (e.g., unfollowing) when retailers add a fan page showroom.

Fan page showrooms may increase customers’ propensity to unfollow the fan page by raising privacy concerns. In order to explain this, we introduce the concept of “social perception” (Zalmanson et al., 2022). Specifically, it has been found that users first rely on salient features to make an initial assessment and further integrate multiple cues to form social perception (Anderson, 1981; Anderson, 1965). During this process, integrating additional cues into this initial assessment is mostly affected by how consistent those cues are (Fiske & Neuberg, 1990). That is, if additional cues are not consistent with the initial cue, the initial assessment could be undermined. In our context, while a fan page with social cues (e.g., users’ ability to interact with the brand and other users by engaging with posts on the fan page) allows users to form a certain level of social perception of the fan page (i.e., perceptions that they can socialize with others on the fan page), introducing the showroom may bring non-social cues that are more relevant to commercial and selling. The inconsistency between the commercial cues of the showroom and the existing social cues of the fan page would undermine users’ initial social perception of the fan page, ultimately raising more privacy concerns (Zalmanson et al., 2022).

Meanwhile, behavior on social media is guided by boundary regulations, with privacy concerns serving as one of the main motives (Stutzman & Hartzog, 2012; Wisniewski et al., 2012). An implication of this theory is that fan page showrooms may violate people’s privacy boundaries on social media, eliciting negative attitudes and driving some customers to unfollow the fan page. Originally, social media was a largely private space that people used to share personal information and keep in touch with family and friends (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Kietzmann et al., 2011). However, it has been shown that retailers collect and use people’s social media data for marketing purposes, leading to more impulsive and unnecessary purchases (Setyani et al., 2019). To protect their privacy information, people may regulate their territories on social media by setting boundaries through which they manage incoming and outgoing interactions with different types of social connections (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010). Previous research has shown that many customers never engage with businesses on social media sites because they believe that communication with sellers on social media violates their privacy boundaries and that by avoiding interacting with sellers, they can prevent advertisements from sneaking into their social lives (Heller Baird & Parasnis, 2011).

Some users choose to follow retailers’ fan pages on social media when their interest in the brand or retailer outweighs their wish for privacy. However, this does not mean they are not concerned about their privacy (Wang et al., 2020). When retailers add a fan page showroom, their commercial purpose would be amplified and clearly recognized by users. While this may fulfill the expectations of some users and lead to more engagement and purchases, it is unlikely that the showroom section will be welcomed by all fan page followers at all times (Gurău, 2008; Tsimonis et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2016). If people perceive a mismatch between their expectations and the utility provided by the fan page, the showroom would intensify their privacy concerns, driving them to limit the information flow from the retailer by unfollowing the fan page (Bapna et al., 2019; Pomfret et al., 2020).

To summarize, a showroom section may increase unfollowing by intensifying customers’ privacy concerns and threatening their boundary control (e.g., separating commercial activities from their social lives on social media). Therefore, we propose:

H3: Customer adoption of showrooms on retailers’ social media fan pages is positively associated with customers’ propensity to unfollow fan pages.

## Moderating Role of Customer Willingness to Disclose Private Information

The previous discussion underscores that both positive and negative outcomes are likely when retailers add showrooms to fan pages. While this may seem counterintuitive, it can be explained by customers’ heterogeneous responses to marketing initiatives (Wattal et al., 2012). That is, whereas some customers will have a positive reaction to showrooms, others may react negatively, leading to the net result that both positive and negative reactions will manifest in different ways—some customers will engage and purchase more, while others will decide to unfollow the fan page. As we believe that customers’ privacy concerns represent a major issue associated with retailers adding a showroom on their fan page, we now examine how the positive and negative effects are moderated by customers’ willingness to disclose private information, an indication of customers’ privacy attitudes (Acquisti et al., 2020; Phelps et al., 2000).

Privacy concerns have been widely discussed in the context of social media (Kane et al., 2014; Kapoor et al., 2018). While people generally consider social media to be a private space, engaging with others on social media always involves privacy concerns (Ellison et al., 2011; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Madden, 2012). When customers engage with a retailer on a fan page, their interactions (e.g., views, shares, likes, and comments) are automatically shared with the retailer along with their private information (e.g., name, gender, location). As a result, any commercial cue during the engagement would likely remind customers of the retailer’s ability and incentive to collect and leverage their private data (Ellison et al., 2011; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Madden, 2012). For example, many users decided to quit the focal social media platform, WeChat, when the platform started to send personalized ads to users based on their browsing history.<sup>5</sup>

Customers who are willing to disclose private information to retailers when interacting with their fan pages on social media demonstrate fewer privacy concerns (Acquisti et al., 2020; Phelps et al., 2000), potentially because of low sensitivity to privacy issues, high levels of trust/interest in the brand, or their stage in the consumption funnel (Hariguna & Berlilana, 2017; Nguyen et al., 2013). In contrast, customers who are unwilling to disclose private information exhibit more privacy concerns; thus, a showroom section would be more likely to elicit negative attitudes and undermine their willingness to engage and purchase (Wang et al., 1998; Wattal et al., 2012). That is, while our theorization in H1 and H2 suggests that the showroom section may have a positive impact on customer engagement and purchases, this impact would be stronger for customers who are willing to disclose private information and weaker for those who are not willing to do so (Stutzman & Hartzog, 2012; Wisniewski et al., 2012). We thus propose the following hypotheses:

H4a: Customer willingness to disclose private information positively moderates the relationship between customer adoption of showrooms on retailers’ social media fan pages and customer engagement with the fan pages.

H4b: Customer willingness to disclose private information positively moderates the relationship between customer adoption of showrooms on retailers’ social media fan pages and customer purchases from the retailers.

Aligned with our argument in H4a and H4b, as customers who are willing to disclose private information tend to have fewer privacy concerns, they would be less likely to protect their privacy on social media by setting boundaries between their social lives and commercial activities. Accordingly, they would be less likely to unfollow a fan page after experiencing a showroom that makes the fan page more relevant to commercial activities. Therefore, we propose:

H4c: Customer willingness to disclose private information negatively moderates the relationship between customer adoption of showrooms on retailers’ social media fan pages and customers’ propensity to unfollow the fan pages.

## Research Context and Data Description

We leveraged a dataset from a traditional fashion retailer that has roughly 10,000 brick-and-mortar stores in more than 200 cities across China. Similar to many other traditional retailers that heavily rely on brick-and-mortar stores, the focal retailer generates the majority of its sales (>92%) offline.<sup>6</sup> The retailer maintains a fan page on WeChat, the leading social media platform in China, and added a showroom section to its fan page in August 2016.

Due to disclosure policies, we used an example from a similar retailer to illustrate the fan page and the showroom section discussed in our setting (Figure 1). All customers can enter the fan page (the picture on the left in Figure 1) through the search panel on the platform. Customers who follow the fan page can also visit through the notification page when there are new updates. To access the showroom on the fan page (the picture on the right in Figure 1), customers need to enter the fan page and click the appropriate button in the menu bar at the bottom right of the fan page. This retailer uses the fan page to post brand-level information only (e.g., brand stories, events, promotions, and news about product launches) for general marketing purposes, which is a common practice on social media. In contrast, the showroom section is more salesoriented, as customers can see detailed information about the products available for sale in stores, including pictures of the products, availability, real-time prices, and information about sales and discounts.

We outline several reasons why our context provides an ideal setting to examine the research questions in our study. First, the focal platform WeChat is similar to other major social media platforms in many ways. For example, both WeChat and Facebook allow users to follow and unfollow fan pages at any time and allow retailers to determine their own posting strategies (e.g., content and time). A survey of the top 10 fan pages on Facebook suggests that the focal retailer’s posting behavior is representative of posting activities in terms of post frequency, content, and quality. As is very common among retailers in the U.S., the showroom section in our setting is a catalog-only add-on to the fan page that does not allow withinshowroom purchases (i.e., customers are directed to online/offline stores outside of the social media platform to make purchases). While other retailers may use a different showroom design that allows within-showroom purchases, we do not believe that our focus on the catalog-only showroom undermines the generalizability of our findings.<sup>7</sup>

Second, the loyalty program of our focal retailer allowed us to track each customer’s purchases. The retailer offers a loyalty program in which customers can enroll for free at any time and receive an extra 5% discount on purchases. This discount encourages the majority of customers to enroll and, as a result, member transactions make up over 75% of the retailer’s total sales. The loyalty program allowed us to track each customer’s purchase frequency and spending level. We focused on offline purchases since online purchases from multiple third-party platforms are minimal and are impossible to track at the individual level. Given that a large share of the retailer’s transactions are captured by the loyalty program, this distinction has little relevance (see the Limitations and Opportunities for Future Research section for a more detailed discussion).

Finally, the rich dataset consists of data from multiple cities that differ in terms of when the showroom section was introduced, which allowed us to conduct robustness checks using different methodologies. When the retailer first introduced the showroom section, it was available only to users located in City A, which is one of the four largest cities in China. Customers in other cities could see the showroom button on the fan page but would be directed to a blank webpage after clicking the button.<sup>8</sup> Therefore, in our main analysis, we focused on customers in City A and used a propensity score matching and a difference-in-differences approach to compare the behavior of customers who adopted the showroom section versus those who did not. As a robustness check, we leveraged a quasi-experiment setup to compare the behavior of customers across cities where the showroom section was available versus not available using a difference-in-differences model and confirmed that the results are consistent (see Quasi-Experimental Setup section).

Since the showroom was first available to customers in early August 2016, we collected data on customers who were active on the fan page between June 2016 and October 2016. As mentioned earlier, in the main analysis we focused on customers from City A who followed the fan page before early July (four weeks prior to the introduction of the showroom section) and did not unfollow the fan page before early August (when the company introduced the showroom section). This restriction led to a dataset of 19,340 customers. Our data contained customer-level transaction records (e.g., user ID, product ID, and transactions) and time-stamped customer activities. While we did not have access to customer browsing records within the showroom, our data included the time at which each customer visited the showroom.

We also captured customers’ willingness to disclose private information. Specifically, when a customer follows the retailer’s fan page, a survey appears on the screen to collect their personal information, such as age, gender, education, and occupation. While customers can use all the functionalities on the fan page without completing the survey, some still choose to complete it and disclose their private information. In this study, if a customer provided any data in the survey and sent it back to the retailer, we considered the customer willing to disclose private information to the retailer. Otherwise, we tagged that customer as not being willing to disclose private information.

During the data collection period, 882 (out of 19,340 randomly selected) customers adopted the showroom section. ## Empirical Strategy

In the main analysis, we focused on customers in City A and relied on two empirical approaches to evaluate the effect of the showroom section on customers’ behavior: (1) propensity score matching combined with difference-in-differences analyses and a falsification test, and (2) the Cox proportional hazards survival model. The first approach was used to estimate the effect on customers’ engagement and purchases, and the second approach was used to investigate the impact on customers’ unfollowing behavior.

## Difference-in-Differences Technique with Propensity Score Matching and Falsification Tests

To identify the effect of customer adoption of the showroom on their engagement and purchases, following Jung et al. (2019), we employed a three-step empirical approach (Figure A1 in Appendix A): (1) propensity score matching (PSM), (2) difference-in-differences (DID), and (3) falsification tests. The PSM allowed us to create matched pairs of treated subjects (e.g., customers who adopted the showroom) and non-treated subjects (e.g., customers who did not adopt the showroom) with similar covariates and the propensity to be treated. The DID compares the difference between the change in the outcomes of the treated subjects to that of the untreated subjects before and after the treatment over the same time period. We do not use the word “treatment” in the conventional sense where treatment is exogenous. One can argue that our treatment was not truly exogenous since customers self-selected into the treatment and were not randomly treated. While this could lead to identification issues, our use of DID combined with PSM allowed us to overcome the limitation that our treatment was not truly exogenous. This technique has been commonly used as an identification technique in prior literature (Jung et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2019; Son et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2017). Finally, the falsification test confirmed that the above two procedures are falsifiable.

First, for PSM, we constructed a profile of each customer using observable characteristics, including demographic variables (e.g., gender and age) and behavioral variables (e.g., number of purchases, average discount rate, days of interaction on the fan page, loyalty membership level, and whether the customer shared private information) from data over the four-week period prior to the introduction of the showroom section. We then built a model to predict the likelihood of each customer being treated (e.g., propensity-to-be-treated scores) based on these covariates. Next, we attempted one-to-one static matching (without replacement) within a caliper width of 0.001. <sup>10</sup> Finally, to evaluate whether our PSM procedure ensured similarity between the treatment (customers who adopted the showroom) and control (customers who did not adopt the showroom)

groups, we compared the differences in covariates between the treatment and control groups; the results are presented in Table 2. As shown in Table 2, we found no significant differences in either user demographics or user behavior in the pre-treatment period, confirming that our PSM procedure was successful.

Second, we constructed a customer-week panel dataset for the matched sample that resulted from our PSM and employed the DID technique to identify the effect of customer showroom adoption on customer behavior. Since the PSM approach indicated that the control group was similar to the treatment group based on observed characteristics, integrating PSM with DID analysis enhanced the inference related to DID analysis and thus improved the consistency of the estimates (Stewart & Swaffield, 2008). Our main model is:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\log (1 + A c t i v i t y _ {i t}) = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \times A f t e r T r e a t m e n t _ {i t} +} \\ & {\alpha_ {2} \times (A f t e r T r e a t m e n t _ {i t} \times T r e a t m e n t _ {i}) + \sigma_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, (1)} \end{array}
$$

where Activity<sub>it</sub> is the dependent variable of interest (e.g., FanPageActivity,<sup>11</sup> NumberOfPurchases, and TotalSpending). Treatment<sub>i</sub> is a treatment dummy variable equal to $^ { \ \mathfrak { s } } 1 ^ { \mathfrak { s } }$ if a customer is assigned to the treatment group and $\mathbf { \vec { \nabla } } ^ { 6 } 0 ^ { 9 }$ otherwise. AfterTreatment<sub>it</sub> is a binary variable indicating whether week t is the post-treatment period or pretreatment period for customer i. The DID model covered the time periods running from four weeks before to four weeks after showroom adoption. <sup>12</sup> Therefore, AfterTreatment<sub>it</sub> is equal to “0” for the four weeks immediately prior to adoption and is equal to “1” for the four weeks immediately after adoption for each treated user and their matched pairs. The coefficient α<sub>2</sub>, the difference-in-differences estimator, captures the average treatment effect of customer showroom adoption, i.e., how customer behavior in the treatment group changes after using the showroom section compared to that of the control group over the same period. Finally, we included customer-level fixed effects, $\sigma _ { i } ,$ and weeklevel fixed effects, $\tau _ { t }$ , to control for time-invariant user characteristics across consumers and common systematic changes over time across all customers, respectively.

To further test how different customer characteristics moderate the effect of showroom adoption (H4), we constructed a binary indicator, SharePrivate<sub>i</sub>, which takes the value of 1 for customers who shared their private information <sup>13</sup> with the retailer and “0” otherwise. We interacted SharePrivate<sub>i</sub> with the difference-in-differences analysis and relied on the coefficient of this three-way interaction to capture the moderating effects.<sup>14</sup>

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\log (1 + A c t i v i t y _ {i t}) = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \times A f t e r T r e a t m e n t _ {i t} +} \\ & {\alpha_ {2} \times (A f t e r T r e a t m e n t _ {i t} \times T r e a t m e n t _ {i}) + \alpha_ {3} \times} \\ & {(A f t e r T r e a t m e n t _ {i t} \times S h a r e P r i v a t e _ {i}) + \alpha_ {4} \times} \\ & {(A f t e r T r e a t m e n t _ {i t} \times T r e a t m e n t _ {i} \times S h a r e P r i v a t e _ {i}) +} \\ & {\sigma_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

While combining PSM with DID is a popular identification strategy in the literature (Dewan et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2019; Rishika et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2017), we further enhanced the reliability of our results by combining this with a falsification test. For the falsification test, the idea was to run the same DID model (Equation 1) by shifting AfterTreatment<sub>it</sub> to one time window earlier (i.e., 4 weeks). That is, we ran the regression model by covering the time periods from eight weeks to one week prior to showroom adoption, where AfterTreatment<sub>i</sub> variable is equal to “1” from four weeks to one week prior to showroom adoption and “0” from eight weeks to five weeks prior to the actual showroom adoption for each treated user and their matched pair. Ideally, we should see no significant effect for the same model based on the shifted time period (i.e., “fake” adoption time). This falsification test is known to be much more robust than other approaches used in the literature, alleviating the concern that we may have incorrectly detected a statistically significant effect when there was none because of inappropriate matching, an inappropriate regression model, or the existence of pre-treatment differences in the PSM and DID procedure (Bapna et al., 2018; Jung et al., 2019).

## Survival Analysis

To estimate the effect of customers’ adoption of the showroom on their propensity to unfollow the fan page, we used survival analysis. Compared to our previous approach using DID, survival analysis takes into account the duration of the active period on the fan page before customers unfollow and the right-censored observations in the data (Kalbfleisch & Prentice, 2011). Specifically, using a Cox proportional hazard model, we modeled how quickly customers unfollow the fan page after using the showroom section as below:

$$
h (t, X) = h _ {0} (t) e ^ {\Sigma \beta_ {i} X _ {i}},\tag{3}
$$

where ℎ(??, ??) represents the rate of unfollowing in each week t, $h _ { 0 } ( t )$ refers to the baseline hazard function, $X _ { i }$ represents demographic and individual-level characteristics of customers, which includes treatment dummy, whether private information was shared, and customer purchase behavior before the treatment.

## Robustness Check

In the main analysis, we used propensity score matching to address the endogeneity issue of customers self-selecting to adopt the showroom or not. To address the concern that our propensity score matching may not account for all unobservable confounders (e.g., customer purchase intention) and to further ensure the robustness of our results, we conducted two additional tests: we (1) leveraged a quasiexperiment setup and (2) introduced an instrumental variable approach. The results consistently support the findings in the main analysis.

Based on this, we collected data on customers who clicked the showroom button and were located in City B, a comparable location to City A based on the socioeconomic development (see Table A1 in Appendix A) during our time window.<sup>17</sup> We leveraged this setup to create a quasi-experiment by collecting additional data from a comparable city and comparing the behavior of customers across the two cities (Chen et al., 2018).

This concern is minimized in our setting because our data suggest that the blank page did not have a significant detrimental effect on customer behavior and we did not observe any significant decreases in purchase, engagement, or unfollowing among customers in City B after they clicked the showroom button.

## Instrumental Variable Approach

Next, we used an instrumental variable approach to account for other endogeneity issues such as strong ignorability (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983) and common support (Becker & Ichino, 2002), which may arise when using a matching technique.

We introduced two instrumental variables. First, we constructed a Hausman-style instrument by computing the average fan page showroom adoption rate of customers at the three nearest stores from the store each focal customer mainly used. This variable measured the likelihood of customers in the region adopting the fan page showroom, which correlates with the likelihood of the focal user adopting the showroom (Pick et al., 2019). However, this variable is less likely to affect the focal user’s engagement, purchase, or unfollowing behavior, conditional on storefixed effects and a range of control variables that account for the focal user’s promotion usage and purchase patterns. We note that this type of instrument is useful when it is difficult to find a variable that justifies the exclusion restrictions (Bound et al., 1995) and is widely used in the IS literature (Tan et al., 2019; Aral et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019). Second, we used the log-transformed rate of how often the focal user checked the fan page before purchasing a product as an instrument. <sup>19</sup> This instrument is conceptually valid since customers who regularly check social media fan pages before making purchases are more likely to be aware of and adopt showroom sections. However, the tendency to check fan pages before making purchases should not be directly correlated with the focal user’s subsequent engagement and purchase behaviors, conditional on the user’s fan page usage and purchase patterns.

## Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the sensitivity of our main results, we conducted further analyses with different conditions. First, we replicated the DID analysis by including more control variables. Specifically, we did not apply user-level control variables in the main analysis because we included user-level fixed effects in our model.<sup>21</sup> To ensure that the results held when adding userlevel control variables, we re-estimated the DID models by using time-fixed effects only and by including multiple userlevel control variables.<sup>22</sup> All results are consistent with our main analysis results. Second, to ensure the robustness of the results with respect to the matched samples, we applied the coarsened exact matching (CEM) procedure (Iacus et al., 2011; Iacus et al., 2012) and one-to-many matching and confirmed that the results are consistent with our main analysis (Appendix B). Third, we conducted several analyses with different time windows to assess the sensitivity of our results. We replicated the main DID models with two-week and eight-week windows to show that our results are robust to shorter and longer windows of analysis. In addition, we conducted the survival analyses using a sixmonth time window to ensure that our results were not sensitive to the cutoff point. All results are consistent with our findings in the main analysis in terms of the sign and significance level of the estimates.<sup>23</sup>

## Understanding the Underlying Mechanisms

Our analysis provides evidence that customer showroom adoption increases both engagement and purchases and that the willingness to disclose private information moderates the effects. To further investigate the underlying mechanisms proposed in our hypotheses, we conducted several mediation and mediated moderation analyses to uncover the interplay among showroom adoption, engagement, purchase, and privacy concerns. The conceptual model for the mediation and mediated moderation analyses is illustrated in Figure 3.

First, we carried out the classical mediation analysis proposed by Baron and Kenny (1986) to understand whether social engagement mediates the impact of showroom adoption on purchasing. The results are presented in Columns 1-3 in Table A3 in the Appendix. The results show that showroom adoption can increase user purchases both directly and indirectly by facilitating customer engagement with the fan page. Specifically, the results of the indirect effects are aligned with our theorization that showroom adoption increases purchases by facilitating online engagement with the fan page.

Second, to understand the role of customers’ willingness to disclose private information in the mediation effect, we used the mediated moderation test outlined by Muller et al. (2005). The mediated moderation test examines a conceptual argument about the moderation effect on the overall treatment effect and tests whether a mediating process is responsible for that moderation (Edwards & Lambert, 2007). Since we argue that the effect of customer showroom adoption on engagement is moderated by customers’ willingness to disclose private information and that engagement, in turn, influences purchasing, this suggests mediated moderation. The results are reported in Columns 4-6 of Table A3. Results show that customer willingness to disclose private information positively moderates the mediation effect of social engagement on purchase behavior (i.e., NumberOfPurchases or TotalSpending), aligning with our main analysis results.

## Online Experiment

In our theoretical development, we made two main arguments regarding the impact of the fan page showroom on customer behavior: (1) a fan page showroom decreases customers’ social perception and increases privacy concerns, which leads to unfollowing, and (2) these changes in social perception, privacy concerns, and unfollowing are unique consequences of adding a showroom in the setting of social media fan pages (versus adding a traditional showroom website). While we have provided empirical evidence to support these arguments (such as using customer willingness to disclose private information as a proxy for privacy concerns), we were unable to directly measure customers social perception and privacy concerns using the secondary data. Therefore, we conducted an experiment using Amazon Mechanical Turk to provide additional evidence.

The purpose of the experiment is to show that (1) adding a fan page showroom decreases customers’ social perception and increases privacy concerns, which leads to unfollowing, and (2) while adding a traditional showroom website does not change social perception, privacy concerns, and unfollowing (i.e., the decrease in social perception as well as the increase in privacy concerns and unfollowing are unique in the setting of social media fan pages). In the experiment, we created a fan page of a fake fashion retailer in the U.S. and randomly assigned experiment participants to one of the three setups (see Figure A2): (1) a fan page without a showroom (Control), (2) a fan page with a showroom (Treatment 1), and (3) a fan page without a showroom but with a separate showroom website (Treatment 2). Following the literature, we measured customers’ social perception by asking whether participants considered this fan page a space for social activities, and we measured privacy concerns using three constructs: (1) concerns about privacy loss, (2) concerns about personal information being shared with the retailer on social media, and (3) concerns about inappropriate use of personal information by the retailer (Malhotra et al., 2004; Xu et al., 2011). Then, we constructed a composite score for privacy concerns (PRIVACY) using factor analysis with these three constructs (Saldanha et al., 2017; Xue et al., 2021). Specifically, in the survey, we asked participants one question related to social perception, three questions about privacy concerns (measured as a score from 1 to 4), and questions to determine participants’ age, the number of social media platforms they have ever used, how many fan pages they have ever followed, and whether they plan to unfollow the brand (assuming they are already following the fan page before the survey). <sup>24</sup> Survey responses were marked as invalid and removed from the pool if any of the answers was missing or in an incorrect format/range (e.g., input irrelevant text for age). Survey responses with duplicate IP addresses were also excluded. We recruited participants for our survey on Amazon MTurk in November 2022 and paid participants the equivalent of 0.2 USD each. In total, 759 surveys were completed, among which 602 were valid.

## Discussion

## Key Findings

In this study, we examined how adding a showroom on a social media fan page influences customers’ decisions to engage with, purchase from, and unfollow the retailer on social media. Our study shows that adding a showroom to a fan page has both positive and negative consequences for the retailer. On the one hand, the showroom can benefit the retailer by eliciting more engagement and purchases from customers. On the other hand, the showroom can also lead to intangible costs by increasing unfollowing. We further found that the impact of the showroom is moderated by customers’ willingness to disclose private information. Specifically, the benefits are greater and the costs are smaller when customers are willing to disclose private information to the retailer on social media.

## Generalizability

We now discuss the generalizability of our findings. First, while our results are from a particular social media platform (WeChat), we believe they are broadly applicable to other instances of social media. After examining Facebook and our focal social media platform, we determined that these two platforms are largely similar in terms of the key features (e.g., design of fan pages). We recognize that WeChat is a mobile application and that mobile users may behave differently from users of other devices. Yet, given that the majority of users worldwide now access social media on mobile phones, <sup>27</sup> we believe our findings can be expanded to general cases.

Second, we examined the external validity of the design of the showroom in our context. The instructions on Facebook for adding showroom sections<sup>28</sup> suggest that the design is largely the same across our focal context and Facebook. We note that the showroom section discussed in our study is a catalog-only addon to the fan page and is non-customized at the individual customer level. While some showrooms on Facebook allow customers to make purchases within the showrooms, our focus on the catalog-only design of showrooms does not undermine the generalizability of our findings for two reasons. First, our manual examination suggests that the catalog-only design of the showroom section is widely adopted by retailing giants (e.g., Target and Walmart) as well as small and medium-sized businesses on leading social media platforms such as Facebook. Second, when retailers allow purchases within the showroom section, we would expect our findings of the positive impact on engagement, purchase, and unfollowing to be more strongly supported due to the ease of purchasing within the fan page, with a consequently amplified effect of product exposure on customer purchase, engagement, and privacy concerns on social media (Bapna et al., 2019; Dhar & Jha, 2014; Yogesh & Yesha, 2014).

Third, our focal retailer is a traditional retailer that mainly relies on offline stores for sales, and the showroom provides information about products available in its offline stores. While data limitations compelled us to focus on offline purchases in this study, we believe our findings can still be generalized to total sales for three reasons: (1) offline sales account for the majority (92%) of the focal company’s total sales, (2) no material change was observed in online sales during the observation period, and (3) since the fan page showroom will likely shift sales from offline to online channels (Hsiao & Chen, 2013), including online sales would likely lead to even stronger support for our conclusions. In addition, this generalizability also stems from the fact that most traditional retailers (e.g., Walmart, Macy’s, and Target) also heavily rely on offline channels for sales, and more than a third of small businesses use social media to drive offline sales exclusively.<sup>29</sup>

## Theoretical and Practical Implications

Three notable contributions stem from this research. First, while prior studies have suggested that retailers’ marketing efforts on social media may increase customer engagement and purchases (Fournier & Avery, 2011; Hutter et al., 2013; Rishika et al., 2013), we contribute to the literature on the business value of social media (Barrett et al., 2016; Culnan et al., 2010; Miller & Tucker, 2013) by considering a novel practice—adding a fan page showroom, a sales-oriented feature that has been adopted by many leading social media platforms and retailers. The advent of social media has reshaped the sales environment, with many scholars recognizing the potential of social media as a place to facilitate product sales (Kapoor et al., 2018; Marshall et al., 2012). However, few studies have examined the returns of adding a showroom on social media. Our work is among the first to analyze how retailers’ attempts to sell products on social media fan pages influence customer behavior, shedding light on the feasibility of leveraging social media as a new sales channel.

Second, while prior studies have investigated the impact of showrooming in various contexts (Bar-Gill & Reichman, 2017; Gallino & Moreno, 2014; Wiesel et al., 2011), our study extends the existing literature by examining this question in a novel setting—social media fan pages, where customer behavior and expectations are different from those of general internet users. We also investigate the interplay among the fan page showroom, customer behavior, and privacy concerns. In particular, our study highlights the importance of further research in this context, as it reveals a positive effect of the showroom on privacy concerns, which is in contrast to the insignificant effect of social cues reported in Zalmanson et al.’s study (2022).

Third, while prior studies have focused on success stories documenting how retailers’ commercialization efforts on social media benefit them (Aral et al., 2013; Hutter et al., 2013; Rishika et al., 2013), our work contributes to the literature by demonstrating both the positive and negative implications. The tension between increased customer engagement/purchases and a jump in unfollowing driven by the fan page showroom yields important implications for both theory and practice.

For practitioners, our results suggest that social media fan pages are not a risk-free place for retailers to sell products and that retailers should strategically target customers when leveraging the showroom section to maximize their revenues. Our findings suggest that when firms add a showroom to a fan page, it lowers users’ social perception of the fan page, which leads to a higher level of privacy concerns. When users have more privacy concerns about engaging with the fan pages, especially those who are privacy sensitive, they are more likely to unfollow the fan page. As unfollowing may lead to long-term losses for retailers (Wang et al., 2020), the possibility of increased unfollowing should be considered by retailers when making decisions about whether and how to leverage the new feature. Our study also indicates that since customers who are not willing to share private information are more likely to react negatively to showrooms, people with fewer privacy concerns could be a better target for this feature. Our findings were presented to the focal retailer and were well accepted by the executives. The retailer has since acted on our findings by leveraging their fan pages more strategically— reducing their sales endeavors among customers who are not willing to disclose private data.

## Limitations and Opportunities for Future Research

Our paper has certain limitations that point toward opportunities for future research. First, in this study, we only used data on offline purchases, although a small portion of the focal retailer’s sales (less than 8%) did come from multiple third-party online marketplaces during this period. If channel switching were to happen at a large scale, our conclusions about the impact of the fan page showroom on purchases would be biased. While the firm manager confirmed that there was no meaningful change in online sales during our observation window, future research could examine whether and how a fan page showroom influences online and offline sales differently. Second, while our analysis suggests that privacy concerns following showroom adoption is the major reason for more unfollowing, it is difficult in our context to distinguish between users who unfollowed due to privacy issues and those with different preferences (e.g., dissatisfaction with the variety of products offered). Future studies could further examine this issue. Third, as the firm did not advertise the showroom feature and we only used a two-month time window following the introduction of the showroom, only 882 (out of randomly selected 19,340) users adopted the showroom. While we believe the condition provided a good setting to study our research questions since the adopters were not driven by any targeting strategy or monetary incentives, future studies could examine whether the effect is consistent when the adoption rate of the showroom is higher.

## Concluding Remarks

Retailers continue to be fascinated by the potential of social media for both brand building and driving sales (Aral et al., 2013; Fournier & Avery, 2011; Laroche et al., 2013). Yet the lack of understanding of the relationship between retailers marketing efforts and customer behaviors on social media, along with limited empirical evidence in practice, creates many opportunities for academic research. Our analysis of data from a traditional Chinese fashion retailer allowed us to explore such issues rigorously. The results from our study indicate that while a showroom section on a fan page can increase customer engagement and purchases, it may also lead to negative outcomes such as unfollowing. We further found that the fan page showroom is more likely to have positive and less likely to have negative outcomes for customers who are willing to disclose private information to the retailer on social media. Integrating these results, we conclude that practitioners need to better understand the trade-offs of leveraging social media for sales purposes and adjust their strategies to account for customer privacy concerns accordingly.

## Matching with Alternative Technique

To assess the robustness of the results with respect to the matched samples, we utilized an alternative matching procedure. Specifically, we applied the coarsened exact matching (CEM) procedure (Iacus et al., 2012), which coarsens the observed covariates and performs an exact match on the coarsened data. This method is applicable when we do not know the correct functional model of how users are assigned to the treatment; therefore, it generates matches that have lower estimation errors compared to PSM (Iacus et al., 2011).<sup>30</sup>

In addition, we adopted a one-to-many matching procedure in which we calculated the propensity scores and assigned multiple control-group users to each of the treatment-group users within the caliper size. We found that the procedure provides balanced covariates and covers a larger proportion of the data sample (5214/19340 = 26.9%); the results based on DID are similar in terms of sign and magnitude to those obtained from the main matching method (Tables B1, B2, and B3). Overall, the results with these alternative matching techniques are very similar to those obtained from the previous main matching method.
