# B2B e-commerce supply chain integration and performance: A contingency fit perspective on the role of environment

## A B S T R A C T

Drawing upon contingency theory ‘‘fit’’ research in the IT and supply chain management literature, we applied the ‘‘fit’’ concept to the relationship between B2B e-commerce supply chain integration and performance. The results demonstrated that the effect of B2B supply chain integration on financial, market, and operational performance decreased as product turbulence and demand unpredictability jointly increased. Managerial implications include the conditions under which IT investments yield performance improvement and the need for firms to actively manage demand uncertainty.

## 1. Introduction

James Harmon, the executive responsible for North American supply chain management (SCM) at GE Industrial, whose annual budget approaches \$1.5 billion, recently said, ‘‘Information technology is my machinery.’’ His statement underlines the notion that advances in IT define the frontier of modern SCM. Part of the IT advance has resulted in B2B e-commerce and the cross-firm process integration it requires.

Point-of-sale technology at Home Depot outlets was connected directly to GE’s IT system and allowed customers to order and schedule delivery of GE, LG, and Whirlpool appliances. SCM allowed efficient, effective, reliable, and flexible systems—that is, performance improvement. However, the literature has not been able to prove a relationship between IT and performance (e.g., [1]). Explanations for this apparent paradox has included methodological problems [inappropriate measures], lag effects of IT impact, and theoretical shortcomings. Recently however, researchers have begun looking at a contingency theory concept of the ‘‘fit’’ of IT with the firm’s environment, structure, and strategy in predicting performance. The ‘‘fit’’ concept, which we adopted, provides a richer perspective and has the potential to explain why some studies have failed to identify a link between IT and performance. The contingency ‘‘fit’’ concept proposes that performance results from an interactive or moderating ‘‘match’’ between IT, strategy, and factors in the environment, highlighting the importance of understanding the nature of the relationships rather than merely the association among such factors.

## 2. Theoretical foundations and conceptual model

Contingency theory is a broad framework that encompasses the interrelations and ‘‘fit’’ among strategy, context and environment, and organizational structure in determining performance. Early researchers focused on the performance of organic versus mechanistic structures in predictable versus unpredictable environments. Context variables were subsequently considered; they included size, production technology and process regularity, and industry competitiveness and concentration. To the initial structure variables of decentralization, formalization, and specialization or complexity were added layers, span of control, and internal integration, and other variables including CEO personality traits and executive tenure. Strategy variables centered on diversification and strategic types. The IT literature adopted this framework by treating IT as an innovation and modeling IT adoption as a function of strategy, structure, context, and environment (e.g., [22,24]).

Table 1 summarizes research adopting contingency theory ‘‘fit’’ in examining the relationship of IT with performance. Moderators of the IT–performance relationship include organizational strategy [3], structure [21], size and industry competitiveness, industry [12], and size reductions [10]. Other researchers have examined process change [11], prior IT levels [23], and knowledge as moderators. The contingency theory ‘‘fit’’ approach dominated the studies in Table 1 because it offered a rich explanatory framework. With one exception, there is a lack of research on the moderating role of environmental variables; the exception [15] modeled technological turbulence and demand uncertainty. However, the environmental clusters were formed from eight variables, including technological turbulence, demand uncertainty, trust, and product criticality. An examination of the ‘‘average’’ situation faced by the firm across numerous environmental variables did not allow separation of the effect of these variables.

Two related streams relevant to our research are operations management and SCM, a key concept of which, derived from contingency theory, is that the supply chain strategy of a firm should follow from its environment. Table 2 presents two environmental profiles that depend on demand uncertainty, inability to predict future needs of the market for a product, and product turbulence, the fluctuation in product demand due to innovation or changes in customer preference. Thus, in the first column, demand is predictable and product turbulence is low. This is labeled ‘‘stable and predictable.’’ Firms in this environment typically deal with ‘‘functional’’ products; product life cycles are long and contribution margins, forecast errors, product variety per category, stockouts, and forced end-of-period, -cycle, or -season markdowns are low. This is contrasted against environments where demand is unpredictable and product turbulence is high: the ‘‘chaotic and unpredictable’’ environment. Products here are more innovative, product life cycles are short, and margins, product variety, forecast errors, stockouts, and markdowns are all higher than in the stable and predictable environment. Obviously, both demand uncertainty and product turbulence are continuous variables, but for theory building purposes, examining these extreme scenarios is instructive.

Two types of costs were important in our analysis:

\- Market mediation cost results from imbalances between supply and demand: (1) when supply exceeds demand, markdown costs increase; and (2) when demand exceeds supply, the cost of lost sales increases. In a stable and predictable environment, these are low, as the firm can predict demand in the short and long term.

\- Physical costs are associated with transporting, storing, handling, and manufacturing products.

The key to performance is to focus on physical cost—designing and implementing routine processes and a low cost network that ‘‘fits’’ the routine environment. However, in a chaotic and unpredictable environment, market mediation costs may dominate the cost structure of the firm. The key therefore is to design a flexible supply chain that responds rapidly to changing market requirements in terms of product features, competitor actions, and unpredictable demand [17]. Despite the popularity of the SCM strategic ‘‘fit’’ model, virtually no empirical research has been performed on it. Our research specifically examined whether B2B supply chain integration correlated with performance in both the chaotic and unpredictable environment versus a stable and predictable one.

A three-way interaction occurs between demand unpredictability, product turbulence, and B2B supply chain integration and was examined using a contingency theory ‘‘fit’’ perspective applied to IT and to SCM. This provides a basis for our conceptual framework as shown in Fig. 1. We justified the effectiveness of B2B supply chain integration on three dimensions of performance: (1) bottom line financial performance; (2) top line market performance; and (3) operational performance, such as on-time deliveries. The interaction effect was expected to inversely associate with each of the three dimensions of performance. The model also incorporated several control variables.

## 3. Hypotheses

## 3.1. The main effect of B2B supply chain integration on performance

B2B cross-firm process integration results in the development of higher-order capabilities, such as streamlined material, information, and financial flows across the supply chains [16]. It contributes to operational efficiencies through improved production and delivery schedules and reduces delays, resulting in ontime delivery, lower lead time, higher inventory turnover rates, fewer out-of-stock situations, lower production and shipment error rates, and improved customer service levels.

B2B supply chain integration should improve financial and market performance. Process integration and real-time information means that firms can reduce inventory and associated costs, including procurement, manufacturing, warehousing, customer service, and order management. Electronic links with trading partners can reduce labor costs, billing, and paperwork, and improve production and delivery schedule accuracy, contributing directly to the bottom-line [18]. The integration process should also affect the revenue or market performance [1]. A superior collaborative planning, forecasting, and replenishment capability, for instance, results in higher customer service levels and improved sales. Higher cash flows resulting from better financial performance may be channeled into improved production, diversification, or expansion, thus contributing to sales growth. Therefore, we hypothesized that:

## 3.2. The ‘‘Fit’’ of B2B with environment in predicting performance

The SCM strategic ‘‘fit’’ model states that efficiency is the appropriate performance optimizing strategy for a stable and predictable environment, while flexibility is appropriate in the chaotic and unpredictable environment. If B2B supply chain integration is more an efficiency than a flexibility mechanism, then it should be less effective in a chaotic and unpredictable environment than in a stable and predictable one—thus, the three-way interaction should be inverse. In contrast, if B2B supply chain integration is more of a flexibility mechanism than an efficiency mechanism, then B2B supply chain integration should be more effective in a chaotic and unpredictable environment than in a stable and predictable one.

We believed that the efficiency component of B2B supply chain integration should outweigh the flexibility component and thus that the three-way interaction should be inverse. Electronic connectivity to vendors increases the buyer’s financial leverage because the supply markets may be monitored at low cost, extra rent is constrained, and lower material prices are obtained. Flexibility, however, is reduced because the need to change procedures will increase switching cost. Furthermore, unpredictable demand and product turbulence suggest that there are unexpected shifts in channels of distribution, critical suppliers, and part specification. The length of time that processes surrounding suppliers, parts, finished goods, and customers are electronically integrated may be shorter in a chaotic environment than a stable one and this may provide insufficient time to recover investment (limiting financial performance) or for learning to occur (hampering operational performance). Undifferentiated, functional products are suited to electronic spot markets because the critical information required to complete a transaction is price-related. In contrast, the innovative products in a chaotic and unpredictable environment are more complex and thus the type of information required for exchange purposes not only includes price, but may also include highly technical data of a short-lived nature that may be less amenable to electronic transmission. B2B supply chain integration in this type of environment may lead to disappointing financial, market, and operational returns. In sum we posited:

H2. The three-way interaction among demand unpredictability, product turbulence, and B2B supply chain integration inversely associates with (a) financial performance, (b) market performance, and (c) operational performance.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Sample

Our hypotheses were tested by collecting and analyzing data employing a cross-sectional field survey having a large enough sample size. The survey population was a cross-section of U.S. manufacturing firms from a wide range of industries, marketing a variety of products. The Council of Supply Chain Management Professionals provided their manufacturers membership list (n = 2468). Because a firm may employ multiple members, this list was culled to 1372 firms by retaining only the member with the highest title level of a firm. Members were randomly selected from this frame and contacted by telephone to discover the best informant. If a potential respondent possessed sufficient knowledge to complete the survey and was willing to participate, they were faxed or e-mailed the survey. Respondents were strategic level managers who had a broad-based organizational perspective and were involved in or had knowledge of SCM and e-commerce process integration. They were expected to provide reasonable and reliable inputs on the operating environment and overall firm performance. In the end, 914 potential respondents, representing two-thirds of the sample frame, were contacted and, of these, 538 were sent a questionnaire.

The scale for B2B supply chain integration is original to this research. A literature review was undertaken for definitions and identification of potential items. A paper that defined the essence of supply chain management identified seven major business processes: identifying target markets, procurement, demand management, customer management, order fulfillment, manufacturing flow management, and product development [6]. A scale was developed asking respondents to evaluate the extent to which B2B e-commerce was used in each process to foster cross-firm process integration. B2B e-commerce was defined as EDI, Weband Internet-based applications. For clarity purposes, respondents were explicitly instructed to ignore e-mail. To improve face validity, the scale was sent to 10 industry experts who collectively suggested: (1) deletion of target market identification due to a lack of fit with B2B supply chain processes; (2) that manufacturing flow management be split into two items: materials inventory management and finished goods inventory management; and (3) that customer service management be split into two items, inbound and outbound information flows relative to customers. A pretest of the scale on a frame of 286 manufacturers with headquarters in the southwest U.S. (34 responses were obtained) provided a Cronbach’s a of 0.84. Appendix A shows the final version of the eightitem scale.

We conducted a principal components analysis on the variables and, using the eigenvalue greater than one criterion, found six dimensions: B2B supply chain integration; demand uncertainty; product turbulence; and financial, market, and operational performance. This structure was then subjected to a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using LISREL. The standardized residuals of one of the B2B supply chain integration items with items loading on other constructs were excessive and the item was therefore removed. The model $\chi ^ { 2 } = 2 8 7 \left( \mathrm { d f } = 1 7 6 \right)$ was significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 ) .$ This suggested an unacceptable fit as the observed and estimated matrices differed significantly. But this was not surprising as the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic tends to significance when the degrees of freedom are large. Given such, other measures of overall fit were utilized. The root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), a measure of the discrepancy between observed and estimated covariances adjusted for degrees of freedom, equaled 0.07, which is below the cutoff maximum of 0.08. The confirmatory fit index (CFI) was 0.95. This measure of overall fit compared the estimated model to a null model and values above 0.90 were considered acceptable. A third measure, the non-normed fit index, an overall measure of fit comparing the estimated to a null model, was acceptable (NNFI = 0.94) as it exceeded 0.90. The goodness of fit index (GFI = 0.85) and adjusted goodness of fit index (AGFI = 0.80) were both acceptable. The scale composite reliabilities and percentage of variance extracted respectively exceeded 0.60 and 0.50 (Table 4). The loadings all exceeded 0.40 and were significant at $p < 0 . 0 1$

## 5. Results

Three multiple regression models were examined. The dependent variables were financial, market, and operational performance. The independent variables were B2B supply chain integration and B2B supply chain integration  demand unpredictability product turbulence. The control variables were demand unpredictability, product turbulence, size, and the three two-way interactions formed from B2B supply chain integration, demand unpredictability, and product turbulence. Mean centering was undertaken prior to creating the interaction effects. Size was operationalized as the natural logarithm of the number of employees. We assessed multicollinearity by examining variance inflation factor scores. The largest score was 1.54, well below the upper acceptable bound of 10. The three regression models, as shown in Table 6, were significant (p < 0.05) and explained 14%, 11%, and 35% of the variance in financial, market, and operational performance, respectively.

Hypothesis 1 stated that B2B supply chain integration would associate positively with the three dimensions of performance. H1a (b = 0.26, t = 3.03, p < 0.01) and H1c (b = 0.38, t = 5.10, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ were supported—that is, the greater the level of B2B supply chain integration, the better the financial and operational performance. H1b (b = 0.04, t = 0.42) was not supported. B2B supply chain integration does not predict market performance.

Hypothesis 2 stated that B2B supply chain integration  demand unpredictability  product turbulence would associate inversely with performance. H2a (b = 0.29, t = 2.91, p < 0.01), H2b (b = 0.36, t = 0–3.63, p < 0.01), and H2c (b = 0.19, t = 2.23, p < 0.05) were supported. The three-way interaction effects are illustrated in Fig. 2. Using the unstandardized regression model parameter estimates, low and high values of B2B supply chain integration (with low and high defined as one standard deviation below and above the mean, respectively) were used to predict performance when each of demand unpredictability and product turbulence were low and high. For the sake of parsimony, we pooled the predicted values when demand uncertainty and product turbulence were both low or just when one was high. This curve in Fig. 2 is labeled ‘‘Stable and Predictable Environment & Moderate Environments Pooled.’’ When both of the environmental variables were high, the curve in Fig. 2 is labeled ‘‘Chaotic and Unpredictable Environment.’’ In the case of financial and operational performance, the curve was flatter when the environment was chaotic and in the case of market performance, the curve sloped downward. This compares to an upward slope for the stable and moderate pooled groups when predicting financial, market, and operational performance.

The two significant control effects were not expected in direction. The positive association of product turbulence with operational performance $( \beta = 0 . 2 5 ; t = 3 . 1 1 ; p < 0 . 0 1 )$ may be the result of a concomitant process change effect—that is, short product cycles may be associated with rapid process change, which is positive with respect to operational performance. The inverse B2B  demand unpredictability (b = 0.22; t = 2.61; p < 0.01) effect provided further evidence of an efficiency imperative. However, it was the only significant two-way environment  B2B supply chain integration interaction effect in the three models. This is important as it illustrates that the assessment of just demand unpredictability (and its interaction with B2B) and in particular of product turbulence (and its interaction with B2B), would have revealed limited if any interactions. This confirms the wisdom of modeling multiple environmental variables and examining interaction effects higher than those of the second order.

## 6. Discussion

Previous research on the contribution of IT to firm value has produced inconsistent results. Such an inability to demonstrate a strong relationship between IT implementation and increased firm performance has been termed a paradox. However, recent research has examined various theoretical frameworks (e.g., [13]) including contingency theory ‘‘fit’’ for richer explanations of the nature of the IT–performance relationship. Ignoring the contingencies in measuring the performance impact of IT by not incorporating related contextual and organizational strategy factors, may seriously underestimate the value of IT applications. Knowledge of this has been accumulating, with a few studies investigating the moderating impact of various factors on IT value. Nevertheless, there has been a lack of research on the unique role of environmental uncertainty, a critical context factor characterized by product turbulence and demand uncertainty. Extant studies have not addressed the issue from a broader perspective combining IS and SCM research streams. The importance of our study lay in its comprehensive investigation of contingency theory ‘‘fit’’ as it applied to both IT and SCM.

In our study, the ‘‘fit’’ concept of contingency theory was utilized from both the IT and from the SCM strategic ‘‘fit’’ model perspective. The latter specifically states that an efficient supply chain strategy is more appropriate in a stable, predictable environment than a chaotic, unpredictable environment. By implication, an efficient mechanism should be more efficient in a stable, predictable environment. We examined whether the environment moderated the effect of B2B supply chain integration on performance. The findings supported: greater B2B supply chain integration implied better financial and operational performance, however, the greater the demand unpredictability and product turbulence, the weaker the relationship of B2B supply chain integration with financial, market, and operational performance (from three-way interaction effects). Structured information refers to straightforward transactional communications including invoices and order status; this contrasts with semi-structured information, such as custom product specifications and price negotiation. From a performance perspective, the high switching costs and process specificity embedded within electronic processes suggest that repetitive procedures (part of which may include the transfer of structured information) under routine and predictable demand conditions are well suited to e-business. This is particularly the case of B2B supply chain integration technologies with functional abilities such as transaction processing, high volume routine-data storage and retrieval, supply chain collaboration, and real-time information sharing and visibility. When the environment is chaotic, such electronic systems become less effective due to procedural rigidity, shifting supplier and customer bases, and semi-structured complex information of a short-lived, non-repetitive nature.

Managers should understand that uncertainties in the environment have to be considered in their evaluation of payoff from IT deployment. Our findings suggested that investments in B2B when demand is unpredictable and products are turbulent might not lead to performance gains. The one exception is that operational performance may not occur when demand uncertainty is high. However, managers should be cautious about leveraging B2B integration mechanisms to a lesser extent in the face of high demand uncertainty. Scaling back on efficiency B2B IT may be counterproductive as it could enhance stability or consistency of operations, which implies better customer service and improved financial performance. The interplay of the efficiency IT with other complementary technologies has to be considered. The combination of efficiency and flexibility ITs under high environmental uncertainty may have a multiplicative effect on performance. An important implication for practice is that firms have to consider various strategic IT options under predictable versus unpredictable environments. To sustain better performance, firms should strategically leverage the efficiency and flexibility capabilities of B2B and other complementary IT mechanisms to varying extents to ‘‘fit’’ environmental predictability levels.

Managers should also understand the implications that follow from demand unpredictability. This may be masked by a lack of forecasting tools, exacerbated by make-to-stock strategies, or inflated by excessive trade promotions and end-of-period discounts. Demand uncertainty may be reduced through the application of greater knowledge (e.g., forecasting tools). At a specific consumer business, managers have indicated that a fivepoint reduction in forecast error leads to a \$20mn decrease in inventory cost. In addition, the adoption of assemble-to-order strategies (e.g., the Dell model) at the appropriate buffer point may also yield performance improvements. Assemble-to-order shifts the goal of forecasting from finished goods inventory planning toward capacity planning and the forecasting of materials and parts. Aggregate forecasts, which tend to be more accurate, reduce forecast error and hence apparent demand unpredictability. Under the condition of high product turbulence, managers should know that one of the returns from controlling demand uncertainty, which includes an assemble-to-order strategy and better forecasting tools, may be improved performance from the extant B2B supply chain integration system.

## 7. Conclusion

Although the relationship between IT and performance has been studied at length, use of the contingency theory ‘‘fit’’ concept has been limited. Our findings supported this methodology, suggesting that the relationship of B2B supply chain integration with financial, market, and operational performance weakens as demand uncertainty and product turbulence increase. These findings are consistent with the SCM strategic ‘‘fit’’ model and offer insight on the conditions under which investments in B2B will yield performance gains.

Our study provides evidence that researchers should examine the fit among strategy, environmental variables and performance when investigating the relationship between IT and performance. Apparently, cross-firm IT process integration via B2B supply chain integration was associated with positive financial and operational performance, but not with market performance.

We found that the full relationship between IT and performance needs models that include multiple environmental variables; the inclusion in our SCM strategic ‘‘fit’’ model of two commonly studied environmental variables showed that a three-way interaction among demand uncertainty, product turbulence, and B2B supply chain integration was inversely associated with financial, market, and operational performance. The more unpredictable and chaotic an environment, the weaker the relationship of B2B supply chain integration with performance, suggesting that the key to performance in chaotic and unpredictable environments is flexibility in cross-firm IT process integration—especially the ability of the supply chain to respond to market change. In contrast, in more stable and predictable environments, the key to performance is efficiency via control of routine supply chain IT processes, such as integrative product development, procurement and inventory management.
