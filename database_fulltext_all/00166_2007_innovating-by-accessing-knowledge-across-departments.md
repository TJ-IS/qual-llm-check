# Innovating by accessing knowledge across departments

Ixchel M. Faniel <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Ann Majchrzak <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Information, University of Michigan 1085 South University Ave. 303A West Hall Ann Arbor, MI 48109-1107, United States <sup>b</sup> Marshall School of Business University of Southern California, Information and Operations Department Bridge Hall 401 Los Angeles, CA 90089-0809, United States

Available online 23 October 2006

## Abstract

It is widely believed that individuals are more likely to be innovative if they are exposed to different perspectives and domains. While it is purported that knowledge management systems can help, they have generally failed to do so, because there are significant thought world barriers associated with the different perspectives and domains that individuals have to overcome. Using the theory of thought worlds as a theoretical lens, we examined how innovative engineers successfully accessed knowledge from other functional departments. We found that the way they accessed the knowledge had implications for designing knowledge management technology specifically focused on fostering innovation. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Knowledge management; Knowledge management technologies; Innovation

## 1. Introduction

Managers are under increasing pressure to ensure that their organizations sustain a competitive advantage. Employee innovation – the ideas for new products, processes, and services that employees generate, refine, and implement – is a major source of competitive advantage [4,11,14,16,17]. As such, how to support employees in their effort to be innovative during decision making and problem solving is a critical management question.

The prevailing line of research on innovation suggests that individuals are more likely to generate new ideas when exposed to knowledge (i.e., “justified true beliefs”) that is new or unique to the knowledge they already have [e.g. 11,12,14,15,17,18,28]. Such knowledge is more likely to stimulate individuals' ability to generate new ideas, because it helps break existing conceptual maps that limit creativity [e.g. 15,17]. Thus, ideally, a manager interested in supporting employees' innovativeness will focus on encouraging employees to expose themselves to knowledge from different perspectives and domains.

While exposure to unique knowledge is important to fostering innovation, it is difficult. Using cross-domain knowledge to solve problems requires individuals to identify potentially relevant knowledge that is outside of their area of expertise. As such, individuals have to be able to understand differences in language, determine ways to evaluate the credibility of the knowledge and its implications for problem solving, and have the ability to apply the knowledge to their unique problem solving context [19]. Thus, despite the importance of sharing knowledge across domains, difficulties associated with crossing these boundaries have been found repeatedly in research studies [e.g. 7,12,20,23,26,27].

Information technology has been offered as a possible solution to disseminating knowledge across domains [e.g. 1,19]. For example, a central repository housing knowledge from a variety of domains, could allow individuals to simply search a knowledge base for relevant knowledge. However, this type of approach has been criticized for not adequately addressing individuals' needs to interpret and adapt the knowledge they are accessing [13,22,23]. This is a criticism that becomes more apparent for individuals accessing knowledge outside of their area of expertise [8]. Additional research to determine knowledge management (KM) technologies that can support access to other domains of knowledge, and thereby foster employee innovation is needed [5,20,21]. Thus, in this paper, we examine how the difficulties associated with accessing knowledge from people in different domains can be overcome to encourage innovation. We focus on engineers who have successfully used knowledge from other domains to generate innovative solutions. Our goal was to develop an understanding of how these innovators accessed cross-domain knowledge. We then identified how this understanding might be used to inform the development of KM technologies to support such access.

## 2. Theory development

Dougherty [9] proposed the theory of thought worlds to explain why the integration of knowledge from manufacturing, marketing, and sales departments was so difficult during new product development. Her research led her to posit that people from the different departments developed different “thought worlds”. “A thought world is a community of persons engaged in a certain domain activity who have a shared understanding about that activity” [9, p. 182]. Dougherty [9] found that employees often attempted to innovate without accessing knowledge outside of their immediate domain. Even when given ready access to knowledge from other departments, these innovators only focused on how their area of expertise could be used to complete their project; their innovation suffered as a result.

Dougherty [9] suggests that people from different thought worlds do not take advantage of each other's knowledge because of differences in the skills and experiences they have built up over time. Given these differences, people often lack suitable familiarity with knowledge outside of their area of expertise to judge whether it is appropriate in the context of their current work [6,19,25]. In the absence of helpful cues that indicate the credibility, usefulness, or quality of knowledge from other domains, it is often ignored [9]. People from different thought worlds also make sense of situations differently [9]. This not only creates inconsistency among people's interpretations of the same situation. Conflicting opinions about what knowledge is relevant to a problem and how it can be applied may have to be resolved as well. Without support to surface the inconsistencies and resolve the conflicts, individuals become more likely to filter out knowledge from other domains, even though it may be critical to their innovation [7,9]. In short, research findings from Dougherty [9] and others [e.g. 18], suggest that accessing knowledge from other domains is not simply a function of factors such as organizational climate and encouragement — as found in other studies [e.g. 2]. It is also a function of the characteristics of the knowledge itself — its credibility, its interpretability, and its flexibility for being adapted to the problem at hand.

In our proposed study, we were interested in determining whether successful innovators were in fact soliciting knowledge from other thought worlds and encountering barriers that could be attributed to the knowledge being solicited. If so, we also wanted to determine how the innovators overcame the barriers and whether there were any implications for the design of KM technology. As an exploratory study, we developed eight case studies of how engineers accessed knowledge from other domains to innovatively solve a specific problem they faced.

## 3. Research methods

The eight case studies were developed from interviews with engineers employed to innovatively solve problems related to large and highly complex space systems for the federal government and its subcontractors. The majority (66%) of the scientific and technical staff in the organization holds advanced degrees in the engineering and science disciplines. The organization is structured so that knowledge domains are organized along departmental lines. Thus, in this organization, accessing knowledge from different domains is equivalent to accessing knowledge from other departments. Examples of different departments include: structures, control analysis, propulsion, and photonics technology.

Data collection proceeded in two phases. In the first phase, our intent was to confirm the following: innovative problem solving was a critical part of engineers' jobs, accessing knowledge from other domains was critical to this innovative process, mechanisms were available to engineers to access other domains of knowledge, and engineers were generally encouraged to use these mechanisms. We interviewed five members of the

KM senior management steering committee, observed six KM steering committee meetings over 6 months, reviewed documents related to the KM committee's work, and interviewed four division directors responsible for engineers engaged in innovative problem solving. During observations of the steering committee meetings, we listened to the conversations among steering committee members for evidence of innovative problem solving, the existence of different thought worlds across the different departments, and a business need and interest by management to bridge the differences. We also used this opportunity to learn about the various KM technologies available for use, including positive and negative experiences with them. Next, one-on-one semistructured interviews with members of the steering committee were used to validate our observations. Then we requested meetings with four division directors responsible for engineers engaged in innovative problem solving. These meetings were used to confirm the value of accessing knowledge from other domains and describe specific stories of how engineers crossed thought worlds.

We used the results of these meetings to develop an interview protocol. The protocol was specifically suited to the problems that the engineers in the organization faced and mechanisms that the engineers had available for accessing cross-domain knowledge. The protocol was developed to elicit stories from the engineers regarding how they “used knowledge from people in other domains to solve a problem in which they were looking for new ideas or approaches and found useful knowledge in a department other than their own”. The protocol also asked them to describe the knowledge they found and the factors that affected its successful use. Finally, they were asked to describe a story of when they had looked for knowledge to help them innovatively solve a problem, but hadn't found anything of value. We asked the four division directors to identify two engineers in their divisions that acquired knowledge from other departments to innovatively solve problems. The interviews with each engineer lasted from 45 min to 2 h. In total, we were able to solicit eight stories (Table 1).

## 3.1. Data analysis

For each story solicited from the engineers, a narrative was prepared. Verbatim quotes from the engineers were also included. The narrative was then coded to identify characteristics of the knowledge that helped the engineer access and use the knowledge. Through iterative coding, we identified three characteristics of the knowledge that affected the innovators' ability to do so. We then categorized the case study data into each of these three characteristics and noted if technology support was used.

## 4. Results

When accessing cross-domain knowledge, three characteristics of the knowledge that affected the ability of innovators to access and use it were: associating the knowledge with an expert source, presenting the knowledge at different levels of detail, and presenting the knowledge in a way that allowed it to be easily manipulated and adapted to the innovators' problem at hand.

## 4.1. Associating knowledge with the expert source

When accessing knowledge from other domains, the engineers distinguished between the content of the knowledge and the source of that knowledge. They often needed to engage in two complementary but separate search activities: they needed to find the right knowledge content as well as find the source behind the knowledge. Take Engineer 6 as an example. He needed to solve a satelliteprocessing problem. He realized that the knowledge content he needed concerned power supply, which was not his area of expertise. He described his search for this power supply knowledge.

After he located Bill and spoke with him extensively, he then located Bill's knowledge (i.e. his personal notes):

There were several mechanisms available to find both knowledge content and expert sources. These included electronic corporate directories (e.g. yellow pages), email, the intranet, and corporate repositories as well as people-to-people interactions such as telephone conversations, technical seminars, and briefings. Regardless of the mechanism, both the content and the expert source were sought. Engineer 5 explained her experience gathering three to four prior problem solutions to help her solve a problem:

I knew some other people in another department that support satellites and had used XML with telemetry and I emailed them, [performed] web searches, and had discussions with people. I gathered 3 to 4 examples and wrote down criteria of what a good solution would be based on what had been done previously.

In sum, the stories indicate that innovative engineers needed to access not just the content of the knowledge from other domains, but also the experts of that knowledge. Accessing the experts helped the engineers assess the credibility of knowledge that came from different thought worlds.

## 4.2. Presenting knowledge at different levels of detail

When accessing knowledge from other domains, the engineers' stories indicated that their ability to examine the knowledge at multiple levels of detail was important when trying to judge its relevance. Engineers spoke of the need to acquire a general overview of the knowledge as well as additional details (e.g. assumptions, constraints). We found that software models supported this need. Engineer 1 provided a description of one of the modeling tools.

[There are] ten different types of hardware with many different vendors [and their technical specs.

The model also contains] calculations from parameters into how the satellite behaves [when there are disturbances in space] which determines the kind of hardware [that is needed]. About half of our specialty [knowledge] is in the model and two-thirds to three-fourths of the hardware for orientation of the satellite [is in the model as well].

Similar to the software model described above, many models were designed to break domain knowledge down into components and component parameters, and then identify relationships among them. In so doing, the engineers were able to move between the details of specific relationships and high level summarized analyses.

As another example, Engineer 5 described the importance of having access to knowledge that provided details and overviews:

Our contractor has been looking for models […] that can be used for new sensors, but can't find anything immediately relevant. […] People have the right generic knowledge but not the specific knowledge. […] so general physics is there but application to our specific waveband is not there […], also the effects of winds, atmospheric chemistry [are not there, we need them to play together. [As a result the model may] need to be built [from scratch].

Similarly, Engineer 4 described the difficulty transferring knowledge from his space program to people in other space programs. From his perspective, the concept development studies provided many detailed specifics at the expense of broad overviews. In absence of the latter, he believed it was difficult for people outside of his area of expertise to easily identify the contributions the knowledge from the studies might make to different contexts.

I suppose I could foresee somebody doing that [reusing knowledge from the prior concept development models or studies]. The problem is that the output [from the studies] is so mission specific or program specific that they don't really [seem to] have any use beyond that one space program.

We also found giving too much detailed knowledge without an overview created apprehension. Engineer 3 explained why he rarely gave outsiders access to his full software model:

I don't normally make [the full model] available [to people outside of my field]. Since [they] are not interested in [all of] the detail, [the models] might be used in the wrong ways. I occasionally give them a limited set of the model [but I am] very apprehensive about giving up the full model, since [they usually] need a person [that has used it a lot] sitting there for it to make sense.

In short, the findings suggest that engineers are more likely to use knowledge from other domains when the knowledge is offered at both a summary and a detailed level. It is believed that the multiple levels help engineers gain a better understanding of the knowledge so that they can determine the contributions it might have to their problem at hand.

## 4.3. Presenting knowledge in a way that encourages adaptation

When accessing knowledge from other domains, the stories indicated that the innovative engineers needed to be able to adapt the knowledge they accessed. However, the engineers were also conscious of the need to maintain the integrity of the knowledge that they were adapting from the different thought worlds. Take Engineer 3 as an example. In order to maintain the integrity of the knowledge from different thought worlds, he and his colleagues argued it out, so that they could rigorously adapt the different perspectives to generate an integrated solution that encompassed expertise in propulsion, orbit and mechanics, and altitude control.

[There was an] on orbit failure problem with ten hours to come up with what to do. A launch vehicle was supposed to take a satellite into geosynchronous orbit, but the vehicle didn't make it. Everybody looked to me the satellite propulsion [expert], but there are others […]. I contacted others in orbit and mechanics and altitude control. Everybody had different [modeling] tools in conflict. I had them argue it out [to come up with a solution that met all our needs].

Many of the software models were also designed to flexibly adapt to current problem solving situations, while maintaining the soundness of the knowledge (i.e. the general principles on which the knowledge was built). For example, the rigor of the calculations underlying how the thrusters and propellant options related to one another was retained, even though the options to model different relationships among these components was highly adaptable. According to Employee 3, this was the best aspect of the model.

I created the substance [of the model. However] whenever someone wants to change it [to fit his needs] he does. [With the model I use] everything is connected [so I can model] different options. […] what's spectacular about [the model] is the layout, [I can model] six different thrusters, with different propellant [options]. The model allows me to do a bunch more calculations [based on my problem solving needs].

In a similar vein, there was a need to support ongoing changes to the models. Engineer 1 highlighted the importance of making changes that improved the accuracy of output.

I do [ongoing] updates to increase the accuracy of [the modeling] tool [for example changes I made improved accuracy] from 60% to 80%.

In sum, engineers who successfully innovated with knowledge from other domains had to be presented with knowledge from that domain in a way that allowed them to adapt it – by updating it, by changing it to try out different solutions, or by overlaying it onto other domain knowledge, making the conflicts more apparent and thus more readily open to resolution.

## 5. Discussion

There are several limitations of this exploratory research study. Most importantly, the small sample size and the lack of triangulated measurement for the engineers' stories clearly indicate a need for replication. The division directors identified engineers who innovated by reusing knowledge from other domains and generally corroborated the engineers' stories. However, it still may be the case that the details of the stories were biased by inaccurate recall. Collecting our stories from a single site allowed us to control for the availability of technology resources and the basic nature of how the work was performed. Nevertheless, collecting the data at one site, from employees who successfully reused knowledge from other departments, also imposed limitations. Most notably, the replicability and generalizability of this research was compromised. Moreover, the lack of a control group means that we cannot rule out alternative explanations. For example, it is possible that the engineers in this study only accessed knowledge from people they knew. In other words, their ability to cross thought worlds might not have been a reflection of the characteristics of the knowledge, but rather whom they knew. It might also be that engineers from the different departments might have shared some common ground, since they all belonged to the same organization. If this is the case, overcoming the barriers across knowledge domains in this sample might have had more to do with the common engineering background than the characteristics of knowledge itself. Therefore, future research is needed to replicate these findings on a larger sample across organizations.

Dougherty's [9] theory of thought worlds and our findings suggest the possibility of three principles that may help innovators access knowledge from other thought worlds. First, coupling knowledge with useful cues that indicate its quality and credibility might help to overcome the barrier associated how to go about evaluating knowledge from different domains. Second, being able to examine knowledge from different domains at both summarized and detailed levels might help innovators better interpret the knowledge and determine if the initial impressions they had about it hold true upon a more rigorous examination. Finally, providing innovators the means to “try out” the new knowledge by quickly adapting it to their problem context to see if it would provide new ideas for their problem solving might help to overcome the barrier of not being able to quickly assess the relevance of knowledge from other domains.

In other words, the findings from this exploratory study suggest that characteristics of knowledge affect the degree to which knowledge from other domains will be used in innovative problem solving. Repeatedly, the use of unique knowledge from other domains has been shown to affect innovation. Therefore, identifying ways that facilitate this kind of knowledge transfer is critical. We found three characteristics that affected cross-domain knowledge transfer. First, we found that successful innovators traversed the different thought worlds by associating the knowledge with a specific expert. For individuals accessing knowledge from other thought worlds, the identification of the knowledge experts may serve as both a cue to the quality of knowledge via source credibility, and a point of contact for help to use the knowledge. These findings are in line with more recent research, which suggests that assessing the quality of knowledge is a critical first step to knowledge integration, but may have to be followed by help from an expert to ensure that the knowledge is being properly integrated into the current problem solving context [e.g. 6,18]. We also found that KM technology can help identify possible sources, a finding that has been demonstrated in previous research on the value of corporate directories [e.g. 3,13,24]. However, unlike the research on corporate directories, this study found that it was important to not only identify experts, but also access their expertise.

When it comes to accessing expertise from other departments, we found that accessing the knowledge in summarized and detailed forms helped innovators make sense of knowledge from another domain. The multiple levels of knowledge detail appear to have enhanced understanding and identification of potential knowledge contributions. We also found that the engineers were better apt to traverse knowledge that was different than their own when they could make changes to the knowledge to suit their current problem solving needs. In both instances, the possibility of supportive KM technologies was demonstrated.

Rather than find that it was problematic to use KM technologies for the dissemination of cross-domain knowledge, our exploratory research findings suggest that there are many ways in which KM technologies can support cross-domain knowledge transfer in a way that facilitates innovative problem solving. Associations between knowledge and the expert source imply that individuals should author their own knowledge. This provides people accessing knowledge from other thought worlds with a means to assess the credibility of the knowledge and a point of contact for consultation if needed. Similarly, Boland et al. [5] suggest that knowledge from different areas of expertise might be better appreciated if it has clear ownership. Thus, instead of anonymous comments being associated with a document, the owner of those comments would need to be identified. Ownership also enables the preservation of each individual's unique point of view, which yields a multiplicity of perspectives [5]. Multiplicity implies that KM technology design not place undue restrictions on the expression of knowledge, but rather support multiple, conflicting points of view. Individuals using KM technologies to capture their knowledge should not have to adhere to strict content, formatting, or space allocations that would cause them to alter how they would otherwise convey their knowledge [e.g. 10]. Moreover, individuals should not be encouraged by third parties to clean, edit, and/or repackage their knowledge to remove biases in order to facilitate the transfer of the knowledge to people in other areas of expertise [e.g. 19]. Instead, KM technology should support the multiple ways in which individuals express their knowledge through content or media.

In this study, accessing the different levels of detail in the different domains of knowledge helped individuals understand how and why different problem solutions came to be. Boland and his colleagues [5] call this easy travel among layers of knowledge. Based on our findings, we suggest that KM technology be designed to support a break down of knowledge into components and component parameters and the creation of relationships among them. Our findings indicated that this would allow individuals greater flexibly to move among different levels of knowledge detail as they saw fit, based on their current needs. As such, the individuals would be better able to identify and appreciate the potential contributions knowledge might make.

Lastly, our framework suggests that during problem solving, individuals will be more open to knowledge from other domains when they believe they are free to change it. Boland et al. [5] suggest that individuals need two specific characteristics of adaptability. The first is indeterminacy, or the general understanding across thought worlds that the knowledge is incomplete and fuzzy. Our findings imply that KM technology should be designed to provide suggested alternative problem solutions not one tried and true approach. However, in so doing, individuals must then take additional steps to compare, contrast, and integrate alternatives. It may be possible to off-load some of this work onto KM technology that provides side-by-side comparisons of alternatives or automatically highlights similarities, differences, and trade-offs [5]. The second is emergence, meaning that it is understood that the knowledge has (and will) change over time and context. If individuals are enabled to freely interpret and change the knowledge, KM technologies should provide a means for individuals to capture and track changes to the knowledge as it evolves. Capturing and tracking changes to problem solutions might be aided by KM technologies that allow individuals to link their new problem solution to the existing problem solutions that were used to derive it. Email notifications that alert individuals to knowledge changes might also be useful.

Managers wanting to influence employee innovation are faced with the challenge of employee resistance to accessing cross-domain knowledge during problem solving. Findings from this research study suggest that KM technologies might help. More specifically, the research implies that managers should consider the extent to which their current portfolio of KM technologies supports access to cross-domain knowledge that can be associated with the expert source as well as cross-domain knowledge that can be presented at different levels of detail and in a way that encourages adaptation. For example, when it comes to using KM technology to access knowledge from other domains, the benefits of authorship may far outweigh the benefits of anonymity. It may also be more useful if KM technology is not designed to reduce information overload, but rather designed to better manage the relationships that result from capturing knowledge at various levels of detail. Our findings also suggest that removing the inconsistencies found in knowledge may not be the best option when designing KM technologies. It may be more helpful if KM technology was designed to retain the inconsistencies and provide a means to facilitate the identification and resolution of them. In addition, it may be that KM technology that supports simple maintenance procedures may not be enough. Implementing KM technologies that support automatic, personalized alerts to knowledge change that result from its ongoing evolution may be more beneficial.

## 6. Conclusion

This exploratory research extends the information system literature, specifically its examination of the design, use, and success of KM technologies for innovative problem solving. Building on the theory of thought worlds, our findings suggest that the barriers to sharing knowledge across domains can be overcome by providing access to certain characteristics of knowledge. Moreover, our findings suggest that supportive KM technology designs are possible. These results hold promise, because they suggest that KM technologies have a place in fostering innovation, a place that has been primarily reserved for personal networks and communication.
