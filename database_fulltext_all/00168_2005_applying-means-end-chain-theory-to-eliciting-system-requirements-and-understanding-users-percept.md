# Applying means-end chain theory to eliciting system requirements and understanding users perceptual orientations

Chao-Min Chiu<sup>\*</sup>

Department of Information Management, National Central University, No. 300, Jungda Rd, Jhongli City, Taoyuan, Taiwan 320, R.O.C.

Received 20 May 2002; received in revised form 6 January 2003; accepted 21 February 2004 Available online 7 June 2004

## Abstract

This paper discusses how the use of a means-end approach in eliciting user requirements for a system results in a better understanding of the user’s perceptual orientation toward the Web-based document management system under design. Our findings imply that, from an overall perceptive, respondents are most concerned with the sense of being comfortable with the system, while respondents are more aware of the need for a security mechanism. However, none sought a sense of accomplishment or of belonging, self-respect, self-fulfillment, excitement, fun or enjoyment of the system. I provide a model that fuses the attribute–consequence–value (A–C–V) model and technology acceptance model (TAM). This model posits that factors at the consequence level lead to other factors at the value level, which in turn lead to behavioral intention to use the system. The model explains how attribute, consequence, and value factors ultimately lead to system use. <sup>#</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Means-end chain; Perceptual orientations; System requirements; Technology acceptance model

## 1. Introduction

User participation in systems development has been considered to be an important factor in achieving system success. Thus, in requirement analysis it provides a more accurate and complete definition of the information requirements [26]. User requirement analysis occurs at the early stage of system development process, and determining correct and complete information requirements sets the stage for an effective development process that increases the likelihood of success in the implementation and allows for early correction of errors while the cost is lower [9].

Requirements analysis is where system analysts interact with end users to identify and specify material needed to develop an IS [2]. This paper concentrates on the determination of needs. Various techniques exist, including interviewing, using questionnaires, observing users, analyzing documents, prototyping, joint application design (JAD), and others. But these are not concerned with how users perceive the benefits produced by the system attributes (features), and what personal values the benefits reinforce.

Means-end chain theory is a methodology to identify the linkages between the attributes that exist in products, the consequences to the consumers provided by the attributes, and the personal values that the consequences reinforce [25]. It has been successfully applied to new product development, brand positioning, advertising strategy development, etc. [30]. However, means-end chain theory has not been applied to IS development. This research demonstrated the value in applying this approach to eliciting user requirements and understanding how users perceive the benefits to users. The sample system is Webbased and used for editing, transmitting, and managing organizational documents: i.e., a Web-based document management system.

To meet the challenge of developing systems that satisfy various user requirements, system developers need to achieve a better understanding of factors that lead to system usage [13]. Values are believed to be centrally held cognitive elements that stimulate motivation for behavioral response (e.g., brand choice and product usage) [12,16,17,20,22,29]. Based on this, the proposed model fuses the attribute–consequence– value (A–C–V) model and the technology acceptance model (TAM), into an A–C–V–I (intention)–U (use) model. TAM assumed that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are fundamental determinants of user acceptance of information technology [3]. However, the A–C–V–I–U model posits that factors at the consequence level lead to factors at the value level, which in turn lead to behavioral intention to use the system. The A–C–V–I–U model allows us to understand the attribute, consequence and value factors that ultimately lead to system usage.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Means-end chain theory

The means-end chain theory involves people’s cognitive structures of purchasing behavior. A means-end chain model [8] results from the linkages between product attributes, consumption consequences or benefits produced by the product, and personal values.

Attributes are features or aspects of products or services. They can be physical, such as color, or abstract, such as quality. Consequences (functional or psychosocial) accrue to people from consuming products or services. Functional consequences accrue directly from consuming the product. Psychological consequences reflect the personal and social outcomes of product use. Rokeach [27] defined values as ‘‘an enduring belief that a specific mode of conduct or endstate of existence is personally or socially preferable to an opposite or converse mode of conduct.’’ He classifies values into: terminal and instrumental. Terminal ones are concerned with preferred end-states (goals), for example, happiness, while instrumental ones are related to the ways of behaving to obtain goals, such as broadmindness. Kahle [15] proposed a list of value (LOV) inventory that contains nine relevant to consumer behavior: self-fulfillment, excitement, being well respected, sense of accomplishment, self-respect, sense of belonging, security, fun and enjoyment, and warm relationships with others.

Means-end data can be gathered through a probing process, termed laddering, which is an in-depth, oneon-one interviewing technique for eliciting A–C–V linkages from consumers. Means-end chain theory has been successfully applied to new product development, advertising strategy development, etc.

The approach consists of laddering interviews and analysis. The interviews consist of two steps: eliciting distinctionsandgettingtheladders[23].Ladderingstarts withaskingindividualrespondenttomakeperceivedand meaningful bipolar differences between brands of products. The respondent is then asked which pole of the distinction is preferred. The preferred one serves as the basis for the probing process. Several methods exist, including: (1) top of mind image, (2) grouping similar brands, (3) contextual environment, (4) preference, usage, and preference–usage differences, (5) timing of purchasingorconsumption,(6)usagetrends,(7)product orbrandsubstitution,and(8)alternativeusageoccasions [24]. Theinterviewercontinuesto use a series ofdirected probes to move the respondent upa ladder of abstraction, uncovering salient attributes, consequences, and values together with their linkages. The probing questions are similar to ‘‘Why is that important to you?’’

The analysis methodology consists of three steps: analyzing contents, constructing an implication matrix, and constructing an aggregate hierarchical value map (HVM). The content analysis attempts to analyze all elements (attributes, consequences, and values) elicited by the laddering procedures. It starts by recording the entire set of ladders across respondents. They refer to individual respondents’ A-C-V linkages. The next step is to classify all responses into A-C-V levels and then break down all responses into individual summary codes. Once the master codes (Table 3) are finalized, numbers are assigned to each.

These are used to score each element in each ladder, producing a matrix with rows representing an individual respondent’s ladder. This matrix, then, serves as the basis for constructing an implication matrix, which aggregates the number of direct and indirect linkages between elements across all respondents.

An HVM is gradually built up by connecting all the chains that are formed by selecting the linkages whose values in the implication matrix are at or above the cutoff value (e.g., 4 for 50 respondents). Chains are the A-C-V linkages that emerge from the aggregate implication matrix. A guideline for constructing the HVM is to attempt to avoid crossing lines. An HVM is a tree diagram that indicates the aggregate associations across three levels of abstraction. Hierarchical value maps graphically depict a summary of information derived in the laddering interviews, thereby increasing marketers’ and market researchers’ comprehension of the dominant perceptual orientation of a group of consumers toward the product and the current market-place environment. They offer valuable information that serves as the development and specification of advertising strategy alternatives.

Means-end chain theory has been successfully used to assess users’ preferences and choices over a wide range of products and services. Valette-Florence [28] applied the means-end chain approach to the study of perfume purchases. Reynolds and Craddock applied the means-end chain theory to the strategy development in the overnight delivery service (ODS) market. ## 2.2. Requirement determination techniques

Methods for determining system requirements can be classified into [19]:

\- Traditional ones, which include interviews, questionnaires, direct observation and analysis of documents [10].

\- Prototyping is an evolutionary design methodology. It has been used for elicitation where the uncertainty of user requirements is high, needs are incomplete or contradictory [14], user requirements change significantly [5], etc.

\- Group knowledge elicitation methods attempts to collect a richer understanding of requirements and foster consensus through group dynamics.

\- Model-driven techniques provide a specific model of the type of information to be gathered and use it to drive the elicitation process.

\- Cognitive techniques were originally developed for knowledge acquisition, including protocol analysis, laddering, and repertory grid.

\- Structured interview techniques aim to gather information associated with business processes.

## 2.3. Technology acceptance model

The technology acceptance model [4] was founded on Ajzen and Fishbein’s theory of reasoned action [1]. Behavioral intention to use is a measure of the likelihood a person will use the system. It is jointly determined by attitude and subjective norms. The attitude is, in turn, a function of belief. The TAM model has been widely used to predict the acceptance and use of information technology. Factors that play an important role in explaining behavioral intention to use IT or actual usage include playfulness [18], perceived risk [21], trust [7], etc.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Sample

My research used focus group to elicit all attribute, consequence, and value elements. The group consists of 32 participants of two types: staff and part-time MIS graduate students in the same university. The staff has experience in editing, transmitting, and managing documents, and some in browsing information and using Web-based systems. The part-time MIS graduate students have more knowledge about the Web technology than the staff. Each group consisted of four staff and four graduate students. To gather data for constructing aggregate association weight matrices, we distributed 117 questionnaires to the MBA students. Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the respondents.

## 3.2. Procedure

Data collecting procedure consisted of two major activities: focus groups and the survey. The focus groups lasted for over a 1-month period, one group per week. Analysis of the group discussion contents came from a set of attributes, consequences, and values, which served as the basis for developing the attribute–consequence (AC) and consequence–value (CV) association matrix questionnaires. The survey questionnaires were administered to subjects 3 months later to assess their opinion of the system attributes, consequences, and values. ## 3.3. Getting the ladders

Our approach used focus group to elicit user requirements for the system attributes, which included user interface design, system functionality, system environment, etc. There were four focus groups with eight participants per group. We used four focus groups because we found that the fourth focus group added no new information and we thought that we knew everything that participants had to contribute. Vriens and Hofstede had indicated that about 30 respondents suffice to identify the possible set of attributes and benefits. All groups worked at one location over a 1-month period. All meetings were recorded and videotaped. Each group session lasted 1.5 h, and all were held over dinner. We expected to elicit system attributes that could be implemented using current Web technology through full communication and discussion between staffs and graduate students. Each group was started by asking participants to share and discuss their experience in using applications to edit, transmit, and manage documents. They were then asked which ways were preferred by them in processing documents. The moderator continued to use a series of directed probes to move the participants up the ladder of abstraction, thereby uncovering salient attributes and consequences together with their linkages. The probing questions were of a form similar to ‘‘Why is that important to you?’’ Instead of deriving values from participant’s responses to the questions, we asked participants to link consequences to existing value inventory; i.e. list of values. As for the response which value was not in the LOV, participants could suggest a value with assistance from the moderator.

## 3.4. Analyzing contents

The content analysis attempted to analyze all elements elicited by the laddering procedures. It started by recording the entire set of ladders across all respondents on a separate coding form. The next step was to classify all responses into A–C–V levels, resulting in a summary content codes table (Table 3). The results indicated that no respondent sought a sense of accomplishment, a sense of belonging, self-respect, self-fulfillment, excitement, fun, or enjoyment toward the sample system.

## 3.5. Constructing association matrices

In the first, the attributes were listed in the columns and the consequences in the rows, resulting in a table of all combinations of attributes and consequences. Each column also contained an importance factor that allowed respondents to indicate the perceived importance of each attribute, with value ranging from 1 to 10. Similarly, the CV association matrix listed consequences and values. Hofstede et al. [11] defined this type of matrix as the association pattern matrix. ## 3.6. Constructing aggregate association weight matrices

The process of constructing aggregate association weight matrices was started by asking respondents to fill out a questionnaire that contained the AC and CV association matrices. Each respondent went through each column of the association matrices to indicate the association strengths and to indicate the perceived importance of each attribute, using a 10-point scale ranging from (1) ‘‘not associated’’ to (10) ‘‘strongly associated.’’

Data were requested from 117 part-time MBA students. Out of these, 93 were completed and returned, representing a response rate of 79%. The exclusion of responses from incomplete questionnaires resulted in a final sample of 84 participants.

We then used the data to construct aggregate AC and CV association weight matrices (Tables 4 and 5). The association weight was calculated by dividing average association rating by 10, whereas the average association rating was calculated by adding all respondents’ rating for each cell in the association matrices and dividing by the number of respondents. The algorithm for calculating importance weight was the same as that of calculating association weight. For example, the formula for calculating the association weight and importance weight of each cell in the aggregate AC association weight matrix was

## 4. Conclusions, implications, and limitations

When developing a large and sophisticated IS, system analysts and developers would like to gather correct and complete information requirements, understand factors that ultimately lead to system usage, detect errors, and take corrective action early, and thus increase the likelihood of success in the implementation of the IS.

Many techniques exist for eliciting user requirements. The techniques, however, generally deal with determination of functional and interface features. Understanding users’ cognitive structure and factors that ultimately lead to system usage are relatively unexplored in system development research. The means-end chain approach has not been used to understand users’ perceptual orientations toward a new IS. A major contribution was to demonstrate the usefulness of this approach in eliciting user requirements for attributes of a system under development, a Webbased document management system.

Another contribution was that we adapt the attribute–consequence–value model to the domain of behavioral intention to use an IS, resulting in the A–C–V–I–U model which is an extension to the TAM. Although our model provided meaningful implications for Web-based document editing, transmitting, and management systems, we could not assess its accuracy. The research focused on applying meansend chain approach to eliciting system attributes and understanding respondents’ perceptual orientations toward the sample system.

Our method was adapted from the means-end chain approach usually used to elicit attribute–consequence–value network from consumers under selfdetermined purchase. Values are beliefs that emerge from both society’s norms and the individual’s core psychological needs and sense of self [6]. Individual values and value system might be suppressed in the context of involuntary usage. Our method, therefore, may be more suitable for voluntary than involuntary usage, since more individual values will emerge and thus can be elicited in the context of voluntary usage. Our method differed from the means-end chain approach since it collected data through questionnaires that requested subjects to indicate perceived importance of each attribute and strength of association (association weight) between elements at different levels. It is also different from administering survey questionnaires to explore relationships among factors or variables by applying statistical techniques. Our method, therefore, is suitable for collecting element associations indicated directly by users rather than by applying statistical techniques.

Although the findings are encouraging and useful, the study has certain limitations. First, the participants were all recruited from a university. However, the survey subjects (part-time MBA students) were from various organizations in several cities. Second, we only considered linkages between concepts at adjoining levels, i.e., the AC and CV linkages. Adding CC and VV matrices may have alleviated this limitation. However, extra matrices would increase respondent burden substantially and the benefit may be questioned. Finally, our analysis was restricted to a single system category.
