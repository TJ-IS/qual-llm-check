# Using organizational influence processes to overcome IS implementation barriers: lessons from a longitudinal case study of SPI implementation

Ojelanki Ngwenyama<sup>1,2</sup> and Peter Axel Nielsen<sup>3,4</sup>

## Abstract

A fundamental tenet of the information systems (IS) discipline holds that: (a) a lack of formal power and influence over the organization targeted for change, (b) weak support from top management, and (c) organizational memories of prior failures are barriers to implementation success. Our research, informed by organization influence, compellingly illustrates that such conditions do not necessarily doom a project to failure. In this paper, we present an analysis of how an IS implementation team designed and enacted a coordinated strategy of organizational influence to achieve implementation success despite these barriers. Our empirical analysis also found that technology implementation and change is largely an organizational influence process (OIP), and thus technical-rational approaches alone are inadequate for achieving success. Our findings offer managers important insights into how they can design and enact OIPs to effectively manage IS implementation. Further, we show how the theory of organizational influence can enhance understanding of IS implementation dynamics and advance the development of a theory of effective IS change agentry. ## Introduction

For decades, the information systems (IS) discipline has held that weak or absent top-management support is a barrier to successful IS implementation and change projects (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1978; Markus, 1983; Markus & Benjamin, 1996; Thong et al, 1996; Sabherwal et al, 2006). The central argument posits that implementing new information technologies in organizations is a complex and challenging undertaking that radically changes social structures, culture and processes, and the behaviour of actors, and it thus requires top management’s sponsorship and considerable influence to succeed (Abrahamsson, 2001; Bo¨rjesson & Mathiassen, 2004; Sabherwal et al, 2006). According to Markus & Benjamin (1996), the following three conditions are ‘structurally incompatible’ with IS implementation success: (1) the absence of managerial authority over the organization targeted for change; (2) negative perceptions of the change agents by organization members; and (3) weak top-management support for the IS change initiative. However, a recent empirical study by

Ngwenyama & Nørbjerg (2010) found that the strategic use of alliances can help overcome these conditions and achieve implementation success. While alliances are a type of organizational influence process (OIP), Ngwenyama & Nørbjerg (2010) did not investigate if or how other types of OIPs might aid IS implementation success under conditions structurally incompatible with success. In this paper, we investigate how organization influence theory can: (1) improve our understanding of how OIPs can contribute to successful IS implementation, and (2) help to build a theory of change agency for challenging IS implementation situations. We use empirical observations of the software process improvement (SPI) implementation and a theory of organizational influence to explain how the change agents designed and enacted a coordinated influence strategy to overcome conditions that were structurally incompatible with success.

SPI is a boundary-spanning organizational change approach for improving both a software organization’s performance and the quality of its software products (Humphrey, 1989, 1992; Aaen et al, 2001; Mathiassen et al, 2002; Hansen et al, 2004; Mustonen-Ollila & Lyytinen, 2004). SPI implementations typically have long time horizons, are large in scale and complex in scope, and affect many different stakeholders and organizational departments (Aaen et al, 2001; Mathiassen et al, 2002; Ngwenyama & Nielsen, 2003; Wangenheim et al, 2006). Studies have shown that the failure rate for SPI projects is very high (Ngwenyama & Nielsen, 2003). Some researchers have argued that to mitigate failure, SPI change agents should be organized as a separate group or department to consolidate SPI knowledge competencies, methods, and practices (Humphrey, 1989; McFeeley, 1996). However, such an organizational arrangement has disadvantages. Often, the SPI group is not high enough in the organizational hierarchy, and thus lacks formal power and influence over departments targeted for change (Cohen & Bradford, 2003; Porter et al, 2003a, b) and experiences weak support from top management. SPI groups can also suffer the burden of organizational memories of past implementation failures (Mathiassen et al, 2002). Finally, stakeholders, software developers, and project managers might support or resist the SPI group’s implementation activities depending on how they perceive its value (Abrahamsson, 2001; Nielsen & Nørbjerg, 2001). Other researchers have also argued that such conditions are structurally incompatible with implementation success (cf. Ein-Dor & Segev, 1978; Markus, 1983; Markus & Benjamin, 1996; Sabherwal et al, 2006). Regardless of how SPI activities are structured in an organization, the received wisdom of the IS discipline posits that strong top-management support is necessary for overcoming these implementation obstacles (Markus, 1983; Markus & Benjamin, 1996; Thong et al, 1996; Dyba˚, 2005; Sabherwal et al, 2006). However, there is paucity of empirical research on how top-management support and commitment develops (Abrahamsson, 2001). Further, there has been little empirical research on how OIPs can be deployed to unblock barriers to IS implementation (Iversen et al, 1999; Nielsen & Ngwenyama, 2002; Ngwenyama & Nørbjerg, 2010). To address this gap, we interrogated the design and deployment of a coordinated organizational influence strategy aimed at overcoming SPI implementation barriers in a software company.

## Organizational influencing theory

Organizational influence theory is one of a body of theories focused on analysing political behaviour in organizations. Although some researchers view political behaviour in organizations as negative and dysfunctional (Mintzberg, 1983; Stone, 1997; Zaleznik, 1997), others view it as central to organizational life (Kakabadse & Parker, 1984; Frost & Egri, 1991; Pfeffer, 1992). This research takes the latter view, positing political behaviour as a central feature of IS implementation that must be investigated and theorized about (Markus, 1983; Hart & Saunders, 1997; Buchanan & Badham, 1999; Jiang et al, 2000; Lee & Myers, 2004; Avgerou & McGrath, 2007). Space does not allow a review of the range of organizational politics theories, so we will outline the central concepts of organizational influence theory that inform this study.<sup>1</sup> From the change agent’s viewpoint, IS implementation is an organizational situation conditioned by conflict and resistance (Meissonier & Houze´, 2010), and change agents must harness and deploy any means required to influence and transform resistance in order to achieve the desired outcome. According to Drory & Romm (1990), these are definitive conditions for political behaviour. We adopt organizational influence theory here because it provides a framework and set of concepts that enabled us to empirically analyse power dependence, resource constraints, and the characteristics of OIPs (Kotter, 2003; Porter et al, 2003a).

## Organizational dependency and influence

The capacity of one organizational actor or group to influence another depends on both their position in the organizational hierarchy and on resources and organizational conditions (Brass, 1984; Ansari & Kapoor, 1987; Cummings & Cross, 2003; Kotter, 2003). Factors such as expertise, group affiliation, and charisma are also important sources of individual influence (Friedkin & Johnsen, 1999; Shea & Howell, 1999; Cohen & Bradford, 2003; Oke et al, 2009). OIPs are networks of social actions enacted by an individual or group to influence the behaviour of another individual or group and thereby achieve an objective. When organizational actors have no authority over individuals on whom they depend to achieve their goals, success depends on their ability to enact effective influence processes (Brass, 1984; Drake & Moberg, 1986; Cohen & Bradford, 2003; Kipnis, 2003). Lack of power and authority over the target of change can result in an intricate web of dependencies in which social influence is integral to success (Krackhardt & Hanson, 1993; Kotter, 2003; Potter et al 2003a; Klein et al 2004). To illustrate the salient features of organizational dependencies in IS implementation, take the example of Stuart, an IS project manager. Stuart is dependent on a departmental manager, Jane, for access or resources to complete an IS change activity in Jane’s domain of authority. If the change activity is in Jane’s interest, Stuart’s negotiating position is strong. But, if not, or if they work in different departments at similar levels in the organizational hierarchy, then the task of convincing Jane is more complex. To improve his odds of success, Stuart might depend on the influence of a higher-level manager who has authority over them both. If this top-management support proves weak, however, such a dependency can result in an implementation failure for Stuart – unless he can deploy some other type of organizational influence strategy to transform Jane’s attitudes and behaviour.

## Analytical framework

The organizational hierarchy which differentiates roles and authority levels vertically and horizontally, defines functional responsibilities and formal power dependencies among organizational actors, is central to any analysis of OIPs (Cummings & Cross, 2003). The organizational influence theory upon which our analytical framework is built defines the key dimensions, the organizational context, direction, targets, and tactics of influence relevant to our investigations (Kipnis et al, 1980; Kotter, 2003). It also defines the common direct (face-to-face) influence processes – namely, downward, lateral, and upward – but does not define indirect and informal influence processes that are common in organizational life (Drory & Romm, 1990; Egan, 1994). By ‘indirect influence processes’, we mean situations in which actors seek an intermediary (or intermediaries) between themselves and their target of influence (Porter et al, 2003a). Indirect influence processes can be either open or covert. An example of an open, indirect influence process is when actors go up the chain of command in an effort to influence peers over whom they have no formal authority. Open, direct influence processes are face-to-face encounters in which the actor attempts to influence an individual or a group. Covert influence processes are also a central feature of organizational politics; however, as some researchers have pointed out, they are extremely difficult to study due to reticence of disclosure on the part of actors (Egan, 1994; Buchanan & Badham, 1999; Buchanan, 2008). The main characteristics of OIPs are: (1) direction of influence (downward, lateral or upward); (2) type of influence (open or covert); (3) nature of influence (direct or indirect); (4) target of influence (individual or group); and (5) influence tactics (see Salancik & Pfeffer, 1977; Yukl & Falbe, 1990; Dansereau et al, 2003; Porter et al, 2003a).

Before discussing the first four characteristics, we will discuss the fifth, influence tactics, as they are a fundamental building block of influencing processes. Influence tactics are the various ways in which actors engage their targets to obtain influence (Kipnis et al, 1980; Erez, 1986; Yukl & Falbe, 1990). As Table 1 shows, actors use eight common tactics when seeking to influence a target. Depending on the actor’s position in the organizational hierarchy and sources of influence, he or she will select the tactic or tactics most likely to be effective in obtaining influence over the target.

For example, in a downward influence process, the actor is in a superior position in the organizational hierarchy and thus holds more formal authority than the target of influence. In such a situation, the actor quite often has the ability to use rewards/recognition or coercion/ pressure to modify the behaviour of a subordinate individual or group (Kotter, 2003; Porter et al, 2003a). However, the extent to which the actor utilizes these influence tactics will vary depending on the circumstances. Although the power imbalance is clear in downward influences processes, prudence might suggest the use of rewards and recognition instead of the hard tactics of coercion and pressure, which can invite resistance from subordinates (Mechanic, 1963; Kipnis & Schmidt, 1988; Schriesheim & Hinkin, 1990; Farmer & Maslyn, 1999). In lateral influence processes, the absence of clear and unambiguous hierarchical differentiation among the actors makes influencing a target a more complex undertaking (Cook & Emerson, 1978; Cohen & Bradford, 2003). In this case, none of the actors have at their disposal formal authority, reward or coercive power (Brass, 1984; Flovd & Wooldridge. 1997: Cummings & Cross. 2003). Consequently, the main strategies of lateral influence are alliances, rational persuasion, reciprocity, personal appeals, and the use of intermediaries. The nature of the power dependency, and the importance of the target to the achievement of the influencer’s objectives, will determine whether the influencer enlists intermediaries, cultivates an alliance, or engages in a exchange relationship with the target (Cook, 1977; Cook & Emerson, 1978; Floyd & Wooldridge, 1997; Cohen & Bradford, 2003; Ngwenyama & Nørbjerg, 2010). In lateral influence processes, the types of influence tactics employed can significantly impact both the initiator and target. Some tactics can lead to resistance, intransigence, and retaliation; the influencer must therefore carefully attend to the social and organizational context when selecting lateral influence tactics.

Upward influence is used when a subordinate attempts to get a superior to behave in a manner that the subordinate desires. Although the subordinate is at a disadvantage with regard to reward and coercive power, he or she might have significant personal power based on expertise and charisma (Mechanic, 1963; Ansari & Kapoor, 1987; Belkaoui, 1990; Farmer & Maslyn, 1999). Typically, subordinates take a rational persuasion and consultation approach to upward influence. However, other approaches might include ingratiation, the use of intermediaries or manipulation of information about critical activities for which the subordinate is responsible (Yukl & Falbe, 1990). On the individual level, upward influence processes are often intended to promote and protect self-interest, such as desirable working conditions, promotional opportunities, and so on; on the group level, it might focus on clearing organizational obstacles to goal achievement.

## Research methodology

The case study method is a well-established approach for conducting IS research (Benbasat et al, 1987; Lee, 1989; Walsham, 1995; Klein & Myers, 1999). However, some researchers take the position that case studies contributions are limited to proposing hypotheses for testing with nomothetic methods (Benbasat et al, 1987), while others have convincingly argued that a single case study can falsify an existing theory (Campbell, 1975; Lee, 1989). Still others have argued that case study research offers the power and possibility to generalize from empirical observations to theoretical statements (Eisenhardt, 1989; Walsham, 1995; Klein & Myers, 1999; Lee & Baskerville, 2003). On the issue of theory development from case studies, Lee (1989) suggests that any three of the following four criteria are sufficient to validate the new theory’s explanatory power:

(1) Does the case study consider predictions through which the theory could be falsified?

(2) Are all of the predictions consistent with each other?

(3) Does the case study confirm the theory through empirical evidence?

(4) Does the case study rule out rival theories?

Our case study research satisfies all four criteria: (a) we postulate a set of propositions grounded in empirical observations and in accordance with the literature on organizational influence theory that can be falsified in future testing; (b) empirical evidence from our case study rules out the knowledge claim that weak or absent topmanagement support is structurally incompatible with IS implementation success; and (c) we offer an alternative explanation of how a coordinated organizational influence strategy can assist in achieving IS implementation success under conditions that are structurally incompatible with success.

This case study was part of a larger seven-year longitudinal research project of SPI implementation in seven Scandinavian companies conducted by an interdisciplinary research team from four universities. The larger project was designed as collaborative practice research (Mathiassen, 2002) aimed at examining SPI as organizational change and how it unfolds over time. For that reason, it was designed as a longitudinal process study rather than a variance study. This enabled the development of an understanding of the dynamics of the organization and the OIPs which can only be observed over an extended time domain (cf. Pettigrew, 1990, 1997). Although both authors were members of the longitudinal research team, one of the authors was a participant observer at the case company and collected the empirical data over a 23-month period. The main venue for the researcher’s interaction with the SPI change agents was 11 full-day meetings during the 23-month period. The purpose of the meetings was to plan, take action, and evaluate the SPI initiatives. The meetings were a major source of empirical data; in addition to detailed minutes. there were audio recordings of all

11 meetings. Other sources of empirical data were the SPI documentation – which consisted of written plans, budgets, reports, and other documents used in the SPI initiatives – and the researchers’ field notes of meetings and other interactions with the SPI change agents.

## Empirical analysis

We conducted our empirical analysis using an abductive approach (Schutz, 1963; Tursman, 1987; Reichertz, 2007; Blaikie, 2008; Osei-Bryson & Ngwenyama, 2011). It started with a puzzle: How did the SPI group manage to achieve success under conditions considered structurally incompatible with success? After an initial analysis of the data, we conjectured that the group might be using some type of OIP. This conjecture led to three empirical questions:

(1) What types of OIPs did the SPI managers enact?

(2) What are the key characteristics of these OIPs?

## (3) What were the dynamics of these OIPs?

We then turned to the literature for help in conceptualizing organizational influence (in particular Drory & Romm, 1990; Yukl & Falbe, 1990); this yielded a set of concepts that we used in our empirical analysis (cf. the previous section). The rest of the empirical analysis unfolded as a multi-stage process in which we used content analysis and coding to identify OIPs and interpretive analysis to reconstruct and understand those processes. In Stage 1 of the analysis, we used the concepts (summarized in Table 1) to help identify and code empirical evidence of open organizational influence (covert influence processes are beyond the scope of our research at this time). In Stage 2 of the analysis, we sorted the empirical observations by the target of influence and observed three distinct categories of OIP: (a) influencing project manager behaviour; (b) mobilizing product manager involvement, and (c) recruiting top-management support. We then examined those empirical observations that could not be categorized by target of influence and discovered another category relevant to our study: the design of influence processes. In Stage 3 of our analysis, we systematically analyzed each category to identify details of its key characteristics and dynamics. In Stage 4, we systematically developed our theoretical explanation of the empirical observations of the OIPs by going back and forth through the literature. We present the results of this theoretical elaboration later in Section ‘The findings: a theoretical discussion’.

To support content analysis and coding of the empirical data, we used Atlas/TI, a data analysis software tool. We conducted the interpretive analysis using the key principles of the hermeneutic circle, abstraction and generalization, dialogical reasoning, and suspicion (Klein & Myers, 1999). The long-term (23-month) engagement with the company enabled one of the researchers to gain an intimate understanding of the organizational culture and conditions, as well as the subtler dynamics of the organization influence. This was important for interpretive analysis, as it assisted us in understanding the meanings that actors ascribed to events in the organizational context.

To build rich descriptions and understanding of the OIPs, we iteratively analysed the data and revised the coding guided by the concepts from organizational influence theory. As we conducted our analysis we observed emerging patterns of influence that were suggestive of the dynamics of the influence processes. The details of the coding are included in the appendix in tables displaying the final codes, their frequencies, and definitions emerging from the empirical analysis. We also used Atlas/TI to conduct a co-occurrence analysis of influence tactics and OIPs to determine the preference for and frequency of specific influence tactics in the different OIPs. The co-occurrence table is included in the appendix. In all, our empirical analysis produced a set of 112 relevant, empirical observations upon which the following discussion is based.

## Organizational context of the case study

The case company, ITC, is a traditional software company that grew out of one of the largest banks in Scandinavia. Its main customer is the bank, and a banking culture still largely pervades ITC. For example, hierarchical organizational structures and the rigorousness of banking procedures still dominate the company. ITC’s top-management team consists of the CEO and four division directors, each located in a different geographic area. Under each division director are several product managers to whom the software project managers report. At the time of this research, there were 100 software project managers, distributed across the four divisions. Each division also had a methods group – a staff function responsible for the software processes and for coordinating with the other methods groups. When ITC’s SPI effort was initiated, top management articulated to a small group of product managers the explicit desire to increase productivity and to improve process maturity. There was a particular interest by many – and by top management in particular – to use the Capability Maturity Model, CMM-SW (Humphrey, 1989; Paulk et al, 1993). The CMM-SW is a five-level maturity model that states what is required to perform at a particular level. The model and its derivatives explain how to measure an organization’s level and, on that basis, point directly at what should be improved to reach the next level (Pries-Heje et al, 2008). The initial consensus was that ITC was below level 2; and that view was subsequently confirmed in a formal measurement. To reach level 2, CMM-SW stipulates that companies must improve project management through key process areas such as project planning, project monitoring, quality assurance, and requirements engineering.

After some discussion, an SPI group was established as a temporary project organization placed lateral to product managers and reporting to the top-management team. The top management defined the SPI project’s two strategic goals as (a) bring ITC to level 2 on the CMM-SW process maturity scale, and (b) increase software productivity by 15%.

A manager from a divisional methods group was selected to lead the SPI project, and the project team was composed of a method consultant from the manager’s division, two product managers from another division, and four action researchers. Over time, several methods and product managers from various divisions were involved in the SPI project. However, from the beginning, the SPI group faced conditions that were structurally incompatible with implementation success (Markus & Benjamin, 1996): (1) The SPI group’s location in the organizational hierarchy did not give it any legitimate authority over their primary target – the 100 software project managers. And, while the product managers in the SPI group had legitimate authority over their own software project managers, they had little authority over other project managers. Consequently, many project managers had the power to constrain or block the implementation. (2) Top-management support was weak due to competing business goals and a lack of high-priority commitment to the SPI project. (3) A method-implementation failure featured prominently in the organizational memory and folklore. Even though it was the methods groups that had failed in the past, the SPI project faced negative perceptions from project managers and many product managers.

## The project management change initiative

The SPI project started with a maturity assessment, which found that ITC was at level 1 on the CMM-SW process maturity scale and at level 1.5 on the Bootstrap scale (cf., Paulk et al, 1993; Kuvaja et al, 1994). The maturity assessors also produced a list of improvement recommendations: (a) establish a metrics programme to measure core performance indicators; (b) improve and formalize quality assurance practices, in particular reviewing of key project decisions; (c) establish test processes and test environments and improve integration and application test; (d) improve diffusion of new and existing methods, techniques, and tools, and ensure their adoption; and (e) improve project management practices and the project managers’ competences. This latter recommendation became the SPI project’s central focus and is the empirical case in our research.

After many meetings and debates on the recommendations, the SPI group agreed that improving project management practices and project manager competence would be critical to achieving the top-management team’s strategic goals. They were aware that the task of inducing 100 project managers – distributed over four geographical locations – to change their practices would be difficult, especially given their reluctance and lowlevel interest. Further, the project managers had a great deal of power because they were central to ITC’s core business. Early in the project planning cycle, the SPI group realized that, to successfully transform ITC’s project management practices, they had to enact a coordinated organizational influencing strategy. Eventually, they defined and evolved a programme of six core activities for achieving the goal of transforming project management practices across ITC:

\- Establish a professional and internal education programme that is relevant and useful for all project managers.

\- Establish and ensure use of the competence centre, an online forum where project managers can exchange knowledge and experience and discuss problems.

\- Define an internal standard for project management in terms of key process areas and ensure that project managers understand and enact this standard.

\- Develop a tool for self-evaluation that is compliant with the internal project management standard and push the tool’s use to all project managers.

\- Create and utilize a metrics programme to collect, analyse, and report project performance data.

\- Enact several incentive schemes and ceremonies to ensure that all project managers are well aware of the importance ITC and its management place on project management, such as creating a Project Manager of the Year Award.

At the end of the 23-month project, the implementation effort was found to be successful because: (1) the project management education programme was established and received very good evaluations from the participants, and all 100 project managers eventually signed up for it; (2) the competence centre for exchange of experience was established, led by a division director and gradually used by most project managers; (3) a project manager’s self-assessment tool was developed and by the end of the SPI project, all project managers were using it; (4) an internal standard for goal-setting and assessing project management practice was designed and integrated with the education programme and self-assessment tool; (5) the incentive schemes and recognition ceremonies focused tremendous attention and excitement on achieving a higher level of project management practice. In a meeting with the SPI project members, top management said it had achieved the desired outcomes. The SPI group also assessed the effort internally at a final meeting and was satisfied with the results. But how exactly did this SPI group, faced with conditions structurally incompatible with success, achieve such a resounding implementation success? This is the primary question of our research investigation.

## Findings from the empirical analysis

Our empirical analysis revealed that, during the SPI project, the SPI group designed and enacted a range of OIPs, the primary goal of which was the transformation of project managers’ behaviour. The project managers were a powerful group because they were responsible for producing ITC’s software products. The SPI group had no direct authority or power over the project managers, who, for their part, had the ability to undermine the SPI project’s success. Given this, the SPI group needed to recruit top management and mobilize the product managers as intermediaries in transforming project managers’ behaviour at key points in the SPI implementation project. The SPI group carefully designed and implemented three categories of organizational influencing processes to: (1) recruit top-management support; (2) mobilize the involvement of product managers; and (3) directly influence the behaviour of project managers. A range of influence tactics were central to the design and implementation of this influence strategy. Our systematic analysis of the empirical data yielded 15 empirical observations relating to the design of the OIPs, and 96 to their enactment. Of the 96 empirical observations of enactment, 41 concerned the SPI group’s attempts to directly influence the project managers’ behaviour, 31 concerned mobilizing the product managers’ involvement, and 24 concerned recruiting top-management support. Design and planning the influence strategy The SPI group developed and evolved a coordinated process for designing, planning, and deploying their influence strategy. Their collaborative process unfolded as a continual dialogue to identify primary targets and determine the appropriate influence tactics to enact depending on the specific objectives. The SPI group also continually assessed the organizational situation and the responses of their targets and adjusted their influence strategy throughout the project. Our empirical observations of the design of their organizational influence strategy suggest that the SPI group members were fully aware of the conditions they faced and asymmetry of power in favour of the project managers. Consequently, very early in the project, they began discussions to identify, target, and enrol key SPI initiative stakeholders to serve as intermediaries of indirect influence on the project managers. The following quotation illustrates the SPI group’s discussions on identifying the type of individuals to enrol:

There are some opinion-makers who are very much against it [the SPI project]. Who are also some of those with a very high level of competence. I’m thinking specifically of [AA] who signals tremendous distance to this [the SPI project] because he thinks it’s a team effort. And [AA] is more than anyone very competent and is managing some of the huge projects and very respected, and I think it will be very important to get hold of him. If we want to move things. (SPI meeting #3, transcript)

The SPI group strategically targeted a set of powerful and influential project managers, product managers, and division directors. From the target pool, they enrolled the most influential to serve as a reference group for the SPI project. The reference group’s role was to provide social capital for the SPI group and to help unblock barriers in the divisions of the reference group members. The following quote from a project meeting illustrates the point:

BB: Much has happened since last. The reference group that we established last time has been very active, I think. There have been some changes over time [of who are in the group], but right now we have a good reference group. There is one from each of the divisions y we got [CC] from department X y then [DD] has been substituted with [EE] and I got it now in my group under my management. FF: That has been necessary. It is very constructive now. (SPI meeting #6, transcript)

The SPI group members also understood rational persuasion would be the key influence tactic for enrolling and maintaining relationships with the reference group members. Arguments had to be carefully constructed so as to be compelling to each class of reference group members. Finding the appropriate arguments to use was a challenging exercise, as one SPI group member admitted:

I still have the challenge of describing to the product managers why this is an advantage for them. (SPI meeting #8, transcript)

Designing influencing processes that directly target the behaviour of the 100 project managers was equally difficult. For example, the design of the rewards and recognition schemes were the subject of intense discussions in the SPI project meetings. The SPI group members were clear that, given the organizational culture, their incentive schemes had to be carefully designed to fit organizational norms while communicating the preferred ideals of the new project management practices:

The idea with selecting Project Manager of the Year is to put the spotlight on and start discussion of what is good project management y with the understanding that project management is a competence in itself. (SPI meeting #5, transcript)

Some of the project managers resented the introduction of incentives and rewards above their normal remuneration. They saw it as introducing unnecessary competition in the organization. In one SPI project meeting, the response of some key project managers was noted:

It is the impression that many were provoked by the idea of Project Manager of the Year. (SPI meeting #8, transcript)

As we describe in more detail later, the SPI group resolved to develop an influence strategy using intermediaries to unblock resistance to the Project Manager of the Year Award. To succeed, this influence strategy required that the SPI group members understood the organizational culture and were sensitive to the perceptions of project managers and other targets of influence. For example, the SPI group had to be cognizant of who they enrolled to help move the specific SPI initiatives forward. Picking the wrong person could lead to resistance or evasion tactics by individuals or groups of project managers.

These observations on the SPI group’s discussions as they assessed the organizational situation and designed and redesigned the influence process illustrate the level of reflection involved in designing and enacting effective OIPs. We will now present a more detailed description of each OIP. Table 2 summarizes the 96 empirical observations on the eight types of influence tactics deployed by the SPI group to realize the three critical OIPs (see Figure 1). Row 5 shows six instances of the use of intermediaries in each of the three OIPs. The last row shows 14 instances of the use of rewards/recognition to influence the behaviour of project managers, and two such instances to mobilize the product managers to influence the behaviour of specific project managers. Influencing project manager behaviour The SPI group designed and enacted a range of initiatives aimed at transforming the behaviour of project managers. They carefully crafted their strategy to directly influence project managers’ behaviour around soft tactics. As Table 2 shows, the SPI group heavily favoured rewards/ recognition and rational persuasion, but they also used intermediaries and consultation. The primary reason for this choice of influence tactics was their understanding of the historical situation and their weak position in the organizational hierarchy. The SPI group’s strategy unfolded through attempts to engage project managers in discussions on the need for changes to comply with the new CMM-based project management standard. The SPI group offered the project managers a self-assessment tool that would help them determine for themselves what they would need to change to achieve the CMM level 2 standard. The SPI group also offered project management education to all project managers. The education programme was organized and managed by a methods manager and two product managers who were members of the SPI group. The education was an intensive 3-weeks course with professional educators as well as ITC’s own highly experienced product managers. The focus was to deliver an education at the same level as a postgraduate university course. The education was in this way designed exclusively for ITC and at the same it covered all aspects of project management. Mapping all sessions to the internal standard for project management ensured the coverage. For example, for project planning it included: how project plans could be made in general; Gantt charts; resource allocation; what the specific ITC templates were and how they should be used; negotiation techniques; contract theory; etc. Pedagogically, the course was a mix of lectures and realistic exercises.

As we noted earlier, project managers reported to product managers. Because many of the project managers had worked for and respected the product managers running the education programme, this move by the SPI group was highly successful. However, the self-assessment tool was also central to enrolling the project managers in the education programme as it offered them a safe and un-intrusive approach to determining their weaknesses in relation to the new CMM-based project management standard. One SPI group member observed the following about his efforts to enrol project managers in the education programme:

There were 16 project managers and the reaction was that they evaluated it [the self-assessment tool] on a scale of relevance y they could see the relevance y on a scale from 1 to 6, we got right below 5, 4.9, y so that was very positive. (SPI meeting #9, transcript)

A second important strategy for influencing the project managers’ behaviour was the initiation of a competence centre. Here again, the SPI group enrolled two product managers to design and run the centre, which offered a space for non-competitive experience sharing and knowledge transfer from seasoned and well-regarded project managers to those with less experience. The SPI group organized events and devised various means to market the idea of the competence centre throughout ITC. However, the activity met with mixed impact – high in some divisions, low in others. The high impact was found in the divisions which had been targeted explicitly.

Another key aspect of the SPI group’s strategy to influence project manager behaviour was the development of a Project Manager of the Year Award and a ceremony to publicly honour exceptional project managers. The ceremony included all project, product, and divisional managers, and the CEO, who presented the award. The basic idea was to create an environment in which everyone could see that compliance with the new project management standard would be rewarded by ITC’s top management. Some project managers initially saw this strategy as inducing unnecessary competition in the workplace. Over time it became successfully institutionalized at ITC because the tactic of rewards and recognition was maintained. It continues to be a central strategy influencing compliance with new project management norms.

Recruiting top-management support An early preoccupation of the SPI group was how to recruit members of ITC’s top-management team to serve as intermediaries to influence project managers. Although the CEO had given the SPI group a mandate to launch the project management change initiatives, and specified a set of expectations, his support was inconsistent. The challenges of launching new business initiatives, along with the changing economic conditions, seemed to top his agenda, while the SPI project was relegated to the bottom of his priorities list. The SPI group thus enlisted the help of a highly reputable university professor in whom the CEO had confidence. This professor functioned as an intermediary to persuade and convinced the CEO that his visible participation was necessary to the project’s success and important to ITC’s other strategic initiatives. The professor also facilitated meetings between the SPI group and the CEO to brief him on key project developments and argue their case for resources.

A second obstacle to top-management involvement was the resistance of some divisional directors to some of the six planned change initiatives. From the SPI group’s perspective, it appeared that the resistance to specific initiatives was motivated by personality clashes and different perceptions of priorities among divisional directors. As one of the SPI group members explained in a meeting:

We sense that there is not the same interest and support for the competence centre in divisions X, Y, Z as in W. This is very likely caused by the fact that the ideas and the initiative came from division W and that the other therefore think that it does not reflect their particulars, culture, traditions, and tasks. (SPI meeting #5, transcript)

To overcome this problem, the SPI group worked diligently to convince one of the most respected and influential divisional directors, MM, to chair the competence centre. In addition, the other members of the competence centre’s steering committee were selected based on their potential to positively influence and motivate the majority of project managers across all divisions to use it. Convincing MM and the other divisional directors to devote this level of attention to an initiative that was outside their portfolios or responsibilities was no easy task; it required several meetings and persuasive arguments. Eventually, just before the competence centre’s launch, the SPI project leader announced in a group meeting:

On January 1st the chairman of the Project Management Competence Centre will be [MM, a division director] and in addition there will be a central product manager from each of the four divisions in the steering committee. (SPI meeting #5, transcript)

Garnering support for project management education was another difficult task for the SPI group. Although the education programme was first on the list of key initiatives identified as necessary to meet the CMM-based project management standard, top management was reluctant to provide adequate resources for its implementation. The SPI group had to launch an effort to lobby the CEO and divisional directors to obtain the necessary resources. Much effort went into making the case that ITC did not have a project management culture and that education was needed to start building it. In an early report to top management, the SPI group made a very bold public statement:

We don’t have a project management culture in ITC, and we would like to be part of creating it. (Internal report)

This was a challenge to the reputation of the 100 project managers and the beliefs of many divisional directors. However, the SPI was able to use the initial CMM assessment conducted by an external agent to argue their case. At several top-management meetings, the SPI project leader presented the rationale for the education programme, the budget, the expected benefits, and the key arguments for top and middle management support of it. The SPI also devised parallel influence processes in which: (a) two product managers from the SPI group lobbied their divisional directors and other product managers; and (b) the project reference group members were lobbied for support and consulted in designing the education programme. The programme’s details were gradually revealed to top management through many appearances at management meetings, and a decision was finally taken with the full commitment of all top managers. These indirect influence processes were very necessary to overcome top management’s reluctance to resource and champion not only the education initiative, but also the competence centre and incentive schemes.

Mobilizing product manager involvement From the project’s very beginning, the SPI group realized the importance of mobilizing the product managers – who oversaw the project managers – as intermediaries to influence the latter’s adoption of the new project management practices. Further, the product managers had all achieved their positions due to success as project managers; as such, they were perceived as experts and held in high repute by the project managers. These conditions made the product managers essential to the SPI group’s strategy for unblocking project manager resistance. If they had been unable to effectively mobilize the product managers, the improvement project would have, in all likelihood, failed. The SPI group was very careful in their efforts in enrolling the product managers, depending heavily on soft influence tactics and using intermediaries. Most of their direct influence efforts involved rational persuasion and consultation tactics. A key aspect of the SPI group’s strategy was to enrol the product managers in the education programme to signal their approval of it to project managers. As one SPI group member explains:

If they [the product managers] will use it proactively, as for example CC is doing y then it is about involving the product managers in the sales work when the project managers get out [of the education] y (SPI meeting #4; transcript)

Rational persuasion was used to convince the product managers of the merits of the CMM-based project management practices to the overall management of ITC’s software product line, as well as the importance of ensuring that project managers develop the relevant competence via the education initiative. Some product managers were consulted when the education programme was designed, while others were selected and enrolled as lecturers for the education initiative; less experienced managers were offered a two-day brush-up course on the education programme’s contents.

The SPI group also worked hard to convince product managers to endorse and use the self-assessment tool and the metrics programme to collect data on project management performance. Initially, the product managers were resistant to the idea and argued that it entailed too much overhead. The SPI group thus had to construct a persuasive argument on the merits of collecting and analysing project performance data. This was a challenging issue, but they were eventually successful. The following quote illustrates the challenge:

I have still not described how the product managers benefit from this. [y] How much will life be easier for a product manager with the instruments we supply. (SPI meeting #8, transcript)

Mobilizing the product managers to act as intermediaries in influencing the project managers occasionally required the use of other intermediaries. In such cases, the SPI group members often called on their relationships with divisional directors to indirectly influence the product managers (their subordinates) to participate in various SPI initiatives when they were reluctant - as with the competence centre and the metrics programme – or to simply ‘talk up’ the Project Manager of the Year Award.

## The findings: a theoretical discussion

The empirical analysis in the prior section showed how the SPI group designed and implemented a coordinated set of OIPs and achieved success under conditions that are traditionally considered incompatible to success (Markus & Benjamin, 1996; Abrahamsson, 2001; Bo¨rjesson & Mathiassen, 2004; Sabherwal et al, 2006). Specifically, the SPI group: (1) had no formal authority over the project managers, whose buy-in was required to implement the new project management practices and standards; (2) suffered reputational damage due to the historical memory of the failure of prior ITC initiatives aimed at implementing new methods; (3) never enjoyed more than weak top-management support. However, our empirical analysis reveals that they overcame these conditions by effectively using OIPs.

For a theoretical explanation of these empirical findings, we turn to the theory of organization influence (cf. Section ‘Organizational influencing theory’; Kelman, 1958; Kipnis et al, 1980; Cohen and Bradford, 2003; Yukl et al, 1990, 1991, 1992; Kotter, 2003). Proponents of this theory argue that, while a workgroup’s location and structural properties have consequences for its performance (Cummings & Cross, 2003; Kotter, 2003; Balkundi & Harrison, 2006), studies have shown that OIPs can improve effectiveness when implementing organizational policies and innovations (Markus, 1983; Bass, 1985; Cohen and Bradford, 2003; Floyd & Wooldridge, 1997; Ngwenyama & Nørbjerg, 2010). The organizational influence theory provides a framework that defines a triad of relationships; a set of influence processes upward, downward, and lateral; and influence tactics (see Table 1) that middle managers (or a similarly located group) can engage to achieve desired organizational outcomes (Cook, 1977; Brass, 1984; Kotter, 2003). As our empirical analysis shows, in the absence of formal authority over the project managers, the SPI group designed and deployed a successful organizational influence strategy using soft tactics and intermediaries. The empirical observations from our case study suggest a need to elaborate on three critical aspects of the influence strategy’s dynamics: (a) structural conditions and the design of influence strategies; (b) power dependencies and choices of direct influence tactics; and (c) choices of influence tactics for enrolling intermediaries.

## Structural conditions and influence strategy design

Our empirical analysis shows that, from the very beginning, the SPI group members understood that they needed several lines of attack to successfully implement the new CMM-based project management standard. They clearly understood their limitations in directly influencing the project managers. They also realized that they could not depend on a single influence process; in planning meetings, they openly discussed a range of potential strategies for influencing project manager behaviour. The empirical analysis also suggests that the SPI group members had a critical understanding of the organizational situation. This enabled them to design and deploy a set of coordinated direct and indirect influence processes to change the behaviour of their primary target: project managers. The SPI group also understood that it lacked legitimate authority and had limited power to directly influence project managers to comply with the new project management standard. In direct interactions with the project managers on this issue, the SPI group deployed various soft tactics (consultations, education, rational persuasion, and rewards) to cultivate cooperation, resocialize the project managers, and obtain their commitment. Yet the SPI group was clear that these direct influence processes alone could not ensure success. Consequently, they systematically employed upward influence processes to recruit top management, the divisional directors, and the CEO to play key roles in further influencing the project managers’ behaviour. And, to maintain the CEO’s commitment to their cause, SPI group members used intermediaries to intercede at key points when he seemed distracted by other organizational concerns. The SPI group also used lateral influence processes to mobilize the product managers – who had direct authority over the project managers – to become involved in the SPI implementation project and assist in obtaining and maintaining the project managers’ commitment to the new project management processes. The details of the empirical observations on these three main OIPs outlined earlier (as in Table 2) and summarized here suggest the following theoretical proposition:

## P1: When implementation managers are dependent upon organizational actors for the realization of project outcomes and yet enjoy limited formal authority over them, they will enact a coordinated set of OIPs to remove barriers to implementation success.

This theoretical proposition derived from our empirical analysis is corroborated in the extant literature. For example, several researchers (Falbe & Yukl, 1992; Rowley, 1997; Farmer & Maslyn, 1999; Gupta & Case, 1999; Cummings & Cross, 2003) have pointed out that managers or groups who lack formal authority over organizational actors who are critical to achieving their goals will find configurational approaches to influence strategies more effective than single influence tactics to transform the actors’ behaviour. Kotter (2003), Cohen and Bradford (1989), and others have also argued that the design and successful deployment of coordinated influence processes depend heavily on the agents’ detailed and nuanced knowledge of the targeted actors involved, the organizational culture, and prevailing conditions. Clearly, in this case, SPI group members had appropriate knowledge of the targeted actors (having worked with them for many years) and adequate knowledge of organizational situation; this enabled them to design and enact appropriate influence strategies. Their configurational influence strategy included the following essential characteristics for success outlined by Yukl & Tracey (1992): (a) consistency with the organization’s prevailing norms; (b) appropriateness to their own base of power; (c) appropriateness to the objective of the influence process; and (d) appropriateness to the level of resistance encountered or anticipated. Had the influencing strategy designed by the SPI group failed to comprise these fundamental characteristics, it likely would have failed and incurred disastrous consequences for future performance (Falbe & Yukl, 1992; Floyd & Wooldridge, 1997; Farmer & Maslyn, 1999).

## Power dependencies and choices of direct influence tactics

Our empirical analysis reveals that the SPI group chose soft tactics, such as consultations, rational persuasion, and rewards in their attempts to directly influence the project managers. At one level, the dynamics of open and direct influence processes are straightforward. However, when designing any direct OIP, it is important to understand the sources of power and influence of both the agents and the targets. From a formal perspective, an agent can have five important sources of power and influence: legitimate authority over the target, the ability to reward, the ability to coerce, expert knowledge, and control of the target’s critical activities (Drake & Moberg, 1986; Dansereau et al, 2003; Kerr, 2003; Kipnis, 2003; Kotter, 2003; Thompson & Luthans, 2003). Although ITC’s SPI group members had expertise, they lacked legitimate authority over and were dependent on the project managers for success. They consequently opted for soft tactics. The SPI group needed the project managers to implement the new project management practices and standards, but the project managers had the power to resist the implementation. The project managers’ power derived from two main sources: (a) they owned the processes and practices that the SPI group needed to change, and (b) they managed the software development that was central to ITC’s profitability. Although the SPI group had power based on expertise, this power was moderated by an unfavourable position in the organizational hierarchy, historical memories of failures, and the CEO’s fluctuating priorities. Consequently, the SPI group could not risk using hard influence tactics with the project managers, which could lead to resistance or retaliation. As Table 2 shows, the dominant influence tactics were recognition/rewards and rational persuasion.

The SPI group started building trust with the project managers via open discussions and consultations. Group members made project managers aware of the details of the new project management standards and offered to provide the necessary assistance for their realization. They also formed an alliance with some project managers by designing a self-assessment tool to help project managers determine what they would need to do to comply with the new standard. When the SPI group decided to offer the project management education, it first engaged in broad consultations with the project managers, and the product managers to whom they reported, to determine the requirements. A similar strategy was taken when the group designed and implemented the competence centre. The SPI group also constructed and implemented various incentive schemes and used rational persuasion to influence behaviour changes in project managers. These empirical observations support the following theoretical proposition:

P2: When implementation managers are dependent upon organizational actors for the realization of project outcomes and yet enjoy limited formal authority over them, they will use soft influence tactics to transform the actors’ behaviour in order to remove barriers to implementation success.

The literature on organizational influence theory corroborates this finding. Open communication and the cultivation of trust in the targets of influence are central to the effectiveness of soft influence tactics (Drake & Moberg, 1986; Markham, 1998; Kotter, 2003; Gattiker & Carter, 2010). For example, as Kelman (1958) pointed out, in situations where the agent enjoys limited legitimate authority over the target of influence, soft tactics such as inspirational appeal, rational persuasion, and consultation are more likely to achieve cooperation, while using hard tactics, such as coercion and manipulation, can result in resistance and retaliation. Some recent empirical studies (Yukl & Falbe, 1991; Yukl & Tracey, 1992; Yukl et al, 1993) have also found that inspirational appeals, rational persuasion, and consultation are more likely to generate commitment and cooperation than resistance. Further, these studies found that using coercion and invoking authority are more likely to result in resistance. Other studies by Cohen and Bradford (1989), Kotter (2003), and Porter et al (2003a) also corroborate our findings on this issue. In any direct influence strategy where the agent has no direct legitimate authority over the target, it is essential to avoid manipulation and/or the appearance of inappropriate influence. As Yukl & Tracey (1992) have argued, influence strategies must be consistent with the prevailing norms of the organization and appropriate to the influencers’ base of power.

## Choices of influence tactics when enrolling intermediaries

As our empirical analysis illustrated, the SPI group targeted and enrolled intermediaries from the ranks of top management, the CEO, the division directors, and the product managers. The SPI group enrolled these intermediaries not only to influence project managers, but also to intercede with the division directors and the

CEO. Although the SPI group was on the same organizational hierarchy level as the product managers, it was one level below the division directors and two levels below the CEO. Consequently, group members had to not only design and deploy lateral and upward influence processes, but indirect ones as well. For example, they deployed a common lobbing strategy of using the reference group and competence centre steering group as intermediaries to secure involvement and support of the divisional directors. They also enrolled a professor to intercede with the CEO. So, while our empirical observations show that rational persuasion and consultations were the dominant influence tactics on the OIPs to mobilize product managers and recruit top management, use of intermediaries had a prominent role as well. These empirical observations suggest the following theoretical propositions:

P3: When implementation managers are dependent upon organizational actors for the realization of project outcomes and yet enjoy limited formal authority over them, they will enrol intermediaries who have formal authority over those organizational actors in order to assist in removing barriers to implementation success.

P3.1: When implementation managers are dependent upon organizational actors for the realization of project outcomes and yet enjoy limited formal authority over them, they will enact upward influence processes to recruit top-management support to remove barriers to implementation success.

P3.2: When implementation managers are dependent upon organizational actors for the realization of project outcomes and yet enjoy limited formal authority over them, they will enact lateral influence processes to mobilize the support of other organizational stakeholders to remove barriers to implementation success.

P4: When implementation managers are attempting to enrol top-management and other organizational stakeholders to help remove barriers to implementation success, they will use intermediaries to assist in enrolling and maintaining their relationships with top-management and stakeholders.

Managers in a position with legitimate authority over others have many resources that can be employed in organizational influence. Lacking such a position makes it difficult for individuals and groups who are responsible for boundary-spanning organizational projects (Brass, 1984; Cummings & Cross, 2003; Balkundi & Harrison, 2006). As Kotter (2003) has shown, overcoming this structural deficit and achieving effectiveness requires great skill in OIPs. In such situations, Porter et al (2003a) stress the importance of using lateral influence processes to create networks of influence, which are required to compensate for the lack of centrality in the organizational hierarchy. According to Rowley (1997), when individuals and groups lack organizational centrality they must move beyond dyadic influence processes and develop alliances with key stakeholders to assist them in realizing organizational influence. Sparrowe et al (2001) also recommend creating social networks to improve performance. Cohen & Bradford (2003), Klein et al (2004), Cummings & Cross (2003), and Balkundi & Harrison (2006) all point to the importance of creating a network of intermediaries to assist in removing barriers to organizational performance. As Lin (2001), Friedkin & Johnsen (1999), and Ngwenyama & Nørbjerg (2010) have shown, these social networks confer a level of social capital beyond the individual or group location in the organizational hierarchy. But, as Krackhardt & Hanson (1993), Zaheer et al (1998), and Sparrowe et al (2001) have argued, the agent must invest heavily in building trust with key stakeholders to assist in the influence process. Ngwenyama & Nørbjerg (2010) also found that robust intra-organizational alliances – which offer the social capital needed to overcome power dependencies and achieve IS implementation success – require continued cultivation and maintenance on the agent’s part.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we presented an empirical analysis of how an SPI group designed and enacted a coordinated organizational influence strategy to achieve implementation success under conditions, which traditional IS implementation theory (Markus & Benjamin, 1996; Sabherwal et al, 2006) would consider structurally incompatible with success. The SPI group at ITC was involved in an enterprise-wide organizational change project to transform project management practice, but (a) enjoyed no formal authority over the targets of influence; (b) was dependent on those very targets of influence to successfully implement the new project management practices and standards; (c) suffered reputational damage due to the historical memory of the failure of prior ITC initiatives aimed at implementing new methods; and (d) never enjoyed more than reluctant and weak top-management support. Nonetheless, as our case study illustrates, the SPI group overcame these conditions by skilfully designing and deploying OIPs. Although our case study’s findings are specific to this context, they do suggests key lessons for IS implementation managers: (1) the common conditions that have been viewed as incompatible with success do not necessarily doom a project to failure; (2) OIPs can be effective in overcoming barriers to success; (3) change agents must possess the capabilities to read the organization if they are to design and enact effective OIPs; (4) when vital influence processes are lateral, much depends on the change agents’ expertise and credibility; and (5) configurational influence strategies are more effective than single influence strategies.

This research makes a contribution to a theory of organizational influence for IS implementation. A key finding of our empirical analysis illustrates that technology implementation and change is largely an OIP, and that technical-rational approaches are inadequate for achieving success. Our research also offers a new direction for empirical studies into IS implementation management. Specifically, it illustrates how organizational influence theory can help us to better understand the dynamics of IS implementation under difficult conditions and breaks ground for future empirical investigation. Although research on IS implementation has advanced our understanding of the many challenges that IS implementation teams face, few researchers have applied organizational influence theory to understanding IS implementation dynamics. This research extends recent work of Ngwenyama & Nørbjerg (2010), which demonstrated that intra-organizational alliances – a specific type of OIP – could help overcome power dependencies and achieve IS implementation success. In extending this work, our research illustrates how a more general theory of organizational influence could help managers design and deploy organizational influence strategies to remove barriers to successful IS implementation. The organizational influence theory framework we used in this study enabled us to empirically analyse and explain how the SPI implementation team designed and implemented a complex network of influence processes that contributed to their success. Without this analytical framework, we would have misunderstood crucial aspects of the implementation dynamics.

A limitation of this study was our inability to interrogate covert influence. However, as other researchers have pointed out, obtaining empirical evidence of covert organization influence is extremely challenging (Egan, 1994; Buchanan & Badham, 1999; Buchanan, 2008). As stated earlier, it was not possible for us to collect any empirical data concerning covert influence processes. The focus of our study has also been on the development of a theory about OIPs for unblocking barriers to implementation. We have thus not focused on other theories of related perspectives, for example, legitimacy creation as a longitudinal process and in particular IT project legitimacy (Brown, 1995, 1998). Future research on IS implementation from an organizational influence theory perspective might overcome these limitations and also systematically investigate the following questions: Are OIPs relevant only to adverse situations, or can they contribute to more effective IS implementation under ideal conditions? What are the essential characteristic of an effective organizational influence strategy for IS implementation? What are the essential organizational conditions for success in using influence processes? Should organizational influence strategies differ for different types of IS implementation projects? To help develop a comprehensive theory of IS change agentry, further empirical research in a range of IS implementation situations is needed.
