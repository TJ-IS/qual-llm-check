## Abstract

Researchers have long known that research methods influence construct measurements and that this influence, or method bias, can lead to false conclusions. Despite much work in the methodological literature on specific aspects of method bias, such as common method bias and self-report bias, the meaning of method bias remains unclear, and there is no comprehensive approach for dealing with it. This paper offers a clear definition of method bias, proposes a more comprehensive approach for dealing with it, and describes a demonstration exercise applying the approach in an empirical study of how individual system use and task performance relate. The demonstration suggests that the approach is feasible and illustrates how it can help researchers test theories and identify new research opportunities.

Keywords: Construct, measurement, validity, method bias, method variance, qualitative, quantitative

## Introduction

A major problem with research methods is that they influence construct measurements. This problem, known as method bias, has attracted significant attention in the methodological literature. Unfortunately, although researchers have found ways to minimize some parts of method bias, such as common method bias and self-report bias, the meaning of method bias as a whole remains unclear and no comprehensive approach exists for dealing with it (Sechrest et al. 2000).

Traditionally, information systems researchers have either not dealt with method bias or dealt with it only partially (King et al. 2007; Woszczynski and Whitman 2004). This is alarming, since method bias can lead researchers (1) to accept poor theories and reject good ones and (2) to base seemingly practical suggestions on biased data. Partially addressing method bias can even exacerbate problems while creating a false sense of security in the validity of results. For example, studies relying purely on cross-sectional questionnaire data may run a statistical test for common method bias, and if the test does not indicate bias, the study authors may conclude that their data is free from method bias. In reality, with this type of data collection, there is no way to know if a different method would have given different results.

To address these problems, this paper presents a clear definition of method bias and provides a more comprehensive process for minimizing it. The spirit of the approach follows foundational research in method bias, emphasizing critical realist assumptions, the importance of theory and research design, the need for researcher judgment, and the necessity of programmatic research. The approach draws on and extends past work by addressing method bias as a whole.

To demonstrate the feasibility and usefulness of the proposed approach, the paper describes an exercise applying the approach in an empirical study of how individual system use and task performance relate. The demonstration shows that simplistic or ad hoc method choices can lead to significantly biased explanations and mask important research opportunities.

Although the proposed approach has its own limitations, it should help researchers understand method bias and address it. Given the importance of method choice in both quantitative and qualitative research, hopefully the approach will empower researchers to obtain more meaningful findings, will help the research community at large progress in dealing with method bias, and will promote a more informed use of IS research results.

## The State of Current Research

Method bias is a decades-old concept, first defined in the behavioral sciences by Campbell and Fiske (1959): “Each test or task employed for measurement purposes is a trait-method unit, a union of a particular trait content with measurement procedures not specific to that content” (p. 81). These authors argued that method bias is a critical problem and that researchers need a way to distinguish the trait, or true, effects from the method effects. They advised researchers

\- to use multiple methods to measure each construct in their theoretical model;

• to enter the correlations among all the measures in a multitrait, multimethod (MTMM) matrix; and

• to use triangulation to converge on the true (unbiased) relationships among the constructs.

Researchers did not widely adopt the MTMM approach, and to this day, no consensus exists on how to handle method bias (Sechrest et al. 2000, Spector 2006). Nevertheless, Campbell and Fiske's paper spawned three vibrant areas of research, summarized in Table 1.

## The Nature of Method Bias

The first research stream concerns how to define method bias, estimate its effects, and examine how researchers deal with it. Although researchers have never managed to define or measure method bias precisely (Sechrest et al. 2000), they have determined that it can seriously affect researchers' measurement models, altering reliability and structural models, creating Type 1 and 2 errors (Viswanathan 2005). Appendix A describes how these problems occur.

In the IS literature, the two studies that have examined the effects of method bias report conflicting results (Malhotra et al. 2006; Sharma et al. 2009), but the picture in other fields is more consistent. For example, a synthesis of meta-analyses reflecting 16,525 studies found that, on average, the estimated relationships among traits was affected by method bias almost as much as it was affected by the true relationships among traits (Wilson and Lipsey 2001). Despite method bias being a serious problem, few researchers attempt to address it (Fiske and Campbell 1992). The same is true in IS research (King et al. 2007; Woszczynski and Whitman 2004).

## Methodological Approaches

Research in the second stream has tried to identify methods associated with method bias and create alternatives with less bias. Researchers have found that the amount of bias associated with a method depends on the context; namely, the instrument used, construct studied, and data source (Harrison et al. 1996). However, some methods such as self-reports, are often considered especially problematic (Spector 1994). To overcome these problems, researchers have proposed better ways to design and administer self-reports (Hufnagel and Conca 1994, Schaeffer and Presser 2003) and alternatives to self-reports such as conditional reasoning, implicit measures, and behavioral traces (Fazio and Olson 2003; James 1998; Webb et al. 2000).

Common method bias—bias that stems from the same method or part of method being used for multiple measurements—has also been a focus of this research stream (Williams and Brown 1994). Although researchers have devised ways to minimize common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003), it is only a subset of method bias. Addressing it requires deeper insight into the problems of method bias in general.

## Statistical Approaches

Researchers in the third and largest stream have created and refined statistical tests that estimate common method bias or method bias in general. Most of these tests, such as MTMM matrices, ANOVA, and structural equation models, focus on estimating common method bias (see Podsakoff et al. 2003). More general tests have emerged, such as tests that attempt to quantify the construct validity of a set of scores by examining the degree to which actual and expected correlations match (Westen and Rosenthal 2003). However, such tests assume that researchers know the method bias in their data prior to data analysis, which reduces the tests' usefulness, and underscores the general point that statistics alone cannot solve method bias (Cronbach 1995).

## Redefining Method Bias

A significant barrier to addressing method bias is that its precise meaning is unclear (Fiske and Campbell 1992, Sechrest et al. 2000). Thus, a necessary first step in addressing method bias is to review current conceptions and propose a clearer definition.

## Current Conceptions

Both explicit and implicit conceptions of method bias are problematical. Campbell and Fiske (1959, p. 81) explicitly defined method bias as bias due to “measurement procedures not specific to [a trait],” and this definition has persisted and influenced other definitions for decades, such as the definition of the related term, method variance:

Variance that is attributable to the measurement method rather than to the construct of interest. The term method refers to the form of measurement at different levels of abstraction, such as the content of specific items, scale types, response format, and the general context (Bagozzi and Yi, 1991, p. 426).

Although such definitions are helpful, they are not as clear as they could be about what method and bias mean. For method, Campbell and Fiske simply refer to “measurement procedures” while Bagozzi and Yi merely list various aspects of the measurement process. The definitions do not explain what bias or variance mean, although one interpretation of variance might be the dispersion of scores around an expected value, in light of its definition in statistical theory.

Implicit conceptions of method bias tend to be narrow. One view implied by many papers is that method bias is a problem only in quantitative research (e.g., Podsakoff et al. 2003; Sechrest et al. 2000; Spector 2006). Another view implied by many articles is that method bias and common method bias are essentially synonymous (e.g., Podsakoff et al. 2003, Spector 2006). These implicit conceptions of method bias are not surprising given that Campbell and Fiske's original discussion of method bias was highly quantitative and emphasized common method bias. Nonetheless, research methods can influence construct measurements in qualitative and quantitative research and such influence can occur without common methods. For example, a known source of method bias is social desirability. If a researcher asks a CIO to state if her company keeps its payroll data secure, she will likely say that the company does, even if it does not, because the answer would be socially desirable. This instance of method bias is not inherently quantitative, nor does its occurrence depend on the use of common methods.

## A New Definition

A definition of method bias that uses critical realist terminology can overcome the confusion. Table 2 defines the key terms in the proposed definition of method bias, but some are worth special mention. Trait refers to a real-world property, such as the property in the human mind that we call intelligence. Trait score refers to a subject's true level on a trait, such as a person's true level of the property that we call intelligence. Construct refers to a concept researchers use to reflect a trait, such as their concept of intelligence, which they hope reflects the true nature of that trait in the human mind. Finally, measured score refers to researchers' estimate of a trait score, such as their estimate of someone's intelligence.

Central to the definition of method bias is a clear definition of method and of bias. The definition of method proposed here draws on past work (Cronbach 1995; Doty and Glick 1988; Sechrest et al. 2000; Viswanathan 2005), viewing a measurement method as comprising three elements:

\- Rater: the provider of the score, such as the subject, researcher, or independent rater

\- Instrument: the device used for scoring, such as questions and response options in a questionnaire or interview, coding scripts for recording observations, or software programs that collect computer records

\- Procedure: the process by which a rater uses instruments to obtain a single score, such as instructions for raters, or to obtain multiple scores, such as the choice of rater, instrument, and procedure for each measurement, and the sequence and timing of measurements

In this definition, score refers to a measured score obtained through a method to approximate the trait score. Bias, then, is the difference between the measured score and trait score. These characteristics of method and bias form the basis for a new definition of method bias: Method bias is the difference between the measured score of a trait and the trait score that stems from the rater, instrument, and/or procedure used to obtain the score.

This definition focuses on method bias rather than method variance. Method variance is an ambiguous term because it can mean the extent to which a single measured score varies from the trait score, or the extent to which multiple measurements of a trait score vary around the mean of those measurements. The first meaning is identical to the proposed definition of method bias, but the second, statistical meaning is more typical of the term's use in past literature. The proposed definition focuses on method bias because it does not have this conceptual ambiguity and because the second, statistical meaning of method variance is only relevant for quantitative research whereas method bias can occur in quantitative and qualitative research.

The definition also makes it clear that method bias is not just common method bias. Although method bias can stem from any element of method (rater, instrument, or procedure) common method bias stems only from the measurement procedure. The proposed definition of method accounts for procedures to obtain one score and procedures to obtain multiple scores. It is the latter class of procedures that causes common method bias. When collecting data, a researcher must have an implicit or explicit procedure for collecting multiple measurements that will specify the sequence of traits measured; the temporal delay between measurements; and the specific rater, instrument, and procedure to be used for each measurement. As Figure 1 shows, common method bias occurs when the data collection procedure uses common methods across measurements that in turn affect the rater, who, consequently, gives a biased score.

In summary, the proposed definition of method bias relies on a clear definition of method and bias, is not restricted to quantitative research, and covers more than common method bias. The definition also reflects the latest research on validity, which states that a measure is valid if it measures the intended trait (Borsboom 2005).

Although the new definition is broad, it does not account for all methodological problems. It assumes, for example, that a trait exists and that researchers can define and conceptualize it. Prior research has created techniques for discovering traits and defining the content that needs to be measured to capture them (Lewis et al. 2005; Rossiter 2002). The definition also does not consider if the measurement model—the way that researchers link measured scores to construct scores—is formative or reflective, first-order or higher-order. Researchers should ensure that their measurements have minimum bias regardless of the measurement model used. Finally, because researchers measure only a sample of units, yet often wish to generalize to a population, they can introduce biases through sampling errors even if their measurements are free from bias. Prior work provides advice for addressing such issues (Sivo et al. 2006).

Even excluding these problems, method bias remains a formidable challenge. In fact, some researchers from philosophical positions such as critical realism assume that method bias is simply unknowable because trait scores are unknowable. This paper rejects that view and draws instead on evolutionary critical realism, which suggests that although theories and measures are fallible, scientists can arrive at true measures and theories over time by continually testing them and rejecting those that fail (Brewer and Collins 1981; Campbell and Russo 2001). From this perspective, the key to solving method bias is to conduct programmatic research to identify and gradually eliminate sources of method bias over time. To that end, the next section describes an approach to minimizing sources of method bias through programmatic research. Appendix B provides further justification for the philosophical position adopted here.

## Sources of Method Bias

Based on a review and extension of past work (Podsakoff et al. 2003, Viswanathan 2005), it is proposed that there are two fundamental sources of method bias: knowledge bias and rating bias. Figure 2 shows these graphically, and Table 3 describes them in terms of the three components of methods (raters, instruments, and procedures). In formal terms, these sources of method bias can be related to the concepts defined earlier, as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r c l} \text {Measured score} & = & \text {trait score + method bias +} \\ & & \text {other forms of error} \end{array}
$$

where method bias is a function of rating bias and knowledge bias, and other forms of error reflect errors not produced by methods. Although the two sources of method bias can combine in various ways, adding to or offsetting each other, this is not a concern because the aim is to minimize and, ultimately, eliminate method bias, which is done by eliminating both sources.

## The Nature of Knowledge Bias and Rating Bias

Knowledge bias is the difference between the rater's best estimate of the trait score and the true trait score that stems from the rater's lack of knowledge of the trait. For example, when a person rates an internal trait of someone else, the rater must use proxies (observable behaviors) to estimate the score. Knowledge bias can also occur when people rate themselves because they can forget past behavior (Blair and Burton 1987) and not be aware of their own mental states (Bem 1972). Because knowledge bias depends on knowledge, it ultimately stems from the rater, but researchers can use instruments and procedures to reduce it, such as developing instruments and procedures that will help raters provide accurate ratings (Hoyt 2000).

Rating bias is the difference between a rater's best estimate of the trait score and the reported score that stems from the rater not providing a best estimate of the trait score. Such bias can occur intentionally, such as when raters have a general tendency or a specific motivation to give a biased response (Murphy et al. 2004). It can also occur unintentionally, such as when the wording of a survey question influences respondents to give a particular response or when questions are asked in a sequence that leads respondents to give a particular pattern of responses.

## Distinguishing Knowledge Bias and Rating Bias

Distinguishing knowledge bias and rating bias is crucial because they have different relationships with real-world traits. A rating that reflects knowledge bias is not a true rating of the intended trait, but it is a true rating of a trait. For example, a researcher may ask a company's CEO to complete a questionnaire on the CIO's performance. If the CEO has a mistaken perception of the CIO's performance, the CEO's rating would be a biased rating of actual performance, but it would be a valid rating of the CEO's perception of the CIO's performance, and this perception could have a real effect on how she deals with the CIO. Thus, this perception is a relevant trait in the domain, just one that differs from the CIO's true performance.

In contrast, a rating that contains rating bias is not a true rating of any trait in the domain. It is a transitory bias that exists only because of the measurement act. The CEO, for example, might give a biased score of her CIO's performance to give a socially desirable answer. This biased score is not a valid rating of any trait in the domain. The one exception is secondary data where the act of measurement occurs in the domain. In this case, a rating that reflects rating bias can be a true rating of a real-world trait, such as when a performance appraisal truly reflects what the CEO wants the CIO to think is her perception of the CIO's performance. However, meaningful instances of rating bias would be virtually impossible to identify in secondary data because researchers do not generally have knowledge or control of such data's production.

The need to distinguish knowledge bias and rating bias suggests strongly that a comprehensive approach is needed to deal with method bias and that different strategies are needed to deal with each source.

## Need for a Comprehensive Approach

Prior approaches for dealing with method bias advise researchers either to measure every construct in a study with multiple methods (Campbell and Fiske 1959) or to use different methods to measure the predictor and criterion variables (Podsakoff et al. 2003). Neither of these approaches deals with method bias as a whole.

This is often considered best practice in IS research, such as measuring predictors of IT usage by self-reports and measuring IT usage by computer logs (Venkatesh et al. 2003), but its use can be counterproductive because it tackles only common method bias—no other aspect of rating bias. The approach could even exacerbate knowledge bias, for example, in studies where computer logs are less able than self-reports to rate the aspects of an individual's IT usage that are relevant for the theory being tested.

## Different Strategies for Different Sources

Researchers have the same objectives when dealing with each source of bias: to minimize the bias that could occur and to understand any bias that has occurred. However, the way to achieve these objectives is different for each source.

For rating bias, many strategies are already in place for minimizing it and understanding its impact on results (Campbell and Fiske 1959; Podsakoff et al. 2003). The approach proposed later in this paper for minimizing method bias incorporates many of these suggestions.

In sharp contrast, no strategy exists that sufficiently deals with knowledge bias, largely because such bias depends on the underlying trait score and is consequently more complex. The way to address knowledge bias depends on whether or not the construct being measured is defined in terms of a rater, such as the user who “perceives” the usefulness or ease of use of a new IT (Davis 1989). If it is, researchers can easily prevent knowledge bias by using the rater that the theory specifies because this rater’s perception, or estimate, is the desired trait.

Knowledge bias is more difficult to address when the construct is not defined in terms of a rater. For example, if a researcher wishes to measure users' task performance, he or she could use several raters (users, managers, and/or peers) to measure it. How could we conceptualize the ratings that each rater could give and the possible bias in each one? Conceptualizing this is important because it could help researchers (1) determine how to minimize bias during research design (when choosing raters), and (2) understand the nature of the ratings they obtain and, thus, draw accurate conclusions from their data. Unfortunately, researchers currently have no theory or taxonomy that can help them conceptualize how knowledge bias affects a rating.

## A Taxonomy for Conceptualizing Knowledge Bias

The taxonomy in Figure 3 draws on research in Information Systems (Chin 1996) and ontology. The ontology it draws on distinguishes between traits that are intrinsic to individuals (beliefs and other mental phenomena) and traits that are extrinsic to individuals and are observable, at least in principle (atomic mass or company profit, for example) (Searle 1995). Two types of extrinsic traits exist: observer-independent traits that exist irrespective of people, such as atomic mass and observer-dependent, or social, traits that depend on a class of observers seeing them (subjectively) in a similar way, such as company profit, which can be viewed from an economic or accounting perspective. The taxonomy focuses on intrinsic traits and extrinsic observer-dependent traits because they are the primary focus of social scientists (Searle 1995). For brevity, observer-dependent traits are referred to simply as extrinsic traits.

As described earlier, knowledge bias results in the valid rating of another trait. Figure 3 illustrates the four ways that knowledge bias can occur and the trait that it will measure in each case. It shows that different types of biases can occur for extrinsic traits and intrinsic traits.

Extrinsic traits: Bias can occur because a rater lacks access to the intended frame of reference for rating the trait. For example, a rater may rate IT spending differently because that rater views IT differently, as in path $K_{1}$ in Figure 3. In this case, the rating is true, but it reflects a different trait. Bias can also occur because the rater lacks access to the trait's true score and instead rates his or her perception of it. Because perceptions are fallible, this perception could be true even though it differs from the theorized trait, as in path $K_{2}$ in Figure 3.

Intrinsic traits: Bias can occur because the rater lacks access to the intrinsic trait (such as a person's intelligence) and instead rates an extrinsic trait (such as the person's performance) in the hope that it is a good surrogate, as in example $K_{3}$ in Figure 3. Bias can also occur because many mental states are unconscious and inaccessible to conscious scrutiny, such as those involved in “perception, attention, learning, evaluation, emotion, and motivation” (Wilson and Dunn 2004, p. 499). Consequently, although self-ratings of intrinsic traits may capture true perceptions, they may not reflect the actual intrinsic traits of interest, as in path $K_{4}$ in Figure 3.

Researchers currently have no theory or taxonomy to help them conceptualize how knowledge bias can occur, which can lead to poor study design and incorrect interpretation of results. Hopefully, the taxonomy presented in Figure 3 can promote an understanding of how knowledge bias can occur, help researchers choose raters who will provide the least bias, and serve as a template for describing measured traits, thereby helping researchers interpret study results.

## Elements of a Comprehensive Approach

The previous sections have made clear that accounting for differences in rating bias and knowledge bias requires a comprehensive way to address method bias. Figure 4 presents the proposed approach, which comprises five steps within two phases: selecting a strategy for dealing with method bias and executing that strategy. The two steps in strategy selection determine if execution will use a monoview or multiview strategy. The remaining three steps address the three elements of method—rater, instrument, and procedure—as well as data analysis techniques associated with these two execution strategies. Many studies have used practices in the proposed approach, as shown in Table 4 and discussed throughout this section.

## Selecting a Strategy

To select a strategy, researchers must first consider the constructs in the theory they are studying and whether these constructs are defined in terms of one rater class. Some IS constructs are defined in terms of one rater class, such as perceived ease of use (an intrinsic trait), which is defined in terms of the end-user's perception (Davis 1989), and IT spirit (an extrinsic trait), which adaptive structuration theory suggests is best rated by researchers (DeSanctis and Poole 1994). However, many IS constructs are not defined in terms of one rater class. For example, cognitive absorption (an intrinsic trait) refers to the extent to which a user is engaged in using a system, not the user's perception of engagement (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000), and observable usage behavior (an extrinsic trait) refers to the observable behaviors of an individual, not the user's perceptions of that behavior (Straub et al. 1995).

If the theory does not specify a particular rater class, or if the rater class is problematic, researchers have two alternatives. First, they may redefine the construct in terms of one or more rater classes. Table 4 provides some useful insights into that process from past studies. Second, they can proceed with the next step in selecting a strategy and ask, “Is there strong evidence that one rater class will provide the least bias?” Because there is little research on the amount of method bias that different raters produce (Spector 2006), evidence will often be lacking. In some cases, the best rater might be obvious even in the absence of evidence. For example, to measure an individuals’ intention, that individual would clearly be the best rater. However, because individuals have limited self-knowledge (Wilson and Dunn 2004), researchers cannot assume that intrinsic traits should always be measured by self-reports. Ideally, researchers would conduct theoretical and empirical work to determine which raters can most accurately rate a given trait, but only a few studies have conducted such work. A recent exception is work by Tallon and Kraemer (2007). Research by Kim and Malhotra (2005) and Kim et al. (2005) are other examples (see Table 4).

## Executing a Strategy

In the execution phase, researchers use the three elements of method (raters, instruments, and procedures) in a way that prevents unwanted biases from influencing the data. They then analyze their data in a way that identifies and accounts for any remaining biases.

## Rater

The monoview strategy is appropriate when a construct is defined in terms of one rater class. The multiview strategy is appropriate when a construct is defined in terms of either no rater class or multiple rater classes, or when no strong evidence points to a best rater class.

Executing the monoview strategy. For intrinsic traits, the monoview strategy requires that researchers use one rater: the rater defined in the theory. For example, for perceived ease of use, the rater is the user. For theories in which all the constructs are intrinsic perceptual traits, this would mean that the same rater will rate all constructs. Although this could lead to common method bias, it is the appropriate strategy because (1) it is the only way to truly test the theory, since the theory defines the constructs in terms of that rater, and (2) common method bias can be minimized through the judicious choice of instruments, procedures, and data analysis techniques.

For extrinsic traits, the monoview strategy requires that researchers use multiple raters from one rater class: the class defined in the theory. For example, for IT spirit, the best rater class comprises researchers (DeSanctis and Poole 1994), so a researcher could use, for example, multiple research assistants to measure IT spirit. A study by Chidambaram and Tung (2005) (see Table 4) employed this type of procedure. The risk of common method bias is lower here because multiple raters from one class are used rather than one rater.

Executing the multiview strategy. For both intrinsic and extrinsic traits, the multiview strategy requires that researchers use multiple raters from different rater classes. Ideally, researchers would select these classes on the basis of theory and evidence that indicates that particular rater classes will view the trait differently and that these differences are important for theory or for practice because, for example, the views are complementary or conflicting. Marcolin et al. (2000) is a rare exemplar of this practice (see Table 4).

Observations. Two points are worth noting about the execution strategy for raters. First, if a construct is not defined in terms of a rater, some degree of knowledge bias will inevitably occur when measuring it because the theorized trait has no associated rater whereas the measured trait is derived from a specific rater with a specific view of the world. Appendix C provides evidence that most IS constructs are not defined in terms of a rater, so knowledge bias will be inevitable in most IS studies. The approach proposed here suggests that researchers can either

\- eliminate the problem by defining constructs in their theories in terms of a rater class and then collecting data from this class (as suggested in Rossiter 2002); or

\- contain the problem by using the taxonomy in Figure 3 to design their study so that they know what type of knowledge bias could affect their measures and then use data analysis techniques to determine the extent to which knowledge bias affected their data.

The second point worth noting is that the proposed execution strategy for raters differs considerably from the guidance in existing approaches, which advise using either multiple raters for each construct (Campbell and Fiske 1959) or one rater for each construct but different raters for the predictor and criterion variables (Podsakoff et al. 2003). The guidance given here is that researchers should use one rater for intrinsic traits defined in terms of a rater (even if this means using the same rater for all constructs in the theory) and should use multiple raters in different ways depending on the particular execution strategy adopted (monoview or multiview).

## Instruments and Procedures

In both the monoview and multiview strategies, the proposed approach recommends that researchers use multiple instruments and procedures that are associated with minimum rating bias. A study by Barki et al. (2007) used multiple instruments in this way (see Table 4). A wealth of literature offers advice on how to select instruments and procedures that will minimize rating bias (Goodhue and Loiacono 2002; Orne 1962; Podsakoff et al. 2003) and IS researchers have even contributed new procedures (Tam et al. 2009).

Even with the best instruments and procedures, however, rating bias is not likely to be zero. Consequently, researchers should use multiple good instruments and/or procedures so that they can later triangulate (converge) on the true trait scores.

## Data Analysis

The monoview and multiview strategies deal with rating bias in the same way, but they diverge in dealing with knowledge bias. In dealing with rating bias, both strategies attempt first to minimize it by converging across scores from different raters within the same class, different instruments, or different procedures. They then attempt to identify and account for any remaining rating bias in the data. For example, quantitative researchers can compare correlations to check for the effect of bias on results (Kim and Malhotra 2005) and can use structural equation modeling to identify and account for common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003). A study by Liang et al. (2007) (see Table 4) used such techniques, but in IS they are quite rare. Although these techniques for dealing with rating bias represent current best practice in quantitative research, they have important limitations (Richardson et al. 2009) and will surely evolve and improve over time.

The multiview strategy also requires that researchers test for knowledge bias, which is challenging because knowledge bias depends on the trait score. In general, the only option is to

\- theorize a difference between the scores obtained from the two rater classes based on the type of knowledge bias that is likely present in their ratings, for example, using the taxonomy in Figure 3, and

\- test if there is, in fact, a difference between the scores obtained from the two rater classes (by comparing means or path coefficients).

This paper provides an example later of how to implement this option. More advanced ways of assessing knowledge bias are emerging. One is to use measurement invariance tests to examine if different raters interpret survey items in different ways because of their varying knowledge or experience (Vandenberg 2002). Another is to use cognitive modeling techniques to scrutinize the act of rating to assess if a rater is indeed scoring the intended trait (Borsboom 2005). Overall, data analysis techniques for addressing rating and knowledge bias exist or are emerging. This section has discussed quantitative techniques, but qualitative techniques also exist (Miles and Huberman 1994). What has been missing is an approach for choosing an appropriate set of techniques for a given study—a gap the approach proposed in this paper aims to fill.

## The Importance of Judgment and Programmatic Research

Admittedly, the requirements in the proposed approach are onerous. The multiview strategy suggests that researchers consider rating each construct in at least four ways (2 raters × 2 instruments or × 2 procedures), which will be infeasible in most studies. Moreover, it provides little guidance for resolving tradeoffs. For example, in measuring an employee's desire to resign, a researcher could reduce knowledge bias by having the employee be the rater because the employee has closer access to his or her desires, but the researcher could reduce rating bias by having the employee's manager be the rater because the manager may have less motivation to give a false rating. Unfortunately, there is no simple rule for resolving this dilemma, nor does the proposed approach offer one.

Thus, researchers must use good judgment. The multiview strategy does not require a researcher to use multiple raters, instruments, and procedures for every construct and to manage all the associated tradeoffs in one study. Rather, it provides guidance for what a researcher should consider addressing in a study, emphasizes the need to justify the aspects the researcher does choose to address, and highlights any areas that require further investigation.

In other words, the approach recognizes the inherent limitations of any study and the need for programmatic research (Berthon et al. 2002; Schmidt 1992). No single study can eliminate method bias completely, but all studies can take steps to minimize it and acknowledge its impact. By systematically varying aspects of the approach over multiple studies, researchers can use it to build consensus on the true relationships among traits and inch closer to validity over time.

## Demonstration

The previous section provided examples of studies that used practices in the proposed approach. However, the approach has not yet been fully implemented. To do so, a research program would have to be conducted, ideally by a community of researchers. The aim of this demonstration is more modest: to show that it is feasible and useful to apply the approach in a single study and to explore issues that can arise when researchers use it.

## Theoretical and Empirical Context

To focus this demonstration on the proposed approach, rather than also to justify a new theory and a new empirical test, the demonstration draws on the theory and empirical test in an existing study, referred to as BJS-06 (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006). The demonstration uses data reported in BJS-

06 as well as data that was collected but not reported in that study.

Figure 5 shows the model tested in BJS-06, which has two constructs:

\- System usage: a user's employment of one or more features of a system to perform a task

\- Task performance: an assessment of task outputs in terms of effectiveness

BJS-06 theorized that in short, cognitively engaging tasks, the usage type most closely related to performance is a user's exploitation of his or her knowledge of the IS to perform the task. In that paper, such exploitive usage was measured through deep structure usage and cognitive absorption, the latter being a reflective higher order construct with five dimensions; the dimension measured in that study was focused immersion (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000). Thus, the system usage construct in Figure 5 has two subconstructs:

\- Focused immersion: the extent to which the user is cognitively immersed in his or her use of the system to perform the task

\- Deep structure usage: the extent to which the user is using the features of the system that support the task's structure

Focused immersion is intrinsic because it exists in the user's mind. Deep structure usage and performance are extrinsic because they are observable, but different observers can view them differently.

To test this model, BJS-06 reported an experiment that examined the extent to which 229 university accounting students could build a spreadsheet model in Microsoft Excel to justify an asset purchase. Measures of system usage were obtained by rating how students employed Excel, while measures of performance were obtained by rating the students' justifications for the asset purchase. This experiment enabled a strong test of the theoretical model because

\- the task was cognitively engaging, which allowed variation in focused immersion

\- the system contained many features that varied in the extent to which they supported the task, which allowed variation in deep structure use

• students had to complete the task within 1.5 hours, which enabled a test of short-run task performance

Knowledge bias. As Figure 6 shows, using self-ratings and observer ratings for each construct created the possibility for several types of knowledge bias. For the intrinsic trait (focused immersion), end users and observers could provide different scores because

\- Observers (and to a lesser extent, even end users) lack access to the end user's actual cognition and are restricted to rating observable surrogates (Bem 1972), as in path $\mathbf{K}_3$ of the figure.

## Strategy Execution: Instruments and Procedures

Table 8 shows the instruments used in the demonstration exercise. In keeping with the multiview strategy's requirement to use multiple instruments and procedures with minimum rating bias, end users were given questionnaires, and observers had structured coding schemes.

Focused immersion. End users rated focused immersion by using a self-report questionnaire scale adapted from Agarwal and Karahanna (2000), while observers used a new coding scheme. Theories of cognition were studied to identify which observable behaviors would be the best indicators of focused immersion, and the observers were asked to rate these behaviors.

Because the experimental case was a problem-solving task, observers had to consider if users were focused on the problem-solving activity and if they were immersed in it. Thus, observers rated two observable behaviors: clear strategy and active work. Clear strategy is defined as the degree to which a user was clearly undertaking a sequence of goal-directed (not haphazard or task-irrelevant) operations in using Excel during the task. It was chosen as the best indicator of being focused on a problem-solving activity because human problem-solving is carried out through a goal-directed sequence of cognitive operations (Ashcraft 2002).

Active work is defined as the proportion of a user's work time in which he or she appeared to be actively working. It was chosen as the best indicator of immersion because research suggests that the behaviors of immersed individuals should flow, since the individual's attention is focused on the task and thus not easily distracted (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000; Csikszentmihalyi 1990). Activity breaks of more than 4 seconds were considered instances of idle time because smaller breaks of time can occur even during active work (Ashcraft 2002).

Users could conceivably be following a clear strategy, yet suffer distractions, or be working actively but off task. However, the more a user is working actively and has a clear strategy, the more likely that user is in a state of focused immersion. The two behaviors were considered formative dimensions, therefore, because both are required although they need not correlate highly in a sample of users (Bollen and Lennox 1991).

Deep structure usage. Because no instruments existed to measure deep structure usage, a self-report scale and observer coding scheme were created. Both rated deep structure usage from the perspective of the course instructors. To implement this idea for the self-report scale, a course instructor was asked to characterize the structure of the task. As described in BJS-06, the statements obtained—analyzing data, testing assumptions, and deriving conclusions—were then used to create items for the scale. These items, listed in Table 8, asked the user to rate the extent to which he or she used features that helped achieve these goals.

To create the observer coding scheme, two course instructors were given the same characterization of the task's structure and a comprehensive listing of Excel features and were asked to indicate the degree to which each feature supported the task's structure. Their ratings were comparable ([ICC (2,2) = .76], Shrout and Fleiss 1979); both indicated that the primary Excel features were functions, which are used to write formulae, and filling, which is used to complete rows or columns of data. Thus, observers were required to rate deep structure usage by rating the extent to which end users employed these features to perform the task. As with the observable indicators of focused immersion, these items are considered formative rather than reflective because a user can trade off the use of one for another, such as by using a fill feature to copy formulae rather than write formulae directly (Bollen and Lennox 1991).

Performance. Two scales were used to measure performance. One was an existing scale that course instructors had created independently of the research in BJS-06. The scale awarded students a single percentage score on the basis of their performance in seven aspects of the task. Before the BJS-06 study, students had received performance feedback using this type of scale on eight prior assignments.

A second scale was created to measure self-reported performance. In the performance measurement literature, work effectiveness has two components: quality and quantity (Meister 1986). Accordingly, the self-report scale contained two questions, one for quality and one for quantity. These items are considered formative because students can trade off quantity for quality, and vice versa (Bollen and Lennox 1991). To ensure that the items measured performance from the perspective of course instructors, users were asked to rate if they completed the required parts of the task and if the instructor would rate their work highly.

Instrument equivalence. A general issue when applying this step was whether or not the observer and end user instruments had to be the same. Past research suggests that they do not. Researchers have often measured traits by asking different raters to use different items, such as measuring the popularity of museum exhibits by asking patrons or by observing the wear and tear of floor tiles around exhibits (Webb et al. 2000). However, although it is valid for different raters to use different instruments to measure a trait, it is important that the two instruments will, in principle, measure the same trait. Regrettably, strong evidence is lacking in this demonstration that each pair of instruments will definitely measure the same trait. Additional studies would be needed to refine the instruments, particularly in terms of their content validity.

Arguably, the instrument most lacking in strong evidence was the coding scheme for observers' rating of focused immersion. Therefore, to obtain extra evidence that this scale was valid, 62 additional students were asked to perform the same task that subjects in the main experiment performed. After performing the task, these students were given a document that defined focused immersion and described the indicators for clear strategy and active work. The students then completed a questionnaire to assess the extent to which these indicators would have served as good indicators of how much they experienced focused immersion during the task. On a scale of 0 to 5, with 0 being not at all and 5 being to a great extent, the students' ratings were 3.5 for clear strategy, 3.4 for active work, and 4.1 for the indicators combined. Although the students' ratings indicated that the combined indicator was not perfect, a rating of 4.1 indicates that they felt it was a valid indicator to a large extent.

Expectations about knowledge bias. For all three constructs, the instruments that end users and observers employed attempted to measure the same trait. Nonetheless, because of knowledge bias, the instruments are likely to measure different traits. For example, measures of focused immersion could measure different traits because observers are rating observable behaviors and users are rating their perceptions of focused immersion (per paths $K_{3}$ and $K_{4}$ in Figure 6). Likewise, even though deep structure usage and performance were rated from the same perspective (that of course instructors), the ratings could still suffer from knowledge bias because end-users and observers might have different levels of knowledge of the system and task solution, and end users might have perceptual errors when rating their observable behaviors (per paths $K_{1}$ and especially $K_{2}$ in Figure 6). One aim of the data analysis step is to examine if this knowledge bias matters empirically.

Even after performing these procedures, common method bias could still affect the data because each end user rated each construct in the study. The observer ratings did not incur this risk because different observers from the same observer class rated the different traits. Determining if common method bias affected the results is an objective in the data analysis step.

## Strategy Execution: Data Analysis

The final step in the multiview strategy is data analysis, which consists of minimizing rating bias where possible, identifying and accounting for remaining rating bias, and testing for the effect of knowledge bias.

Rating bias was minimized by converging across ratings from multiple raters in the same class. Tests revealed that each pair of observers' ratings were comparable: ICC $[2, 2] = .74$ for focused immersion, .73 for deep structure usage, and .87 for performance. Therefore, it was appropriate to take the mean average rating from each observer pair as the observed score for that trait. Identifying and accounting for the remaining rating bias in the data involved including a common method factor in the structural model and testing if the items loaded significantly on it (Podsakoff et al. 2003). Finally, testing for knowledge bias involved examining if raters with different degrees of knowledge bias led to different estimates of trait relationships.

To formalize the last two objectives in this step (identify and account for rating bias and test for knowledge bias effects), hypotheses were created to assess the impacts of each bias source. As Figure 7 shows, each pair of measures should result in different scores, partly because of knowledge bias and partly because of rating bias. However, hypotheses can be drawn that allow the maximum isolation of each bias type.

The importance of rating bias should be apparent in the results from the self-report data. Specifically, self-report items should load on the common method factor, and the relationships between the independent variables (IVs) and the dependent variable (DV) should be stronger when self-report measures are used for the IVs and the DV. This is likely because obtaining the IVs and the DV on one questionnaire is likely to induce subjects to align responses in order to avoid cognitive dissonance (Podsakoff et al. 2003). ## Results of the Demonstration Exercise

The results were analyzed in two phases. The descriptive statistics and instrument reliability and validity statistics were examined first and then the hypotheses were tested. Because the second phase is the most important for demonstrating the proposed approach, the results of the first phase, which were satisfactory, are presented in Appendix D.

Partial least squares (PLS) was used to test the theoretical model. It was more appropriate than covariance-based structural equation modeling because the latter requires that each “latent variable emit at least two paths to other latent variables measured with [reflective] indicators” (Diamantopoulos and Winklhoffer 2001, p. 271), a condition this theoretical model did not meet. Figure 8 and Tables 10 and 11 present the results for the hypotheses.

Table 10 supports the hypothesis for rating bias (H1), because it shows that when the structural model included the common method factor, all of the items that shared the common method (the questionnaire) loaded significantly on it. Table 11 further supports H1 because it shows that the estimated relationships between constructs increased when methods were shared (supporting H1A and H1B). Table 11 suggests that knowledge bias affected the relationship between deep structure usage and performance (supporting H2A and H2B), but not the relationship between focused immersion and performance (not supporting H2C and H2D).

## Assessment of the Approach

The results from the demonstration support the usefulness of the proposed approach because the approach suggested using the multiview strategy, and the results demonstrate that rater choice could significantly affect the results. If rater choice had been ad hoc, not in accordance with the approach, the conclusions would have been very different because the results in Figure 8 show that different choices could change the significance of the IV→DV relationships, and the results in Table 11 show that different choices could affect the variance explained in the DVs up to 20 percent.

The results for each type of bias also highlight the potential benefits of the approach. In relation to rating bias, the results for H1A and H1B confirmed that such bias can alter estimated relationships. The results show that the approach is a useful way to identify such bias.

The multiview strategy made it possible to test the impact of knowledge bias and thus identify if different raters were indeed measuring different traits and if this difference matters empirically. For example, knowledge bias in measures of deep structure usage and performance led to differences in the relationships between these constructs (H2A and H2B). The methods that led to stronger relationships are not necessarily better; rather the measures may be measuring different traits, signaling the need for further investigation using the taxonomy in Figure 6.

Similarly, the differences in knowledge bias between different measures of focused immersion and performance did not appear to lead to differences in the relationships between constructs (H2C and H2D). The self-reported and observed ratings of focused immersion had a low correlation $r = 0.29$ , indicating that they were nevertheless rating different traits. Future research could use the taxonomy in Figure 6 to identify precisely how knowledge bias affected each rating and if other issues such as the content validity of the self-report and objective instruments also affected the results. Such research would contribute to recent work on ways to use self-report and objective methods to measure focused immersion and its converse, mind-wandering (Smallwood and Schooler 2006).

Neither of these research opportunities could have been discovered had the exercise used an existing approach for dealing with method bias. For example, if the demonstration had used an existing approach for dealing with common method bias (such as that in Podsakoff et al. 2003), it would have used only one rater class per construct. Consequently, there would have been no way to determine how the results varied across rater classes.

## Conclusion

Research methods serve as the interface between the theories of a discipline and the phenomena it studies. Minimizing method bias is crucial, therefore, because doing so helps a discipline reach a truer understanding of the phenomena it studies and build theories that more accurately account for these phenomena. The aim of this study was to provide an approach for dealing with method bias that builds on past research and extends it by addressing method bias as a whole. It extends past methodological work especially through its consideration of knowledge bias and the raters used to measure traits.

The approach aims to be generic, practical, and empowering. It aims to be generic in the sense that the criteria for choosing strategies for dealing with method bias and the steps involved in executing the two strategies offered are general enough to be applicable in any research in which accurate measurement is desired. It aims to be practical in the sense that it comprises just five steps and contains clear guidance for carrying out each step. It aims to be empowering in the sense that it offers researchers a way to reach a deeper understanding of the phenomena they study and the theories they test.

The empowering aspect is particularly important because it is easy for researchers to become bound by their methods. For example, a recent review of research on system usage found that most studies used self-report questionnaires, but that few studies offered good reasons for choosing this instrument (Burton-Jones 2005). In the absence of an approach for dealing with method bias, however, it is not surprising that researchers simply choose the instruments with which they are familiar. The proposed approach offers a way for researchers to make more informed choices of methods (raters, instruments, and procedures) and step outside the strictures of traditional methods when it is appropriate to do so.

Of the two strategies offered in this paper for dealing with method bias—monoview and multiview—the multiview strategy is the more novel and least restrictive. It will also be the more applicable strategy in most IS research because most constructs in IS are not defined in terms of a rater class, as Appendix C highlights, and because researchers often lack strong evidence that confirms one rater will provide the least bias. Uncovering the local meanings of constructs has long been a focus of interpretive research (Lee 1991), but positivist research can also contribute by identifying relevant observer classes and testing if their different viewpoints have major implications for theory and practice.

Although efforts by individual researchers in individual studies should be lauded, no single study can address method bias fully. Rather, this ambitious goal requires programmatic research. Just as it is often beneficial to have multiple independent raters to rate a construct, so it is beneficial for such programmatic research to be carried out by a community of researchers, not just one. Hopefully, the approach offered in this paper will enable and motivate the IS research community to improve its use of research methods and to support programs of research that have this objective.

As mentioned earlier, the proposed approach lacks selection criteria for choosing techniques that deal with method bias and evaluating the tradeoffs among these techniques. One way to address this shortcoming would be to add an ethical decision-making framework. Past research has suggested that such a framework could help researchers plan research programs and make methodological choices (Messick 1989).

Existing approaches for dealing with method bias have not incorporated this idea, but it could be a valuable addition to the proposed approach.

A final implication of this study stems from the results of the demonstration. The demonstration showed that individual studies can benefit from the approach by obtaining more accurate conclusions. Had the approach not been used in the demonstration, misleading conclusions would have been likely because the results showed that method bias could change the significance of path estimates and lead to changes of up to 20 percent in explained variance. The exercise also revealed new research opportunities. It appears, for example, that the ratings of both extrinsic and intrinsic usage aspects are sensitive to rater choice, but this may not always alter the estimated relationships between usage and performance. The results underline that research is still at an early stage of understanding how best to measure system usage and its impacts.

The demonstration exercise is meant to pave the way for follow-on studies. For example, much more research is needed to determine which raters and which instruments would be the best for measuring each trait in this study. Although this paper has provided some arguments and evidence to support the choices, more evidence is needed. More research is also needed to determine if different raters, instruments, or procedures would yield the same results. It would be particularly useful to extend the study to identify or create alternative instruments that could be used more easily in practice or that would provide more precise measurements. Finally, future studies could investigate the specific types of knowledge bias in this study and more precisely tease apart the individual and joint impacts of knowledge bias and rating bias on research results.

## About the Author

Andrew Burton-Jones is an assistant professor in the Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia. He holds a B.Comm. and M.I.S. from the University of Queensland and a Ph.D. from Georgia State University. His first research stream seeks a deeper understanding of the nature and consequences of system usage. His second seeks to improve methods used in systems analysis and design. He has published in Data & Knowledge Engineering, Database for Advances in Information Systems, Information & Management, Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and MIS Quarterly. Prior to his academic career, he was a senior consultant in a Big-4 consulting firm.
