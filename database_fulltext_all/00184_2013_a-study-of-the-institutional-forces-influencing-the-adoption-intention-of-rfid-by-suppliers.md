# A study of the institutional forces influencing the adoption intention of RFID by suppliers

Ming-Chih Tsai <sup>a,1</sup>, Kee-Hung Lai <sup>b,2</sup>, Wei-Che Hsu <sup>a,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Department of Marketing, National Chung-Hsing University, Taichung City, 402, Taiwan

<sup>b</sup> Department of Logistics and Maritime Studies, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hung Hom, Kowloon, Hong Kong

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 7 October 2011 Received in revised form 25 July 2012 Accepted 4 September 2012 Available online 24 September 2012

Keywords: Diffusion of innovation Institution theory RFID Relational investment

## A B S T R A C T

We extended the application of Roger’s model for evaluating the institutional forces influencing the adoption intention of RFID by retailer’s suppliers. Drawing on institutional theory, we examined how different institutional forces experienced by retailer’s suppliers were related to their relational investment on inter-organizational information sharing. Data collected from 130 maior Taiwanese suppliers was used in our empirical analyses. We found that relational investment was the most critical adoption factor, which also indirectly enhanced organizational readiness for technological adoption intention. From a Taiwanese perspective, our findings should be helpful in understanding what factors significantly impact the adoption of RFID technology.

## 1. Introduction

Retailers have recognized the importance of the distribution channel in enhancing their performance and have implemented supply chain management (SCM) to help by decreasing costs [13]. In a supply chain, information sharing is increasingly emphasized by enterprises in pursuing mutual beneficiary goals, facilitating inter-organizational collaborations, building trust and developing long-term relationships [25]. In the past, barcodes were adopted in the retail sector for identifying products, but major retailers (e.g., Wal-Mart, Metro, and Tesco) have now required their suppliers to adopt radio frequency interface devices (RFID) as the new identification device.

RFID systems allow tighter inventory control in the supply chain, resulting in cost savings, improved customer services, reduced stock-out, and customer tracking [27]. As suppliers are essential components of the supply chain, it is important for retailers to acknowledge the perspective of suppliers in interorganizational technology adoption.

The theory of diffusion of innovation (DOI) has been widely used to explain technology adoption resulting from the relationship between perceived innovation attributes and adoption intention [9]. Although prior studies before have confirmed the influence of DOI in supply chains, some have used Rogers’s innovation diffusion model to explain the behavior of supply chain suppliers and others have confirmed its influence on supply chain collaborative relationships [19]. But a major weakness lies in a lack of consideration for inter-organizational and environmental influences [14]. A large retailer with dominant market share is able to pressurize the technological adoption decision of their suppliers, initiating information sharing, and mandating compliance by its suppliers in the suppliers. Prior studies have found that supply chain collaboration [21] and inter-organizational cooperation were the major success factors in implementing supply chains. Whilst trust is required for a collective adoption of interorganizational system, complexities arise when the adoption of RFID conflicts with individual firms’ specific objective. However, analytical models examining the social behaviors pertinent to the adoption of technology sharing are limited [17].

Today, there seems to be a lack of unified view on how factors relating to environmental and institutional inputs affect interorganizational technology adoption [28]. Thus, recent studies of RFID technology have suggested the need to include other factors, such as institutional isomorphic forces [24]. It is important for retailers to understand, from a resource perspective, what investments by suppliers show their commitment and willingness in investing in inter-organizational technology.

Thus we wished to answer three questions:

What are the institutional isomorphic forces affecting the relational investment by suppliers on adopting RFID to service their retailer customers?

Does the relational investment affect suppliers’ organizational readiness for the adoption of RFID?

In what ways do institutional isomorphic forces become significant, contrasting to conventional innovation characteristics pertinent to RFID adoption?

## 2. Theories and hypotheses

DOI theory has been used to identify the different attributes influencing the adoption of an innovation; attributes to identify include relative advantage, complexity, compatibility, trialability and observability. At the organizational level, relative advantage and technical complexity have been identified as major factors influencing the willingness of enterprises to adopt or not to adopt a new technology. Relative advantage is the degree that a new innovation surpasses current practice. Its adoption benefit plays a key role in enhancing the rate of innovation adoption to the extent that the innovation itself is perceived as advantageous by adopters. In contrast, technical complexity involves the perceived difficulty in learning to use, and understand a new system or technology. Complexity of an innovation produces some uncertainty about the success of adopting the change. As such, high complexity has been recognized as an inhibitor to adoption [6]. Its cost and its environmental influences have not been fully investigated, highlighting a major weakness of DOI by ignoring the effect of organizational and environmental influences. Typically, these pressures originate from two sources due to the operational environment of the enterprise: macro-social and inter-organizational relationships [3].

## 2.1. Institutional environment

Institutional theory (INT) argues that organizations’ actions may be affected by the environment. Three institutional isomorphic forces may be involved: coercive, mimetic, and normative leading an entity to comply with political and institutional legitimacy in order to remain in a social network. These institutional forces prompt organizations to conform to the norms, traditions, and expectations of the institutional environment, and expedite the process of homogenization of effort at an interorganizational level. They originate from industries, governments, and cultures that shape the organizational structure and operations.

In supply chain, the relationship between supplier and retailer is bilateral; greater productivity gains can only be achieved through co-operation, and its success is dependent on the dyad’s willingness to commit to investments and combine intraorganizational resources in unique ways that may be specific to the relationship. Many have argued that firms facing environmental uncertainty have greater incentives to adopt interorganizational technology as an attempt to improve relationships and interdependence between organizations and other activities. In confirming this, buyers have been found to make investment in the development of suppliers to accrue tangible benefits [12]. In return, suppliers may be expected to share information, adapt production processes, or make additional investment in skills or technologies specific to the retailer. By suppliers working closely together with the retailer, competitive advantages can be created through information sharing, joint decision making, benefit sharing, and commitment resulting in positive performance.

INT posits that when organizations compete for resources, customers, political power, economic and social fitness, they are subject to pressures to conform to the behavior agreed to in the contract.

The evaluation of the RFID investment in SCM is still relatively under-researched [15]. Companies tend to focus on the applications of IT, paying scant attention to the development of interfirm relationships [16]. In a trading relationship, weak suppliers have been found to bolster their adaptive behaviors in an attempt to safeguard relational investments committed to dominant retailer.

To illustrate suppliers’ institutional pressure of making relational investment in retailers, we hypothesize three relationships:

H1a. Coercive pressures encountered by suppliers are positively associated with their relational investment in retailers.

H1b. Mimetic pressures encountered by suppliers are positively associated with their relational investment in retailers.

H1c. Normative pressures encountered by suppliers are positively associated with their relational investment in retailers.

The success of an information sharing network depends on how well the network is implemented by all the trading partners. To succeed as coordinators of supply chain, retailers seek opportunities to integrate their suppliers through information sharing [1]. Therefore, suppliers’ investment in the sharing system can extend beyond information interchange with a target to nurture cooperative and collaborative relationship with downstream customers. Further, a mutually beneficial inter-organizational relationship may also serve as an important driver for adopting related information sharing technology. Therefore:

H2. Relational investments by suppliers are positively associated with their adoption intention for RFID.

## 2.2. Organizational readiness

Organizations are increasingly required to incorporate new technology into business practices to improve competitiveness. Prior research indicates that the success of adopting an innovation depends on an organization’s preparation for the innovation. As an important factor for adoption of RFID, organizational readiness is used to measure the capabilities of an enterprise for adopting common systems with supply chain partners [23]. Successful implementation of an information sharing system occurs when sufficient organizational resources (such as developer and user time or funding and technical skills) are directed, first towards motivation, then towards sustaining the implementation. The relationship can be indicated as

H3. Organizational readiness is positively associated with the suppliers’ adoption intention towards RFID technology.

As SCM emphasize the building of power relationships and interdependence between organizations that affect their managerial process and activities, suppliers depend on a few dominant trading partners for revenues, their investments are oriented towards promoting long-term and stable relationship and encouraging retailers to increase their level of cooperation. External relationships are considered as part of the organizational structure, and can be viewed as a configuration of a firm’s resources. Through relational investment, a supplier may eliminate or reduce resource shortage and enhance organizational restructuring. The restructuring activities used to link and simplify processes help firms allocate, align, and utilize their internal and external resources. Thus we hypothesized:

## 3.2. The samples

In 2009, Taiwan was recorded as having 24,148 general merchandise chain stores, with over 1500 suppliers. We decided to contact 500 suppliers, whose financial capability seemed to be assured (i.e., they consisted of well-established suppliers possessing major market share). Further, these suppliers were either currently involved in frequent transactions or were partners with retailer. Thus the selected supplier candidates were consistently heavy traders with their retailing customers. The suppliers exhibited high tendencies to invest with their retailers, and were regarded as the potential pioneer adopters of RFID technology.

We followed the normal rules of questionnaire design while developing a 20 question survey instrument (see Appendix B). A 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) was used as the measure. Before the launching the survey, we performed a pilot to ensure validity and wording correctness of the questionnaire.

Spanning over a period of 9 months, the chosen supplier candidates were first contacted by email or telephone soliciting their agreement to participate in our research. Those that agreed were contacted by telephone to ensure their understanding of the purpose of this research, to answer any questions, and to ensure confidentiality in our use of their answers.

In total, 137 survey questionnaires were finally collected, of which 7 were invalid and thus excluded from further analysis.

Demographically, 64% of the respondents were division managers or above, with an average industry work experience of 13.5 years in the profession of purchasing or supply. On average, annual return of suppliers was US\$47 million. Where, IS costs included order processing, warehousing management, supply chain management, and CRM. ## 5. Findings and managerial implications

Our study extended the DOI model with an analysis on the social behaviors of suppliers when they adopt a new technology. An empirical study on downstream suppliers confirmed our assumptions on the effect of pressure in influencing supplier’s investment.

Recently, an increase in exit barriers for retailers has strengthened long-term relationship between them and suppliers. In turn, this has stimulated collaboration and information exchange [22]. Among the three types of institutional influences, (non)coercive pressure serves as the dominant power driver, followed by normative and mimetic influence. According to the factor loadings that are used to analyze the effects between the measurements of a construct (see Appendix A), retailer’s noncoercive powers such as favorable supply terms and promotion assistance are more effective than any negative coercive powers such as sanction and refusal to deal. The focal firm’s economic outcomes decrease if the partnering firm exercises punishments.

Our study found that suppliers had to be socially acceptable to secure status within the industry. The influences from trading partners, government and public were found to have a very similar effect.

Mimetic power may depend on the system’s development stage. As RFID technology is still at an early stage in Taiwan, focal retailers or suppliers as paragons for imitation are not common in industry.

In terms of relational investments, suppliers sometimes position themselves to form moderate relationship with retailer. Apparently, suppliers are more willing to engage in a bond that increases mutual communications for shared vision and cultural adaptation than to provide financial incentives for lower mutual dependence. Until trading parties acknowledge mutual shared values, suppliers are hesitant towards investing in bonds like information exchange, service/productivity enhancement, and joint resource investment with retailers.

According to the factor loadings, suppliers’ technological expertise is the principal requirement when determining readiness for RFID adoption.

In our study, suppliers’ organizational readiness was found to be significantly affected by its intention to invest in relationships with suppliers. A strengthened buyer-seller relationship enables the development of more flexible structure and capabilities.

As the technology of RFID matures, associated uncertainty and risk concerns outweigh potential benefits. A large variety of enterprises have been found to employ RFID to improve their operation efficiency and to gain a competitive advantage [2]. Information is often less readily available to the suppliers. In turn, which may increase suppliers’ concern about the technical complexity involved and risk associated in adopting RFID technology. Furthermore, suppliers may adopt innovative technological solutions to reduce uncertainty and sustaining their relationship with the retailers. Therefore, retailers are advised to work more closely with suppliers to lower risk concerns and increase their adoption of RFID technology.

## 6. Limitations

There are several limitations in our study. First, it results merely reflect Taiwanese perspectives. Different cultural contexts in different business settings may generate different results. In addition, surveys targeting organizational representatives tend to have lower response rates than those targeting individuals. A larger sample would have allowed a more robust modeling approach. Moreover, the use of RFID-related technology is still at an early stage. The impact of this technology is thus not yet known.
