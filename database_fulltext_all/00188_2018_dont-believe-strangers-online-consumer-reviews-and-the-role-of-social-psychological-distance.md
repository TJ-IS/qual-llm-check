# DON’T BELIEVE STRANGERS: ONLINE CONSUMER REVIEWS AND THE ROLE OF SOCIAL PSYCHOLOGICAL DISTANCE

## Abstract

Participants in online consumer review (OCR) communications are complete strangers. There is barely any research that has examined the relationship established between these participants. Nevertheless, when the receiver evaluates an OCR, he/she connects with the reviewer and perceives the social psychological distance from him/her. This study aimed to analyse whether the social psychological distance is the underlying mechanism that mediates the effect of OCR aspects on the receiver’s responses. The OCR aspects tested are profile photo, linguistic style and reported experience, while final variables reflect responses related to the review and the product.

Keywords: Online consumer reviews; Social psychological distance; Mediating effect; Profile photo; Linguistic style; Reported experience.

## Highlights:

Three OCR aspects—profile photo, linguistic style and reported experience—determine the social psychological distance.

Social psychological distance mediates the effect of OCR aspects on the receiver’s responses.

Less psychological distance between strangers improves the receiver’s responses.

## 1. Introduction

Your family wants to go on holiday next summer. You have already chosen the destination but need information about accommodation. Your 20-year-old son has offered to help you to choose the hotel. As your friends do not have enough information to give you recommendations, you decide to search on an online platform where other consumers post reviews and explain their past experiences with different hotels. After spending several days separately looking for information to choose the hotel that best suits your needs, you and your son compare the hotels that you have chosen. As you both have the same goal and have used the same online platform as your source of information, you should have chosen the same hotels. However, surprisingly, you realise that you have selected different hotels, basing your decisions also on different reviews. You also observe that your son has rejected some reviews that have been important for you.

From this situation, various questions arise. Why is one review relevant for one individual and not for others? Why does an individual value two apparently similar reviews differently? How does an individual evaluate an online consumer review? What aspects of online consumer reviews (OCRs) influence individuals’ decisions and purchase intentions? Answering these questions will advance our understanding of OCRs and social interactions of consumers.

OCRs are defined as a form of electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM), written by consumers on the Internet, that enable one to easily access the opinions and personal experiences of unknown individuals about different products (Blazevich et al. 2013; Purnawirawan, De Pelsmacker, and Dens 2012). OCRs are becoming one of the most important sources of information for consumers to make their purchase decisions (Ye et al. 2011), being considered less intrusive and more effective than recommendations provided by marketers such as advertising or promotion messages (Zhang, Craciun, and Shin 2010). Research reports have shown that, in 2014, 88% of consumers read and used online reviews, blogs and social information to make purchase decisions, trusting the reviews as much as they trust personal recommendations (BrightLocal 2014). Furthermore, 65% of consumers are more likely to use a business that has positive online reviews. Therefore, 92% of marketers indicate that OCRs are very important for their business performance.

Increasingly more number of studies are paying special attention to the effectiveness of OCRs. Studies on individuals<sup>1</sup> collect attitudinal data from surveys and experiments and examine what aspects of OCRs are

#

relevant to explain consumers’ decisions. All these studies apply a similar approach that tests the effect of including some aspects of OCRs (e.g. graphical content, the number of followers of the reviewer or the reviewer’s level of expertise) on receivers’ perceptions and behaviour, and they take for granted that participants interact to exchange information about a product without knowing each other. This approach usually assumes that the effect of the aspect tested is not conditioned by the relationship that the receiver establishes with the reviewer when he/she evaluates the OCR. As a result, the findings are generalised to all individuals and all contexts, generating ambiguity about the underlying psychological mechanism that drives the receiver’s responses.

This paper resolves this ambiguity by examining how OCR aspects influence the receiver’s responses and exploring the role of the social psychological distance in OCR communication to reflect the importance of the relationship between the participants. We propose that if OCR aspects are to influence the receiver, he/she should feel socially close to the individual who provides the information (i.e. the reviewer), although they are complete strangers and have no ties. Therefore, to understand why an OCR is influential not only ‘who says what’ (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2010) but also who comprehends ‘who says what’ (Huang, Tan, and Ke 2011) should be studied.

This paper aimed to study whether the social psychological distance perceived by the receiver when he/she evaluates an OCR is the underlying mechanism that mediates the effect of OCR aspects on his/her responses. Likewise, if an individual feels that he/she is socially similar to a reviewer, it is more likely that he/she will examine the reviewer’s opinion carefully, give greater credibility to his/her words and thus adapt his/her expectations of service quality to the OCR. By contrast, if an individual feels distant from a reviewer, he/she will probably not find the OCR useful and will not change his/her purchasing intentions.

We build our study by combining the social psychological distance concept based on the construal level theory (CLT) (Trope, Liberman, and Wakslak 2007) and research about eWOM and OCRs (Cheung et al. 2009, Lee and Lee 2009, Luo et al. 2015). Using these theoretical lenses, we select aspects that are always present in every OCR platform and give the receiver information about the reviewer’s physical, psychological and experiential characteristics. Specifically, we consider the profile photo, linguistic style and reported experience. These aspects directly determine the social psychological distance between participants and indirectly explain the receiver’s responses with regard to the review (i.e. credibility and usefulness) and the product (i.e. expectations of service quality and purchase intentions).

#

To develop this research, we focus on experience services and, specifically, on tourism services such as hotel reservations. Experience services have the characteristics that consumers do not know their attributes before the purchase and are not able to judge them (Lee and Youn 2009; Park and Nicolau 2015). Consumers require more external information to reduce uncertainty and perceived risks, so they usually rely on WOM and eWOM to make inferences about the quality of these services (Wirtz and Chew 2002). Likewise, OCRs, which typically contain advice and comments from experienced users, are perceived as unbiased and trustworthy, reduce the likelihood of regretting a decision and promote purchase decisions (Park and Lee 2009; Yoo and Gretzel 2008).

This paper makes three main contributions (see Figure 1). First, it goes beyond the traditional approach of OCR research, which is based on what aspects of OCRs determine the consumer’s responses, and studies how these aspects influence these responses, considering the relationship between the participants. In this way, the role of social psychological distance is established in a context of communication in which the participants are complete strangers and have no ties. Second, this paper establishes what aspects of OCRs determine social psychological distance, analysing three of these aspects that give indirect information about the reviewer. Third, it examines the receiver’s responses, considering not only perceptions about the OCR (i.e. credibility and usefulness) but also consequences related to the product, such as expectations of quality and purchase intentions. Thus, it addresses the situation that the individual encounters when he/she evaluates OCRs and determines whether the social psychological distance perceived by the receiver with regard to the reviewer conditions the effect of OCR aspects on his/her responses and decision-making.

## 2. Online Consumer Reviews

OCRs have undoubtedly become a powerful marketing force. They allow potential consumers to carry out exchanges of information with strangers and possess unprecedented properties of scalability and speed of diffusion (Hung and Li 2007). In recent years, there has been an explosion of literature focussed on the effects of OCRs on consumer purchase decisions. This literature develops studies based on individuals and postulates that OCRs promote personal influences by which a communicator (reviewer) transmits a content (message) to several communicatees (receivers), modifying their perceptions and behaviour (responses) (Cheung et al. 2009; Park and Kim 2008; Zhang and Watts 2008). These studies work within a context of communication defined as ‘who says what to whom and with what effect’ and try to determine the aspects of the message and/or of the reviewer that significantly influence the receiver’s responses (Cheung and Thadani 2012). Appendix A provides an integrated review of empirical studies about OCRs that analyse different kinds of variables related to the message, the reviewer, the receiver and the responses obtained.

We distinguish two groups of responses: those related to the review and those related to the product. Of the former, we highlight usefulness and credibility. Usefulness measures how consumers evaluate the information included in the OCR (Mudambi and Schuff 2010). If receivers perceive that this information is useful, they will have the intentions to follow the recommendation and will judge the OCR as a reference to make their decisions (Cheung, Lee, and Rabjohn 2008). Credibility is defined as the extent to which one perceives sources of information provided as unbiased, believable, true or factual (Hass 1981). If receivers perceive that the credibility is not high enough, they resist the persuasive content of the message. Thus, the receiver’s judgement on message credibility is fundamental to determine its acceptance (Luo et al. 2015).

With regard to responses related to the product, we underline expectations of quality and purchase (i.e. WOM) and generally unknown (i.e. eWOM) sources with the aim of determining what to expect from a specific product. Therefore, expectations of quality have been traditionally posited as a consequence of the WOM communication (Grönroos 1982). Mauri and Minazzi (2013) demonstrated that expectations of product quality differ depending on the information that the consumer has acquired during the evaluation of OCRs. Finally, purchase intentions are highlighted as one of the most important consequences of OCRs because information obtained in review platforms influences various steps of the consumer purchasing process (Buhalis and Law 2008; Goldsmith and Horowitz 2006).

Variables related to the message are those that have received the most attention in recent studies because the message acts as the main stimulus in the communication process. These studies try to determine what aspects of the message determine the receiver’s perceptions and behaviour, testing the effect of their presence versus their absence through experimental methodology. They analyse aspects such as the number of reviews (e.g. Liu 2006; Zhu and Zhang 2010), valence (e.g. Purnawirawan et al. 2015; Ye et al. 2011), argument quality (e.g. Awad and Ragowsky 2008; Cheung, Lee, and Rabjohn 2008) and review type (Park and Lee 2008; Xia and Bechwati 2008).

Variables related to the reviewer give information about the person who provides the OCR. During the OCR communication, consumers exchange knowledge with people outside their personal network. They have to form judgements about others’ credibility, without evaluating characteristics that appear in the offline environment, such as physical appearance or attractiveness (Cheung and Ho 2015; Fillieri 2015; Xu 2014). Therefore, the inclusion of information about the reviewer in an OCR, such as his/her expertise, photo, reputation or personal identity, develops the receiver’s trust, helps him/her evaluate the message and conditions his/her responses (Liu and Park 2015; Racherla and Frisk 2012; Vermeulen and Seegers 2009).

Studies of variables related to the receiver argue that the same OCR can engender different influences in different receivers because personal characteristics and prior experiences vary from person to person (Cheung and Thadani 2012). We can distinguish three groups of studies. The first group analyses the effect of characteristics intrinsic to the individual such as gender, scepticism or conformity (Awad and Ragowsky 2008; Lee and You 2009; Tsao et al. 2015). The second group addresses issues related to the individual’s knowledge and information adoption process (Gupta and Harris 2005; Lee and Koo 2012; Xia and Bechwati 2008). Finally, the third group considers the individual’s perceptions about the product such as involvement and familiarity (Doh and Hwang 2009; Park and Lee 2008; Vermeulen and Seegers 2009).

Despite the large number of studies about OCRs, they have a wide range of scope and findings and, however, do not reach common conclusions. From our analysis of the literature, we can state that certain important issues have been overlooked.

First, most of studies are focussed on only one type of response (review-related or product-related), and therefore, their analyses of the impact of OCRs are limited. Little research has simultaneously examined both types of responses.

#

Second, although studies based on individuals usually apply the same approach (based on the receiver’s perceptions and behaviour), they obtain mixed findings about the effect of the same OCR aspect. Therefore, it is difficult to reach general conclusions, and inconsistencies are generated. For example, while some studies about valence intensity found that OCRs with an overall rating of three can enhance source credibility and lead to positive attitudes about the product (Eisend 2006; Schlosser 2005), other studies state that extreme ratings with one or five stars are the most influential (Forman Ghose, and Wiesenfeld 2008; Pavlou and Dimoka 2006). Research on valence sense, which analyses the influence of OCRs depending on whether they are positive or negative, also reports different findings (Xue and Zhou 2010; Yang and Mai 2010). A first group of studies suggests that negative OCRs are the most influential, informative and difficult to resist (Lee, Rodgers and Kim 2009; Pan and Chiou 2011). A second group of studies determines that positive OCRs have a stronger impact than the negative ones on consumer attitudes, perceptions and purchase intentions (Casalo et al. 2015; Doh and Hwang 2009; Vermeulen and Seegers 2009). Finally, a third group of authors argue that OCR valence does not produce any significant influence (Cheung et al. 2009; Doh and Hwang 2009; Duan, Gu and Whinston 2008). These inconsistencies in the literature are justified by the existence of contextual moderating and mediating variables that reflect specific conditions and that have not been tested, which modifies the final results (e.g. Herr, Kardes, and Kim 1991; Purnawirawan et al. 2015). The effect of these variables on the influence of OCR aspects remains an unexplored area to date.

Third, research on OCRs tries to determine what aspects influence the receiver’s perceptions and behaviour. Nevertheless, it does not study how these aspects exert their influence, generating ambiguity about the underlying psychological mechanism that drives the receiver’s responses.

Fourth, studies that analyse variables related to the receiver only consider personal characteristics (see references in Appendix A). They do not address the relationship that the receiver establishes with the reviewer because it would seem to make no sense to test it in the OCR context where the participants are strangers, do not belong to the same group and, usually, interact just once. Therefore, we can deduce that the receiver does not feel the same subjective social pressure experienced in other online social platforms, such as virtual communities, blogs and brand communities, where participants have daily contact, are part of the same group or even share personal information (Baker and White 2010; Cheung and Lee 2010; Shiau and Chau 2016). Nevertheless, although peer pressure may not be significant in OCR communication, the mere reading of the information published by another person can make the receiver feel that he/she is establishing a relationship with that person. This feeling may determine the transmission of information and condition how he/she interprets the message (Racherla and Friske 2012). Thus, not studying the relationship between the participants of OCRs leads to a fragmented understanding of the phenomenon.

To sum up, we conclude that further research on OCRs is required to fill in the gaps identified in the literature and to explain the inconsistencies in past research.

## 3.- CLT and Psychological Distance

The CLT establishes that the individual forms different mental representations of the same object, event or other people, depending on the psychological distance that the individual perceives between the object and the self as the egocentric reference point (Liberman and Trope 1998; Trope and Liberman 2003; Trope and Liberman 2010). It posits that an object is psychologically distant when it is not part of the individual’s direct experience in the here and now and therefore has to be construed (Liviatan, Trope, and Liberman 2008). In this way, when the psychological distance from an object increases, the individual uses higher levels of mental construal, which means that the object is conceived in a more abstract, simple and decontextualised way. As the psychological distance from an object decreases, the individual employs lower levels of mental construal; therefore, more concrete, complex and detailed information is employed to represent the object (Henderson, Trope, and Carnevale 2006; Liberman and Trope 2008; Trope and Liberman 2010). Therefore, high psychological distance implies that the individual focuses his/her attention only on the superordinate and primary features of the object, while low psychological distance means that he/she considers subordinate and incidental features.

Psychological distance is a multi-faceted concept made up of four different dimensions, which jointly contribute to its formation: temporal distance (when; short vs. long), spatial distance (where; close vs. distant), social distance (who; in-group vs. out-group) and hypothetical distance (whether; certainty vs. uncertainty) (Trope, Liberman, and Wakslak 2007). These dimensions have an inherent common meaning and are interrelated; therefore, the activation of one distance dimension automatically activates the other dimensions (Trope and Liberman 2010). Several studies have addressed these interactions trying to determine the effects of experiencing two dimensions of psychological distance simultaneously on construal and consumer perceptions (e.g. Huang et al. 2016).

#

Social distance distinguishes between the self and others, similar and dissimilar others, or in-group and out-group individuals (Trope, Liberman, and Wakslak 2007). The individual usually establishes more intimate interactions with others who are socially close because he/she can observe their behaviour and accumulate information about their thoughts, feelings and personality (e.g. Andersen, Glassman, and Gold 1998). Therefore, the individual can make mental representations based on more concrete, contextual and detailed features of them than of more distant individuals (Idson and Mischel 2001; Prentice 1990).

Social distance has been operationalised as interpersonal similarity by many researchers (e.g. Liviatan, Trope, and Liberman 2008; Miller Downs, and Prentice 1998). Interpersonal similarity refers to the degree to which individuals who interact with one another are congruent or similar in certain attributes, and it is closely related to the concept of homophily (Herrero-Crespo, San Martin-Gutierrez, and Hernández-Mogollón 2015; Rogers and Bhowmik 1970). The assumption that interpersonal similarity conveys social closeness has formed the basis for research on comparison processes with regard to close and distant others (Liviatan, Trope, and Liberman 2008; Mussweiler, Rüter, and Epstude 2004). The less similar another is to oneself, the more socially distant he/she seems (Miller, Downs, and Prentice 1998; Mussweiler, Rüter, and Epstude 2004). Therefore, the behaviour of a dissimilar other (socially distant) requires a higher level of construal than the behaviour of a similar other (socially close), the former being evaluated through more primary and abstract aspects (Jones and Nisbett 1972; Kim, Zhang, and Li 2008).

Psychological distance has been widely documented in many fields of social psychology, emerging as a key factor for the study of different behaviours. Nevertheless, in the domain of marketing, research is scarce on the role of psychological distance in consumer purchase behaviour. In the OCR context, the few studies that consider psychological distance are usually focussed on temporal and spatial dimensions, not considering the effect of social distance (Huang Tan, and Ke 2011; Huang et al. 2016; Jin, Hu, and He 2014; Tangari, Burton, and Smith 2015). In this research, we propose the importance of the social dimension to address the relationship between the participants that is established during OCR communication.

Intuitively, when an individual gives a recommendation, close others will be more affected than distant others. Related findings in previous studies support this assumption (see e.g. Duhan et al. 1997). These studies assume that the effect of recommendations given by in-group individuals will differ from the effect of those made by out-group people (Maglio, Trope, and Liberman 2012). However, these studies do not analyse whether there are differences in the effects derived from the recommendations of out-group people because they consider that the effect of these recommendations will not vary.

We propose that strangers can establish a socially close relationship because of the interpersonal similarity perceived by the receiver in the reviewer when he/she evaluates an OCR. It is well accepted that people prefer interacting with those who are similar to themselves, often termed the ‘like-me’ principle (Laumann 1966), it being implicitly assumed that similar people share certain characteristics and have similar preferences (Herrero-Crespo, San Martin-Gutierrez, and Hernández-Mogollón 2015; Wangenheim and Bayon 2004). Even in cases in which the receiver does not aim to establish a relationship, if he/she perceives interpersonal similarity with a previously unknown reviewer, he/she will feel socially closer to him/her, so he/she evaluates the information differently and communication will be more influential and trustworthy. Social closeness emotionally involves the receiver with the reviewer and his/her message (e.g. Aron et al. 1991), predisposing the receiver towards greater levels of empathy and trust than would be expected in interactions among strangers (Liviatan, Trope, and Liberman 2008; Ruef, Aldrich, and Carter 2003).

## 4. OCRs and Social Psychological Distance

Research on traditional communication processes states that to achieve the effective transmission of a message, the communicatee should believe and trust in the communicator. However, OCR communication has particular characteristics that make it difficult for the communicatee to judge the trustworthiness and credibility of the source. The receiver cannot evaluate attributes of the reviewer such as attractiveness and physical appearance (Chatterjee 2001; Cheng and Ho 2015); the participants had not previously interacted and the receiver does not even know the reviewer’s identity. Therefore, the participants are total strangers, and it makes no sense to talk about close bonds and long-term interpersonal relationships.

Trying to compensate for these limitations, receivers seek cues in the OCRs that give information about the reviewer. These cues allow the receiver to define the kind of person who posted the OCR and condition his/her evaluation of the message (Liu and Park 2015). Recent studies analyse the effect of the information displayed in the profile (Xu 2014), such as photo and reputation (Park et al. 2013), and whether the inclusion of OCR cues about the reviewer improves the receiver’s perceptions of credibility and usefulness. Nevertheless,

they do not study how these cues determine the relationship that the receiver establishes with the reviewer and the psychological mechanism that, connected with this relationship, explains the receiver’s responses.

We propose that OCR aspects that give indirect information about the reviewer lead the receiver to perceive a certain social psychological distance from the reviewer, which conditions his/her evaluation of the review and influences his/her responses. Accordingly, we analyse three OCR aspects, namely profile photo, linguistic style and reported experience, and examine four receiver responses: credibility and usefulness (with regard to the review) and expectations of service quality and purchase intentions (with regard to the product). [A1]

These aspects were chosen for several reasons. First, they allow the receiver to delineate the reviewer’s characteristics in a broad way, considering three dimensions: external aspect (e.g. age and gender), internal psychology (e.g. personality, likes, politeness and education) and past experiences. Second, these aspects are included in most online social platforms and only influence the social distance between participants, without modifying spatial and temporal dimensions. Third, these aspects have been previously studied in other types of communication and have been related to concepts such as the degree of interpersonal similarity between people and the homophily perceived (e.g. Sweeney, Soutar, and Mazzarol 2008). Nevertheless, in the online context, the predominant focus to study these OCR aspects was based on their content, so it [A2]analysed the information provided about the product and not about the reviewer. We propose that these OCR aspects allow the receiver to obtain indirect information about the reviewer with which to define the similarity between them. The influence of these OCR aspects on the receiver’s responses is mediated by the social psychological distance perceived by the receiver when he/she evaluates the review (see Choi and Winterich 2013, who address the mediating effect of psychological distance between individuals and brands). ## 4.1. Profile photo

In the online environment, the profile photo is a self-created cue, a virtual representation of an individual and shows how he/she wants to be seen by others (Xu 2014). It reveals the individual’s identity and improves his/her social presence (Sussman and Siegal 2003; Tidwell and Walther 2002).

#

Some studies have tested the effect of the presence (vs. the absence) of the reviewer’s photo in OCRs on receiver’s responses (e.g. Park and Nicolau 2015). These studies have demonstrated that inclusion of the reviewer’s photo facilitates the information processing by increasing the receiver’s perceived credibility and, indirectly, his/her purchase intentions (Lee and Shin 2014).

According to the uncertainty reduction theory (Berger and Calabrese 1975), individuals generally find uncertainty in interpersonal relationships unpleasant; therefore, they try to reduce it by various methods (Xu 2014). In OCRs, the display of the profile photo is one such method that raises the receiver’s awareness of the reviewer and reduces the discomfort associated with uncertainty. The reviewer’s photo gives a vivid and visual cue that brings the impersonal interaction of reading an OCR closer to a face-to-face conversation with friends about a product. Likewise, it increases the receiver’s interest in the associated OCR and makes him/her feel that he/she is communicating with a real social entity. This feeling enhances the social affection of the receiver (Walther et al. 2011), eliciting emotional responses (Xu 2014). Therefore, it can be concluded that the inclusion of the reviewer’s photo in an OCR positively influences the receiver’s perceptions and responses, this influence being homogeneous for all individuals.

From the literature review, we accept that the inclusion of the reviewer’s photo allows the receiver to determine if he/she shares some physical features with the reviewer and establish the level of interpersonal similarity between them (Amit, Algom, and Trope 2009). Nevertheless, contrary to previous research, we do not consider that the inclusion of the reviewer’s photo per se exerts a significant effect on the receiver but that this effect depends on the social psychological distance that the receiver perceives with regard to the reviewer after seeing the photo. If the profile photo leads the receiver to feel socially close to the reviewer, it will positively influence the receiver’s perceptions about the review (i.e. credibility and usefulness). Moreover, close social psychological distance makes the receiver expect, think and behave in accordance with the OCR. On the contrary, if the photo causes the receiver to feel distant from the reviewer, it will not influence the receiver’s perceptions and purchase intentions. Therefore, we propose that the social psychological distance that the receiver perceives from the reviewer’s photo mediates the effect of the latter on the receiver’s responses. We formulate the following hypotheses:

H1a: The influence of the reviewer’s profile photo on the receiver’s perceived credibility of the review is mediated by the social psychological distance between them.

H1b: The influence of the reviewer’s profile photo on the receiver’s perceived usefulness of the review is mediated by the social psychological distance between them.

H1c: The influence of the reviewer’s profile photo on the receiver’s expectations of service quality is mediated by the social psychological distance between them.

H1d: The influence of the reviewer’s profile photo on the receiver’s purchase intentions is mediated by the social psychological distance between them.

## 4.2. Linguistic style

Linguistic style is defined as the way text is written, referring to the type of language used, syntactic and semantic elements included, and the rhetorical strategies applied (Korfiatis, García-Bariocanal, and Sánchez-Alonso 2012; Liu and Park 2015). It is closely connected with the capacity of the text to be read and understood (i.e. readability), which leads the individual to accept the information involved (Liu and Park 2015; Zakaluk and Samuels 1988). Recent research has demonstrated that an OCR written with a refined style has a positive impact on product sales because it can be understood more easily (Archak, Ghose, and Ipeirotis 2011; Shah and Oppenheimer 2008). This style reflects the reviewer’s sentiments and thus influences how the receiver perceives the message and the pricing power of firms (Archak, Ghose, and Ipeirotis 2011; Korfiatis, García-Bariocanal, and Sánchez-Alonso 2012).

Despite the importance of linguistic style in OCRs, few studies have analysed this subject, and most of them have focussed on the content and not on the reviewer information (Korfiatis, García-Bariocanal, and Sánchez-Alonso 2012). These studies analyse the message in an objective way and measure its abstractness applying classification schemes depending on levels such as descriptive action verbs, interpretative action verbs, state action verbs, state verbs or adjectives (see e.g. the Linguistic Categorization Model employed by Huang et al. 2016). Our approach to linguistic style is not related to the textual content of OCRs; therefore, we did not test the importance of the type and/or number of words included in the message. On the basis of principles of social psychology about interpersonal relationships, we suggest that consumers make their evaluations about an OCR with limited knowledge of the reviewer’s characteristics; therefore, they have to create fully formed impressions based mainly on linguistic aspects (Brown, Broderick, and Lee 2007; European Commission 2014). If the reviewer’s language matches the receiver’s code, he/she will invest less cognitive effort and will have a greater ability to understand the product evaluation (Liviatan, Trope, and

#

Liberman 2008). Moreover, the linguistic style affects the receiver’s perceptions about reviewers and allows him/her to get a rough idea about their personal profile (Ludvig et al. 2013), assessing characteristics such as their education, personality, intelligence, culture and ability to comment on a product. Therefore, the linguistic style of the OCR is not only related to the message but also to the reviewer and facilitates information about him/her depending on how the text is written (e.g. vocabulary, language and writing style).

We consider that the more similar the participants’ linguistic styles are, the greater interpersonal similarity will exist between them, and the closer social distance perceived by the receiver. This social distance will positively influence the receiver’s perceptions about the review, his/her expectations of quality and his/her purchase intentions being coherent with the content of the OCR. On the contrary, if there is certain dissonance in the participants’ linguistic style, the receiver will feel socially distant from the reviewer, so the linguistic style will not influence the receiver’s responses. Therefore, we suggest the following hypotheses, reflecting the mediating effect of the social psychological distance between the linguistic style of the OCR and the receiver’s responses:

H2a: The influence of the reviewer’s linguistic style on the receiver’s perceived credibility of the review is mediated by the social psychological distance between them.

H2b: The influence of the reviewer’s linguistic style on the receiver’s perceived usefulness of the review is mediated by the social psychological distance between them.

H2c: The influence of the reviewer’s linguistic style on the receiver’s expectations of service quality is mediated by the social psychological distance between them.

H2d: The influence of the reviewer’s linguistic style on the receiver’s purchase intentions is mediated by the social psychological distance between them.

## 4.3. The reported experience

OCRs can be divided into two types: attribute-based and experience-based (Huang et al. 2014; Pan and Zhang 2011). Attribute-based consumer reviews discuss the evaluation of a product from factual search-type information such as prices and product specifications. Experience-based consumer reviews are characterised by evaluations with consumer-oriented information that describe overall experiences in terms of usage situations (Bickart and Schindler 2001). While attribute-based consumer reviews are rational, objective and concrete, experience-based consumer reviews are emotional, subjective and abstract and reflect consumer feelings (Mantel and Kardes 1999; Pan and Zhang 2011; Park and Lee 2008; Xia and Bechwat 2008).

Most studies that analyse this aspect of OCRs try to determine which type of review is more useful for consumers and therefore which influences their behaviour more (e.g. Lee and Koo 2012; Park and Lee 2008). The results obtained in the literature are not consistent; three groups of papers can be distinguished.

The first defends attribute-based reviews and concludes that they are more informative, allowing the receiver to know the product beyond the particular reviewer’s point of view (Park and Lee 2008; Petty and Cacioppo 1984; Petty, Cacioppo, and Schumann 1983). These papers state that experience-based consumer reviews are biased because they are more personal and can be interpreted subjectively. In fact, experiencebased consumer reviews are not supported by arguments and will differ between individuals with different experiences (Park and Lee 2008).

The second group establishes the importance of experience-based reviews. They declare that the consumption experience is subjective by nature; therefore, when consumers read OCRs, they are looking not only for numerical ratings and objective information about product quality but also for other’s opinions and usage experiences (Mauri and Minazzi 2013; Park and Kim 2008). Consumers perceive reviews that communicate personal stories and contain strong emotions and feelings as more useful (Lin and Zhang 2011).

The third group of papers considers that this aspect of OCRs is not relevant because they find no significant different effects between attribute-based and experience-based reviews (Klein and Ford 2003).

The present study does not aim to compare the two types of OCRs again because this would only add to the confusion. Instead, it will try to determine why the same experience-based review influences one consumer and not others. From our findings, we will explain the discordance in the literature regarding the effect of experience-based consumer reviews.

We propose that the influence of experience-based reviews depends on their connection with the receiver’s prior experiences. If the experience-based review is consistent with the receiver’s own life, he/she feels more like an actor than a mere observer (Bansal and Voyer 2000; Xie, Zhang, and Zhang 2014). The receiver will evaluate the information in a self-referential manner, feel more emotional intensity and perceive psychological proximity with the reviewer (Boven et al. 2010; Maglio, Trope, and Liberman 2012; Mauri and Minazzi 2013). This proximity will increase the receiver’s perceptions of credibility and usefulness and

#

will make him/her expect quality in accordance with the review. On the contrary, if the experience-based review differs from the receiver’s experiences, he/she will not experience strong emotions and will feel distant from the reviewer, and his/her responses will not be conditioned by the review. Therefore, we propose that social psychological distance mediates the influence of experienced-based reviews on the receiver’s responses and hypothesise the following relationships:

H3a: The influence of the experience-based review on the receiver’s perceived credibility of the review is mediated by the social psychological distance regarding the reviewer.

H3b: The influence of the experience-based review on the receiver’s perceived usefulness of the review is mediated by the social psychological distance regarding the reviewer.

H3c: The influence of the experience-based review on the receiver’s expectations of service quality is mediated by the social psychological distance regarding the reviewer.

H3d: The influence of the experience-based review on the receiver’s purchase intentions is mediated by the social psychological distance regarding the reviewer.

## 5. Methodology

## 5.1. Research design

Although a growing number of papers about OCRs are based on firm data (see Godes and Silva 2011; Moe and Trusov 2011), direct consumer surveys are still valid to capture motivations and perceptions (e.g. Awad and Ragowsky 2008; Gupta and Harris 2005; Lee and Shin 2014; Liu and Park 2015). The aim of this research is to analyse the consumer’s perceptions and behaviour that cannot be directly observed; therefore, surveys are the most appropriate research methodology for data collection and statistical analyses (Hair, Bush, and Ortinau 2006).

Our field study lasted between 7 and 10 minutes and was divided into four sections. First, a filter question was asked to focus the study on individuals who frequently use OCRs and to ensure the suitability of the participants. Second, the study included questions related to the participant’s experience as an Internet user, the number of hours per week that he/she spends on the Internet and his/her use of OCR platforms. Third, the study explained the purchase situation into which the participant was asked to put himself/herself. This situation addressed the choice of a holiday hotel. Then, four positive OCRs that the participant had to read and evaluate were presented (see Appendix B). The number of OCRs was chosen on the basis of 2014 statistics, which stated that 56% users need to read between two and six reviews to make their purchase decision (BrightLocal 2014). These OCRs reflect personal experiences. Trying to make the experience as realistic as possible, they were created using real reviews posted on TripAdvisor.com, the largest online travel network in Europe (Casalo et al. 2015). The length, positive valence and time of publication were kept constant in the four OCRs. Price information was excluded.

Finally, the study included a questionnaire about (1) the OCR aspects (profile photo, linguistic style and reported experience), (2) perceptions about the review (credibility and usefulness) and (3) the participant’s evaluations with regard to the purchase and to the product (attitude towards the purchase, purchase intentions and expectations of service quality).

## 5.2. OCR aspects and operationalising measures

To determine what OCR aspects are the most relevant, we used a three-step process. First, we performed a thorough review of the literature on eWOM and OCRs to obtain a compendium of the most frequently analysed aspects. Then, we removed, on the one hand, aspects that refer exclusively to the content and not to the reviewer (e.g. review length, valence, volume and variability) and, on the other hand, aspects that, despite being related to the reviewer, do not deal with the receiver’s perceptions about similarity or the social psychological distance (e.g. the reviewer’s number of followers and reputation). Second, we evaluated online social platforms and eliminated aspects that either are not applied at a general level or influence the individual’s perceptions of other psychological distances, such as the geographical location or the publication time. Third, we conducted two pretests aimed at OCR users and social media experts. In these pretests, participants had to evaluate the OCR aspects that resulted from the two previous steps, considering the information that each aspect gives about the reviewer as the main criterion. From this process, the profile picture, linguistic style and reported experience were finally chosen.

Following the recent trend in research of using longer scales, the latent constructs were measured on an 11-point Likert scale, the lowest perception being scored with 0 (strongly disagree) and the highest with 10 (strongly agree) (see Table I). For the variable expectations of service quality, individuals had to score on three indicators, 0 meaning extremely poor and 10 extremely good. This trend in research demonstrates that 11-point scales perform better than other scales of five or seven points and highlights the following reasons (Darbyshire and McDonald 2004; Loken et al. 1987). First, confirmatory factor analysis results for 11-point

#

scales consistently have higher reliability and validity coefficients and lower invalidity coefficients (e.g. Alwin 1997; Loken et al. 1987). This higher quality of 11-point scales, preferably with labelled fixed reference points, verifies the results of a meta-analysis conducted by Saris, Satorra and Coenders (2004) and has also been found to hold for cross-national studies (Fitzgerald and Widdop 2004). Second, 11-point scales generate mean scores similar to five- and seven-point scales (Dawes 2002; 2008), but the dispersion in responses is higher than that in scales with fewer categories. Therefore, 11-point scales produce data with higher levels of variance (coefficient of variation) and have a greater discriminatory power (Myers 1999, p.135). Eleven-point scales allow the respondents to provide a greater spread of ratings, reduce the incidence of extreme responses and increase the sensitivity of the scales (Darbyshire and McDonald 2004; Wittink and Bayer 1994). Finally, the evaluation system usually applied in the country analysed, i.e. Spain, is based on scales from 0 to 10, so Spanish individuals are familiar with the system of rating ‘out of 10’. Therefore, employing these scales reduces the time required by the individual to respond, diminishes the possibility of confusing two adjacent categories and facilitates the interpretability of the findings (Dawes 2002).

Scales for the profile photo, linguistic style and reported experience factors were obtained from research on the concept of interpersonal similarity. Specifically, we considered the following works: Ayeh, Au and Law (2013), Chu and Kim (2011) and Liviatan, Trope and Liberman (2008), among others. An initial set of items was proposed to measure the latent constructs, but it was necessary to adapt them to the context of OCRs because of the lack of such scales. The adaptation had the objective of guaranteeing the face validity of the measurement instruments. Face validity is defined as the degree to which respondents judge that the items are appropriate to the targeted construct (Anastasi 1988) and is habitually confused with content validity (Casalo, Flavian, and Guinaliu 2007). However, content validity is the degree to which items correctly represent the theoretical content of the construct, and it is guaranteed by the in-depth literature review undertaken. Face validity was tested through a variation of the Zaichkowsky (1985) method, whereby each item is qualified by a panel of experts as ‘clearly representative’, ‘somewhat representative’ or ‘not representative of the construct of interest’. Finally, items were retained if a high level of consensus was observed among the experts (Lichtensteins, Netemeyer, and Burton 1990).

## 5.3. Data collection and sample profile

To carry out the definitive study, participants were recruited through an online panel of a market research company<sup>2</sup> that was composed of frequent Internet users. This company designed a website specifically for this research project and delivered the link associated with the study to its online panel through email. Moreover, the company manages access to participants, response collection and database design. Data were collected from a sample of Spanish participants in June 2015. A total of 522 responses were received. In total, 121 of these were discarded for various reasons, such as outliers and missing cases. A total of 401 usable questionnaires were obtained.

All participants were residents of Spain. We collected data in this country for several reasons. First, the market of Spain is different from those of the European leaders in e-commerce, which have been frequently addressed by online research, and provides an adequate representation of other understudied countries. According to the Eurostat statistics (2015), 42% of Spanish people have chosen the Internet as a shopping channel, whereas European leaders surpass 70% (e.g. 75% in Luxemburg and 79% in Denmark). The participation of Spain in social networking is also similar to the European mean (approximately 50%) but shows differences of up to 17 points with the European leaders. Despite these differences, at present, the absolute number of e-shoppers and participants in social networks is higher in Spain, and it has great potential for development. Second, we chose Spain because of the important role of the sector to which the product analysed belongs. This country occupies a key position in the international tourism sector, being among the top 10 most visited places in the world. Third, the study of the development of the Internet in the tourism sector reveals that although Spain does not lag far behind others in terms of online penetration, the

Because our independent and dependent variables were obtained through a single collection method, our results could be affected by common method bias. To deal with this issue, we used some of the procedural remedies suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003) and MacKenzie and Podsakoff (2012) during data collection and analysis. First, during data collection, the anonymity of the participants’ responses was guaranteed and the exact aim of the study was not disclosed to avoid conditioning the participants’ responses. Second, questionnaire items related to the dependent variables were placed after indicators that measured independent variables. In addition, the participants could not access their responses to previous questions so that their subsequent responses would not be determined by their previous answers. Finally, to statistically address the issue of common-method bias, we performed Harman’s single-factor test (Morgan, Kaleka, and Katsikeas 2004; Podsakoff et al. 2003). This technique consists of loading all the variables onto an exploratory factor analysis and examining the unrotated factor solution to determine the number of factors necessary to account for the variance of the data. Consequently, if there was a significant amount of common method bias in the data, a single factor would emerge from the factor analysis or one general factor would account for the majority of the covariance among the measures (Morgan, Kaleka, and Katsikeas 2004; Podsakoff et al. 2003). An exploratory factor analysis with no rotation on one construct was conducted. The results indicated that the most variance explained by a single factor was approximately 45%. Therefore, Harman’s test suggested that common method bias was not a major issue in our research.

## 7. Contributions, Implications and Future Lines

7.1. Theoretical contributions

Although research on OCRs is widespread and the subject has been approached from different disciplines, several gaps remain in the literature. This paper tries to fill some of these gaps and makes the following contributions.

First, this research studies not only what aspects of OCRs influence the receiver’s responses but also how the influence of these aspects comes about, considering the importance of developing a close social relationship between the participants. Receivers and reviewers are complete strangers who interact just once through an OCR with the aim of exchanging information about a product. Therefore, hardly any research has examined the relationship between them. Nevertheless, when the receiver evaluates the OCR, he/she establishes a connection with the reviewer and perceives the social psychological distance from him/her. This social psychological distance acts as the underlying mechanism that mediates the effect of OCR aspects on the receiver’s responses.

Second, this research explores what aspects of OCRs determine the social psychological distance. Contrary to previous studies that focus on aspects of OCRs related to the review (such as valence and sequence), we consider aspects of OCRs that give indirect information about the reviewer. Likewise, we go beyond the demographic characteristics of the reviewer, such as age and gender, and deal with aspects inherent to the OCR: profile photo, linguistic style and reported experience. Receiver–reviewer similarity in these aspects of OCRs is a result of common interests and worldviews and explains the formation of expressive bonds on the basis of the social psychological distance. The mediation of the social psychological distance is different for these aspects because it fully mediates the effect of the profile photo and partially mediates the linguistic style and the reported experience. Therefore, we can state that the development of close social psychological distance is essential for the profile photo to influence the individual, while it is important but not necessary for the other aspects.

Third, this research simultaneously examines several receiver responses considering perceptions about the review, such as usefulness and credibility, as well as responses related to the product, such as expectations of quality and purchase intentions. In this way, we can confirm the effectiveness of OCRs on the basis of a wide range of responses.

These contributions allow us to explain why studies that analyse the effect of including a certain aspect of OCRs, such as the profile photo or the explanation of the individual’s experiences, obtain mixed and inconsistent findings. The inclusion of these aspects does not influence all receivers in the same way because their effects depend on the similarity that each receiver perceives regarding each reviewer and therefore on the participants’ characteristics. It is not possible to draw general theoretical conclusions about a specific aspect of OCRs that are applicable to all individuals, as previous studies have suggested. Premises about the relevance of an OCR aspect should consider the social psychological distance that this aspect promotes between participants. Testing the direct influence alone between OCR aspects and the receiver’s responses has led to a limited understanding of the phenomenon.

## 7.2. Managerial implications

In recent years, it has been demonstrated that OCRs are a significant source of information for consumers; therefore, the analysis of how this information influences their decisions can facilitate the identification of new needs, the implementation of effective marketing strategies and the achievement of competitive advantages for businesses (Jun, Vogt, and MacKay 2010; Phillips et al. 2015; Yacouel and Fleisher 2012). From this research, we highlight three managerial implications: The firm should encourage the posting of OCRs that include the profile photo, different linguistic styles and positive personal experiences with the product.

OCRs that show the consumer’s photo and that explain personal testimonials in a good linguistic style will generate a greater social proximity of the receiver to the reviewer and therefore will boost the receiver’s perceptions of usefulness and credibility. Moreover, this type of OCR can create anticipated experiences in the receiver about the product, modifying his/her expectations of quality and purchase intentions. Therefore, these aspects of OCRs can generate a socially close relationship between the participants and improve the effectiveness of OCRs. Consequences derived from these aspects of OCRs are even more important if we consider that they can influence individuals across social groups. Finally, it can be highlighted that the inclusion of different linguistic styles and personal experiences also directly influences the receiver’s responses due to their relationship with the content of the message.

Reviewers with different profiles acting as referrals will identify interesting targets and provide new customers.

Posting OCRs from different reviewers’ profiles will make a wide range of potential customers feel identified with them, and therefore, they will have a greater predisposition to purchase the product. If a particular business (e.g. a hotel) aims to address potential customers (e.g. retired people over 70), it should try to attract reviewers socially close to these individuals, encouraging them to discuss their positive

consumption experiences in OCRs. Socially close reviewers allow the firm to access new targets more naturally and directly than other media related exclusively to the firm (e.g. promotions and advertising). Firms should encourage reviewers to act as social connectors and referrals with non-customers who are similar people. Close social distance between OCR participants leads to the creation of influential groups that condition customer perceptions and purchases.

The interaction between participants that occurs when they are evaluating OCRs may give rise to socially close relationships that promote the creation of influential groups of customers. The interaction of customers in these influential groups will increase the value of their experience and generate effects consistent with the literature on ‘hot-cold empathy gaps’ (Loewenstein 1996) because when the receiver evaluates an OCR written by a close reviewer, he/she is in a hot state and considers the consequences as socially close. Moreover, these groups generate a contagion effect that determines the perceptions and behaviours of individuals who, although they do not directly know the product, interact with and rely on close reviewers. Firms should try to take advantage of these influential groups to strengthen their relationship with their customers because a satisfactory relationship with a certain reviewer will lead to additional positive responses from other socially close individuals (friends of my friends are my friends).

## 7.3. Limitations and future lines

Our study presents some limitations that should be considered in future research.

First, we should highlight that this research has collected data from questionnaires addressed to a sample of users of online social platforms that usually read OCRs. Psychological variables such as individuals’ perceptions, attitudes and intentions have been analysed. Nevertheless, objective data about the individuals’ behaviour have not been tested. In future research, it would be interesting to observe and collect information about the individual’s behaviour during his/her experience in an OCR platform, studying the effect of OCR aspects on business sales and profits.

Second, we have studied the influence of positive OCRs, but we have not checked whether the social psychological distance is equally relevant for negative OCRs. In future research, it would be interesting to deal with negative OCRs and compare the mediating role of social psychological distance in both types of reviews.

Finally, we should highlight that we have only considered social psychological distance. In future studies, we will test the interaction between this distance and the other dimensions of the concept. We will determine whether the fact that an OCR is outdated affects the perceived closeness to the reviewer (i.e. interaction between temporal and social psychological distances).

## 8. Conclusions

The present research addresses the importance of the relationship that participants establish during the evaluation of OCRs. Although these participants are complete strangers and have not previously interacted, the social psychological distance perceived by the receiver is crucial to explain the effectiveness of OCRs, conditioning his/her perceptions and intentions to purchase the product. Findings demonstrate that social psychological distance plays a fundamental mediating role, channelling the effect of OCR aspects, the profile photo, linguistic style and/or reported experience, on the receiver’s responses.

The profile photo included in the OCR significantly influences the receiver’s responses as long as it makes the receiver feel similar and therefore close to the reviewer. The profile photo is a cue that gives information about the reviewer’s physical appearance, age, gender, lifestyle and hobbies. It deletes barriers inherent to computer-mediated communication and allows the reviewer to develop a more social and real presence. If the profile photo makes the receiver perceive a certain similarity to the reviewer, he/she will consider that the social distance between them is small, and the OCR will be effective.

The linguistic style of the OCR determines its readability and the effort required of the receiver to evaluate the message. If the receiver’s linguistic style is similar to that employed by the reviewer in the OCR, he/she will feel identified with him/her, diminishing the distance between them. Thus, he/she will perceive more credibility and usefulness, and this will condition his/her expectations of quality and purchase intentions in accordance with the OCR.

The experience reported in the OCR allows the receiver to know the reviewer from a personal point of view because the latter is sharing his/her previous experiences about the consumption of the product. From these experiences, the receiver develops some level of empathy with the reviewer, although they are strangers because he/she perceives that he/she has already had similar experiences and felt similar emotions to those described in the OCR. Sharing personal experiences creates a communication of trust that lowers the social distance between participants. As a result, the reported experience, in particular, and the experience based review, in general, will have subjective consequences that will be intense and arousing.

#

It is interesting that these aspects do not influence the individual in the same way. Although the profile photo influences the individual’s responses only indirectly through social psychological distance, linguistic style and the reported experience exert a double effect both directly and indirectly through the mediating variable. This difference is probably because linguistic style and the reported experience aspects are important for the information provided about the reviewer and for their linkages with the content of the message. However, the profile photo is exclusively related to the reviewer, so it will influence the receiver only if the social psychological distance perceived is close.

Overall, findings have demonstrated how individuals evaluate OCRs, verifying the mediating role of the social psychological distance as the underlying mechanism that, linked to the relationship between participants, drives the receiver’s responses. Findings demonstrate that the mere inclusion of certain OCR aspects does not per se affect the receiver’s perceptions and behavioural intentions. In fact, the social psychological distance that these aspects make the receiver perceive with regard to the reviewer is what really influences the receiver’s responses. Therefore, an OCR will have a positive influence on a receiver if the OCR aspects allow him/her to feel close to the reviewer, while the same OCR will not affect another receiver if these aspects distance him/her from the reviewer. Similarly, two OCRs containing, apparently, the same information have different influences on the same individual because of the social psychological distance that he/she perceives with regard to the reviewers who wrote them.
