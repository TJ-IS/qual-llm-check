# Do less active participants make active participants more active? An examination of Chinese Wikipedia

Yan Lin<sup>⁎</sup>, Yan Chen

Department of Management Science and Engineering, Dalian Maritime University, Dalian 116026, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Knowledge collaboration Online communities Organizing form Less active participant Wikipedia Causality

## A B S T R A C T

Knowledge collaboration in online communities often involves a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant proportion of less active participants who make only scant contributions to their communities. This has become a pervasive characteristic of collaborative work organized through this new form. However, there is ambiguity regarding the role of less active participants in knowledge collaboration in online communities. In this study, we probe the indirect in<sup>fl</sup>uence of less active participants' contributing behaviors on the quality of knowledge collaboration. We propose the fol lowing two-step causal path: 1) less active participants' participation causes active participants to increase contributions and 2) the additional contributions of active participants that result from less active participants' participation substantively improves the quality of knowledge collaboration. Using the edit data of featured articles in the Chinese Wikipedia, we examine the proposed causal path. The main <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study are as follows: the productivity of active participants of a Wikipedia article increases when they are triggered by less active participants' editing activities; the additional edits of active participants triggered by less active participants can improve the quality of an article; and less active participants play a major role in reviving the editing work of dormant articles. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that less active participants play a substantial role in knowledge collaboration in online communities, as their contributing behaviors sustain collaborative work and eventually improve the quality of outputs

## 1. Introduction

With the widespread popularity of internet technologies, such as web 2.0, web 3.0, social media, and the wiki, there has been an increase in knowledge collaboration in online communities. Knowledge collaboration in online communities involves “individual acts of ofering knowledge to others as well as adding to, recombining, modifying, and integrating knowledge that others have contributed” and then “benefit them personally, while contributing to the community's greater worth” (pp. 1224) [14]. Examples of knowledge collaborations in online communities include online editors' collaboration for revising articles on Wikipedia, volunteer developers' collaboration for developing software projects on SourceForge, and users' collaboration for remixing music on ccMixter.

The main purpose of these types of online applications is to produce high-quality knowledge-based products [7,25]. For example, Wikipedia co-founder Jimmy Wales has described Wikipedia as “an efort to create and distribute a free encyclopedia of the highest possible quality to every single person on the planet.”<sup>1</sup> An online community is a phenomenal platform for quickly gathering a large number of participants with highly diverse cognitive pro<sup>fi</sup>les and interests [7,19,38]. Thus, knowledge collaboration in online communities can lead to high-quality outputs. For example, open source software products have fewer bugs than competing closed-source software,<sup>2</sup> and the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c contents of Wikipedia are almost as accurate as that of Britannica [17].

Knowledge collaboration in online communities has organized itself in a new form that is very di<sup>f</sup>erent from the traditional ways [6,36]. This new organizing form lacks a centralized organizational structure [31], stable membership [14], and traditional mechanisms to divide task and assign subtasks to knowledge workers [3]. Individuals constantly participate in the collaborative work without formally prede<sup>fi</sup>ned roles and decide when and how to work [41].

Therefore, the following phenomenon is often observed in online communities: a small group of participants are very active and contribute most contents in the collaborative work, whereas several others that are less active contribute only an insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant amount [7,11,15]. This phenomenon is prevalent for activities concerning Wikipedia article editing [7,26,47], open source software development projects [13,43], and online music remixing [21,42].

The existence of many less active participants has been a non-negligible characteristic of knowledge collaboration in online communities, but this phenomenon rarely occurs in knowledge work in traditional contexts. The question that arises regarding knowledge collaboration in online communities is what is the role of less active participants in the production of high-quality knowledge? We argue that the research on this question is important for two reasons.

On the one hand, an inquiry into this question will deepen our understanding of the new organizing form wherein knowledge collaboration can occur. Faraj and his colleagues analyzed the characteristics of knowledge collaboration in online communities and stated that “failure to examine the critical role of … inactive participants in the functioning of the community is to ignore that passive (and invisible) partici pation may be a step toward greater participation” (pp. 1226) [14]. Aaltonen and Kallinikos argued that in order to capture the essential logic of knowledge creation under the new form of organizing, scholars should realize “what is inactive [but] is not useless” and make clear the process that “what is immediately inactive may well shape both what becomes active and its quality” (pp. 187) [1]. These viewpoints emphasize that the less active participants' contributing behaviors may play a key role in the new form of organizing. Studies focusing on less active participants' contributing behaviors shed light on the intrinsic char acteristics of the new organizing form.

On the other hand, there is ambiguity regarding less active parti cipants' role in knowledge collaboration in online communities. In this context, it can be stated that some individuals believe in the creed of the wisdom of crowds that “nobody is smarter or faster than everybody” [12]. These individuals think that one of the essential merits of online communities is their capability to gather a large number of participants for knowledge collaboration quickly and easily [24,25,28]. Gathering many participants, regardless of whether they are active or less active, increases the group size, and thereby bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the knowledge colla boration. An increase in the number of participants increases the di versity of experiences and interests and broadens the range of knowledge sources, thereby achieving neutral viewpoints [19] and improving the quality of output [10,38]. Arazy and Nov noted that an appropriate degree of contribution inequality (that is, the existence of many less active participants) in Wikipedia article editing work exerted a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on article quality [5]. Nevertheless, the role of less active participants has been persistently questioned. The openness of online communities may attract some less active participants with incompetence or malice who provide misleading materials and cause con<sup>fl</sup>icts [45]. A recent paper showed that the overall tone of a Wikipedia article is mostly decided by a dominant few (active participants) rather than by a trivial many (less active participants) [27]. In other words, less active participants although they account for a major part of the overall population in knowledge collaboration, have a negligible DIRECT e<sup>f</sup>ect on the output. There are opposing opinions on the role of less active participants' behaviors in knowledge collaboration in online communities, and hence it is essential to conduct carefully designed examinations.

In this study, we probe the INDIRECT in<sup>fl</sup>uence of less active participants' contributing behaviors on the quality of knowledge colla boration. We propose the following two-step causal path: 1) less active participants' participation causes active participants to increase con tributions and 2) active participants' additional contributions, which are the result of less active participants' participation, substantively improve the quality of knowledge collaboration.

From the literature on individuals' motivations to participate in collaborative work in online communities, we obtain some clues to the <sup>fi</sup>rst causality proposed. Factors that motivate individuals to contribute to online communities include gaining reputation [9,22,23] and social identity [16,39,40] and displaying altruism [35,44]. Individuals who are keen to build and enhance their reputations or identities in the community or are willing to bene<sup>fi</sup>t others tend to contribute frequently to collaborative work, that is, act as active participants. When the work to which an active participant has devoted e<sup>f</sup>orts attracts more participants, the former gets an opportunity to enhance own reputation and identity. Likewise, when the work contribution of the participant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts more people, the participant experiences an increased enthusiasm to increase own contributions. Zhang and Zhu's work provides circumstantial evidence to our prospection. They used the Chinese Wikipedia's data and found that each editor showed a tendency to increase contribution with an increase in the group's size. They also found that editors who were likely to care more about social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts reacted more strongly to changes in the group's size [46]. Therefore, when many less active participants contribute to a certain work, active participants of this work may be triggered to make additional contributions.

The veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of the causal relationship between less active participants' participation and active participants' additional contributions leads to the following interesting question: do these additional contributions e<sup>f</sup>ectively improve the quality of work? If active participants' reaction is con<sup>fi</sup>ned only to eliminating or correcting some contents provided by less active participants, then the impact of the former's additional contributions to quality will be negligible. Conversely, if active participants contribute substantial content in response to less active participants' participation, then active participants' additional contributions will signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the quality.

In summary, the following two research questions on knowledge collaboration in online communities are investigated in this study: 1) do less active participants' contributing activities trigger active participants to contribute more and 2)do active participants' additional contributions improve the quality signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly? By employing the editing data of featured articles in the Chinese Wikipedia, we verify that the answer to both questions is “yes.” The results reveal the following indirect impact of participation by less active participants on quality: less active participants' contributions will cause active participants to be more active, and thereby contribute to an increase in quality.

This work responds to the call of Faraj et al. for studies examining the role of less active participants in the functioning of the online community [14]. The new form of organizing in online communities lacks the traditional mechanisms that can control the process of knowledge collaboration. This study exposes the unique way in which less active participants maintain the activeness and fruitfulness of knowledge production. This work o<sup>f</sup>ers empirical evidence to support Aaltonen and Kallinikos's arguments: “what is inactive is not useless” and “what is immediately inactive may well shape both what becomes active and its quality” (pp. 187) [1]. Therefore, this study contributes to the research on knowledge collaboration in this new organizational form.

This study determines the way less active participants in<sup>fl</sup>uence the quality of collaborative work in online communities. As Lee and Seo show, the overall tone of knowledge collaboration in online communities is determined by active participants (who are called a “dominant few”) [27]; however, we indicate that less active participants cannot be named as the “trivial many” because they are indispensable in the generation of high-quality knowledge. We reveal that even if less active participants do not directly determine the overall tone of the output, their participation makes active participants more productive, which leads to high quality. To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to expose the indirect impact of participation by less active participants on the quality of knowledge collaboration.

## 2. Data and research design

## 2.1. Data collection

We collect data from featured articles in the Chinese Wikipedia. The Wikipedia uses a wiki platform to host an open-source encyclopedia, which is a typical online community for knowledge collaboration [32]. The editing process of a Wikipedia article is a typical example of collaborative work. A Wikipedia article is created by a registered user. Subsequently, the article can be edited by anyone with access to the Internet, even those without a valid identi<sup>fi</sup>cation for the platform. Unequal contributions by editors are pervasive in article editing activity; in other words, most edits on an article are performed by a small group of active participants, whereas many less active participants contribute only a small portion of the content [5,25,33]. During the article editing process, both active participants and less active participants are involved in the collaborative work.

The Chinese Wikipedia was started in October 2002. With development spanning a period of one-and-a-half decades, the Chinese Wikipedia currently has approximately 954,000 articles and approximately 2,398,000 registered users. The Wikimedia Trafic Analysis Report shows that the major editors of the Chinese Wikipedia come from three regions: Mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong.<sup>3</sup> The Chinese Wikipedia has been blocked many times in Mainland China (see also [46]), thereby leading to a frequent change in the composition of editors. Taking advantage of the frequent changes in the editors' compo sition, we can <sup>fi</sup>nd numerous di<sup>f</sup>erences in the editing activities of both active participants and less active participants at di<sup>f</sup>erent times.

Featured articles are considered best in terms of accuracy, neutrality, completion, and quality of writing. Wikipedia's internal quality categorization scheme includes seven levels to indicate how good articles are,<sup>4</sup> and a “Featured Article” occupies the top level. Several editors perform numerous edits on a featured article experiences numerous edits by numerous editors during a relatively long period; this process enables us to collect many observations on the article over its edit history. It is worth mentioning that the main purpose of this study is to reveal the role of less active participants' behaviors in the new organization form rather than to give an overall scenario of Wikipedia. Although featured articles comprise only a small fraction of all articles in Wikipedia (< 0.1%), this sample selection will not bias our research. Seven hundred and twenty-six Chinese Wikipedia articles were titled as “featured” at the end of the sampled period (May 31, 2016).

When an editor revises an article and saves the edits, a version of the article is automatically generated. All versions of an article are listed in its “view history” page. The “view history” page records the time, editor, and changed contents of each revision as well as that of the length of each version. For each article, we collect data from its <sup>fi</sup>rst to its last version until May 31, 2016.

Since the research focuses on the contributing behavior of humans, we use the same method that Aaltonen and Seiler use to exclude both the edits performed by bots (automated programs that implement automated revisions) and those that restore a previous version of the same article<sup>5</sup> [2]. We also refer to Aaltonen and Seiler's method of counting edits [2]. A new version of the article is automatically recorded when the editor publishes an edited article on Wikipedia. Editing habits are di<sup>f</sup>erent among editors. Some editors save an article several times within a short period, whereas others click the save button only after completing a revision. We aggregate all edits on an article performed by the same editor within 8 hours into a single edit. Ultimately, we obtain 216,232 edits made by 53,416 editors for 726 featured articles.<sup>6</sup> Following the previous method [34,37], we measure the aggregate editing activities of a certain article over a <sup>fi</sup>xed monthly time window. We obtain unbalanced panel data with 23,537 article/monthly observations.<sup>7</sup>

## 2.2. Active participants vs. less active participants

We classify active and less active participants of an article in line with their editing frequency on the article. We list all editors in descending order according to the count of their edits on the article. Subsequently, we calculate the ratio of each editor's edit count to the total edit count and cumulate the ratios from top to bottom. We place an asterisk at the point where the cumulative ratio exceeds 0.8 for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time as a sign to demarcate and classify di<sup>f</sup>erent editors. As an example of this method, Table 1 shows the editor classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the article “2007年欧<sup>洲冠军</sup>联赛<sup>决</sup>赛 (2007 UEFA Champions League Final)”.

A total of 27 editors edited the aforementioned article. The most productive editor, “Teekkari,” contributed to the article by performing 86 edits, and 18 editors edited this article only once. The cumulative ratios are shown in the fourth column. The cumulative ratio increases from top to bottom, and it exceeds 0.8 for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time when estimating the cumulative ratio of editor “JAK”; thus, we place an asterisk mark at this point. Three editors appearing after “JAK” in the table made the same number of edits (two edits) as JAK; hence, we draw a line below the last editor (“如<sup>沐</sup>西风”) who made two edits. In this article, we classify the 9 editors above the line as active participants and the other 18 editors below this line as less active participants. In other cases, if the marked editor and all editors below the former shared the same edit count, then we draw the line above the <sup>fi</sup>rst editor who shared the same edit count as the marked editor.<sup>8</sup>

Ten thousand and sixty-three editors were selected from 53,416 contributors as active participants. This classi<sup>fi</sup>cation was done at the article level: that is, an editor who acted as an active participant in one article may be a less active participant in another.

## 2.3. Measurement of variables

## 2.3.1. Active participants' contribution

The variable AP is introduced to express active participants' contribution. $A P _ { i t }$ is measured by the edit count of active participants of the article i in the month t. When we include this variable in the regression models, we standardize it by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation. We standardize most variables because this processing may unify the presentation of the results and facilitate comparisons [37]. To analyze the autocorrelation of active participants contributions, we introduce APlag, which is the lag term of AP. APlag is the sum of the value of AP during month (t-3) to month (t-1). To guarantee the completeness of the lag terms, we cancel the <sup>fi</sup>rst three months of each article from the sample.

## 2.3.2. Less active participants' contribution

We develop two variables, LP and LPlag, to express less active participants' contribution. $L P _ { i t }$ is measured by the edit count of less active participants of article i within month t. LPlag is the three-month lag term of $L P _ { i t } .$ The inclusion of these two variables in the regression model will reveal both the simultaneous and triggering e<sup>f</sup>ects of less active participants' editing behavior on active participants' contributions.

## 2.3.3. Article's quality

Guidelines for assessing the quality of a Wikipedia article consider the extent to which the article is well written, factually accurate, veri<sup>fi</sup>able, comprehensive, neutral, and stable [25]. Di<sup>f</sup>erent methods were used to measure the quality of Wikimedia article in previous studies— specialist assessment [5,7,17], employing Wikipedia's internal quality categorization scheme [2,10,25,34,37], and assessing the number of references cited in the article [2].

Following Aaltonen and Seiler [2], we employ two measures to quantify an article's quality. We calculate the ratio of the number of references to the article length (measured by counting the number of Chinese characters in thousands) in article i in the month t, denoted as $R / L _ { i t }$ as the <sup>fi</sup>rst measure of quality. The reliability and veri<sup>fi</sup>ability of information sources are important criteria to evaluate the quality of Wikipedia articles. A prominent reason for an article being identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as low quality is the lack of su<sup>fi</sup>cient references [2]. According to the grading scheme for Wikipedia article quality, a good article must contain a list of all references.<sup>9</sup> Therefore, the number of references an article contains (relative to article length) can act as a proxy for the article quality.

The second method takes advantage of Wikipedia's internal quality categorization scheme, which categorizes most articles into seven quality levels: Stub, Start, C, B, Good Article, A, and Featured Article. This quality categorization relies on the comprehensive assessment of an article's accuracy, neutrality, completion, and the quality of writing, among others. We give values from 1 to 7 to the variable Qual\_level , according to these levels. For example, Qual\_level is equal to 5 if article i belongs to the “Good Article” level in the month t. Since all sample articles eventually reach the “Featured Article” level, each article acquires at least one quality score during it history. We use logarithms for this variable ln(1 + Qual\_level ) in the analyses below.

## 2.3.4. Other variables

Aaltonen and Seiler veri<sup>fi</sup>ed that the volume of the article will signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the user's editing activities [2]. To control for this in<sup>fl</sup>uence, we include three variables in the regression model. The <sup>fi</sup>rst variable, length , which expresses the length of the article, is measured by counting Chinese characters (by thousands) in article i on the <sup>fi</sup>rst day of month t. The second control variable is change , which is measured by the change in article i's length during month t (the article length on the last day minus that on the <sup>fi</sup>rst day of the month). The third control variable, sctchange , is a dummy variable that is used to detect changes in the article section. If the content section of article i is modi<sup>fi</sup>ed during month t, then sctchange equals 1; otherwise, it equals 0. All the three variables are standardized when they are included in the regression models.

To address possible problems that may bias the estimation of the causality between AP and independent variables (LP and LPlag) (see below), we construct some variables to run the robustness tests. First, age is constructed to denote the monthly age of the article i in the month t. Second, we introduce the variable reference to control for the variability of external information, which is measured as the number of references of the article i in the month t. Third, we develop three variables to indicate the period chosen by editors to perform the edits. We introduce two month-level dummies, hkcb and twcb , which indicate whether the months chosen to perform the edits coincide with college break periods in Hong Kong and Taiwan, respectively. Another concern is that the Chinese Wikipedia has been blocked in Mainland China, and hence we introduce a third month-level dummy, block , to denote if the sampled month coincides with the period in which the website was blocked. Finally, we construct two instrumental variables to address the possible endogeneity problem. We use the proportion of anonymous users to less active participants for the article i during the month t, which is denoted by anony , to instrument the endogenous variable $L P _ { i t } ,$ and calculate the lag term of anony , anonylag , as the instrumental variable of LPlag .

3. Impact of less active participants' edits on active participants edits

## 3.1. The basic mode

To test the e<sup>f</sup>ect of LP and LPlag on AP, we <sup>fi</sup>rst examine the basic regression model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & A P _ {i t} = \beta_ {1} L P _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} L P l a g _ {i t} + \gamma A P l a g _ {i t} + \left(\sum_ {j} \alpha^ {(j)} c o n t r o l s _ {i t} ^ {(j)}\right) + \theta_ {i} + \psi_ {t} \\ & \quad + \delta_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

We include three controls, length , change , and sctchange , in the model. To control the autocorrelation of active participants' contribution, we include the lag term of AP, APlag, in the model. To control for variation among di<sup>f</sup>erent articles as well as during di<sup>f</sup>erent periods, we control for the article- and month <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects and cluster standard errors at the article level. This speci<sup>fi</sup>cation includes $\theta _ { i }$ and $\psi _ { t } ,$ which are a set of the article and month <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects, respectively, and $\delta _ { i t } ,$ which is the error term. If less active participants randomly participate in the editing work across articles as well as in di<sup>f</sup>erent months, then either LP or LPlag will be uncorrelated with the error term; subsequently, the estimations of $\beta _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 }$ would be valid. However, less active participants' participation is not random and usually acts as a function of the general appeal, popularity, and potentially controversial nature of the article topic [2]. We argue that after controlling for the factors indicating the characteristics of an article's existing contents, article-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics (for example, its general appeal) via the article-<sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects, and a general time trend across all articles via the month-<sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects, the edits of less active participants can be treated as exogenous [2]. Nevertheless, the editing activities of less active and active participants may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by common factors. Hence, many other concerns should be carefully considered to interpret the estimations of $\beta _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 }$ as causal e<sup>f</sup>ects. We will discuss this in robustness checks.

## 3.2. Robustness checks

The dependent variable (AP) and independent variables (LP and LPlag) may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by some identical factors, such as time trends, information availability, and the timings chosen to perform edits. We should control for these possibilities to guarantee the validity of the above estimation. Moreover, reverse causality may exist, where active participants' edits may induce less active participants to engage in editing work for the article. There may be identi<sup>fi</sup>cation bias from possible endogeneity, which should be eliminated to guarantee the existence of the hypothesized causality.

## 3.2.1. Time trends

The time trend to perform an edit on an article may threaten our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. At the <sup>fi</sup>rst draft stage, an article would need more contributions to attain the appropriate level of accurateness and completeness, and be transformed into a well-written piece. In comparison, when an article is mature, the demand for additional contribution weakens. Both active and less active participants display a strong potential to contribute to a newly written article; when the article un dergoes multiple edits over a prolonged period, both types of editors may display a lower frequency to edit the article. It is possible that the positive correlation between LP (LPlag) and AP is caused by the fact that both are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the time trend factor. In other words, there is a risk that the correlation between LP (LPlag) and AP cannot be inter preted as a causal e<sup>f</sup>ect.

We add the variable $a g e _ { i t }$ to the basic model to control for the in-<sup>fl</sup>uence of time trends. The results are reported in column (5) of Table 3. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the article's age has a negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect on active participants' edits. Importantly, the coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of both LP and LPlag in column (3) are very similar to those in column (1). Therefore, the estimation is robust to the inclusion of time trends.

## 3.2.2. External information

Wikipedia editors usually refer to external information to do their editing work. External information varies across articles as well as during di<sup>f</sup>erent periods. Some articles are related to popular topics, and their information sources are abundant, whereas other articles focus on a niche for which the information sources are sparse. Even for a single article, information sources may vary with the article's editing history. The occurrence of an event that relates to a certain article may lead to an increase in the information sources (for instance, corresponding news reports and discussions on social media) and provide people with abundant information to contribute to the article. The correlation between LP (and LPlag) and AP may be the result of both these variables being determined by external information availability.

We use two methods to address this problem. First, we add the variable reference to the basic model. The number of references is treated as a proxy for the richness of external information. Column (6) of Table 3 provides evidence that reference is positively and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to active participants' edits. Importantly, both LP and LPlag's regression coe<sup>fi</sup>cients are positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and are similar to the results in column (3).

The number of references is just a rough proxy of external information sources. Second, we use a complementary method to control for the external information problem. An article may experience a boom period with heavy contributions after a related event occurs. During this period, active participants will be inspired to contribute more, and many less active participants will also be attracted to perform the editing work. This phenomenon may distort the relationship of editing frequencies between active and less active participants. Therefore, we drop extreme samples with a large volume of contributions (the volume of contributions is calculated by the number of Chinese characters being added and removed).

Column (7) of Table 3 reports the results of the basic model, which excludes samples with a volume of contributions larger than 10,000 characters. The coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of both LP and LPlag are positive and sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cant but smaller than those for the overall sample (column (3)). We also tried 5000- and 2000-character contributions as standards for excluding extreme samples, and the results were qualitatively the same.

These two methods exclude the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of external information on our estimation, and we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the result is robust in this regard.

## 3.2.3. Timing chosen to perform edits

Most editors make contributions to Wikipedia in their spare time. If both active and less active participants share the same period for editing Wikipedia articles, then the correlation between LP and AP may stem from their common reason for selecting the same period to perform the edits. According to Wikipedia's Editor Survey 2011,<sup>11</sup> a large part of Wikipedia editors are college students. During their college break period, they get abundant time to edit Wikipedia articles. Nearly 65% of Chinese Wikipedia editors come from Taiwan and the Hong Kong regions.<sup>12</sup> Hence, we introduce two month-level dummies, hkcb and $t w c b _ { t s }$ which indicate whether the months chosen to perform the edits coincide with the college break periods in Hong Kong and Taiwan, respectively. Another concern is that the Chinese Wikipedia has been blocked in Mainland China, and hence we introduce a third month-level dummy, $b l o c k _ { t s }$ to denote if the sampled month coincides with the period in which the website was blocked. Since hkcb and twcb are highly correlated (their correlation is 0.503), we include them in the regression model in succession.

Columns (8) and (9) of Table 3 report the regression results. Either hkcb or twcb is positively related to active participants' edits. Surpris ingly, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that active participants' edits are hampered because of a Wikipedia-block; this is because regression coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of the block are insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. This result implies that most active participants of featured articles in the Chinese Wikipedia do not belong to Mainland China. Importantly, the coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of both LP and LPlag reported in column (8) and (9) are similar to those reported in column (3), which indicates that the main result is robust after we consider the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of editing timing.

## 3.2.4. Endogeneity concerns

We have controlled for some factors, such as time trends, external information sources, and the period chosen to perform edits, to avoid distorting the causal identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. However, less active participants may be attracted by active participants' contributions. Therefore, the editing activities of less active participants cannot be hypothesized as exogenous.

To address the endogeneity problem, we implement an instrumental variable analysis. We use the proportion of anonymous users to less active participants for the article i during the month $t , a n o n y _ { i t s }$ to instrument the endogenous variable $L P _ { i t } .$ In Wikipedia, it is not necessary to create a valid account to edit an article because this can be done “anonymously” in the exact same way. In this case, Wikipedia records the IP address of the computer that was used to perform the edit. Several anonymous users participate in article editing work, and most of them contribute only a few times [4]. Most anonymous editors in Wikipedia are article readers. Some readers occasionally edit an article when they <sup>fi</sup>nd some content that needs to be revised, and most of them do not make more than two or three contributions [18].

The participation of these anonymous editors is determined by exogenous reading demand. Active participants are not be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the proportion of anonymous editors among the less active participants. Registered users are informed by a tool called “Watchlist” whenever an article edited by them is modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by another editor. However, there is no evidence that an active participant will have a special reaction to a revision done by a particular IP addresses. Therefore, the proportion of anonymous users among the less active participants does not directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the active participants' contribution: that is, it is not correlated with the error term of the basic model.

Among the less active participants, the number of registered editors interested in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c article is limited, whereas the number of anon ymous editors can be seen as in<sup>fi</sup>nite. For example, potential less active participants originate from a small pool of registered users and a vast expanse of in<sup>fi</sup>nite anonymous users. As the “<sup>fl</sup>ow” of less active participants for the editing work increases, the pool becomes increasingly exhausted, but the expanse remains changed. Therefore, the more the number of less active participants that join the editing work, the higher is the probability of enlistment of anonymous users. The correlation between LP and anony is 0.14 and highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Therefore, anony is a valid instrumental variable for LP. We also calculate the three-month lag term of anony, anonylag, as the instrumental variable of LPlag. Both instrumental variables are standardized.

Table 4 shows the main results of the instrument analysis. For convenience in comparison, we list the estimates the results of ordinary least squares (OLS) and instrumental variables (IV) together. For the IV analyses, we show only the results of the second stage, except for the Fstatistic value of the excluding instrument in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage. In the <sup>fi</sup>rststage analyses of both IV models, the instruments (anony and anonylag) are positively and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to the endogenous variables (LP and LPlag). The F-test values of the excluded instruments are very high, which indicates that the instrumental variables are valid.

The coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of LP in the second stage of the IV model, as reported in column (2) of Table 4, is 0.363 and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 0.1% level. Compared to the OLS result in column (1), the regression coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of LP in the IV model is slightly larger. Column (4) shows that the coef-<sup>fi</sup>cient of LPlag in the second stage of the IV model is also slightly larger than it is in the OLS model. These results indicate that LP and LPlag remain positively related (even more tightly) with AP after endogeneity has been excluded. This con<sup>fi</sup>rms that active participants are indeed triggered by less active participants' editing activities to become more active.

## 5. Less active participants' role in reviving dormant articles

We implement an additional analysis on less active participants' role in reviving dormant articles. Wikipedia lacks the traditional mechanisms to divide a task and allocate subtasks to certain members, and hence it is not unusual for articles to remain unedited for longer periods. Many articles do not attract people's attention and eventually become dormant. Even some featured articles have experienced in active periods. In our sample, there were 392 dormant observations among 234 articles that have remained unedited over a year. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that 287 of these 392 dormant observations (73.4%) were broken by less active participants: that is, less active participant perform the <sup>fi</sup>rst edit to end the long-term dormancy, thereby reviving the editing work on the article. For comparison, less active participants contribute only approximately 26% of edits in the whole sample. Therefore, less active participants play a more important role than active participants in reviving dying articles.

We use data of the wakeup month and the next 3 months for each revived article to re-estimate the basic model. Table 6 reports the summary. We list only the regression coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of LP and LPlag of the overall sample and the sample for the period in which the articles were revived. ## 6.2. Theoretical implications

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have several important implications for knowledge collaboration in online communities. With the emergence and devel opment of knowledge collaboration in online communities, a new form of organizing for knowledge work has been emphasized [14,30,36]. Previous research has discussed many important characteristics of this organizing form, such as membership turnover [34,37], participant composition [7,38], and contribution inequality [5,27,33]. This study contributes to the literature by clarifying the role of less active parti cipants in this new form of organizing. A general characteristic of on line communities is that many less active participants participate in knowledge collaborations. Previous research has analyzed the dynamic process of participants from inactive to active [6]. However, the important role of these many less active participants has been ignored; even worse, they are called the “trivial many” [27]. Even though less active participants do not determine the overall tone of a Wikipedia article directly, we verify that their participation can sustain collaborative work as they can trigger active participants to make more contributions and can revive dormant articles. Moreover, the partici pation of less active participants eventually leads to the quality im provement of output. To the best of our knowledge, we are the <sup>fi</sup>rst to verify the indirect way through which less active participants' contributing activities impact the quality of the collaborative work.

Additionally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that active participants care about less active participants in terms of the frequency of their participation rather than the quantity of their contributions. This implies that the active participants' participation is mainly driven by their social concerns, such as reputation or identity [44]. This result complements new evidence in the literature on participants' motivation to join collaborative work in online communities [9,16,22,23,35,39,40,44]. This result also complements Aaltonen and Seiler's <sup>fi</sup>ndings. They found that the content volume of past contributions led to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more editing activity [2]. We further probe the active participants' reaction to less active participants' contributions. However, our result shows that the volume of less active participants' contributions does not inspire active participants to make more edits. This can be attributed to the fact that less active participants do not contribute substantive content to the article, and thus active participants do not respond to the volume of content that the latter has contributed. This leaves questions that need to be addressed with text mining and topic analyses in the future.

## 6.3. Practical implications

Wikipedia is a platform for knowledge generation. The essential merit of this platform is its capability to gather numerous people to join in collaborative work with ease. However, social norms are formalized with the passage of time. As Halfaker et al. veri<sup>fi</sup>ed, the formalization of norms makes it increasingly di<sup>fi</sup>cult for new participants to contribute [20]. In other words, Wikipedia will gradually lose its periphery editors. According to a review in the New York Time, one of the biggest challenges Wikipedia is facing now is the drain of its potential editors.<sup>14</sup> External shocks, such as the rise of mobile phones as the dominant personal computing device, have worsened the situation. Less active participants would be the <sup>fi</sup>rst to be a<sup>f</sup>ected by such shocks. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that many less active participants are indispensable in Wikipedia, and hence measures should be taken to retain them. We suggest that Wikipedia must develop a recommendation tool that would enable a user to view not only own edited articles but also other “new” articles that might cater to the user's interests. We also suggest that some inactive articles should be posted on the front page of the websites to attract edits from readers.

## 6.4. Limitations and future work

This study contains several limitations that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the potential generalization of its <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, we conducted this research was entirely based on the Chinese Wikipedia. The results should be tested for other language versions of Wikipedia. In other online com munities, the collaborative activities and social conditions may be dif ferent from Wikipedia. Do less active participants play the same role in other communities? This should be investigated in future research.

Second, as mentioned above, we include only featured articles as part the research sample. Featured articles have many advantages that suit our research questions. However, most articles never reach the featured article level. Further research should explore whether the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study can be maintained for underdeveloped colla borative work.

Third, in this study, we detect only the causal e<sup>f</sup>ect of less active participants on active participants. The existence of causality between the two parts is more complicated. There is a likely existence of a bidirectional causal relationship between the two parts. Future work should focus on the dynamic, bilateral interactions between the less active and active participants.
