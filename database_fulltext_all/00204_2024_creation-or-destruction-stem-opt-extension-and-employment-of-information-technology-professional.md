# CREATION OR DESTRUCTION? STEM OPT EXTENSION AND EMPLOYMENT OF INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY PROFESSIONALS<sup>1</sup>

Xue Guo

Department of Business Information Systems and Operations Management, Belk College of Business, University of North Carolina at Charlotte, Charlotte, NC, U.S.A. {xue.guo@uncc.edu}

Jing Gong McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA, U.S.A. {fnh9zj@virginia.edu}

Min-Seok Pang Department of Management Information Systems, Fox School of Business and Management, Temple University, Philadelphia, U.S.A. {minspang@temple.edu}

Information technology (IT) professionals play a critical role in digital transformation, innovation, and entrepreneurship, contributing to significant economic growth. The use of temporary work visas and related immigration policies has attracted significant controversy and policy debates in developed nations. On the one hand, foreign IT professionals can complement domestic IT professionals by bringing new skills and knowledge, which benefit domestic workers. On the other hand, foreign IT professionals may substitute for their domestic counterparts due to intensified labor-market competition. In this study, we focus on an extension in the Optional Practical Training (OPT) program in the U.S. for foreign science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) graduates from U.S. institutions. We explore the effects of the STEM OPT extension on the number and wage of domestic workers in STEM occupations and how these effects differ between IT and non-IT STEM occupations. We found that an increase in the supply of foreign STEM professionals from the OPT extension has boosted the employment of domestic workers in STEM occupations and that the effects are stronger for IT occupations. This study contributes to the information systems literature by demonstrating the unique effects of immigrant policies on IT occupations and provides significant implications for evidence-driven policy making.

Keywords: IT occupations, high-skilled immigration, OPT extension, STEM, local IT employment

## Introduction

Skilled information technology (IT) professionals play a critical role in the advanced economy by facilitating technology development, innovation, and entrepreneurship (e.g., Bresnahan et al., 2002; Kleis et al., 2012). The growth in the information economy and digital business is exacerbating the need for skilled IT professionals (Ang et al., 2002; Mithas et al., 2011). To address shortages in the IT workforce, developed nations such as the U.S. have instituted various work visa programs and immigration policies (e.g., H-1B) to attract high-skilled foreign workers to the domestic labor market. These policies, however, have been a controversial topic of political debate (Peri et al., 2015). On the one hand, proponents argue that the supply of foreign professionals can reduce the shortage of highly skilled workers. On the other hand, critics argue that high-skilled foreign workers may potentially displace domestic workers (e.g., Doran et al., 2022; Matloff, 2013). Thus, it is paramount to better understand the impact of high-skilled foreign professionals on domestic labor markets using concrete empirical evidence.

This study examines how one of the immigration policies that has expanded the supply of foreign talent—an extension in the Optional Practical Training (OPT) program in the U.S. for foreign students in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) majors—affects domestic labor markets. OPT is a temporary employment program that allows foreign students to work in the U.S. for a certain period after completing their academic studies in the U.S. Before 2008, all eligible students could apply for up to 12 months of employment authorization upon graduation. For STEM majors (including IT), this employment period was extended to 29 months in 2008 and 36 months in 2016. This policy provides greater chances for foreign students to secure permanent jobs, increasing the supply of high-skilled foreign workers.

We aim to assess the effect of the STEM OPT extension on the employment of domestic workers in STEM occupations and explore how this effect differs between IT and other STEM occupations. To do so, we build upon the information systems (IS) literature on IT labor (e.g., Ang et al., 2002; Tambe & Hitt, 2014) and the labor economics literature (e.g., Friedberg & Hunt, 1995; Peri et al., 2015) to theorize the complementary effect of the OPT extension on domestic employment and investigate the underlying mechanisms. We propose and empirically test two potential mechanisms through which foreign workers complement domestic workers: (1) boosting innovation and (2) facilitating entrepreneurial activities. Our study also examines whether the complementary effect is stronger for IT than non-IT STEM occupations by theorizing IT workers’ greater skill transferability in various industries compared to other STEM workers. To further explore the different skills that foreign and domestic IT workers possess, we explore the moderating roles of occupation modularizability and work experience in the complementary effect of the OPT extension.

Empirically, we constructed a novel dataset at the occupation-MSA-year<sup>2</sup> level from various sources including the U.S. Census Bureau and O\*Net. STEM occupations, i.e., those affected by the OPT extension, comprised the treatment group, and the other occupations formed the control group. For identification, we used two recently developed counterfactual estimators—generalized synthetic control (GSC) (Xu, 2017) and matrix completion (MC) (Athey et al., 2021)—to compute counterfactual outcomes for the treated occupations (i.e., STEM occupations). We also employed several alternative empirical strategies, including the triple-difference approach, which has been widely adopted in both the labor economics literature (Egan et al., 2022; Pierce & Schott, 2016) and the IS literature (e.g., Chen et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020).

Our empirical analyses yielded several notable findings. First, we found that the OPT extension has significantly increased the employment of domestic workers in STEM occupations compared to non-STEM occupations. This suggests that highskilled foreign workers in STEM occupations are complementing their domestic counterparts. Second, we found that this complementary effect of OPT extension is stronger for IT occupations than it is for non-IT STEM occupations. Third, we found that the complementary effects are driven by the specialized skills of foreign workers that boost innovation, promote entrepreneurial activities, and are transferable across industries. Finally, we found that the OPT extension has stronger complementary effects for occupations with lower modularizability and ones that require more work experience, suggesting that the OPT extension has stronger complementary effects on domestic IT workers who possess skills that are different from foreign IT workers.

Our study makes several contributions to the IS literature. First, we contribute to the IS literature by theorizing and demonstrating the heterogeneous effects of immigration policies on IT and other occupations. Our study theorizes the wide applicability of IT skills to different industries, which contributes to the stronger complementary effects of the OPT extension for IT versus non-IT occupations. Second, our findings suggest that high-skilled foreign workers can complement domestic IT workers rather than “crowding them out” by promoting innovation and entrepreneurial activities, and thereby contribute to the stream of literature on the impacts of high-skilled foreign workers, especially IT workers, on the employment of domestic workers.

## Background and Theory

## Foreign STEM Workers in the United States

Foreign workers in developing countries emigrate to developed countries to seek economic and employment opportunities (Gong et al., 2018). More than 1.5 million immigrants enter the U.S. every year (Salzman et al., 2013). A large portion of immigrants are high-skilled professionals, which is of critical importance to immigration policies in developed nations (Kerr & Lincoln, 2010). One major path for high-skilled foreign workers to enter the U.S. labor market is through education and temporary work visas (Neufeld, 2019). As outlined in Figure 1, foreign students graduating from U.S. institutions are eligible to apply for OPT, a temporary employment authorization that allows them to work upon graduation.

The extended OPT period has increased incentives for prospective employers to hire and retain foreign STEM workers. ## High-Skilled Foreign Workers and Domestic Labor Market Outcomes

The labor economics literature has examined how the influx of high-skilled foreign workers affects domestic labor markets (Mayda et al., 2018; Peri et al., 2015). One stream of research argues that foreign workers complement the demand for domestic counterparts, as they possess a different set of skills and knowledge (e.g., Mithas & Lucas, 2010). Prior studies have shown that high-skilled foreign workers offer unique skills that can facilitate innovation and entrepreneurship (Kerr, 2013), expanding the overall size of labor markets (e.g., Peri et al., 2015). Kerr and Lincoln (2010) showed that a 10% growth in H-1B workers is associated with a 0.3%-0.7% increase in the total number of inventions. To the best of our knowledge, however, few studies have leveraged the OPT extension—a relatively new policy that only applies to highskilled STEM workers—as a supply shock to explore the relationship between foreign and domestic STEM workers and the underlying mechanisms.

In contrast, another stream of research argues that the inflow of high-skilled foreign workers reduces opportunities for domestic workers (Hira, 2018; Matloff, 2013), as it intensifies labor-market competition and suppresses overall wages. For instance, using the 1960-2000 decennial censuses, Borjas (2006) found that the supply of foreign workers reduced the wages of domestic workers. Matloff (2013) found that firms pay high-skilled foreign workers 15%-33% less than comparable domestic employees. Thus, an increase in the supply of high-skilled foreign workers in STEM professions may “crowd out” domestic workers or even discourage prospective domestic workers from pursuing STEM-related fields (e.g., Borjas, 2005).

## Impact of the OPT Extension on the Employment of Domestic STEM Workers

In this section, we theorize the impact of OPT extension on the employment of domestic STEM workers. The effects of OPT extension on local employment depend on the relationship between foreign and domestic STEM workers, as the OPT extension has increased the supply of foreign STEM workers. If foreign STEM workers possess skills similar to domestic STEM counterparts, employers may replace domestic workers with foreign workers, as employers would be indifferent to hiring either type of workers for a given position. Thus, an increase in the supply of foreign STEM workers could lower the demand for domestic STEM workers. On the contrary, if foreign and domestic professionals in STEM have different skillsets, an increased supply of foreign workers could complement the demand for domestic workers. In particular, foreign workers could fill positions that are difficult to fill using domestic workers or provide specialized skills that are in high demand. This could help firms maintain a strong and diverse workforce and ensure that they have the necessary skills and expertise to sustain their business and boost growth without using foreign workers as a substitute for domestic workers. We next argue that the complementary effect is likely to dominate a potential substitution effect, resulting in a net positive effect of the OPT extension since foreign workers possess different skillsets than their domestic counterparts. Specifically, we propose two unique characteristics of foreign STEM workers—promoting innovation and entrepreneurial activities.

## Innovation

First, the OPT extension could complement the employment of domestic workers by boosting innovation activities. In the innovation literature, the size of the knowledge stock and talent pool determine the rate of innovation (Furman et al., 2002; Riddel & Schwer, 2003). As STEM skills are considered fuel for innovation activities (Atkinson & Mayo, 2010), the OPT extension could boost innovation activities by providing a larger talent pool and thicker knowledge stock in STEM fields. More importantly, foreign professionals promote knowledge diversity by bringing business, social, and cultural knowledge outside the U.S. (Mithas & Lucas, 2010). The integration of different knowledge and expertise facilitates the production of new ideas by generating more novel solutions to problems and providing a greater chance of finding the best solution (Berliant & Fujita, 2011; Hunt & Gauthier-Loiselle, 2010). For instance, Parrotta et al. (2014) showed that ethnic diversity increases firms’ patent applications. Accordingly, increased innovation activities encourage firms to devote more resources to R&D, create more job opportunities, and increase the demand for domestic STEM workers (Kerr & Lincoln, 2010).

## Entrepreneurship

Second, the OPT extension could complement domestic workers by stimulating more entrepreneurial activities. Prior literature has shown that foreign workers are more willing to take risks and have stronger motivations to succeed and greater adaptability to new situations (Jaeger et al., 2010; Kahn et al., 2017), leading to more vibrant entrepreneurial activities. Mithas and Lucas (2010) argued that those who choose to migrate to a foreign country need to overcome a series of challenges caused by differences in culture and environment. Hence, they are more likely to take risks and learn new skills, a reason why many of them become successful entrepreneurs (Duleep et al., 2012). A survey of high-tech startups showed that 25% of new high-tech companies founded in 1995-2005 had at least one immigrant founder (Wadhwa et al., 2008). Such increased entrepreneurial activities expand the employment of domestic STEM workers (Wu & Atkinson, 2017). According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) (2017), newly founded firms created more than 1.7 million jobs in the U.S. in 2017, and technology-based startups expanded employment 10% faster than other startups. Hence, an increase in the supply of foreign STEM workers boosts domestic entrepreneurial activities, which, in turn, expands the demand for domestic STEM workers. Combining the above two arguments, we hypothesize:

H1: The OPT extension is positively associated with the number and wage of domestic workers in STEM occupations.

## Do the Effects of the OPT Extension Vary for IT Workers vs. Non-IT STEM Workers?

Next, we theorize that the complementary effect of the OPT extension on domestic workers in STEM is stronger for IT occupations than for other STEM occupations. Specifically, we argue that since IT skills tend to be more transferable across different industries, foreign IT workers complement the IT workforce in most industries and generate greater complementary effects on domestic workers than non-IT STEM workers.

IT workers have higher skill transferability than non-IT STEM workers in that they can be employed in different industries and have high career mobility across organizations (Joseph et al., 2012; Slaughter et al., 2007). IT skills, such as programming and data analytics, are widely applicable and valuable to firms in almost any industry (Markow et al., 2019). By comparison, the labor market is less fluid for non-IT STEM occupations (e.g., Nania et al., 2019). For instance, biochemists mainly work in the pharmaceutical or biotechnology industries; likewise, aerospace engineers are unlikely to have job opportunities in industries other than aerospace. According to the BLS, IT workers are 11% more likely to be employed in non-science and technology industries than non-IT STEM workers.<sup>3</sup>

The OPT extension provides fresh IT graduates with advanced and general IT skills that further increase skill transferability and flexibility in finding jobs in different industries. General IT skills (e.g., software design) are more flexible than firm-specific IT skills (e.g., tacit knowledge about organizational systems) since they are not constrained to a specific industry (Joseph et al., 2012). Furthermore, IT workers can adapt their skills to new technologies as they emerge (Slaughter et al., 2007). Thus, foreign IT graduates can complement the existing IT workforce in most industries and generate greater complementary effects on domestic workers than non-IT STEM graduates. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2: The positive effects of the OPT extension on the number and wage of domestic STEM workers are stronger for IT workers than for non-IT STEM workers.

## Empirical Analysis

## Data

We utilized data from four major sources. First, we acquired domestic employment data from the Integrated Public Use Microdata Series (IPUMS) from the U.S. Census Bureau, which is a microlevel dataset that covers 1% of the U.S. population each year and includes anonymous individual-level information on occupation, wage, and birthplace. Second, we obtained MSA-level demographic data from the American Community Survey by the Census Bureau. Third, we used detailed information on the OPT extension and associated STEM occupations (Table 1) from the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS). Lastly, we obtained data on occupational skills and abilities from the O\*Net database.

Our panel dataset contained 458 occupations in 290 metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs) that have been consistently observed in all years over a 15-year period (2005- 2019) from the IPUMS dataset. We included MSAs in our analyses to account for regional heterogeneity in economic, demographic, and other aspects. We divided occupations into two categories—(1) non-STEM and (2) STEM. Specifically, non-STEM occupations comprised the control group, and STEM occupations represented the treatment group, as they are subject to the extension. Among the STEM occupations, eight occupations were classified as IT occupations.<sup>4</sup> The OPT extension applies to STEM degrees (Table 1),<sup>5</sup> which we mapped to STEM occupations with the crosswalk from the Classification of Instructional Programs (CIP) to the Standard Occupational Classification (SOC).<sup>6</sup> The USCIS rule requires that to be eligible for an extension in the OPT employment period, foreign STEM graduates must be employed in occupations that are directly related to their majors.<sup>7</sup> The Census Bureau categorizes workers based on their citizenship at birth: those who were U.S. citizens at birth are classified as domestic workers, and those who were not are classified as foreign workers.

We note that labor market dynamics may have had a material impact on our dependent variables, influencing the estimation results. To account for potential time-varying labor demand and supply, we included three control variables—GDP Occupation, Google Trends, and H-1B Applicants in all model specifications. ## Empirical Models and Results

A common approach to estimating the effect of a policy change such as the OPT extension is a difference-indifferences (DiD) model. However, two major concerns may threaten causal identification. First, the identification in DiD would require that STEM and non-STEM occupations have parallel trends for the outcomes before the treatment. Second, there should be no unobservable time-varying confounders that influence STEM and non-STEM occupations differently after the OPT extension. For instance, STEM majors might have been selected to be eligible for the extension based on expected increases in labor market demand.

To address the above concern, we employed two recently developed counterfactual estimators—the generalized synthetic control (GSC) estimator (Xu, 2017) and the matrix completion (MC) estimator (Athey et al., 2021). Both methods have been increasingly adopted in recent IS studies (Pattabhiramaiah et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021) and other business research (Pan & Qiu, 2022) for causal inference with panel data. Specifically, the counterfactual estimators helped (1) generate a better control group that resembles the STEM occupations more closely and (2) account for the potential time-varying confounders after the policy change.

To further address the potential increase in demand for STEM occupations after the OPT extension, we adopted a second identification strategy—a triple-difference approach (DDD) (Gruber, 1994), which has been widely adopted in empirical studies in both IS (e.g., Chen et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020) and labor economics (Egan et al., 2022; Pierce & Schott, 2016). The triple-difference approach considers another comparison group that likely follows a general increase in demand but is less affected by the OPT extension.

## Counterfactual Estimators

We considered two counterfactual estimators—GSC and MC. GSC combines a synthetic control method (Abadie et al., 2010) with an interactive fixed-effects model (Bai, 2009). It estimates causal effects by constructing a weighted combination of control units (i.e., “synthetic” control units) that closely resembles each treated unit in the pre-treatment periods. The interactive fixed-effects model incorporates latent factors to account for unobserved time-varying confounders across units and time. The combination of these two methods enables GSC to address the limitations of DiD. MC further generalizes GSC to a broader setting that fits a panel with a short time period and works in cases in which there is a time-series dependency structure of outcomes (i.e., number and wage in our focal context) (Athey et al., 2021).

Figure 3a depicts a graphical explanation of the counterfactual estimators. The average treatment effect is generated by taking the average differences between the observed and the counterfactual outcomes for the STEM observations. The rationale for identification is that once the STEM occupations and the counterfactual occupations are matched regarding outcomes and covariates in the pre-treatment periods, the differences in the outcomes in the post-treatment periods can be attributed to the treatment (i.e., OPT extension). The GSC and MC methods vary in how the counterfactual outcomes for the STEM observations are predicted (Liu et al., 2022).

For both equations, the unit is occupation-MSA. $\mathrm { Y _ { i j t } }$ is the number or average wage of domestic workers in occupation $i ,$ MSA j, and year t. In Equation $( 1 ) , f _ { t }$ is a vector of unobserved common factors, and $\lambda _ { i }$ is a vector of unknown factor loadings.<sup>10</sup> If an unobserved demand trend affects STEM and non-STEM occupations differently, a latent factor could capture these time-varying unobservable confounders. The only difference between Equations (1) and (2) is that instead of the latent factors in Equation (1), Equation (2) uses a lowerrank matrix L to absorb the fixed effects and potential timevarying confounders.<sup>11</sup> Equation (3) outlines the average treatment effects on the treated (ATT).

$$
A T T = \frac {1}{N _ {t r}} \sum_ {i j \in \Gamma , t > T _ {0, i j}} \delta_ {i j t}\tag{3}
$$

In Equation (3), Γ denotes the set of units in STEM occupations, ij represents the occupation-MSA pair in STEM occupations (i.e., treated), and $N _ { t r }$ represents the number of treated pairs. $T _ { 0 , i j }$ is the number of pre-treatment periods for occupation-MSA pair ij. $\delta _ { i j t }$ denotes the heterogeneous treatment effects for each treated occupation-MSA pair ij at time t. Accordingly, the heterogeneous treatment effects (e.g., IT vs. non-IT STEM occupations) are naturally embedded and can be directly decomposed from the main treatment effects.

The main estimation results with GSC and MC are presented in Table 3. In each column, we first report the overall ATT estimate and then report the estimates of the heterogeneous treatment effects for IT and other STEM occupations obtained based on Equation (3). The GSC estimation results in Columns 1 and 2 show that the OPT extension is associated with a significant increase in the number and average wage of domestic workers in STEM occupations. It increases the number of domestic workers in STEM by 4.4% and their average wage by 3.3%. The MC estimation results in Columns 3 and 4 are consistent with GSC. These findings provide credence to the complementary effect and support H1.

With respect to the heterogeneous treatment effects for IT and other STEM occupations, the GSC estimates in Columns 1 and 2 show that the OPT extension has stronger effects on the number and average wage of workers in IT occupations than in non-IT STEM occupations, respectively. Column 1 suggests that the OPT extension is associated with a 26.2%<sup>12</sup> increase in the number of domestic workers in IT occupations, while the effect for non-IT STEM occupations is positive but nonsignificant. According to Column 2, the OPT extension is associated with a 6.0% increase in the average wage of domestic IT workers, compared to a 2.6% increase in non-IT STEM occupations. The MC estimates in Columns 3 and 4 are largely similar. Collectively, the results support H2 that the OPT extension has a stronger positive effect on IT workers than non-IT STEM workers.

## Triple-Difference Estimations

As discussed earlier, a remaining concern is that there may be other unobserved factors (e.g., increasing occupation demand) influencing STEM and non-STEM occupations differently after the OPT extension, which may not be fully addressed by the counterfactual estimators. The triple-difference approach addresses this concern by considering a new comparison group that likely follows a general increase in demand for STEM workers but is less affected by the OPT extension. Figure 3b depicts a graphical explanation of this approach.

We implemented the triple-difference estimation by considering two additional comparison groups. First, we compared workers at colleges/universities to those at forprofit firms, an approach that has been commonly used in the labor economics literature (e.g., Mayda et al., 2018; Sparber, 2019). Like those at for-profit firms, STEM workers at universities are affected by the general upward demand trends for STEM occupations. But they have been affected by the OPT extension to a lesser extent, given that nonprofit organizations such as universities are exempt from the H-1B quota restriction (Mayda et al., 2018), making it much easier for these workers to obtain H-1B visas and reducing their reliance on the OPT extension. The triple-difference estimator takes the difference between two DiD estimators (i.e., DiD for workers at universities and DiD for those at for-profit firms), in which the omitted unobservable (e.g., increasing demand for STEM workers) are differenced out. That is, the triple difference estimates the relative changes in the number and wage of STEM and non-STEM workers at for-profit firms, relative to universities, allowing us to measure the treatment effect free from the time-varying increasing demand for STEM workers.

Second, we compared MSAs with a small foreign population to MSAs with a large foreign population. The rationale is that if the observed complementary effects are driven by the OPT extension, the effects should be stronger in MSAs with a larger number of foreign workers than in MSAs with a smaller foreign population,<sup>13</sup> as MSAs with a larger foreign population would receive a stronger treatment intensity. One concern is that MSAs with a larger foreign population may also have a larger population or be more economically advanced, resulting in greater demand for foreign workers. To acquire comparable control groups, we used a propensity score matching (PSM) method to match the top and bottom MSAs with foreign populations based on the observable MSA characteristics (e.g., population, income, GDP, education).

This procedure substantially reduced differences across the top and bottom MSAs among the controls.<sup>14</sup> We then ran a triple-difference estimation on this matched sample.

Table 4 presents the triple-difference results. Columns 1-4 show the results using workers in universities as a new comparison group. As seen, the OPT extension has stronger effects on the number and wage of workers in for-profit industries compared to universities. The results using workers in MSAs with a small foreign population as a new comparison group are presented in Columns 5-8 and show that the effects of the OPT extension are mainly driven by MSAs with larger foreign populations. The triple-difference results provide further evidence of the complementary effects of the OPT extension on the number and wage of domestic workers.

## Diagnostic Tests and Robustness Checks

Both the counterfactual estimators and the triple-difference estimator require several assumptions to generate valid estimations. To gauge the validity of the counterfactual estimators, we conducted a series of diagnostic checks, including dynamic treatment effects and an equivalent test (Liu et al., 2022). For the triple-difference estimator, the results held when no reverse causality existed (i.e., the OPT extension should not be taken as a response to the increasing demand for STEM workers), no trends in outcomes existed prior to the OPT extension, and no other confounding shocks existed that influenced workers differently. We carefully examined all these possibilities and found no evidence to invalidate the assumptions. Table 5 presents a list of the diagnostic tests.

Table 6 lists our additional robustness checks. To further address the omitted variable bias (e.g., different demand for STEM workers), we performed a falsification test using the employment data in Canada, which shares similar trends with the U.S. but should not have been impacted by the U.S. policy. We regressed the number and wage of Canadian workers on the OPT extension and did not find a significant effect, suggesting that the effects observed in the U.S. are unlikely to have been driven by unobserved demand trends. In addition, to reduce potential imbalance between the treated and control groups, we restricted the control group to STEM occupations that had not yet received the treatment in 2008 but would eventually receive the treatment (i.e., new occupations added to the STEM list in 2011 and 2012). Thus, the treated and the control groups are likely to share similar unobservable characteristics, further alleviating the concern of time-varying confounders. We observed consistent complementary effects for domestic IT workers.

## Mechanisms

So far, we have shown that the OPT extension has had a complementary effect on domestic STEM workers, which has been found to be stronger for IT occupations. In this section, we further explore the underlying mechanisms.

## Innovation and Entrepreneurial Activities

To empirically test the innovation and entrepreneurship mechanisms, we collected data from two sources. First, we collected patent data from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office to examine the effect on innovation. We used the number of patents filed as a proxy for innovation activities and built a panel dataset in 2005-2018 at the industry-MSA-year level. We classified patents based on the industries that assignees belonged to and identified the MSAs where the assignees were located. Second, we collected data on establishments at the industry-MSA-year level as a proxy for entrepreneurial activities from InfoGroup.

We conducted a GSC estimation with non-STEM industries as the control group and IT and non-IT STEM industries as the treatment group.<sup>15</sup> To alleviate concerns that large MSAs (e.g., San Francisco Bay Area) may both attract more foreign workers and generate more innovation and entrepreneurial activities, we replicated the analyses for subsamples without the top 5% MSAs and without the MSAs with high STEM employment. As seen in Table 7, the OPT extension is positively associated with the number of patents and establishments, and this effect is stronger for IT industries.<sup>16</sup> These results are consistent across the different subsamples.

This finding supports the mechanisms that the OPT extension has had complementary effects on domestic employment by boosting innovation and entrepreneurship.

## Skill Transferability

To further examine the role of skill transferability, we adopted the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) (Rhoades, 1993) to measure skill transferability across industries. This measure has been widely used to measure market concentration (e.g., Cheng & Nault, 2012). Here, we used it to measure the concentration of occupations across industries. Equation (4) outlines the HHI formula.

$$
H H I _ {i} = s _ {1 i} ^ {2} + s _ {2 i} ^ {2} + s _ {3 i} ^ {2} + \dots + s _ {n i} ^ {2},\tag{4}
$$

where $s _ { n i }$ represents the employment share of workers in occupation i in industry n. Empirically, we utilized the industryoccupation matrix from the BLS, which offers detailed occupation distributions across the industries. For example, according to a 2020 figure, 45% of computer programmers are in the professional, scientific, and technical services industries, and 11.3% are in the information industry. A higher HHI indicates lower skill transferability (i.e., occupations are concentrated in a few industries). On average, the skill transferability of IT occupations is 45% higher than that of non-IT STEM occupations $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Within the IT occupations, software engineer is a highly transferable occupation, and information systems manager is a less transferable one.

We separated our treatment occupations into two groups—high and low transferability (above and below median) and calculated ATT for the two groups from the GSC estimates in Table 3. Table 8 shows that the complementary effects are stronger for occupations with high skill transferability. This finding provides further insight into the role of skill transferability in explaining the stronger complementary effect for IT occupations than for non-IT STEM occupations.

To further explore the potential impact of different skillsets possessed by foreign versus domestic STEM workers, we considered the moderating role of two occupation characteristics—modularizability and work experience.

Modularizability is “the extent to which the activities in an occupation can be separated into components so that the components can be performed independently by separate people and then be integrated” (Mithas & Whitaker, 2007). The OPT extension can help fill positions that require high modularizability. Prior studies have shown that foreign workers are more specialized in highly modularizable skills such as programming and computation (Mithas & Whitaker, 2007; Peri & Sparber, 2011). Thus, the OPT extension affords employers greater bargaining power in more modularizable occupations with alternatives to substitute domestic workers with foreign workers or outsource labor (e.g., Tambe & Hitt, 2012). Therefore, we expect the complementary effect of the OPT extension on domestic workers to be weaker for more modularizable occupations.

Empirically, we adopted the modularizability measurement from Mithas and Whitaker (2007). Specifically, we manually coded the modularizability for all IT occupations and estimated the ATT for the occupations with modularizability above and below the median. In Panel A of Table 9, we observed that while the OPT extension has complementary effects on domestic IT workers in occupations with both high and low modularizability, it significantly increases wages for domestic IT workers in low-modularizability occupations only. This result suggests that the OPT extension has further increased the demand for domestic workers in less modularizable occupations, which are less replaceable by foreign workers.

We further explored whether the effect of the OPT extension varies by work experience—entry-level versus more seniorlevel positions. To the extent that the OPT extension increases the supply of new foreign graduates, employers are in a strong bargaining position with respect to hiring foreign workers for entry-level positions. In comparison, senior-level positions require more work experience, an important attribute of human capital (Ang et al., 2002; Mithas & Krishnan, 2008), and are less likely to be substituted with foreign graduates. We therefore expect that the complementary effect of OPT extension on domestic workers is weaker for entry-level positions than for senior-level positions.

We measured work experience using the number of years an occupation requires from O\*Net database. An occupation was considered to be entry-level if it typically requires less than four years of related work experience, while an occupation was categorized as senior-level if it generally necessitates more than four years of related work experience. Panel B of Table 9 shows that the OPT extension has a stronger complementary effect on the number and wage of senior-level workers than entry-level workers. This result suggests that the OPT extension has further increased the demand for domestic workers in senior-level occupations, which require more work experience and are less replaceable by foreign graduates. Overall, the results in Table 9 further suggest that foreign IT workers possess different skills from domestic IT workers. Thus, our findings indicate that OPT extension workers complement rather than replace domestic IT workers.

## Discussion and Contributions

## Discussion

In this study, we examined the effects of the STEM OPT extension, which has increased the inflow of high-skilled foreign workers in STEM, on domestic employment, and obtained several intriguing findings. We found that this policy change is positively associated with an increase in the number and wage of domestic workers in STEM occupations. More interestingly, it has a stronger effect on the number and wage of domestic workers in IT occupations than in other STEM occupations.

We further explored the underlying mechanisms in the observed complementary effect. The results suggest that the OPT extension complements domestic employment by spurring more local innovation and entrepreneurial activities and that this effect is more prominent in IT industries. In addition, we found that the complementary effects are stronger for occupations with higher skill transferability. Given the high skill transferability of IT occupations, this result further reveals the underlying mechanism for the stronger complementary effects of the OPT extension for IT than for non-IT occupations.

## Contributions

Our study makes several crucial contributions to IS and related disciplines. First, it contributes to the IS literature by theorizing the unique characteristics of IT workers and demonstrating the different impacts of the OPT extension on IT and non-IT STEM occupations. We theorize the wide applicability of IT skills across industries. Empirically, our results demonstrate that the impact of the increase in the supply of high-skilled foreign talent on domestic employment is stronger in the IT field than in the other STEM fields, providing new theoretical insights for the IS literature.

Second, our study contributes to labor economics by examining the effects of a key immigration policy—STEM OPT extension—on domestic labor markets. While most prior research has focused on immigration policies that apply to all occupations (e.g., H-1B cap) with mixed findings (e.g., Doran et al., 2022; Kerr, 2013; Matloff, 2013), our study specifically focuses on an immigration policy that only applies to highskilled workers in STEM. We also found that its impact is heterogeneous by occupational characteristics. Specifically, our findings indicate that the complementary effect of the OPT extension is stronger for occupations that have higher skill transferability, low modularizability, or require more work experience. These findings greatly expand the body of knowledge in labor economics on high-skilled foreign labor.

## Policy Implications

Our study also provides important policy implications. The OPT program is considered the “largest high-skilled worker recruitment program” in the U.S. (Neufeld, 2019). However, limited attention has been paid to examining its role in the domestic labor market. Our findings that the OPT extension complements domestic employment through innovation and entrepreneurial activities offer crucial implications for policy makers to design more effective immigration policies that attract and retain more capable foreign talent. Greater retention of high-skilled foreign workers will allow the U.S. and other developed countries to continue to benefit from their talent. Policy makers should leverage more concrete empirical evidence on the effect of immigration policies rather than focusing on political or ideological viewpoints. It is even more important, considering that many developed nations are fiercely competing with each other to attract foreign talent (Beechler & Woodward, 2009).

In addition, our study reveals the unique role of foreign IT workers in complementing domestic workers. Even though IT workers are considered to be an important asset for the U.S. economy, few immigration policies have specifically focused on this group of high-skilled workers. The increasing need for digitalization and the boundaryless nature of IT workers have made them the essential elements for innovation and entrepreneurial activities for almost all industries. Foreign IT workers can provide specialized skills that are in high demand or are difficult to fill using the domestic workforce. Thus, a policy to retain high-skilled foreign IT workers will further fuel economic growth in the long run.

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, our identification strategy leverages a policy shock that results in variations in OPT periods across occupations (STEM vs. non-STEM). Although our analyses adopted a comprehensive set of control variables and advanced econometrics methods for causal identification, further work is necessary to ensure that few confounding factors are influencing the findings. Second, even though we used several measures (e.g., college-educated foreign population) as proxies for foreign workers, due to data limitations, we were unable to directly estimate the effects of the OPT extension on the number and wage of foreign OPT workers or explore the direct relationship between foreign OPT workers and domestic workers.<sup>17</sup>

Our study provides fruitful opportunities for future research. First, while the unit of analysis in our study is geographic areas (i.e., MSAs), future studies could explore the impact of immigration policies on firm-level employment. It would be interesting to conduct more in-depth analyses of how the employment of foreign IT professionals influences domestic workers at the individual level. Second, our study focuses on the role of the OPT extension, a policy that applies to all STEM occupations. If future immigration policies emerge that specifically apply to IT occupations, scholars could examine their effects to better understand the role of foreign IT labor.

Her research delves into areas such as digital labor markets, IT human capital, two-sided online markets, and information privacy. She employs a diverse set of methodologies in her studies, including econometrics, field experiments, machine learning, and text analytics. ORCID: https://orcid.org/0000-0002-0580-9488

Jing Gong is an associate professor of commerce at the McIntire School of Commerce, the University of Virginia. Her research interests include digital marketing, digital platforms, IT and labor markets, and the economics of information systems. Her work has been accepted for publication by journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, The Accounting Review, Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Journal of Retailing. Min-Seok Pang is an associate professor of management information systems and a Milton F. Stauffer Research Fellow at Fox School of Business, Temple University. He received a B.S. in industrial engineering and an M.S. in management from Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST) and holds a Ph.D. in business administration from the University of Michigan. His research interests include, among others, strategic management of information technology in the public sector and technologyenabled public policies. His research has been published in top-tier academic journals such as Management Science, MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research (ISR), and Strategic Management Journal. His research has been featured in several news outlets such as The Wall Street Journal, Computerworld, Federal Computer Week, and TechCrunch.
