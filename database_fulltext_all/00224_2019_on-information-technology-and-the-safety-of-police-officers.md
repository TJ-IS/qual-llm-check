# On information technology and the safety of police oficers

Min-Seok Pang<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Paul A. Pavlou<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Fox School of Business, Temple University, 1810 N. 13th St, Philadelphia, PA 19122, United States of America

<sup>b</sup> Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, 4750 Calhoun Rd., Houston, TX 77204, United States of America

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Police IT Oficer safety Crime intelligence Crime prediction Crime investigation Occupational safety

## A B S T R A C T

Police oficers perform their duties every day under the constant threat of violence, and each year, more than 50,000 police oficers in the United States sufer assaults. In this paper, we examine the relationship between IT use by the police and the number of police oficers killed or assaulted in the line of duty. To do so, we theorize how IT use helps organizations identify, predict, and avoid risks to its personnel and put forth that IT use for crime intelligence, prediction, and investigation reduces violence against police oficers. We also conducted an interview with a police captain, who provided first-accounts of how IT use helps police oficers on the ground. Our empirical analysis of a panel dataset from 4325 U.S. local police departments shows that IT use by the police is associated with a significant decrease in the number of police oficers killed or assaulted in the line of duty. We seek to expand the scope of the IS literature by theorizing and empirically demonstrating the role of IT in the occupational safety of personnel in organizations that operate in unpredictable, risky, and life-threatening en vironments.

## 1. Introduction

According to the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), more than 50,000 oficers in the United States (U.S.) each year are assaulted, and as many as 50 police oficers lose their lives in the line of duty. Policing is one of the few professions that perform its duty under a constant threat of danger in peacetime. Distrust and animosity that the public may hold against police oficers—something we have witnessed in recent upheavals in many U.S. cities—pose yet additional risks to them. An oficer's death is not only tragic in itself, but it is also a tremendous loss to society, as the public would lose the skills, experience, and ties with the community that the oficer has accumulated over the years. Injuries to oficers are painful to them and their families, but they are also costly to the public since the police department has to compensate the oficers if they become disabled for an extended period of time or permanently incapacitated. Therefore, aside from the society's moral obligation to protect all of its citizens, it is in the society's best interest to ensure the safety of police oficers, given that they are responsible for protecting and serving society.

Like many organizations, law enforcement agencies rely heavily on information technology (IT) for intelligence gathering, crime investigation, personnel and performance management, and communication with citizens [1,2]. Police departments use IT for gathering and analyzing crime intelligence, such as incident reports, on-street intelligence, and crime patterns [3,4], which the police leverage to deter and solve crimes. In this study, we seek to examine the potential role of IT in reducing violence against police oficers. Accordingly, our research question is: Can IT help keep police oficers safer?

This study examines the relationship between IT use and violence against police oficers in local police departments in the U.S. Drawing upon the information systems (IS) literature [5–8], we first theorize how IT use enables an organization to reduce risks to its personnel in life-threatening settings. Specifically, we posit that IT uses helps organizations identify, predict, and cope with danger to its human resources. Second, integrating the IS literature with the criminology research [9–11], we hypothesize that IT use for (a) crime intelligence, (b) prediction, and (c) investigation (Table 1) enables police departments to lessen risks of violence against police oficers. To buttress our theoretical discussions, we conducted an interview with a police captain, who provided first-hand accounts on how IT use helps police oficers better protect themselves.

To test the relationship between IT use and violence against the police, we conducted an empirical analysis with data from local police departments in the U.S. We obtained data on deaths and assaults of oficers from the FBI. We also used data on police IT use from the U.S. Bureau of Justice Statistics (BJS). We collected demographic and socioeconomic data from the U.S. Census Bureau. Combining these data sources, we built a dataset of 16,320 observations from 4325 local police departments for six years. To account for department-level unobserved heterogeneity that afect both IT use and violence against oficers, we adopted fixed-efects Poisson regressions.

Our analyses yield interesting findings. We find that IT use for crime intelligence, prediction, and investigation is associated with a significant decrease in the number of police oficers killed or assaulted in the line of duty. It is estimated that extensive use of IT by the police can lead to a reduction in violence ranging between 42% and 50%. For an average-sized police department, this amounts to 6–7 fewer assaults or deaths (5.7% of the number of oficers); for a large police department that serves more than 1 million people, this efect represents up to 199 fewer assaults/deaths. Our results are found to be robust to diferent empirical methods, alternative measurements, and other control variables.

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies, if any, to examine the role of IT in the occupational safety of workers in or ganizations that operate in unpredictable, risky, and life-threatening environments. The IS research to date has largely neglected the safety of personnel in an organization, which is perhaps the most vital resource for any organization [12,13]. We contribute to the IS literature by showing that IT can enhance the safety of workers (police oficers in our case) who perform risky, high-stake work in life-threatening environments. We believe that our results can be generalizable to several other occupations that operate in hazardous conditions, such as the military, firefighters, emergency responders, intelligence agents, pilots, truck drivers, or workers in nuclear plants. With a new interdisciplinary approach that integrates IS and criminology literatures, our research aims to expand the boundary of IS research toward public safety, an important policy area to all citizens and provide meaningful policy implications.

## 2. Theoretical development

## 2.1. Prior work

Prior research in the criminology literature has examined how IT use by the police facilitates efective policing [1,3,14–16]. Among such studies, Garicano and Heaton [17] found that IT use does not have a significant impact on crime occurrence and clearance rates but is significantly related to an increase in education requirements and training hours for newly hired oficers. Nunn [18] showed that more computerized police departments are associated with higher expenditures but with fewer sworn oficers. Rosenfeld et al. [11] claimed that after the introduction of Compstat, a data-driven policing program in the New York City Police Department (NYPD) in 1994, homicide rates decreased to a greater (but marginal) extent than in other large U.S. cities. Weisburd et al. [4] showed that police departments that implemented Compstat-like policing were associated with higher flexibility and a more decentralized decision-making authority. Our study extends these prior studies by looking at the efect of IT use on the safety of police oficers, which few prior studies in the criminology literature have examined.

The criminology literature has identified key factors that afect violence against police oficers. Prior studies posit that social instability, political division, and resource deprivation in the community are significantly associated with police homicides and assaults. For example, Jacobs and Carmichael [10] and Kent [19] showed that violent crime rates and income disparity between white and black populations are associated with police oficer deaths. Kaminski et al. [20] and Batton and Wilson [21] showed that police oficers are in more danger in economically deprived places with higher poverty and unemployment. Kaminski [22] showed that social instability indicators, such as divorce rates, are associated with more violence against the police. Nonetheless, to the best of our knowledge, our study is one of the first, if any, to examine how IT use can reduce violence against police oficers.

## 2.2. IT use and the safety of personnel

Drawing upon the IS literature [23,24], we explain how IT use helps organizations protect their employees, particularly in violent and lifethreatening environments [7,8]. Examples include police oficers, soldiers, intelligence agents, firefighters, or workers in chemical or nuclear plants. Such workers are required to perform day-to-day duties with substantial occupational risks. We theorize that organizations can leverage IT to protect its personnel in three key ways: (i) identify what risks endanger their personnel, (ii) predict when and where hazardous situations may occur, and (iii) plan for how to cope with such dangerous and life-threatening situations.

First, organizations can leverage the monitoring functionality of IT to identify what risks workers are exposed to and which of their actions or behaviors contribute to such risks. For example, by analyzing where and when critical incidents occur, factory managers find out which parts of factories are dangerous to workers, what facilities or machinery create risks, and what worker behaviors may compound danger. Information collected from monitoring devices helps the organization uncover the risks that endanger its employees as well as their underlying causes. Second, after identifying risks and underlying causes, the organization can take advantage of the predictive functionality of IT to anticipate when and where critical situations might occur. This functionality enables the organization to proactively avoid dangerous situations. With intelligence on when and where danger is incoming, front-line workers can be warned if a life-threatening situation is predicted to occur. The organization can also take precautionary measures to reduce such risks before an emergency situation occurs.

Third, the decision support functionality of IT helps the organization devise plans and training for how to deal with dangerous situations. Providing employees with adequate information on potential risks help them better understand the nature of danger and the best way to cope with the danger. Workers who perform dangerous duties can be trained with appropriate and suficient preventative measures that decision support systems can provide, so that they safely deal with a critical situation should it occur. For example, workers in chemical or nuclear plants are educated for possible life-and-death situations and trained with drills that simulate the situations. Firefights are regularly trained on how to protect their own lives while saving others' in emergency. With intelligence on the nature of danger and the possible timing and locations of critical situations, the organization can leverage the deci sion support systems in devising efective education and training plans on how to cope with impending danger.

## 2.3. IT use and violence against police oficers

Based on the above discussion that IT use helps (a) identify danger, (b) predict danger, and (c) plan strategies to cope with danger, we posit that IT use by the police reduces the likelihood of violent encounters with criminals as follows:

## 2.3.1. IT use for intelligence

We argue that IT use for crime intelligence supports eforts by the police to identify the nature and extent of danger to oficers. The criminology literature views intelligence an essential tool of policing. Criminologists promote intelligence-led policing, which is an efort to collect and analyze “information related to crime and conditions that contributed to crime, resulting in an actionable intelligence product intended to aid law enforcement in development tactical responses to threats and/or strategic planning” [25] (p. 317). It originated from Compstat, a signature police management program introduced by the NYPD in 1994 [11,26]. It is one of the first data-driven policing initiatives, in which the NYPD analyzes crime patterns to better understand underlying community problems based on crime statistics at the precinct level.

The police rely on a wide range of intelligence to control and solve crimes [1]. Typically, to identify a suspect, police oficers begin an investigation with information and evidence obtained from crime scenes, conduct interviews with victims, witnesses, and neighbors, and sift through copious records of previous crimes and convicted felons. After identifying a person of interest, they collect intelligence on the person, such as his residence, behavior, personality, and modus operandi, all of which they leverage to apprehend and eventually convict him. Without a systematic intelligence-driven approach, it would typically take more time for the police to identify culpable suspects, and they might pursue and apprehend innocent people, wasting time and resources and sacrificing goodwill with the community. This increases the chance of violence against police oficers.

Intelligence-led policing is supported by three IT functionalities – intelligence gathering, in-field report writing, and inter-agency information sharing (Table 1). The police can collect intelligence from internal sources, such as police oficers in the field, external agencies, or ordinary citizens. Use of IT for intelligence gathering, therefore, is imperative to acquire and integrate a variety of relevant intelligence about the suspects from multiple sources. Without efective use of IT, it would be challenging and time-consuming for oficers to obtain credible, relevant intelligence from a haystack of information in a timely manner.

One of the important intelligence sources are oficers on the field who can use IT to write incident reports [27], constituting an efective tool for real-time intelligence. Without such a tool, oficers would have to go back to their precincts at the end of their shift and write incident reports based on their recollection after a long shift or even after several days. Studies have shown that police oficers sufer from chronic stress, fatigue, and burnouts [28–31], which could lead to short-term memory loss and impairment of their cognitive abilities [32–34]. Thus, it is dificult for the oficers to render an accurate account of what exactly happened after their shifts. In-field report writing enables them to produce more fresh and unadulterated information on the spot in real time when their memory is clear, ofering real-time actionable in telligence for combating crimes. It is emphasized that “automating the transmission of incident reports is a critical element in building timely, accurate information and information-sharing capabilities” [27] (p. 1).

To obtain necessary crime intelligence, the police rely on external agencies, such as federal law enforcement agencies (e.g. the FBI), state authorities, and peer police departments. To do so, many police departments use IT to share with and obtain information from external entities. It is unlikely that one single police department possesses all the necessary intelligence to solve and deter crimes. Since criminals frequently move across the jurisdictional boundaries, a police department needs to obtain intelligence on criminals outside of its proper. Hence, it is necessary for the department to share information on crimes, suspects, and other community problems with other departments. It is also critical for local police to acquire information related on national-level crimes or terrorisms from state or federal authorities. Consequently, inter-agency sharing of digital information is a key intelligence source for the local police department.

## 2.3.2. IT use for prediction

IT use helps the police predict future crime occurrences, allowing them to control crimes more proactively rather than reactively responding to crimes that already occurred [1,14,15,35]. By being proactive, the police can anticipate and deter crimes before they take place. For instance, the police leverage intelligence to forecast future crime occurrences, an approach called “predictive policing” [36–38]. Moreover, the police can pinpoint areas where crimes are most likely to occur [39–41]. Intelligence used for predictive policing includes tracking the movement of organized criminals, illegal trades of weapons or drugs, conflicts among gangs, and other socioeconomic conditions in unstable communities [35]. With such intelligence, the police can deploy necessary resources to violence-prone areas, helping suppress violent encounters with police oficers.

We consider three IT functionalities for crime prediction – crime analysis, crime mapping, and hotspot identification (Table 1). With crime analysis, the police can understand, for example, the timing and location of crimes, the spatiotemporal patterns in the movement of criminals, the type of weaponry, or the relationships among criminals or crime groups. Such information helps the police comprehend how oficers in the field can be harmed in a clearer and more precise manner. Crime mapping is used to visualize past crime occurrences on a map, so that the police can have a clear understanding of which neighborhood is susceptible to crimes and from which major problem the community may sufer [4].

Going one step further, the police can identify “hotspots” or areas where criminal incidents are likely to occur in a near future [39–42]. Since the resources of a police department (e.g., oficers, patrol cars, and firearms) are not unlimited, it is critical for the department to allocate these resources to the right places at the right time. With prediction on where and when crimes are likely to occur, the police can move the resources out of other safer places and deploy more resources to such hotspots in a targeted manner [43]. The increased level of police presence in hotspots sends a clear signal to criminals that there is a greater chance of getting arrested if they commit crimes. Sherman and Weisburd [40] found that increased police presence in hotspots reduces crimes and disorderly conduct. Hence, we argue that IT use in crime analysis, crime mapping and hotspot identification can lead to a lower chance of police officers being attacked by criminals.

## 2.3.3. IT use for investigation

IT use for crime investigation enables the police to devise an effective plan for responses to upcoming danger. Reliable intelligence about criminals, such as behavioral patterns, known associates, financial traits, and locations that they loiter helps investigators apprehend them safely with less violence. Without solid intelligence, the investigators would have to resort to their hunches and gut feeling, which in many cases are inaccurate or ambiguous. Hence, crime investigations in the absence of credible intelligence could lead them to try to apprehend innocent but violent citizens who believe that they are not culpable of the crimes, possibly resulting in greater violence against the oficers.

With credible intelligence on suspected criminals, police oficers can plan strategies that reduce violence during chases and apprehensions of the criminals. For instance, if the oficers who try to apprehend a suspect are aware that he is armed and poses danger to the public, they can exercise extra caution in approaching the suspect by equipping themselves with necessary firearms, wearing protective gears, and asking for backup [44]. With such intelligence, the police can prioritize the allocation of resources (e.g. oficers, patrol cars, and weapons) [3,4]. The more responding oficers know about where suspects are or where they are moving to, the more precisely they can target and overpower the suspects before they resist, and the better the oficers can protect themselves from potential violence. With better intelligence, the oficers can efectively adjust their approach and better coordinate with fellow oficers in an informed way that reduces the risk of harm in the line of duty.

## 2.3.4. Interview with a subject matter expert

To further understand how police oficers benefit from IT use, we conducted email and in-person interviews with a subject matter expert who is a captain of a police department in the New York City metropolitan area. He is with a 22-year experience in the force and leads more than 200 oficers in the patrol and SWAT divisions in his department. The city has a population of about 130,000 people, more than half of which are minorities.

In the email and in-person interviews, we asked him how having intelligence about a potential criminal afects an oficer's behaviors with a potential criminal. The captain afirmed that knowing about the criminal makes a crucial and potentially life-and-death diference in dealing with him. He stated that “The more knowledge that I have the more confident I am in my actions and the decisions are made to act properly.” He stressed that crime intelligence leads the oficer to conduct policing in what he calls an “action” mode. Intelligence such as previous criminal histories, weapon possession, or mental conditions allows the oficer to anticipate upcoming danger and interact with the criminal in a way that minimizes violence between them. For instance, he wrote to us: “If I had info immediately, I would know for example no criminal history, no warrants, most likely a working person and my ag gression would be lower."

On the other hand, in the absence of intelligence, the oficer would be in what he calls a “reactionary” mode, without fully understanding how the criminal would respond to an approach by the oficer. He stated that in this situation, the oficer would be “on edge with every move.” In the reaction mode, he explained that: “The element of surprise is on me, my decision making will be poor and based not on cognitive thinking but falling back upon training (which in many cases is very poor).” In other words, the oficer is more likely to react to slight aggression by the criminal more violently or make a mistake in protecting himself. The oficer may assume the worst possible scenario and is likely to use lethal force more readily, further provoking the criminal and poten tially causing more violence between the two.

The captain summed up his perspective by explaining: “The bottom line is the intelligence provides information which allows for action rather than reaction. Action is a conscious efort, reaction is a slower response clouded by noise.”

## 2.3.5. Hypothesis

It is important to note that IT use for crime intelligence, prediction. and investigation is not mutually exclusive, and the police use IT systems in conjunction with other systems for multiple objectives. For example, crime intelligence is needed to predict future crime occurrences and to investigate crimes that occurred. Crime analysis can be used to provide intelligence for crime investigation as well. Therefore, when the police use one IT function for one purpose, they are likely to use the same function for other purposes as well. Hence, based on the above theoretical discussions, coupled by the first-hand accounts from our subject matter expert, we propose one following hypothesis for testing.

Hypothesis. IT use for crime intelligence, prediction, and investigation by the police is associated with a decrease in violence against police oficers.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Data

We examined the efect of IT use on violence against police oficers in U.S. local police departments located in cities, counties, or townships. We collected data on police IT use and other operational information from the Law Enforcement Management and Administrative Statistics (LEMAS) published by the BJS. Mandated by the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act, the BJS has periodically collected comprehensive information on personnel, operations, equipment, and policies from law enforcement agencies across the U.S. since 1987. The BJS conducted mail surveys in 2003 and 2007 on a random sample of 3000 law enforcement agencies, such as local police departments, state police, and specialized police agencies. The response rates to the 2003 and 2007 surveys were 90.6% and 91.8%, respectively.<sup>1</sup> These LEMAS surveys have been regarded as one of the authoritative data sources in criminology research [45–47].

We obtained data on violence against police oficers from the Uniform Crime Reports (UCR) database published by the FBI. The UCR covers a wide range of annual crime statistics and other public safety data from more than 18,000 law enforcement agencies every year. It also publishes the number of police oficers assaulted or killed in the line of duty in each department. Combining the LEMAS and the UCR datasets, our sample consists of 16,320 observations from 4325 police departments in six years (2003–2005 and 2007–2009).<sup>2</sup> One-sample ttests showed that these police departments are not significantly different from others in terms of population and crime occurrence and clearance rates. Other indicators, such as median household income, ethnicity, or educational attainment are not significantly diferent from the national average.

## 3.3. Estimation strategy

Since our dependent variable (the number of oficers assaulted or killed) is a count variable, we estimated a fixed-efects Poisson regression model as follows.

$$
Y _ {i t} = \mathrm{Exp} (\beta \mathbf {X} _ {i t} + \alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t})\tag{1}
$$

where $Y _ { i t }$ is the number of assaults/deaths for department i in year $t , \mathbf { X } _ { i t }$ is the covariate matrix, $\alpha _ { i }$ represents departmental-level fixed-efects, γ refers to year fixed-efects, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is an idiosyncratic error term. Estimating departmental-level fixed-efects allow us to control for timeinvariant unobserved heterogeneity that may afect both IT use and violence against oficers. Such heterogeneity includes socioeconomic conditions related to violence and criminal behaviors (e.g. disorganization in neighborhoods) that are not measured by the control variables, department-specific policies in policing and use of force, and state-level laws and regulations in law enforcement.

We do not expect reverse causality to create substantial bias in our estimation, since the number of oficer assaults or deaths is not among the key performance objectives for police departments. As introduced above, IT use by the police in the U.S. has become widespread after the introduction of Compstat by the NYPD in 1993, which is a short for “Computerized Statistics” [21]. Conceived from strategic management from the business sector [26], one of the underlying principles in Compstat is to provide accurate and timely information made at all levels in the organization [49,50]. According to William Bratton, a former commissioner of the NYPD who created Compstat, the biggest motivation for Compstat was to improve performance of the NYPD, which is measured by crime rates and clearance. We began to run the NYPD as a private profit-oriented business. What was the profit I wanted? Crime reduction. I wanted to beat my competitors – the criminals – who were out there seven days a week, 24 hours a day. I wanted to serve my customers, the public, better; and the profit I wanted to deliver to them was reduced crime.

Our literature review on data-driven policing and police performance management [1,17,18,50–53] universally suggests that the key objective for law enforcement in general, or specifically for data-driven policing reforms such as Compstat, is crime reduction and clearance. In this body of research, however, reducing violence against police oficers does not appears as a performance objective, to the best of our knowledge. For Bratton and Malinowski [54], the desired outcomes in law enforcement are lower crime and higher safety, which take a higher priority over all other outcomes. In Compstat-like data-driven strategic management programs in policing, commanding oficers are evaluated, promoted, or disciplined based on the crime statistics data generated by IT [21,49,50,52]. This implies that major strategic and investment decisions (e.g. IT use) are primarily driven by a need for crime reduction. This is why all of our estimations control for crime occurrence and crime clearance rates.

Also, in addition to departmental fixed-efects, we controlled for a variety of factors in police operations, oficer characteristics, and conditions of neighborhoods (Tables 2 and 3) in order to reduce simultaneity in our estimations. For example, IT may lead to a decrease in violence against police oficers by reducing crime occurrence rates, which are controlled for in our fixed-efects estimation. Alternatively, IT use is linked to an increase in educational requirements for police oficers [17], who could become more adept at avoiding violence against themselves. We controlled for the educational requirements in order to rule out this possibility. Finally, measurement error in our dependent variables is not a major concern since it is dificult to conceal assaults or deaths of police oficers [55]. With respect to measurement error in the IT variables, we have little reason to believe that local police departments would respond to oficial federal surveys (LEMAS) in a misleading or incorrect manner. The Justice Systems Improvement Act requires full cooperation from state and local governments for data collection. We note that as we use the IT variables from LEMAS 2003 and 2007 for the violence measure in 2004–2005 and 2008–2009, respectively, IT use by the police in 2003 or 2007 could change in 2004–2005 or 2008–2009, raising a measurement error concern. We do not believe, however, that measurement errors in the IT use variables are systematically correlated with residuals, conditional on departmental fixed-efects. We will show below that excluding observations in 2004–2005 and 2008–2009 does not change our results.

In sum, the foregoing discussions assure us that conditional on the covariates in Tables 2 and 3 and departmental-level fixed-efects, the IT variables are likely to be exogenous to the residuals in Eq. (1).

## 4.1. Main results

Table 5 presents fixed-efects Poisson regression results with the overall IT use variable. Column 1 shows that the coeficient of overall IT use is negative but statistically insignificant. One reason for this insignificant result might be that the efect of IT use on violence against oficers is not monotonic, in a way that the marginal efect of additional IT use is decreasing. To see if this is the case, we interact the overall IT use variable with a dummy variable that is equal to one if overall IT use is greater than 4. Column 2 shows that the coeficient of overall IT use is negative and significant, but that of its interaction term is positive and significant. In other words, at a lower level of IT use, an increase in IT use for crime intelligence, prediction, or investigation is associated with a significant decrease in the number of oficers who are killed or assaulted in the line of duty. But this efect diminishes at a higher level of IT use; an additional level of IT use does not lead to a much decrease in violence against oficers. Descriptive statistics in Table 4 suggests that the number of officer killed/assaulted is widespread (its standard deviation is larger than the mean). As a robustness check, we run a fixedefects negative binomial regression in Table 5, Column 3, which shows a somewhat weaker but similar result to Column 2.

We used the same cutof for IT use for prediction. Since IT use for investigation is a dichotomous (0/1) dummy variable, we divided the sample by whether it is equal to one or not. ## 4.2. Robustness checks

As a robustness check. we ran fixed-effects Poisson regressions with diferent cutofs for IT use for intelligence and prediction with respect to the group assignments (Table 7). These regressions produce similar results. In order to demonstrate the robustness of our use of the count variable (number of oficers killed or assaulted) as the dependent variable, we used alternative continuous dependent variables. Specifi cally, we normalized the dependent variable by the number of violent crimes that occurred, as police oficers are more exposed to danger when they pursue more violent perpetuators. We also normalized the number of oficer deaths/assaults by the number of oficers and the size of population. As Tables 8 and 9 with linear fixed-efects regressions show, normalizing the dependent variable produces similar results. One might argue that crime investigation is part of gathering intelligence, rather than a separate policing activity. For this, we combined IT use for intelligence and investigation and group the departments into four groups. This exercise provides consistent results (Table 10). As we explained above, the dependent variable is the number of oficers killed or assaulted in each of the six vears (2003–2005 and 2007–2009), and Tables 5 and 6 use a six-year panel dataset. We replicated the estimations with the number of oficer assaults/deaths in a two-year and a one-year window and obtained similar results (Tables 11 and 12).

While we estimated a fixed-efects Poisson model to account fo potential unobserved heterogeneity, there might still be unaccounted factors that afect violence against police oficers. To address this concern, we added a lagged value of the number of assaults/deaths in a preceding year as a control variable. This does not change our results.<sup>4</sup> In addition, we used an alternative dependent variable – the number of assaults in the line of duty (excluding deaths). Once again, the result is consistent with Tables 5 and 6. We also used alternative control vari ables that are not included in the main estimations due to multi collinearity concerns such as the number of violent crimes, the number of police oficers, and poverty level. Using alternative control variables produces consistent results.

## 5. Discussions and conclusion

## 5.1. Key findings

Our analyses on police IT use and violence against the police with a six-year panel dataset provide interesting and meaningful findings. We found that a high level of IT use for intelligence, prediction, or in vestigation by the police leads to a substantial decrease in violence against oficers in the line of duty (Table 6). Our estimation that accounts for a number of factors for violence against police oficers as well as department-level fixed-effects offers credible evidence for the ob. served significant efect of IT use. This finding is in accordance with the theoretical prediction that IT use enables the police to conduct policing in an informed manner that reduces the chance of violent encounters with criminals. We theorize that IT use for crime intelligence, prediction, and investigation allows the police to (i) better identify the nature of risks posed by criminals, (ii) predict impending danger against oficers, and (iii) plan efective responses to the danger. IT use for these three objectives (identify, predict, or plan) lessens the danger to police oficers in the field.

## 5.2. Contributions

To our knowledge, the IS literature has paid scant attention to how an organization can use IT to improve the occupational safety of its employees, one of the most important organizational assets [12,13] in hazardous and life-threatening environments. Since human resources are a key prerequisite to organizational success, keeping employees safe is critical for all organizations, in both the public and the private sector. We thus contribute to the IS literature by conceptualizing the nature and role of IT use in occupational safety and by theorizing how organizations discover, predict, and plan for risks that could endanger its personnel in life-or-death situations. We also contribute to the IS literature [5,6,23,24] by ofering empirical support for the role of IT in the occupational safety in dangerous conditions.

This study also expands the literature on the business value of IT in the public sector [17,23,56–58]. The public sector remains a blind spot in the IS literature in general, and in the IT business value literature in particular, compared to the sheer number of studies in for-profit settings. While public-sector organizations spend a considerable amount of tax dollars on IT to ofer public services [17,56,59], we do not have suficient knowledge of how IT in the public sector creates value to the society. One could argue that since governments, in most cases, are a monopoly supplier of public services within their jurisdictions and not under direct competitive pressures, they hardly have an incentive to efectively leverage IT to deliver greater value to the public [23]. However, our research shows that this argument may not necessarily be the case, at least when it comes to the occupational safety of police oficers. We demonstrate that IT use in police departments can fulfill the broader interests of the community by keeping police personnel safe.

## 5.3. Implications for practice and public policy

This study provides managerial implications for practitioners and public oficials in law enforcement. This study illustrates that police departments can use IT to preemptively identify potential risks and take steps to reduce violence against police oficers. Like many firms in the for-profit sector, police departments heavily invest in training both their new hires and incumbent oficers. This is because efective law enforcement requires capable and professional police oficers who are robust both physically and intellectually [9,60]. Thus, it is critical for police departments to fully ensure the safety of their oficers. The police are also expected to take suficient measures to reduce the risk of duty, and we show that IT can be one such measure to achieve this goal. This has practical implications for law enforcement oficials and policymakers who appropriate funds to the police to invest in appropriate tools to ensure the safety of police oficers on duty. Police departments, particularly in municipalities with limited resources, can use our findings to justify investing in digital technologies for crime intelligence, prediction, and investigation.

Our study also ofers important implications for other government organizations or private-sector firms whose employees perform hazardous duties, such as fire protection, counter-terrorism, construction, biochemical, or transportation. Our theoretical framework and empirical evidence inform managers in these fields that they can utilize IT to identify, predict, and plan for fatal risks. It is paramount for an organization to well train the employees on how to predict risks, avoid perilous situations, and efectively respond to them, and it can utilize intelligence produced by IT to devise training programs and response plans. It is not only moral but economically sound for the organization to protect its workers against danger.

## 5.4. Limitations and suggestions for future research

This study has a few limitations as follows. We acknowledge that the IT functionalities we consider (Table 1) are not comprehensive, and they are limited to commonly-used functionalities in practice. Also, we were not able to obtain more granular data on, for example, what kind of data each police department in our sample uses in crime analysis, how it identifies hotspots, or how police oficers in the field use intelligence. Future research can explore other technologies in law en forcement with more comprehensive datasets or in-depth qualitative evidence. Also, the UCR database does not ofer much information on circumstances of oficer assaults or deaths (e.g., how oficers were injured, by whom, and their ranks and duties). Such data would allow us to conduct more in-depth analyses and obtain deeper insights on police safety.

Public safety provides IS researchers with various opportunities for future research:

First, they can examine how IT afects the efectiveness of law enforcement. For instance, with crime prediction, will police oficers be able to catch ofenders more quickly and clear more crimes? Can police departments, armed with IT, suppress crimes with fewer oficers or weapons? Second, future research can examine how IT use improves the relationship between citizens and the police. Third, IS researchers can study how IT can be used to protect lives in other public safety or military settings and to prevent terrorism or other risks to national security. By doing so, the IS discipline could expand its horizon to an unchartered territory and bring new audiences from the public sector, including organizations that operate in hazardous, life-threatening en vironments.

## 5.5. Concluding remark

Law enforcement is a unique and potentially influential setting for IS research. The police are required to fulfill diverse and often com peting objectives [9,23]. These competing objectives pose significant challenges to law enforcement. Moreover, in enforcing laws, the police are expected to preserve the rights and freedom of citizens who are subject to law enforcement, be they innocent or even culpable. It is also imperative for the police to keep their finest sound and safe. Despite these competing objectives, the mandate of law enforcement is to protect all people's lives. As this study attests, IT plays a significant role in helping the police fulfill their challenging mandate. Notably, our study illustrates that IT is a valuable resource for the police that has the potential to lessen these competing objectives and achieve their multiple competing objectives. Accordingly, we believe that IT can render a substantial public good by keeping oficers safe. More broadly, our findings are likely to generalize to other organizations that operate in hazardous and life-threatening environments, and they can leverage IT to protect their personnel. We thus seek to entice future research on better understanding how IT can have a positive efect on personnel, organizations, and society.
