# Optimal Management of Digital Content on Tiered Infrastructure Platforms

Anna Ye Du, Sanjukta Das

Department of Operations and Information Management, University of Connecticut, Storrs, Connecticut 06269, ram.gopal@business.uconn.edu

Complex migrations in tiered environments are currently possible in a seamless, nondisruptive manner. We model digital content as a network capturing the inter-item impacts and use this network structure to develop optimal migration policies that partition media content into tiers. Addressing the context of large content providers such as video-on-demand providers that employ infrastructure platforms for content storage and delivery, we develop a bilevel programming model to maximize the profits of a price-setting platform and a tiered allocation-setting content provider. We model two fundamental effects with digital content: a revenue effect emanating from the tiered architecture and a traffic generating effect among media objects. Using a detailed longitudinal simulation study, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed provisioning policy and pricing strategy and illustrate the existence and impact of these effects in media markets. Finally, we show that repeated execution of the model can help providers respond effectively to a changing environment and thus better manage the risk from demand fluctuations.

## 1. Introduction and Motivation

Multimedia content providers have faced an exponential growth in digital content in recent years, and this explosive trajectory is likely to be sustained in the future. Information lifecycle management (ILM) using multiple tiers of infrastructure at varying performance and operating cost levels is a natural solution to this growth and the shifting popularity of digital content over time. However, content migration across tiers has traditionally tended to be expensive because of extensive planning, debugging, and reconfiguring. Firms (both content providers using inhouse facilities as well as infrastructure-as-a-service— IaaS—providers) therefore rarely tended to archive data and thus overprovisioned the top-of-the-line storage infrastructure (such as the solid state drives or SSDs) to ensure scalability and prevent service disruptions. Putting all of a firm’s multimedia objects in such a highly accessible high-performance storage and delivery system may assure exceptional quality of service for all objects at all times.

However, this extreme strategy ignores the temporal and media-specific characteristics of digital content and assumes the same revenue earning potential for all objects across the board, thereby being inefficient from a profit maximization perspective. This practice leads to significant inefficiencies in terms of resource utilization, cost, returns on infrastructure investments, and ultimately net returns of the firm. For instance, SSDs provide better performance, higher throughput, decreased power consumption, and increased resiliency against harsh environments than other storage infrastructure. They are also very expensive, have lower capacity, and suffer from low reliability in the long term (TechTarget 2009). However, using intelligent file virtualization technologies, an IaaS provider can now strategically use tiered storage and migrate a client’s data nondisruptively and seamlessly without impacting end user experience (Shen 2011). The state-of-the-art practices in ILM attempt to classify data into tiers of storage and distribution where the tiers represent different tradeoffs between quality of service and cost. Tiered services provisioning is a critical component of managing data migration and necessitates strategically moving media objects between expensive high-end and relatively inexpensive low-end infrastructures.

In response to the growing data needs in recent years, ILM platforms such as F5 ARX, BlueArc, and EMC<sup>2</sup> Invista with Brocade’s application platform have emerged to enable automatic storage tiering and nondisruptive data migration across tiers. Such platforms use a mixture of storage technologies (hardware and software) such as SSDs, serial advanced technology attachment (SATA), data deduplication, and cloud storage. Media companies increasingly recognize this seamless migration capability and hence, as a recent International Data Corporation report indicates, there is a growing need for tiered storage to cost-effectively deliver music, movies, and gaming software to end users (Reinsel et al. 2009). Content distribution firms (media and entertainment companies, for example) are increasingly streaming movies and other media content online using tiered storage solutions offered by providers such as BlueArc and F5 ARX (F5.com 2009, Coughlin 2010). Since 2006, Turner Broadcasting System has been using a multitiered storage system to deliver videos (cartoons, movies, sitcoms) online and to cellphones (Pariseau 2006). In sum, tiered storage solutions enabling ILM are now becoming the cornerstone of the entertainment industry.

Although the technology for seamless and nondisruptive migration is offered by tiered storage providers, multimedia content providers still need to supply the IaaS providers with the object allocation policies to be implemented via automatic storage tiering. Using naïve or ad hoc content allocation policies, even with the most advanced tiered storage technology, will still result in suboptimal profits. A recent survey (Castagna 2009, p. 1) highlights the challenges in designing appropriate policies: 42% of respondents chose “classifying data so that it is sent to the right tier” as their “biggest pain point related to their tiered storage system.” The design of optimal policies should be driven by the consumption patterns of each of the digital objects held by a media firm. These patterns change over time, driven by factors such as recency, relevance to current events, media mentions, etc. Injudicious and ill-timed relegation of specific multimedia objects to low-end infrastructures may cause quality of service and revenues to suffer, and keeping a stale object in a higher tier for longer than necessary consumes precious capacity that may be better utilized to increase firm profits. Hence the design of optimal allocation policies for use in tiered storage systems is critical for such voluminous data- and communication-intensive applications and to truly take advantage of the cost-effective delivery model promised by such systems.

Digital media objects evolve fast, as newer videos and music are released frequently. Object popularity changes over time, as seen by weekly Billboard charts and movie earnings. Videos, for instance, may be frequently accessed during the first few weeks of release, followed by sporadic access; they may again experience a surge in popularity after months or years. Furthermore, the pervasive use of recommender systems for such objects indicates that they rarely are isolated entities. Recommender systems may be constructed using machine learning techniques such as association rule mining and cluster analysis. In association rule mining or sequential rule mining, customerlevel sales data are utilized to derive conditional probabilities between pairs of digital media objects. For cluster analysis, any number of product attributes may also be incorporated in conjunction with aggregated sales data to construct similarity matrices. These conditional probabilities or similarity metrics such as Jaccard coefficients then depict the strength of the associations or similarities between a set of objects. These associations or similarities can be subsequently exploited to drive traffic in a strategic manner through a combination of recommendation links and wellthought-out Web page design. Thus, recommender systems and portal designs together leverage content popularity and more crucially, use many, often strong, linkages to drive traffic to and spur revenue growth from other content.

Existing migration strategies, however, do not address these effects. Consequently, we envision a digital media network where nodes depict multimedia objects and arcs capture the impact of one object on another in terms of customer consumption habits. These links capture the cognitive associations between media objects that form organically in the minds of the end users as a result of a combination of underlying similarities between the objects and strategic recommender system or Web portal design that exploits observed browsing or purchasing behavior. A significant body of literature focuses on the derivation of recommender systems based on a variety of object attributes. The multimedia object provider may follow any of these prescribed strategies for building and periodically updating her recommender system. We acknowledge the important role that such systems play in the revenue-generation process for multimedia objects and thus, focus on developing a revenue-driven migration strategy for such digital media networks.

Media firms typically contract with an IaaS provider such as Limelight or Akamai for content distribution solutions. In addition to managing and migrating data across heterogeneous storage platforms, IaaS providers may offer services for objects allocated to high-end tiers, such as replication or demand-based caching to distributed servers. IaaS providers must optimally price tiers such that net profit less tiered infrastructure costs is maximized. Pricing of tiers would affect media providers’ allocation strategies; when a tier is priced high, fewer objects would be allocated there. However, high-end tiers could generate higher revenues. Thus, the media provider must optimally allocate objects across tiers to maximize net profits. Apportioning media objects between tiers would affect revenues generated from the objects as well as their delivery cost to the market. Thus, a conjunction of temporal and media-specific factors and differential tier pricing are key to determining optimal digital content migration policies.

The first and most important contribution of our work lies in the modeling of this mutually dependent pair of problems as a bilevel programming problem and subsequently developing an optimal polynomial algorithm for general media networks. As we elaborate in the next section, none of the existing work in the area of data migration considers relevant market and product characteristics, which we explicitly consider, particularly through the unique concept of digital media networks. We also identify how, unlike the extant ILM literature, our model considers not just the factors that cause the value of information to change over time but also incorporates the networkwide contribution of each piece of information in the pricing and allocation process. We also make contributions to the multimedia infrastructure platform research by explicitly aligning resource needs against revenue-generation capabilities of the media objects.

We use online video objects as an example; however, our model is valid for other digital objects. Online videos vary in popularity as well as pricing. Video objects are typically large and infrastructureintensive, which affects the quality of streaming service and end-user sensitivity to it. Thus, demand for a video could depend on its declared quality of service.

We refer to the IaaS provider as the seller and the media provider as the buyer. General ILM strategies tend to be temporally oriented, driven by factors such as recency, frequency, usage volume, and relevance. In digital media migration, both media-specific and temporal factors jointly affect end users’ access behavior and thus revenues. Some of these factors in the context of digital media networks include node size, node popularity, links to other content via recommender systems and portal design, traffic flows along these links, revenue-generation capabilities of objects, and resource factors such as pricing of online storage and communication services. Based on these factors, we define two important primary effects: the direct access differential revenue effect of tiered services and the traffic-generating effect of the nodes. Conventional wisdom would indicate streaming only videos with high direct access from the high-end tier. In sharp contrast, as a consequence of the two above-mentioned effects, we show that in an optimal allocation of videos across two tiers, sometimes it is beneficial to stream videos with high direct access from the low end and videos with low direct access but high trafficgenerating effect from the high-end tier. This is the second key analytical contribution of our work.

We carried out a 13-week longitudinal data collection exercise from Amazon video on demand (VOD) where we studied migration strategies as many factors change over time: popularity, connections to other videos, traffic volumes on existing arcs, and video download prices. All of these necessitate video reallocation across tiers as the direct access and traffic-generating effects change. The experimental contributions of this paper are (1) a detailed characterization of the optimal solution to the bilevel problem, particularly the dominant traits of objects placed in Tier I; (2) demonstration of the performance of the proposed migration policy vis-à-vis those of practical alternate data management strategies, including the policy of serving all content from the high-end tier; (3) assessment of the revenue-generation effect from the long tail objects when placed into tiers according to the proposed policy; and (4) a demonstration of how a dynamic demand estimation process can improve a seller’s revenue and manage risk from demand uncertainty. The organization of the paper is as follows. Section 2 summarizes related work. Section 3 develops the proposed bilevel allocation and pricing mechanism, and §4 presents comprehensive simulation results. Section 5 concludes with managerial implications and directions for future research.

## 2. Background and Related Work

The domains related to our research are the areas of data migration and archiving, ILM, multimedia storage, VOD, and tiered storage. Research in data migration and archiving has typically focused on network load (Gandhi et al. 2006 and Kokkinos et al. 2008) and redundancy elimination and scalability (You et al. 2005). Kokkinos et al. (2008) consider the problem of migrating data across grid computing networks and consolidating data sets based on task demands; Gandhi et al. (2006) focus on devising efficient processes for converting (or migrating) data from an initial to a target layout with minimal downtime. You et al. (2005) propose an archival storage architecture that addresses various technical aspects of the archival problem, such as data-dependent variability of data compression. Data migration research has thus far focused primarily on technical issues and has ignored the crucial market and product characteristics that our model captures. In our tiered approach to data migration, we consider not just the factors that cause the value of information to change over time but also the return from each piece of information by virtue of the download prices of the online videos. This allows us to integrate revenue management for both the buyer and seller in the migration process of multimedia objects.

Our work is also related to the area of ILM where studies have emphasized the nontrivial nature of information valuation, as not all data are of equal value to the firm and, further, this value tends to change over time (Tallon 2010). ILM research has usually focused on information valuation policy design such as strategies based on usage over time (Chen 2005), policy frameworks for data handling by online service providers (Li et al. 2009), and lifecycle and privacy obligation policies associated with private data collected online (Mont and Beato 2007). The ILM approaches thus far have been largely tactical in nature, emphasizing infrastructure cost minimization. In contrast, as Tallon (2010) points out, addressing the growing information management problem at an operational level using a tiered information framework is important, particularly for unstructured data such as videos and images. In our tiered approach to object allocation, we consider not just the factors that cause the value of information to change over time but also the return from each piece of information by virtue of the download prices of the online videos. This allows us to integrate revenue management for both the buyer and seller in the object allocation decision-making processes of multimedia objects. Note that our research does not predict the information lifecycle trajectory of digital media objects; rather, based on multiple factors associated with the objects (particularly their relationship to one another) at any given point in time, our model decides how to best use the tiered infrastructure platforms typically used for ILM purposes.

Infrastructure platform research in multimedia has mostly focused on optimizing streaming capabilities such as disk scheduling algorithms for video streams (Reddy et al. 2005) or improving load balancing strategies in large-scale multimedia storage systems (Zeng and Veeravalli 2008). In contrast, our polynomially solvable optimal pricing and allocation algorithm tackles key resource management questions by aligning resource needs of multimedia objects with their revenue-generation capabilities. Such alignment must be a precursor to optimizing streaming and delivery of videos, if true value is to be realized by VOD providers from large-scale multimedia systems.

Industry reports (such as Lebeck 2004, Arrington 2006) discuss policies for classifying data for tiered storage architectures. However, they fail to account for the cost and quality of service implications inherent in that tradeoff. Our research fills this lacuna. Current research in tiered systems focuses on business continuity and recoverability of data stored in multiple tiers (such as Aguilera et al. 2007). Minimization of response time is also considered, as in Vengerov (2008), where migration of files between tiers is determined based on recent access patterns and the storage system’s current state, as depicted by queuing times, etc. Focusing exclusively on response time may come at the expense of suboptimal revenues, as that could neglect the revenue-generating potential of the media objects as captured by our digital media network characterization. Our research therefore provides a comprehensive assignment framework of videos to tiers in conjunction with revenue management by observing not just sales of online videos but also the linkages between videos.

Early academic research (e.g., Little and Venkatesh 1995, Brubeck and Rowe 1996) in VOD technology, proposed the use of tiered storage, where objects are stored permanently on tertiary devices and either moved to video servers on demand or delivered directly to viewers, depending on the popularity of the videos. Thouin and Coates (2007) identify the significant effect of the increasing popularity and long tail of video content on storage requirements of VOD systems. However, in the vein of other recent VOD research, they have focused on peer-to-peer exchanges as a way of addressing this challenge. Industry evidence, in contrast, points to a distinct preference by well-established providers such as Amazon and Hulu for large tiered solution providers such as Limelight and Akamai. Hence, although state of the art in VOD research address the highly technical challenges pertaining to VOD start-up times and playback rates on peer-to-peer networks (Annapureddy et al. 2006), a clear lacuna exists in the efficient deployment of tiered systems as part of revenue and media object migration strategies for VOD providers.

## 3. The Bilevel Allocation Policy Determination and Tier Pricing Problems

We consider a content distribution grid with two tiers, both online but with differing performance levels: Tier I has strictly superior quality of service with faster average response time and value-added services such as replication and caching to geographically dispersed edge servers, and Tier II has slower response time without the aforementioned services.

First, we present a profit-maximizing buyer’s allocation problem, given the tier prices of the seller. Consider a digital media network that is a directed graph, as described earlier. Each arc is an inbound link from a source video to a sink video and indicates a referral to the sink from the source’s recommendation list. Videos vary in download prices and inherent popularity. Inherent popularity determines the direct access hits generated by end users finding the video through targeted searches. However, the direct hits are also affected by the tier choice for a video. A tier with a lower quality of service would potentially generate lower direct hits as evidenced by a recent industry report on Web usability (King 2008). Prematurely moving a popular video to a lower tier could therefore affect the buyer’s profits.

The traffic along an arc represents end users who are guided to the sink video through the recommender system. The Amazon VOD recommender system provides for each video a list of videos also purchased by those who bought that video. The idea here is to help product discovery and spur consumption. The strength of an arc is determined by the extent to which the generated traffic at the source video would result in purchases of the sink video. An arc yields indirect hits to the sink video, and hence, the indirect hits represent a positive externality in terms of sales. Finally, the videos differ in terms of their sizes as well, which affect their respective infrastructure costs, depending on the tier chosen for each video. The decision to place a video in a particular tier is therefore a nontrivial problem, as it has networkwide ramifications and thus an ad hoc allocation strategy can adversely affect the buyer’s profits.

Parallel to the buyer’s problem, the price-setting seller solves his own profit maximization problem. The buyer reacts to the pricing by allocating the videos optimally. Given their close relationship, we model them as a bilevel program and develop an optimal algorithm that derives the best pricing and allocation strategy for a given digital media network. As essential building blocks for our optimal algorithm, we define a set of metrics that encapsulate the tradeoffs modeled in the bilevel problem. We define two effects specific to each video: the direct access effect, which is the direct marginal revenue effect of moving a video to the higher tier, resulting in higher service quality and hence higher direct revenues; and the traffic convergence effect, which is the indirect marginal revenue effect of moving a video to the higher tier, hence generating higher indirect revenues from the videos that serve as sinks to its outbound arcs. The traffic upsurge factor is the ratio of these two effects and serves as the key metric determining the seller’s pricing and the buyer’s allocation in the optimal solution to the bilevel problem.

## 3.1. Model Setup

We model a client’s digital media network as a directed graph $G = ( V , { \check { E } } )$ , where the set of nodes is denoted by the vector $( v _ { 1 } , v _ { 2 } , \ldots , v _ { | V | } )$ . The set of revenue-generating nodes is denoted by $S , \ S \subseteq V ,$ with $r _ { i }$ as the unit price for consuming node i. Each node $v _ { i }$ corresponds to a video and needs capacity $c _ { i }$ for storage. Each node $v _ { i }$ attracts $h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I } }$ and h<sup>II</sup>—expected direct hits if stored in Tiers I and $\mathrm { I I } ,$ respectively— with $h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I } } > h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I I } }$ because of differences in quality of service between tiers signaled to the end users. Our model entails fully informed end users—a reasonable assumption, given the current highly connected, social media-aware user population. It may also be in the VOD provider’s best interest to explicitly provide the quality of service information by virtue of average download times for that tier, for example, and thus maintain transparency in its transactions. Further, since video price $r _ { i }$ remains unchanged regardless of which tier it is stored in, we find support for our assumption of $h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I } } > h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I I } }$ in the extensive work done in the well-established fields of industrial organization and vertical differentiation in economics. Hallak and Schott (2011) and Hallak (2006) demonstrate that higher-quality products have higher consumer demand. Hence analogously, for the same movie, Tier II will have a lower demand than Tier I because of its lower quality of service.

When there is a recommendation link from node i to node $j ,$ transition probability $p _ { i j }$ captures the possibility that a user who consumes node i also consumes $j .$ This probability can be directly calculated from purchase data, which is routinely captured by both the VOD provider and its infrastructure platform partner, that ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ the seller. In our experiments, we calculate this transition probability by proxy by analyzing the review trails for movies linked through the recommender system. This approach is provided for illustrative purposes only to demonstrate how our algorithm may be operationalized. The seller and the VOD provider having access to the actual sequence of purchases over time can generate the accurate transition probabilities and rerun the algorithm to derive more appropriate results for their setting.

H _ {i} = h _ {i} + \sum_ {j \in V \setminus i} H _ {j} p _ {j i}.\tag{1}

However, in §3.3, we demonstrate that our bilevel model has the integrality property with respect to allocations; the optimal allocation in response to any price will always be binary for a given node; that is, a video is stored in its entirety in a chosen tier. Based on this, let $x _ { i }$ denote the proportion of $c _ { i }$ stored in Tier I. Then $1 - x _ { i }$ is the proportion of $c _ { i }$ stored in Tier II. Therefore, $H _ { i } = h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I } } x _ { i } + \hat { h } _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I I } } ( \hat { 1 } - x _ { i } ) +$ $\textstyle \sum _ { i \in V } H _ { j } p _ { j i } .$

Following current practice of firms such as Limelight and Akamai, where nonanonymous pricing is the norm, the price in our case is customized for each client’s digital media network. Finally, using reliable infrastructure such as dedicated T1 lines and high-performance expensive solid state drives in Tier I implies higher operating costs for the seller (Mazeika 2014). The greater the amount of content in Tier I, and the greater the popularity of this content, the higher the operating costs faced by the seller. Without loss of generality, as in the above modeling of prices, we also model the marginal cost of serving content from the highperformance Tier I over that of Tier II. 3.1.1. Contract Definition. We now describe the underlying contract structure between the buyer and seller in our model. The seller charges a price $\pi ^ { \prime }$ for streaming each unit of data (measured in gigabytes, megabytes, etc.) directly from Tier I, and the buyer determines the allocation of videos across the two tiers based on this price. For the sake of simplicity, we assume that streaming from Tier II is free. This is not a limitation of the model, as $\pi ^ { \prime }$ is actually the marginal cost of Tier I over and above the Tier II price. Thus, without loss of generality, we present Tier I pricing relative to that of Tier II.

The buyer pays for each unit of data download, regardless of whether streaming was incurred as a result of a direct or indirect hit. But during contract formation, neither party will know, nor reliably estimate, the traffic generating effects. Hence, we assume that the contracted unit price incorporates a predetermined traffic convergence factor, $\alpha ,$ allowing us to configure the buyer’s total cost as a proportion of direct access hits. This traffic convergence-adjusted price () is modeled as follows: $\pi = \alpha \pi ^ { \prime }$ , where $\alpha \geq 1 .$ . The buyer’s total cost is therefore $\textstyle \sum _ { i \in V } h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I } } \pi c _ { i } x _ { i }$ . In essence, this price is based on the estimated total hits for each video $i ,$ which includes both direct and indirect hits. Because $\alpha \ge 1$ , the traffic convergence factor basically adjusts the direct hits upward to also account for the estimated indirect hits.

During contract formation, direct hits to various videos can be reliably estimated using box office sales data and high-quality sales statistics available from studios. Indirect hits, however, are harder to estimate at that time, because the discovery process adopted by the users in the search for each video is not known. So the parties to the contract cannot conjecture on how much every other video will be contributing to a particular video. Therefore, we introduce the traffic convergence factor  to capture the additional costs to the buyer from the potential indirect hits; this factor is negotiated between the buyer and seller at the time of contract formation. We thus model $\pi$ as a negotiated price between the buyer and seller to reflect the real-world scenario in the online video sector, where the VOD provider and the infrastructure provider are both large entities and negotiated contracts are the norm. We refer the reader to the earlier example of Amazon VOD (buyer) and Limelight (seller), where both are substantially large firms and contracts between them are nonanonymous and negotiated.

Once the contract has been executed and parties begin their engagement, over time both can examine the purchasing sequence data from which indirect hits can be estimated. Contracts may also indicate the frequency with which the seller may reevaluate the convergence adjusted price using our bilevel model and purchase data sequences. The contract may also specify how often the buyer can reallocate movies across the two tiers and may follow some industryestablished protocol such as weekly reallocations, since movies are typically released on one specific day of the week. Once the convergence-adjusted price  is set for the contract-ordained length of time, the buyer can periodically reallocate the movies across the two tiers based on the latest demand for each video and the strength of links between them, as revealed by the past sequence of purchases. Our bilevel programming algorithm also provides this optimal allocation for the buyer, based on the convergence adjusted price and demand characteristics of the videos.

The buyer has an incentive to increase hits to the revenue-generating nodes by allocating them and their inbound-linked source nodes in Tier I. However, the cost of serving them from Tier I, $\textstyle \sum _ { i \in V } h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I } } \pi c _ { i } x _ { i } ,$ may limit the buyer to selectively allocating nodes to Tier I. The above formulation represents a buyer such as Amazon VOD, but may be generalized to any setting where the media object provider gets paid on a per-consumption basis, as opposed to a subscription model, regardless of payer. For example, with some minor adjustments, our model can portray VOD providers such as YouTube, which earn ad revenues (either pay per click or pay per impression) instead of charging end users. Recommender systems are featured prominently on such sites. Taking into account the objects displayed in the recommender system, actual browsing data by Internet provider address would provide the trail of video consumption, which may then be transformed to generate transition probabilities. This will enable the construction of the fullfledged digital media network. The browsing data will also yield the number of direct and indirect hits for each video.

The biggest difference (in terms of modeling) between the Amazon VOD provider studied here and the YouTube case lies in the price $r _ { i } ,$ which for YouTube would represent revenue earned on an average from each click or ad impression. We currently see two types of ads on YouTube: those that preface a video and static ones that appear on various landing pages. Most of the ads that preface a video do not allow the viewer to skip the ad; for such ads, each hit (in this case, view) of the video translates into a quantum of ad revenue, $r _ { i } ,$ earned by the seller. A YouTube viewer can watch a video any number of times, so $r _ { i }$ would be accrued for each view, regardless of the uniqueness of the viewer. Since $r _ { i }$ is specific to a video, it can capture the entire gamut of videos with or without ads and capture price differences across ads. For instance, the $r _ { i }$ for videos without ads could be set to zero, whereas videos that start with car ads around the time of the Super Bowl may be much higher than average. In another example, the longer the viewer is forced to watch an ad, the higher the $r _ { i }$ may be set for that video. For the static ads that appear on various landing pages, data will also come from the browsing data, which will also reveal the clickthroughs. If the policy is to charge by impression, then the appearance of a particular page in the browsing data is enough to accrue $r _ { i }$ to the seller. If it is based on clickthrough, then $r _ { i }$ would accrue only if the end user actually clicks on the ad. Finally, for experimental purposes only, our proxy approach of calculating the transition probabilities (as described in §4.1) would be applicable here as well, since review trails can also be collected from YouTube.

First, we address some foundational metrics and discuss the impact of Proposition 1 on the solution framework, including the seller’s pricing strategy. Each node will face two effects: direct access and traffic convergence. Direct access effect measures improvement in quality of service resulting from putting a node in Tier I instead of Tier II, whereas traffic convergence effect measures corresponding improvement from putting its inboundlinked nodes in Tier I instead of Tier II. Direct access effect can be measured for each node in the network, but the traffic convergence effect may be determined for any node with at least one inbound link. We now define the traffic upsurge factor, which is a comparison of these two effects for a given node.

<sup>Definition</sup> <sup>1.</sup> The traffic upsurge factor $\lambda _ { i j } ,$ where node i is the inbound-linked node to $j ,$ is the ratio of the traffic convergence effect caused by node i’s movement from Tier II to Tier I and the direct access effect of node j caused by its movement from Tier II to Tier I.

In Proposition 1, $\lambda _ { 1 2 }$ is the ratio of the traffic convergence effect from node 1, $( h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } - h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I I } } ) p _ { 1 2 }$ , and the direct access effect of node 2, $h _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } \ - \ h _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I I } }$ . The traffic upsurge factor $\lambda _ { 1 2 }$ therefore compares the traffic convergence effect of node 2 via node 1 with the direct access effect of node 2. Proposition 1 compares the traffic upsurge factor against the load placed on Tier I by each video to determine the conditions under which it is optimal to place each video in Tier I. As node 1 becomes more popular, node $2 ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ traffic convergence effect and consequently the traffic upsurge factor also increase. When $\dot { \lambda _ { 1 2 } } \leq ( h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 1 } ) /$ $( h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 1 } ^ { \phantom { } } + h _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 2 } ^ { \phantom { } } )$ , the traffic convergence effect is low and the buyer decides not to put node 1 in Tier $\scriptstyle \mathrm { I , }$ as node 1 is not as significant to node $2 ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ revenues. In that case, the convergence-adjusted price is determined largely by the direct access effect of node 2 and its load on the streaming infrastructure. When the traffic convergence effect is high enough that $\lambda _ { 1 2 } \geq$ $( h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 1 } ) / ( h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 1 } + \check { h } _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 2 } )$ , the buyer has an incentive to put node 1 in Tier I. When $\mathrm { \bar { ( } } h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 1 } \mathrm { ) } / ( h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 1 } + h _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 2 } ) \leq \lambda _ { 1 2 } \leq$ $( h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 1 } + h _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 2 } ) / ( h _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 2 } )$ , the traffic convergence effect is moderately $\mathrm { \ h i g h } ,$ so that with a lower price, the seller is able to entice the buyer to put both nodes in Tier I and earn a greater profit. When traffic convergence effect is even higher, such that $\lambda _ { 1 2 } \geq ( h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 1 } + \check { h } _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 2 } ) /$ $\left( h _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 2 } \right)$ , the contribution of node 1 to node $2 ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ revenues offsets node 1’s streaming load and thus the cost of streaming it from Tier I. Thus, the seller could charge a higher price and earn a greater profit at a point where the buyer continues to put node 1 in Tier I but now decides to put node 2 in Tier II.

Condition $\lambda _ { 1 2 } \overset { \mathrm { ~ \ r ~ } } { \geq } ( h _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 1 } + h _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 2 } ) / ( h _ { 2 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { 2 } )$ implies that the hits from node 1 are relatively high (that is, node 2 has a high traffic convergence effect) and the load placed on the streaming infrastructure by node 2 is relatively large. Thus it is now optimal for the seller to set a relatively high convergence adjusted price to induce the buyer to allocate only node 1 in Tier I rather than setting a lower price to incentivize Tier I assignment for both nodes. Therefore, we derive the following corollary from Proposition 1.

<sup>Corollary</sup> <sup>1.</sup> Under a relatively high traffic convergence effect for a revenue-generating node that consumes relatively larger streaming capacity, it may be optimal for the buyer to put an inbound linked node in a higher performing tier, even if such a node is nonrevenue generating.

The above proposition and corollary highlight the importance of node popularity on the buyer’s allocation and the seller’s pricing strategy. We formally model the traffic upsurge factor in §3.3; it is then utilized in the optimal algorithm to derive the critical values for the seller’s price.

## 3.3. The Profit-Maximizing Allocation and Pricing Algorithm

Based on the buyer’s profit-maximizing problem and the nature of the $\textstyle \sum _ { i \in V } f _ { i } ( { \boldsymbol { \pi } } ) x _ { i }$ expression, we first establish a set of metrics that have certain special properties that are essential to the algorithm profitmaximizing allocation and pricing (PMAP) algorithm.

Note that transition probability affects the indirect hits to a video, and a user may browse through multiple videos before discovering one. Because there are $\dot { V }$ nodes, ignoring cycles and assuming a connected underlying graph, multiple ways of traversing the network from node i to j with a minimum of 1 and a maximum of $V - 1$ edges between the two nodes may exist. Using the concept of transition probability, $p _ { i j } ,$ which denotes the proportion of users consuming i who also consume $j ,$ the expression denoted as $A _ { i }$ presents the total contribution of node i to the revenues generated by the entire network.

$$
\begin{array}{l} A _ {i} = (h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{I}} x _ {i} + h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{II}} (1 - x _ {i})) \\ \cdot \bigg (r _ {1} + \sum_ {i _ {1} \in V \setminus i} r _ {i _ {1}} p _ {i i _ {1}} + \sum_ {i _ {2} \in V \setminus i} r _ {i _ {2}} \sum_ {i _ {1} \in V \setminus i, i _ {2}} p _ {i i _ {1}} p _ {i _ {1} i _ {2}} \\ \quad + \sum_ {i _ {3} \in V \setminus i} r _ {i _ {3}} \sum_ {i _ {2} \in V \setminus i, i _ {3}} p _ {i _ {2} i _ {3}} \sum_ {i _ {1} \in V \setminus i, i _ {2}, i _ {3}} p _ {i i _ {1}} p _ {i _ {1} i _ {2}} + \dots \\ \quad + \sum_ {i _ {V - 1} \in V \setminus i} r _ {i _ {V - 1}} \sum_ {i _ {V - 2} \in V \setminus i, i _ {V - 1}} p _ {i _ {V - 2} i _ {V - 1}} \dots \sum_ {i _ {3} \in V \setminus i, i _ {4}, \ldots , i _ {V - 1}} p _ {i _ {3} i _ {4}} \\ \cdot \sum_ {i _ {2} \in V \setminus i, i _ {3}, \ldots , i _ {V - 1}} p _ {i _ {2} i _ {3}} \sum_ {i _ {1} \in V \setminus i, i _ {2}, i _ {3}, \ldots , i _ {V - 1}} p _ {i i _ {1}} p _ {i _ {1} i _ {2}} \bigg). \end{array}
$$

Both the direct and indirect revenue effects of a node are incorporated in $A _ { i }$ . The direct revenue effect is generated from the video’s direct access hits; the indirect revenue effect captures the video’s impact on the other objects’ revenues. The indirect revenue effect is the end result of cascading traffic over the network from the traffic-generating effect, leading to sales via network traversal. In the expression $\begin{array} { r } { \breve { A _ { i } } , ~ i _ { 1 } } \end{array}$ denotes the nodes that are connected to i by a single arc; $i _ { 2 }$ denotes those connected to i with two arcs in sequence between them and so on in this manner; and $i _ { V - 1 }$ denotes the nodes connected to i with $V - 1$ arcs between them. We express the resultant proportions in the above expression in a compact manner using a matrix, $P = \hat { \{ } P ^ { v } ( i , j ) ; ~ i , j \in V , i \ne j \}$ , where $P ^ { v } ( i , j )$ is the referral probability that the end user who buys video node i also buys node j by traversing v arcs. Therefore, cell $P ^ { v } ( i , j )$ is also the percentage of node $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ hits that lead to indirect hits to node j through a path consisting of v arcs. Since there can be $\nu = 1 , \cdot \dots , \mathbf { \hat { \boldsymbol { V } } } - 1$ possible arcs between any node pair i and $j , P ^ { v } ( i , j )$ can be expressed as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} P ^ {1} (i, j) = p _ {i j}; \quad P ^ {2} (i, j) = \sum_ {i _ {1} \in V \setminus i, j} p _ {i i _ {1}} p _ {i _ {1} j}; \\ P ^ {3} (i, j) = p _ {i _ {2} j} \sum_ {i _ {1} \in V \setminus i, i _ {2}, j} p _ {i i _ {1}} p _ {i _ {1} i _ {2}}; \quad \dots ; \\ P ^ {V - 1} (i, j) = \sum_ {i _ {V - 2} \in V \setminus i, j} p _ {i _ {V - 2} j} \dots \sum_ {i _ {3} \in V \setminus i, i _ {4}, \ldots , j} p _ {i _ {3} i _ {4}} \\ \cdot \sum_ {i _ {2} \in V \setminus i, i _ {3}, \ldots , j} p _ {i _ {2} i _ {3}} \sum_ {i _ {1} \in V \setminus i, i _ {2}, i _ {3}, \ldots , j} p _ {i i _ {1}} p _ {i _ {1} i _ {2}}. \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

Thus the total percentage of node $i \prime \mathrm { s }$ hits that contribute to indirect hits to node j through paths with 1 to V −1 arcs is $\textstyle \sum _ { v = 1 , \dots , V - 1 } P ^ { v } ( i , j )$ . Utilizing this indirect hit effect from node $i ,$ we provide a general formulation of the traffic upsurge factor $\lambda _ { i j }$ based on the discussion in §3.2

$$
\lambda_ {i j} = \frac {(h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{I}} - h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{II}}) \sum_ {v = 1 , \dots , V - 1} P ^ {v} (i , j)}{h _ {j} ^ {\mathrm{I}} - h _ {j} ^ {\mathrm{II}}}.\tag{6}
$$

For any node $i , \lambda _ { i j }$ measures the revenue impact of node $j$ by improving the quality of service on i as opposed to improving it on j. Note that when the traffic convergence effect from node i to node j is higher than the direct access effect of node $j ,$ the revenues of node j are decided more by node $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ service quality and consequently, this stimulates the traffic upsurge factor. Proper allocation of node i therefore becomes more critical for node j. Thus, in the PMAP algorithm we first calculate the traffic upsurge factor for every node pair $( i , j )$ , as this quantifies the relative importance of every node i to the buyer’s total revenue. The traffic upsurge factor is then utilized by the seller to set the profit-maximizing convergence adjusted price for Tier I.

As a precursor to constructing the generalized node-specific price function utilizing the traffic upsurge factor, we formalize the expression $f _ { i } ( { \boldsymbol { \pi } } ) x _ { i }$ introduced intuitively in §3.2 by substituting the expression for referral probability into Equation (2) as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} f _ {i} (\pi) x _ {i} \\ \qquad = ((h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{I}} - h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{II}}) x _ {i}) \bigg (r _ {i} + \sum_ {j \in V \setminus i} r _ {j} \sum_ {v = 1, \ldots , V - 1} P ^ {v} (i, j) \bigg) - h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{I}} c _ {i} x _ {i} \pi \\ \qquad = \bigg ((h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{I}} - h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{II}}) \bigg (r _ {i} + \sum_ {j \in V \setminus i} r _ {j} \sum_ {v = 1, \ldots , V - 1} P ^ {v} (i, j) \bigg) - h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{I}} c _ {i} \pi \bigg) x _ {i}. \end{array}
$$

This expression captures the networkwide effect of improving the service quality of node i as it models the proportion of its hits that contribute to revenues of all other nodes within the digital media network. Following the intuitive rationale in the numerical example in §3.2, all critical values of $\pi$ can be obtained by setting $f _ { i } ( \pi ) x _ { i } = 0 ; \forall i \in V .$ Let $\pi _ { i }$ denote the critical value obtained from the Equation $f _ { i } ( { \boldsymbol \pi } ) x _ { i } = 0$ . We therefore derive the following expression:

$$
\pi_ {i} = \sum_ {j \in V} (h _ {j} ^ {\mathrm{I}} - h _ {j} ^ {\mathrm{II}}) r _ {j} \frac {\lambda_ {i j}}{h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{I}} c _ {i}}.\tag{7}
$$

For a given node, this critical value indicates the most the seller can charge to stream it from Tier I. Using the traffic upsurge factor, the algorithm determines the critical value associated with each node i.

The higher the traffic upsurge factor $\lambda _ { i j } \mathrm { i } s ,$ the higher the critical price $\pi _ { i }$ is. In Equation $( 7 ) , \lambda _ { i j } / ( h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I } } c _ { i } )$ can be considered as the unit contribution of node i to $j ,$ which also evaluates the importance of node i to the revenues of j against the capacity consumed to serve i. Then the critical price determined by $\pi _ { i }$ is the sum total of the unit contribution of node i to the entire digital media network, weighted by the direct access effect of all nodes, $h _ { j } ^ { \mathrm { I } } - h _ { j } ^ { \mathrm { I I } }$ , and their respective end user prices, $r _ { j } .$ Therefore, as the unit contribution of a node increases, the node steadily gains in importance vis-à-vis other nodes in the network. When the seller sets the convergence-adjusted price to a particular critical value $\pi _ { i } ,$ any node $k \in V \backslash i$ whose critical price $\pi _ { k }$ is less than $\pi _ { i }$ would not be put in Tier I. The condition $\pi _ { k } < \pi _ { i }$ implies that the weighted unit contribution of node k is less than that of node $i ,$ which implies that node k has a lower direct and indirect revenue impact than node i. This could be because of lower popularity of node k or because node k does not drive enough traffic to other nodes in the network, or both. Then by setting the price at $\pi _ { i } ,$ the seller chooses to earn greater profit from nodes that are at least as popular as node i against the tradeoff of giving the less popular node k to the end users free. Based on this intuition, algorithm PMAP derives the seller’s profit, $z _ { i } ,$ by setting the price at each critical value $\pi _ { i } ,$ using expression (8), where $x _ { j } ( \pi _ { i } ) ; j \in V$ is the allocation strategy for node j given price $\pi _ { i }$

$$
z _ {i} = (\pi_ {i} - \delta) \sum_ {j \in V} h _ {j} ^ {\mathrm{I}} c _ {j} x _ {j} (\pi_ {i}).\tag{8}
$$

The allocation $x _ { j } ( \pi _ { i } )$ is set to 1, if the critical value corresponding to node j is at least as much as this price; that is, $\pi _ { j } \geq \pi _ { i }$ and is zero otherwise. The algorithm then selects the profit-maximizing critical value as the optimal price and produces the optimal node allocations in Tiers I and II in response to this optimal price, along with the corresponding optimal profit to the seller.

## 4. Empirical Evaluation

We carried out a longitudinal empirical study to evaluate the proposed migration and revenue-management strategy. We collected data on 1,334 unique videos from Amazon VOD over 13 weeks and used these to construct the complete digital media network for each week and to approximate their expected direct hits. Review data for the videos in our data set were also collected to infer the transition probabilities within the network. The complete simulation consisted of six major experiments pertaining to the empirical contributions outlined in §1. Without loss of generality, in all of these experiments, except in the experiment (included in the online supplement) where we specifically study the impact of increasing marginal cost of streaming, we employ zero marginal costs in the analyses. Incorporating a positive marginal cost would quantitatively change the results, but the qualitative results and implications continue to hold, as shown by the sensitivity analysis for the streaming cost. We introduce a metric termed the revenue-generation effect that measures the indirect networkwide revenue consequences of any video from its traffic generation potential. This metric is a measure of the contribution of a video toward the revenues of other videos in the network and is a result of its popularity, allocation, prices of other videos, and the strength of its links with other videos. This metric is utilized in the long tail analysis and is modeled as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} B _ {i} = h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{I}} x _ {i} \sum_ {j \in V \setminus i} r _ {j} \sum_ {v = 1, \ldots , V - 1} P ^ {v} (i, j) \\ \qquad + h _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{II}} (1 - x _ {i}) \sum_ {j \in V \setminus i} r _ {j} \sum_ {v = 1, \ldots , V - 1} P ^ {v} (i, j). \end{array}
$$

## 4.1. Experimental Environment

The Amazon VOD data were collected between February $^ { 6 , }$ 2010, and May 8, 2010. The Saturday of every week has been picked as the day for data collection since the digital media network is refreshed at Amazon VOD on that day. This is also consistent with the standard movie distribution practice of releasing new theatre and home videos on Fridays. Each week, the Web pages pertaining to the 800 best-selling movies in that week were downloaded. These pages yielded the following items for each video each week: the ranking on the bestseller list for purchase and/or rental, which is based on recency of release and sales volume; running time; overall Amazon VOD ranking or sales rank, which is based entirely on sales volume; purchase and/or rental price; and other videos listed by the recommendation system for that video. The running time of the movie is used to estimate the size of the video, with one minute of video assumed to be 0.01 GB.

From each video’s recommender system in each week, we obtained the list of videos that form a logical connection to it, with the given video serving as the inbound linked video (source) to each of these videos (sinks). On average, each video connected to 25 other videos, with a minimum of 0 and a maximum of 75. This allowed us to construct a directed network for the digital media each week. We also downloaded the reviews for each video, specifically collecting the user name of the reviewer and the date of review. To allow adequate time for users to leave comments, we collected these reviews in mid-September 2010. In the absence of actual user-specific sequential purchasing data, this proxy method allowed us to infer the transition probability for each arc in the digital media network using Equation set (5), as explained in full detail in the online supplement, starting with a simple example.

Next, we derive the direct hits for each movie. There is presently no evidence that Amazon VOD uses a tiered infrastructure. Hence, we assume that the data gathered from their site pertain to media objects stored in infrastructure equivalent to Tier I in our model, and we use this assumption to derive the total hits $H _ { i }$ from the raw data. Note that the downloaded data show the Amazon sales rank; they do not explicitly provide units sold, that is, hits. However, Brynjolfsson et al. (2003), Chevalier and Goolsbee (2003), and Ghose and Sundararajan (2006) have all studied strategies for extracting sales amounts from the Amazon sales rank. Adopting their approach, we use the following log-linear distribution:

$$
\log [ H _ {i} ] = \log [ \alpha ] + \beta \log [ s a l e s r a n k ].\tag{9}
$$

To derive estimated coefficients that are representative of movie products, we collected weekly box office earnings from http://www.imdb.com/ over the same collection period. The weekly earnings are available only for the top 10 highest grossing movies that week.

We therefore collected data on 130 movies over a 13-week period. One of these movies was identified as an outlier and thus removed from the data set because it was 1,305% higher than the second highest grossing movie that week; the top-ranking movie in all other weeks earned on average only 122% more than the corresponding second highest movie. We assumed that the ticket price for all movies is \$10 and thus derived the unit sales for each of the 129 movies. We recognize that even for the same movie, box office revenues are normally substantially higher than those earned from other distribution channels. Furthermore, Amazon VOD is only one provider in a very competitive online video market that includes players like Hulu and Netflix. We therefore normalized the unit sales derived from IMDB by a factor of 1,000. Note that the entire experimental analysis may be replicated using any scaling factor. The results will be quantitatively different but will not change qualitatively. For real-world implementations of our model, this scaling will not be necessary, as the company will have the exact count of hits for any given movie. Using the normalized unit sales and the IMDB ranks (1–10) as inputs to the regression model (9), we obtained the estimated coefficients $\hat { \alpha } = 8 . 2 2 2 8 8 4$ 4 and $\hat { \beta } = - 1 . 0 5 6 2 2 6 7$ with an $R ^ { 2 }$ value of 48.12%. We assume that the trend established by this model persists for less popular movies as well and thus extrapolated to higher values of Amazon sales rank using these coefficients.

Because of space limitations, we explain the various data transformation issues that arose in the above process, the way we dealt with these issues, and then our process of using unit prices to derive $H _ { i }$ in the online supplement. We subsequently derive $h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I } }$ using Equation (1). Based on an industry report on Web usability design by King (2008), which demonstrates an average 20% degradation in traffic from lower quality of service, we estimate $h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I I } } = \rho h _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { I } } ,$ , where $\rho = 2 0 \%$ . Finally, note that the sales rank may change from week to week, which would reflect in the change in weekly hits as well.

We carried out our simulation study using publicly available data; thus, no empirical claims are being made because of the nature of the proxies described in this section. Barring an Amazon VOD– specific data set, the proxies allowed us to derive sales estimates for our experiments and demonstrate the testability of our analytical results. However, the study may be replicated using actual detailed revenue data and viewer logs from the digital media provider when available to generate more fine-tuned and realistic results; this would not change the qualitative nature of our results. Finally, even though extended free previews are not present in the weekly top 800 movie data set that we study, we briefly discuss here how such previews may be handled in our model. The extended free preview and the movie itself may be treated as two distinct objects, each with its own node characteristics, including transition probability matrices, whose allocations then need not be identical. In fact, Amazon VOD does treat these objects separately, as evidenced by ample examples where the full movie appears in the recommendation set of the extended preview, where there are rating differences between the two objects, and, most important, where there are marked differences in the recommendation sets of the two objects. Treating the extended preview and the full movie as two separate objects would not place any substantive burden on the PMAP algorithm.

## 4.2. Experimental Study

4.2.1. Characterizing the Optimal Allocation Policy and Pricing Strategy. First, we summarize the optimal allocation and pricing for all 13 weeks of data. The optimal convergence-adjusted prices lie in the range of [\$0.016, \$0.047] per GB per hit with an average price of \$0.026. The maximum price of \$0.047 was achieved in week 7 and can be considered an outlier, as it is 62% higher than the second highest price. The percentage of videos allocated to Tier I in each week lies in the range of [5.10%, 93.8%], with an average of 51.12%. To conserve space, we represent the allocation patterns for only weeks 4 and 10 in Figure 2; allocation for all 13 weeks can be found in the online supplement. These two weeks are the most representative of the norm. The critical values pertaining to each video are plotted on the y axis and, as can be seen from the clusters, any video with a value of at least as much as the corresponding $\pi ^ { * }$ is placed in Tier I. Interestingly, we find several videos in the long tail (that is, those with higher Amazon VOD rankings or fewer direct hits) getting placed in Tier I, whereas many popular videos are placed in Tier II. Note especially in week 4, under the optimal allocation policy, the buyer would still prefer to store some of the movies in the long tail even if the seller were to charge approximately \$0.06. These movies drive revenues in other videos because of the strong linkages established between them. The revenue-generation effect metric in §4.2.3 captures the importance of some of the movies in the long tail.

In the absence of an optimal algorithm, one could intuitively think that the movies in Tier I would be the more popular ones, that is, those with smaller sales ranks (H1) or with relatively higher prices $r _ { i }$ (H2). Our algorithm uses not just the direct revenue impact (which is an effect of both popularity and price) in the allocation and pricing strategy but also incorporates the revenue-generation effect of each video by virtue of the traffic upsurge factor, which allows us to evaluate the ability of a video to drive revenues of other videos. We would therefore expect that movies allocated to Tier I would tend to have a higher revenue-generation effect (H3). Here we investigate if the optimal allocations in Tier I show any obvious predominance on an average of any of these three factors. 4.2.4. Risk Management Through Dynamic Demand Estimation. A static, one-shot model may lead to situations where one party unfairly benefits over the other, when real demand deviates from the assumed demand. Here we explain with the help of an experiment how a dynamic demand estimation process can be used to learn from the continuous stream of incoming sales and browsing data, to effectively overcome inadvertent unfairness resulting from demand fluctuations. We then compare the performance of our learning-enabled model against that of a truly static model that assumes perfect information. We are limited to publicly available data in this research and thus had to rely on proxies such as Amazon sales ranks and reviews. In reality, the seller can observe not only the actual sales as they happen but also end users’ browsing behavior over time, which would allow the seller to track both direct and indirect hits and accordingly estimate the transition probability in each period. Absent access to such data, we simulate the process of arrival of hits and reviews. Our simulation mimics the process of observing the arrival of review data; this process is similar to the real-world decision maker’s process of learning by observing end users’ browsing behavior over several periods, in that both processes learn from observations. Our experimental design is described in detail in the online supplement.

We compare the weekly profit and revenue obtained in the learned approach against the omniscient approach (using perfect information). The average revenue and profit ratios over 30 runs of the experiment are shown in Figure 5. The seller benefits from learning more frequently as his revenues under learning are at least 90% of the revenue under perfect information when he learns every three hours or more frequently. The VOD provider’s revenue under learning can sometimes be over the revenue under perfect information, as, in the former case, by definition the seller does not possess perfect data and thus may occasionally compute prices that are not equal to that under perfect information. The VOD provider then benefits from such pricing. Figure 5 shows that the difference between the learned approach and the omniscient approach closes when learning happens at a faster rate, that is, when the frequency of dynamic demand estimation and node reallocation increases. Thus, if the decision maker runs the model more frequently, he can respond faster to the changing environment and thus better manage the risk from demand fluctuations. Therefore, any inadvertent advantages gained by one party are only temporary, as the system learns and adapts quite quickly, as shown in our experiments. This leads to a more fair pricing and allocation system in the long run. Our dynamic demand estimation process provides a mechanism to effectively manage the economic risk associated with uncertain demand. This set of experiments and additional analysis pertaining to Figure 5 are discussed extensively in the online supplement, along with a demonstration of how the frequency of learning affects the quality of estimates.

## 5. Theoretical and Practical Implications

We have developed a joint strategy by which a tiered service provider such as Limelight can set quality of service–differentiated infrastructure prices and a media content provider such as Amazon VOD can allocate digital content across tiers. We propose a bilevel model where both parties maximize profits and develop a polynomially-solvable optimal algorithm. We model two primary effects—direct access differential revenue effect caused by the tiers and traffic-generating effect caused by the media objects— which together lead to a traffic convergence effect on any node, resulting in direct and indirect marginal revenues from a node. We show the circumstances under which traffic generating effect can outweigh direct access effect and thus ensure placement in a higher tier even when a video does not generate revenues itself. This traffic-generating effect is used in conjunction with the direct access effect within the proposed bilevel optimization algorithm to determine the profit-maximizing convergence adjusted price for the seller, in response to which the buyer determines the optimal profit-maximizing allocation.

We ran detailed experimental studies with 13 weeks of data from Amazon VOD to obtain complete digital media network structures, from which we derived the optimal weekly prices, allocation strategy, and buyer and seller profits. We find that optimal allocation of a node in Tier I is significantly driven by its price (via the direct access effect) and by its traffic-generating effect on the rest of the network, not by the more conventionally used popularity metrics such as sales rank. We compare our proposed strategy with a recency- and frequency-based allocation policy and demonstrate the profit impact of considering network characteristics in allocation strategies. We find evidence justifying Tier I placement for videos in the long tail by examining their proportional revenue-generation effect. We also demonstrate that both the seller and the buyer benefit from frequent learning through the dynamic demand estimation process. Finally, in the online supplement, we test the impact of a stronger network on profits, convergence adjusted prices, and allocations and find that although it has a dampening effect on Tier I allocation, it increases profits to both parties because of higher unit prices. We also examine the surplus in our model vis-à-vis that of a vertically integrated market and find that the former performs favorably.

Some key managerial implications emerge from this study. First, sellers of tiered services would do better to maintain a narrow technological gap between the two tiers; because of the highly connected nature of digital media objects, a larger difference in quality of service would mean greater erosion in networkwide revenues. Second, if the buyer and seller are interested in forming a vertically integrated alliance, the bilevel solution can serve as the basis for negotiation and the evaluation of the transaction costs in that process. Finally, videos in the long tail can justifiably be placed in Tier I, as they enable discovery of other videos; even though their direct access effect is relatively lower, their traffic-generating effect has larger networkwide revenue implications, often more so than more popular items.
