# Investigating the moderating role of uncertainty avoidance cultural values on multidimensional online trust

## A R T I C L E I N F O

## 1. Introduction

## A B S T R A C T

Building online trust and understanding its relationship to online customer purchase decision making is important to e-commerce companies. We decided to investigate the moderating role of uncertainty avoidance on the relationship between subjective norms and online trust (integrity, ability, and benevolence) as well as purchase intention on a model we had developed. We generated a questionnaire and first pilot tested it (n = 116), and then modified it before performing the main test of our model (n = 209). Specifically, trust (cognition and affect based) was adapted to better understand social and cultural factors in online customer behavior. Uncertainty avoidance was found to moderate the relationships between subjective norms and two dimensions of cognition-based trust, (integrity and ability). Benevolence, however, was not found to have any relationship to cultural values or purchase intention. Normative influence on ability belief about the website was significant only when the online consumer had a high sense of uncertainty avoidance. Theoretical and practical implications of these findings are discussed.

Building online trust and understanding its relationship to online consumers’ purchase decision making are important topics for e-commerce system managers and information systems (IS) researchers [1,6,8,19,27]. Venkatesh et al. [23] recently argued that questions related to consumer trust in the use of technology, the risk involved, and their expected consequences with the design characteristics and features are a few of the potentially important and fruitful research areas that can deepen our understanding of individual-level technology adoption and use with a specific focus on services. Online shopping has grown steadily in popularity in the U.S. in recent years as online and hybrid retailers offer an attractive Internet-based service to customers. For example, Amazon.com topped out among the nation’s 10 fastest-growing retailers with a growth rate of 219% and 2010 revenues of \$34.2 billion. Thus, online trust and consumer behavior should be investigated both online and hybrid companies [18].

Even though there have been many attempts to explain online trust and purchase intention, e.g. [17], one of the main questions is how social and cultural factors influence online trust and consumer behavior. Anecdotal evidence has suggested that 30– 75% of customer relationship management initiatives fail because organizations implement them without assessing the initiatives cultural readiness and social aspects.

Thus, we had three important factors to investigate:

1. Social influences as they affect ‘‘multidimensional’’ online trust. For this purpose, we included subjective norms as antecedents of multidimensional trust in our model.

2. Cultural moderating effects on these relationships.

While social and environmental factors on trust building are crucial issues to e-commerce, this is the first empirical study that has tested the specific relationships between subjective norms and multidimensional trust beliefs. Given that uncertainty and risk are the main constructs related to trust beliefs we investigated the moderating role of uncertainty avoidance of cultural values in our model.

3. The influence of integrity and ability on online purchase intention since these two aspects of trust should be important factors for technology-mediated behavior (as cognition-based trust), while benevolence would not (since it is affect-based trust).

Further, given the importance of demographic variables in technology adoption, gender, age, and Internet experience were included in our analysis as control variables.

This model was intended to show how the individual’s cultural values causing avoidance of uncertainty or risk influenced different dimensions of online trust beliefs and online purchase intention. By understanding these relationships, e-commerce system designers should be able to improve website functionalities based on social presence and network, cross-cultural issues, and human– computer interaction.

## 2. Literature review

Both the cultural psychology and IS literature [4,9,10,20] suggest the potential advantage of using measurement of culture at the individual level. Specifically, uncertainty avoidance is posited as a moderator to trust. Srite and Karahanna stated that while national culture is a macro-level phenomenon, acceptance of technology is an individual concern. They assessed cultural traits by using personality tests and argued that individuals espouse national cultural values to differing degrees. They found a moderating effect of uncertainty avoidance in the relationship between subjective norms and behavioral intention. More recently, Yoon [26] showed how consumer ecommerce acceptance depended on the effect of culture on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, trust, and intention to use. He adopted Hofstede’s well-known cultural dimensions as moderators.

One can argue that culture is a collective phenomenon and irreducible to the individual level of analysis. Nonetheless, culture can only manifest itself through the individual and then be aggregated to the collective. Thus, assessing each individual’s espoused cultural values is both appropriate and meaningful for predicting individual-level behavior.

McCoy et al. [15] recently suggested that a cultural dimension based on nationality should not be directly applied to technology adoption at the individual level. Data collected in the U.S. and Uruguay also supported this argument. Hofstede’s four dimensions of culture (uncertainty avoidance, power distance, individualism/collectivism, and masculinity/femininity) were adapted and revised as individual-level cultural values by Srite and Karahanna. Venkatesh et al. also commented that culture should be considered in specific social situations as an antecedent to user acceptance of IT.

Collective culture and individual-level cultural values are neither theoretically irreconcilable nor mutually exclusive: they provide complementary perspectives on the impact of culture on behavior [3]. Uncertainty avoidance is related to prediction, intentionality, capability, and transference; an individual’s intent to avoid uncertainty or risk remains as a major predictor of intent to purchase.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

3.1. Subjective norms and multidimensional online trust

There are three sub-dimensions of online trust:

\- Integrity is the belief that a trusted party adheres to accepted rules of conduct, such as honesty and keeping promises.

\- Ability is the belief in the skills and competence of the trusted party.

\- Benevolence is the belief that the trusted party, while hoping to make a profit, wants to do good to the customer.

Gefen and Straub found that high social presence, typically in face-to-face communication, had positive effects on integrity and benevolence, but not on ability.

Venkatesh et al.’s UTAUT showed that social influence in voluntary contexts influenced perceptions about technology adoption but the three sub-dimensions have not been tested with subjective norms, and we therefore decided to determine their moderating effect on family, media, and friends’ influences. We therefore hypothesized:

H1-1. Subjective norms will positively influence integrity of online trust beliefs.

H1-2. Subjective norms will positively influence ability of online trust beliefs.

H1-3. Subjective norms will positively influence benevolence of online trust beliefs.

## 3.2. Uncertainty avoidance

Given the importance of individual-level issues to online trust and consumer behavior, we used Srite and Karahanna’s uncertainty avoidance values as moderators at the individual level. In strong uncertainty avoidance cultures, individuals feel threatened by unknown or uncertain situations, resulting in increased nervous stress and anxiety and the need for formal rules and structure in the workplace and personal relationships.

Srite and Karahanna [21] found that only uncertainty avoidance consistently moderated the relationship between subjective norms and intention to adopt. People with high uncertainty avoidance culture would frown on conflict and value compromise. ‘‘Loose’’ relationships with low uncertainty avoidance cultures are associated with less regard for stability, permanence, and integrity in relationships and with greater risk taking; on the other hand, the stability of a ‘‘tight’’ society permits trustors to develop confidence and integrity. Tight societies with high uncertainty avoidance valued durability, permanence, and solidarity, which may be considered to be in the integrity dimension of online trust.

Uncertainty is reduced through the influence of friends who inform them of their personal experiences and perceptions, or when they observe peers using the system. In addition, normative pressure from supervisors and peers to use the system reduce uncertainty since it provides strong that the system is deemed socially desirable and appropriate. Thus, we hypothesized:

H2-1. The relationship between subjective norms and integrity is moderated by the uncertainty avoidance cultural values such that the relationship is stronger for individuals with high uncertainty avoidance cultural values.

The value placed on a target’s capability is determined by uncertainty avoidance. People with low uncertainty avoidance deal easily with risk or uncertainty, common sense would be the dominant trust-building mechanism. There is a strong emphasis on experts and expertise in high uncertainty avoidance cultures. Consequently, we hypothesized:

H2-2. The relationship between subjective norms and ability is moderated by the uncertainty avoidance cultural values such that the relationship is stronger for individuals with high uncertainty avoidance cultural values.

We decided that we would not examine the moderating role of uncertainty avoidance on benevolence, since it is an affect-based trust focusing on caring and emotional connection to each other in a personal relationship. The formation and maintenance of trust in temporary work settings, such as virtual teams, would therefore depend more on the cognitive element (integrity and ability) than the affective element (benevolence).

## 3.3. Online trust beliefs and purchase intention

Many IS studies have used purchase intention as a surrogate for purchase behavior. Gefen et al.’s Trust-TAM found a direct positive relationship between unidimensional trust belief and purchase intention. Unlike cognition-based trust, which has been studied mainly in the context of working groups, affect-based trust has typically been studied in the context of close relationships. We hypothesized that integrity and ability would influence online purchase intention:

H3. Integrity of online trust will positively influence online purchase intention.

H4. Ability of online trust will positively influence online purchase intention.

Fig. 1 presents our research model. Thus, our study includes uncertainty avoidance as moderators of the relationships between subjective norms and multidimensional online trust beliefs and technology adoption [12]. But we posited that benevolence would not be significant in technology-mediated behavior.

## 4. Method and measures

## 4.1. The survey instrument

Most of the measurement items were adapted and revised from previous research on multidimensional online trust beliefs, TRA, and espoused cultural values section (see Appendix A for details). All questionnaire items used a 5-point Likert-type scale with 1 = completely disagree, 3 = neither agree nor disagree, and 5 = completely agree.

We developed an online survey website and posted this URL to the class management system for students to access. As a result of a pilot test using 116 participants, we reworded some items and showed that the valid items had more than a 0.70 composite reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) and more than 0.50 item-to-total correlations using SPSS (see also Appendix A for the items and reliabilities in the pilot test). Based on these results we excluded three items in uncertainty avoidance cultural values, resulting in the use of the same two items as did Srite and Karahanna in their study.

## 4.2. The population for the survey

Our target university was in the northern region of the U.S. and was selected as high in the ‘‘Diverse Student Population’’ category by the annual survey of The Princeton Review (this ranking was the result of a survey of more than 115,000 college students nationwide at 361 universities and showed that our sample represented diverse cultures). Our online survey used undergraduate business students, who voluntarily participated.

As we expected, the nationalities of the sample was representative of a wide range of cultural backgrounds with: 9% African-American, 8% Asian-American, 10% Hispanic, 61% Caucasian, and 12% international.

## 4.3. The methodology of the survey

The experiment was conducted in an Internet classroom in which each student used his or her own PC. The students, an additional 209, who were not pilot test participants, were told to navigate to www.amazon.com, and work through the procedure of purchasing a book without actually submitting the final purchase transaction. Then they were told to complete an online survey based on their experience on the website. The main objective of this experiment was to refresh the participants’ memory without manipulating the participant or creating trust.

We awarded bonus points to the final grade of survey participants. The same procedure was adopted in the mail test as was used in the pilot. The test took about 20 min for participants to navigate the website and 25 min to complete the survey.

The average age of these participants was 22 years, and 56% were female. Of the total, 92% reported having already used an ecommerce website to buy products while 66% reported using the Internet 4–20 h per week, 20% reported more than 20 h and 15% used it less than 3 h per week.

## 5. Data analysis and results

The testing was conducted using Partial Least Squares (PLS) Version 3.0. The PLS approach [7], like other SEM techniques such as LISREL and EQS, allowed researchers to simultaneously assess measurement model parameters and structural path coefficients. The component-based PLS uses a least squares estimation procedure. PLS avoids many of the restrictive assumptions underlying covariance-based SEM techniques. Furthermore, it allows both formative and reflective constructs to be tested together. In our model, all items were modeled as reflective indicators because they were viewed as effects (not causes) of latent variables, except for subjective norms which are formative in nature. The largest number of independent variables estimated for a dependent variable or formative factors is three. Thus, our sample size of 209 was more than adequate for the PLS estimation procedures.

The measurement model in PLS was assessed by examining internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. Internal consistencies (similar to Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.7 or higher were considered adequate. Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed by applying two criteria: (1) the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) by a construct from its indicators was at least 0.707 (i.e., AVE > 0.50) and was greater than that construct’s correlation with other constructs, and (2) item loadings were at least 0.707, and that item loaded more highly on the construct it was intended to measure than on any other construct. The structural model and hypotheses were assessed by examining the significance of the path coefficients and the variance accounted for by the antecedent constructs. Table 1 shows the internal consistency reliabilities and correlations among constructs. Table 2 shows the factor structure matrix of the study variables, demonstrating strong convergent and discriminant validity, as all items exhibited high loadings (>0.707) on their respective constructs, and no item loaded higher on other constructs. Collectively, the psychometric properties of the constructs were excellent.

Fig. 2 provides the results of hypothesis testing. Bootstrapping (with 500 sub-samples) was performed to test the statistical significance of each path coefficient using t-tests. Moderating effects were modeled using the Chin et al. [2] approach; crossproducts were created by multiplying indicators of each of the interacting constructs. These cross-products were then used as the indicators in the interaction term. The model explained the variance of the dependent variable, purchase intention $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 8 )$ Overall, all hypotheses are well supported by the empirical test results except for the direct effect of subjective norms on ability (H1-2). As expected, benevolence, the affect-based trust, showed no significant moderating effect with uncertainty avoidance cultural values, or direct influence on purchase intention. We also tested demographic variables, such as age, gender, and Internet usage experience, as control variables for purchase intention. None of the three control variables were significant, as expected.

To assess common method bias in the survey design, we first ran Harman’s one-factor test, in which all the principal constructs were entered into a principal components factor analysis. Since each of the principal constructs explained roughly equal variance, the test results did not indicate common method bias. Second, a partial correlation method was used in which the first factor from the principal components factor analysis was entered into the PLS model as a control variable on the dependent variable. This did not produce a significant change in variance that the model explained in the dependent variable, again indicating a lack of common method bias.

## 6. Discussion

Our results confirm the primary hypotheses. First, uncertainty avoidance moderates the relationships between subjective norms and the integrity and ability dimensions of online trust. This is an important and interesting finding, which made it possible to include cultural aspects at the individual level of analysis and relating them to multidimensional trust. When people have a high tendency to mitigate uncertainty by adopting strict codes of behavior and by establishing formal rules, their belief that the trusted party adheres to these accepted rules of conduct is moderated by the belief. Thus, to increase the integrity dimension of online trust, website designers should emphasize the subjective aspects (such as providing feedback mechanisms) as well as reducing uncertainty by formal rules of conduct.

Second, the influence of subjective norms on ability of the website is significant only when the customer has high uncertainty avoidance.

Third, the integrity and ability dimensions have positive effects on purchase intention, while benevolence does not influence it. The belief that the website adheres to accepted rules of conduct, such as honesty and keeping promises, and the belief in the skills and competence of the website to provide high quality customer service positively influence the purchase intention of the website.

## 6.1. Limitations of our study

Our study used only one popular website, rather than including multiple websites or a new website unfamiliar to the student participants. This reduces the generalizability of our findings.

Second, our study included only uncertainty avoidance, and excluded the other cultural dimensions (power distance, collectivism/individualism, and masculinity/femininity). The other potential relationships were beyond the scope of our study.

Third, the model did not include other important antecedents of trust, such as situational normality, structural assurance, propensity to trust, and institutional influence on technology adoption, such as commitment. Finally, uncertainty avoidance focused on adherence to rules and regulations rather than the anxiety. While the study considered risk taking/avoidance, the informants were not making payment and thus were not exposed to an actual risk.

## 7. Implications for research

Uncertainty avoidance has been integrated into our online trust and consumer behavior model; this helps us understand the ‘‘situating culture’’ [25]: explaining that culture is locally situated, grounded in actual behavior, and embedded in everyday, social practice. Further, our model explained the complex relationships using multidimensional trust and subjective norms to provide an understanding of the relationships. Second, our study shows the role of trust—both cognition- and affective-based—in determining online customer behavior.

## 8. Implications for practice

Website designers and e-commerce managers can draw interesting implications from our study. The social aspects of a website should be carefully designed and implemented to increase purchase of products or services. For example, the Lands-End’s website tries to enhance the customers’ shopping experience through extra services like ‘‘Shop with a Friend,’’ which enables two shoppers to access, browse, and shop together on the Web. Li et al. [13] also emphasized these social and cultural aspects of website design by arguing that website design can incorporate one-to-one personal connections between a consumer and a business representative [24] via real-time communication technologies. Furthermore, chat rooms and discussion forums are other ways to enhance communication between a website and its users.

Customers are more likely to decide to purchase on the website when feedback from other customers and information from the mass media enhance their trust about the website. Third party seals, such as WebTrust or TRUSTe, would be an effective intervention in this relationship.

## 9. Conclusion

Uncertainty avoidance is an important moderator of the relationships between subjective norms and the integrity and ability dimensions of online trust to consumer behavior. Normative influence on ability belief about the website is significant only when the online consumer has high uncertainty avoidance. In today’s e-commerce environment, where online trust and purchase are the main issues affecting the success and survival of a company, our findings should help by providing a more comprehensive understanding of these phenomena.
