# The assimilation of RFID technology by Chinese companies: A technology diffusion perspective

Jie Wei <sup>a,</sup>\*, Paul Benjamin Lowry <sup>b</sup>, Stefan Seedorf <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Information Management, Sun Yat-sen University, China

<sup>b</sup> College of Business, Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information Systems, University of Mannheim, Germany

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 17 May 2013 Received in revised form 3 April 2015 Accepted 3 May 2015 Available online 27 May 2015

Keywords: Innovation assimilation RFID technology TOE framework DOI theory

## A B S T R A C T

RFID (radio frequency identification) is an emerging technology that attracts the attention of supply chain participants. However, most Chinese firms adopt this innovation without fully utilizing its benefits. Extant IS research only focuses on factors that impact its adoption (rather than post-adoption factors). Assimilation theories suggest that most information technologies exhibit an ‘‘assimilation gap’’ such that widespread usage tends to lag behind their adoption. Drawing upon the TOE framework, we investigate antecedents of RFID assimilation into these Chinese companies. Empirical results indicate that factors such as IT infrastructure, managerial capability, absorptive capacity and environmental uncertainty significantly influence RFID assimilation.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, RFID (radio frequency identification) has emerged as a new technology that increases operations efficiency in warehouse management and inventory monitoring. Compared to the traditional barcode, RFID can identify tagged products without line of sight and track the status of products when they are received or shipped away from the warehouse in real time. Therefore, product inventory status can be captured with less labor force and, thus, can increase inventory management efficiency.

Most recent RFID-related research focuses on its adoption. These research studies include Lin and Ho’s [39] empirical study on the RFID technology adoption of logistics service providers in China, Tsai et al.’s [67] investigation of RFID adoption intention of Taiwanese retail chains, and Wang et al.’s [70] investigation of RFID adoption in Taiwanese manufacturing industries. However, adoption is only one part of an assimilation process and cannot ensure wide-scale assimilation. The benefits of RFID technology can be fully realized only through wide-scale assimilation. As indicated by Chatterjee et al. [9], many firms have failed to achieve deeper integration of their organizational practices, which extends beyond initial adoption; thus, they cannot realize the full potential of this innovation. Because of the distinction between adoption (or acquisition) and its degree of deployment or routinization, the antecedents and mechanisms of innovation deployment may differ from the drivers of innovation adoption. According to the literature reviews of Fichman [21] and Zhu et al. [76], a focused study on the post-adoption stages of assimilation is especially warranted.

Moreover, China’s RFID market remains in its infancy, especially in the manufacturing industry [34]. Compared to developed countries such as the USA, where RFID industry chain includes mature and large-scale projects from giant business (such as Wal-Mart), most RFID projects in China are relatively small and are in a pilot test stage. Because China is a world-class manufacturing center, Chinese manufacturing companies are facing pressure from more retailers, distributors, and suppliers to provide RFIDcompatible technology platforms. Thus, it is worth exploring the assimilation process in Chinese firms and promoting the technology’s deep integration.

From a theoretical perspective, most RFID-related research focuses on Western developed countries rather than developing countries such as China. However, different cultural contexts and environmental settings may generate different results. For example, Chau et al. [10] investigated the assimilation of Internet technologies in China and found that contrary to the popular Western idea that environmental uncertainty has a positive influence on a firm’s proactive and innovative strategies, environmental uncertainty plays a negative role in Chinese firms’ Internet assimilation. According to Chau et al. [10], Chinese firms also have the greatest concern for the regulatory environment in which they and their business partners reside. This is because the Chinese government continues to maintain powerful political influence and control over the development of innovations. These differences motivate us to investigate how RFID can be assimilated in Chinese firms and how this process differs from that in Western countries.

In this paper, we follow Zhu et al. [55] and conceptualize assimilation as the full life-cycle that includes not only a company’s evaluation of an innovation and its adoption but also its full-scale deployment, in which an innovation ‘‘becomes an integral part of the value chain activities’’. This conceptualization is different from adoption in several aspects: the involvement of vendors is lowered, the system is ‘‘rolled out’’ for routine usage by the operational-level users, and radical customizations such as process reengineering are complete at this stage.

Motivated by the abovementioned theoretical gaps, we propose an integrative model to address the following question: What factors (including technological, organizational, and environmental factors) impact RFID assimilation in the Chinese context? An integrative theoretical model that combines diffusion of innovation (DOI) theory and institutional theory is built to address this problem. The research model is tested with data that were collected from logistics companies in Hong Kong and the Pearl River Delta Region of China that are at different stages of RFID technology assimilation. Structural equation modeling is used to test each factor’s influence across the assimilation processes.

## 2. Review of theories on innovation assimilation

In this chapter, we first illustrate the differences between adoption and assimilation and then review the innovation assimilation literatures in an attempt to determine the antecedents of different innovations.

## 2.1. Differences between adoption and assimilation

Adoption is a widely investigated research topic in IS research. It refers to the physical acquisition or purchase of an innovation [60]. Many existing RFID-related research studies focus on this stage. For example, Lin and Ho [39] conducted an empirical study on the RFID technology adoption of logistics service providers in China. They found that explicitness and accumulation of technology, organizational encouragement for innovation, quality of human resources, and governmental support all significantly influence the RFID adoption intention of Chinese logistics companies. Tsai et al. [67] investigated determinants of the RFID adoption intention of Taiwanese retail chains and found that relative advantage, complexity, supply chain integration and organizational readiness all have significant influences on RFID adoption intention of Taiwanese retailers. Wang et al. [70] studied determinants of RFID adoption in Taiwan’s manufacturing industry and found that the following six variables significantly influence adoption intention: compatibility, complexity, firm size, information intensity, competitive pressure and trading partner pressures. According to Rogers, adoption refers to management’s authorization of the purchase of technology rather than whether the firm has actually deployed the technology or moved beyond an early stage toward routinizing the technology for everyday use. Especially for technologies with high implementation complexity, little or no actual implementation occurs after technology adoption, which is known as the assimilation gap [22].

Previous literature has presented different conceptualizations of innovation assimilation. According to Fichman and Carroll [21], assimilation is the extent to which a firm has progressed through stages of innovation deployment—from initial awareness and adoption to general deployment and routinization. In Zhu et al.’s [76] investigation of e-business assimilation, assimilation is defined as a series of stages from a firm’s initial evaluation of e-business at the pre-adoption stage (i.e., initiation) to its formal adoption and finally to its full-scale deployment at the postimplementation stage, in which e-business becomes an integral part of the value chain activities (i.e., routinization). Bala et al.’s [4] investigation of interorganizational business process standards conceptualizes four stages of IBPS assimilation: awareness, adoption (or rejection), limited deployment, and general deployment. These authors focus on only the subsequent stages (excluding awareness) because of the practical and theoretical importance of the subsequent stages. Consistent with previous research, we define RFID assimilation as ‘‘the extent to which a firm has progressed through stages of innovation deploymentfrom initial awareness and adoption to general deployment or routinization.’’

## 2.2. Antecedents of innovation assimilation

In this section, we introduce literature related to the TOE framework to identify the antecedents of the assimilation of different innovations. The TOE framework identifies the technological, organizational, and environmental factors that can impact IS-related decisions [46]. Most innovation-related research employs this framework to investigate factors that can impact innovation assimilation. This type of research includes Furneaux and Wade’s [26] investigation of discontinuance intention of information systems. Wang et al. [70] draw upon the TOE framework in their investigation of the determinants of RFID adoption in the manufacturing industry in Taiwan. Kuan and Chau [33] employ the TOE framework to investigate EDI adoption in small businesses. Drawing upon this framework, Hong and Zhu [30] investigate six variables that successfully differentiate nonadopters from adopters of e-commerce. Zhu et al. [77] explore how factors within the TOE framework influence the e-business assimilation at the organizational level.

As indicated by Wang et al. [70], the TOE framework has substantial consistent empirical support in the literature; thus, it provides a foundation for the analysis and consideration of factors that can aid in the understanding of an innovation– adoption decision. Therefore, we draw upon this framework to understand the influence of antecedents within each subcategory.

Regarding the technological context, the classic DOI theory proposed by Rogers [61] identifies the following five innovation characteristics: (1) relative advantage, which refers to ‘‘the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than the idea it supersedes’’([61], p. 213); (2) compatibility, which is defined as ‘‘the degree to which an innovation is consistent with existing business processes, practices and value systems’’ [61]; (3) complexity, the degree to which an innovation is difficult to use ([61], p. 230); (4) observability, the degree to which the results of an innovation are visible to others ([61], p. 232); (5) trialability, the degree to which an innovation can be experimented with ([61], p. 231). However, most studies emphasized the impact of the first three attributes: relative advantage, complexity and compatibility. Tornatzky and Klein [65] conducted a meta-analysis of 75 articles to examine the relationship between innovation attributes and innovation adoption. They concluded that three attributes, i.e., relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility, enhance the likelihood of innovation adoption. By contrast, TAM is an established theory that explains the antecedents of IS innovation from a technology perspective. This theory considers two key drivers, namely, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are equivalent to relative advantage and complexity, respectively. However, recent innovation research that investigated Internet-based innovations such as supply chain technologies and e-commerce argues that compatibility has become congruent with existing experiences and practices among trading partners and therefore should be excluded from the relevant research or merged with the attributes of perceives ease of use. Accordingly, we only include the first two technology attributes, relative advantage and complexity, in our research model.

According to Lin et al. [40], organizational context describes the characteristics of an organization, which mainly include firm size, degree of centralization, formalization, complexity of its managerial structure, the quality of human resources, and amount of slack resources available. These factors could help explain why some organizations are more innovative, while others are less prone to innovate. As indicated by Mishra et al. [46], the diversified performance differences of innovation diffusion is due to the significant differences in the resources that the firms possess, which include managerial knowledge, technology infrastructure, and prior experiences with information technology (IT). Some other literatures also suggest that the value that firms obtain from IT is dependent on their skills in leveraging it [45]. Firms that possess strong managerial capability and prior IT experiences can utilize IT more efficiently than their competitors. Therefore, we include managerial capability, IT infrastructure and absorptive capacity, which are organizational resources that are regarded as antecedents.

As indicated by Tornatzky and Fleischer [66], environmental context is the area in which a firm conducts its business – the industry, competitors, and government. DiMaggio and Powell’s [17] institutional theory proposes that institutional environment provides rule-like social expectations and norms for appropriate organizational structures, operations, behaviors and practices. A firm’s perceptions of these pressures affect its interpretation of the environment in general and innovation intentions in particular. Thus, we investigate factors within the institutional pressures that impact RFID assimilation processes. Institutional pressures are classified into three categories: coercive pressure, normative pressure, and mimetic pressure.

Coercive pressure is defined as pressure that originates from political influences exerted by the powerful firms on which the focal firm depends [17]. This pressure is mainly from dominant suppliers and customers because these dominant partners hold resources that organizations need, such as new business contracts or funding.

Normative pressure refers to the perceived extent to which members of the dyadic relational channels have adopted the innovation and the extent to which the government and industry agencies promote the use of IT [17]. In our model, we use regulatory support as the normative pressure that will influence RFID’s assimilation processes.

Mimetic pressures are those that cause an organization to imitate others when the organizational technologies are poorly understood, goals are ambiguous, or the environment is uncertain [17]. Because RFID standards remain uncertain and its investment is irreversible, the RFID market remains uncertain. Companies will follow other companies that have successfully implemented this technology. Meanwhile, fierce competition will cause companies to imitate others that have already successfully adopted this technology into their enterprises. In our research, we include market uncertainty and competition intensity as sources of mimetic pressure.

## 3.1. Predictions related to technological factors

In this section, we introduce influencing factors within the technological context. These factors include relative advantage and complexity. The reasons why we included these factors are discussed below.

## 3.1.1. Relative advantage

Several studies have used relative advantage to predict innovation adoption and diffusion. For example, Tsai et al. [67] investigate RFID adoption in the Taiwanese retail industry and find that relative advantage has a positive impact on RFID adoption. In addition to this study, Wang et al. [70] investigate determinants of RFID adoption in the manufacturing industry and also find that relative advantage has a positive effect on RFID adoption.

Zhu et al. [76] investigate determinants of post-adoption stages of innovation diffusion using enterprise digital transformation as an example of innovation. Their results indicate that relative advantage positively influences e-business usage. Ramdani and Kawalek [56] predict SMEs’ (small and medium enterprises’) adoption of enterprise systems and suggest that the greater the perceived relative advantage of enterprise systems, the more likely they will be adopted by SMEs.

Kuan and Chau [33] investigate the adoption of EDI technology and suggest that relative advantage is a key factor within the technological context that can influence EDI adoption.

According to previous literature, relative advantage is an important factor that motivates organizations to adopt an innovation. This is true because when assimilating RFID technology, decision makers should evaluate its benefit and check whether it has a relative advantage over traditional barcode systems. Compared to traditional barcode systems, RFID can help retailers track and control stock in real time. If integrated with other backend systems, RFID can reduce the lead time and then improve replenishment efficiency and, thus, reduce product misplacement, stock-level, out-of-stock and labor costs [67]. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1. Organizations that perceive the relative advantage of RFID will have a high degree of assimilation.

## 3.1.2. Complexity

Complexity is defined as ‘‘the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use’’ ([61], p. 230). RFID is a radical innovation that is resource intensive and requires substantial changes to existing work processes and organizational contexts [67]; thus, it is necessary for us to include complexity as an antecedent. In the RFID context, Wang et al. [70] operationalize RFID complexity as the immaturity of RFID technology, lack of common standards, and the difficulty of integrating RFID with the existing enterprises’ information systems and business processes. Thus, complexity of innovation should be analyzed to ensure that an organization has sufficient financial and human capital to overcome difficulties during the implementation process.

According to Tsai et al. [67], complexity includes two components: the challenges of customization and high costs.

RFID systems should be customized for a specific working environment; for example, RFID hardware must withstand high temperatures and humidity and operate in a variety of work environment and product materials. There is a great need to adjust the RFID backend system and existing IT systems for better data transmission. The second complexity involves the high investment/maintenance costs. Costs related to RFID operations, including tags, readers and IT infrastructures, are high and irreversible. Costs are further exacerbated by the absence of uniform RFID standards. Companies that perceive high technology complexity will be very cautious of adopting RFID and assimilating it into their enterprises. Accordingly, we predict the following:

H2. Organizations that perceive high complexity of RFID will have a low degree of assimilation.

## 3.2. Predictions related to organizational factors

In addition to factors in the technological context, factors in the organizational context can influence RFID assimilation processes. We include IT infrastructure, managerial capability and absorptive capacity in the organizational context and investigate their influence on the assimilation of RFID into Chinese logistics companies.

## 3.2.1. IT infrastructure

Regarding IT infrastructure, Grant [29] classified IT-based resources into three categories: (1) the tangible resources, comprising the physical IT infrastructure components; (2) the human IT resources, comprising the technical and managerial IT skills; (3) the intangible IT-enabled resources, such as knowledge assets, customer orientation, and synergy.

According to resource-based theory, tangible resources enable firms to assimilate innovations more quickly and improve products [6]. Compared to a less developed and non-integrated IT infrastructure, a highly integrated IT infrastructure provides a platform for a firm to launch innovative IT applications faster than its competitors [6]. Therefore, tangible resources are relevant factors that might influence RFID assimilation processes.

Human resources include two components: technical IT skills and managerial skills. Because RFID assimilation process always entails significant changes to business processes and IT infrastructure, managerial capability plays an important role in coordinating activities that are related to process redesign [77]. Technical IT skills become important in the analysis, design, and implementation of changed business processes.

Intangible resources include customer orientation, knowledge assets and synergy [6]. Previous research suggests that customer orientation has a significant role in innovation assimilation. If a company is more customer-oriented, it will consider improving customers’ satisfaction by introducing innovations such as RFID technology because such technology can shorten the lead time from manufacturers to customers, increase the traceability of products that can make the products more visible and, therefore, improve customer service. Thus, customer orientation might be a significant sub-factor that influences RFID assimilation processes.

Knowledge assets refers to how the knowledge, skills and experiences of the company’s employees is embedded in its processes, policies, and information repositories [6]. Knowledge assets are also critical for the RFID assimilation processes because if a firm’s employees have strong knowledge and skills, it will be easier for them to assimilation new innovations.

Synergy is defined as the sharing of resources and capabilities across organizational divisions [6]. A firm that can share knowledge and information across its functional units is more flexible and can react faster to market needs. Because RFID technology enables information sharing across a company’s warehouse division, purchasing division and production division, it provides a good means of sharing resources and information. Thus, synergy of intangible resources should also be positively related to RFID assimilation; thus, we include it in our research model.

Based on the above review, physical IT infrastructure, human IT resources, and synergy should all have significant positive relationships with RFID assimilation. As indicated by Bharadwaj [6], a firm’s IT infrastructure is a major business resource and a key factor in maintaining long-term competitive advantage. Therefore, we include IT infrastructure as an antecedent of the RFID assimilation process.

According to Bharadwaj [6], IT as a resource can generate competitive value only when it leverages or enables pre-existing resources and skills. Hence, IT infrastructure is critical to RFID assimilation. This limitation should also apply to RFID technology implementation. RFID can achieve its greatest benefits only through integrating with other backend systems such as enterprise resource planning (ERP), customer relationship management (CRM), and decision support systems (DSS) and sharing data and information with them [34]. Therefore, companies that have sophisticated IT infrastructure have sufficient capabilities to assimilate RFID technology into their business operations and processes. Thus, we predict the following:

H3. Increased sophistication of infrastructure leads to increased RFID assimilation.

## 3.2.2. Managerial capability

Asif and Mandviwalla [3] suggest that the deployment of tags and readers alone cannot push companies ahead of their competitors. A more important factor is how an organization uses the fine-grained and real-time data derived from RFID to change and improve its business processes. It is the leveraging of these data that will determine the extent of strategic benefit that the companies can obtain from RFID technology. RFID is a radical innovation that can radically change the operational process of an organization, but doing so requires substantial managerial capability. Fichman [20] thus suggests that technologies that enable more radical improvements require substantial complementary changes to organizational structures, routines, and policies. Consequently, RFID assimilation requires organizational and process adaptations [9].

Roberts et al. [59] note that not all firms can manage adaptation effectively because they lack managerial skills and know-how for changing management. Thus, it is important to investigate the effect of managerial capability, which refers to the capability of managing organizational adaptation to accommodate RFID assimilation [77].

Organizational adaptations regarding RFID assimilation include changing organization structures and coordination mechanisms [9] and acquiring the new expertise necessary to use the innovation [19]. Several studies explain IT failure as a frequent result of management issues such as lack of synergy between business and IT skills, knowledge of how to integrate the technology with the business strategy, and knowledge of how to acquire skilled technical people and train them to use the RFID systems. Such broad management failures suggest that managerial obstacles can impede RFID assimilation when organizations cannot make organizational changes, redesign business processes, and acquire new expertise. Therefore, organizations that have advanced managerial capabilities can assimilate RFID more smoothly than those that do not have sufficient managerial capabilities. Accordingly, we predict the following:

H4. Managerial capability has a positive influence on companies RFID assimilation.

## 3.2.3. Absorptive capacity

As defined by Cohen and Levinthal [13], an organization’s absorptive capacity is represented by its ability to recognize the value of new, external information, absorb it, and apply it for commercial ends. They also note that effective absorptive capacity can be determined by prior relevant knowledge and intensity of effort. Especially in an uncertain environment, absorptive capacity can affect the formation of expectations and enable a firm to more accurately predict the nature and commercial potential of technological advances. Because RFID is a radical innovation with substantial uncertainty, absorptive capacity is needed to recognize its value, absorb it and apply it to companies’ operations.

The existing literature regards absorptive capacity as a knowledge base, especially the extent of prior knowledge that the firm possesses [35]. This is similar to path dependency, which is a firm’s ability and incentive to adopt an innovation. Path dependency can be largely determined by its level of related experience with prior relevant technologies [13]. As indicated by Cohen and Levinthal [13], such skills and knowledge are critical for the successful adoption of new technology standards. Thus, firms that have prior experiences with and knowledge of related technology such as EDI or barcode systems may have developed more technical and managerial skills for deploying RFID technology than firms without EDI or barcode experiences.

In their investigation of migration to open-standard interorganizational systems (IOS), Zhu et al. [78] suggest that firms have fostered technical and managerial skills for IOS implementation and developed a deeper understanding of the economic and organizational impacts of IOS. These skills and knowledge are vital for the successful implementation of new related technologies [13]. Therefore, firms that accumulated rich technical and managerial experiences in using related technology such as EDI or barcode systems may be more skillful for deploying RFID technology than firms without such experiences. Accordingly, we predict the following:

H5. Companies with strong absorptive capacity have a high level of RFID assimilation.

## 3.3. Predictions related to environmental factors

In this section, we introduce environmental factors that can impact RFID assimilation processes. In our research model, environmental factors include competition intensity, regulatory support and market uncertainty. The reasons why we include these factors are illustrated in the following section. Based on these reasons, we propose three hypotheses.

## 3.3.1. Competition intensity

Competition intensity is ‘‘the degree that the company is affected by competitors in the market’’ ([75], p. 24). Porter’s [51] five-force competitive model indicates that competitive pressure is an important external driver in initiating the deployment of IOS among trading partners. Hence, competition intensity likely plays a role in RFID assimilation in China.

According to Wong [72], [49], since 1979, China’s economic reforms toward a market economy have promoted more trade and encouraged more foreign direct investment (FDI). These incremental trades and FDIs contribute to China’s economic growth. As suggested by Fu [25], China has become the second largest FDI recipient in the world—after the United States—and is the largest host country among developing countries. These FDIs bring capital, knowledge, and new managerial skills to China. Their participation increases competition in the domestic markets, which results in challenges in terms of Chinese enterprises’ technology and managerial capabilities.

Moreover, China’s economy is an export-oriented rather than a domestic consumption-oriented economy. According to Wong [72], export-oriented economies are dependent on foreign demand. If there is a slight recession in the international markets, this type of economy will be influenced [18]. For example, most manufacturing factories in Shenzhen recently shut down, and the production of these products has been outsourced to other countries due to the recession of the international economy.

Due to the characteristics of export-oriented economies, Chinese companies face fierce competition from the local, nationwide and international markets. To meet these challenges, they will try to adopt new innovation technologies such as RFID to increase their competitive advantage. Therefore, we posit that competition intensity will promote Chinese enterprise’ RFID assimilation process.

According to Bolloju and Turban [7], competition intensity is positively associated with the rate of adoption of new technological innovations. The benefits derived from first movers’ RFID adoption might arouse decision makers’ awareness of this technology and make them consider adopting it. Compared with traditional barcode systems, RFID can track the status of products in real time and thus improve inventory visibility and asset management. These improved efficiencies are critical for companies to maintain their competitive advantage. Therefore, competition intensity is likely to drive companies to initiate and adopt RFID technology. Accordingly, we predict the following hypothesis:

H6. Competition intensity positively influences RFID assimilation in China.

## 3.3.2. Regulatory support

Regulatory support is a critical factor that influences innovation diffusion [77,74]. Williamson [71] suggests two ways in which government could affect innovation diffusion. ‘‘One way is to take tax and other measures to increase or decrease payoff, the other way is to alter the climate in which they are received’’ [71]. Zhu et al. [77] investigate the assimilation of e-business and find that governments can encourage e-business legislation through supportive regulations and policies.

These issues are particularly important in China. Chau et al. [10] investigate the assimilation process of Internet technologies in China and find that Chinese companies have the greatest concern for the regulatory environment in which they and their business reside. The Chinese government is currently proposing the twelfth five-year plan and plans to invest in R&D of the ‘‘Internet of Things’ and cloud computing and develop digital and virtual technologies. RFID technology is the key enabler of the Internet of Things. Regulatory support from the government can create an encouraging environment that will make decision makers aware of this technology and consider adopting it in their enterprises. Therefore, we predict the following hypothesis:

H7. Government regulatory support positively influences the assimilation of RFID technology in China.

## 3.3.3. Environmental uncertainty

As indicated by previous research, firms that face environmental uncertainty have greater incentives to adopt IOS to improve information exchange and reduce uncertainty between trading partners. Sharma [63] also indicate that firms that face higher environmental uncertainty sense more opportunities, are proactive and innovate more than other firms. Furthermore, Bolloju and Turban [7] indicate that market uncertainty forces organizations to adopt and implement new technological innovations to remain competitive.

However, this situation might be different in China. As indicated by Chau et al.’s [10] investigation of Internet assimilation status in China, market uncertainty has a negative influence on a Chinese firm’s proactive and innovative strategies and behaviors. The reason for this phenomenon is that Chinese firms are more risk averse than Western firms. Consequently, without external support from their business partners in the industry, they are less likely to take initiative to adopt Internet technologies.

Moreover, the adoption of RFID technology requires considerable irreversible investment costs, which Chinese enterprises’ view as risk. Thus, these enterprises are less likely to run the risk and be the pioneers to adopt RFID technology. The cost of an RFID tag is much higher than that of a barcode system, especially for lowprofit products [55]. According to Lai and Hutchinson [34], the costs may decrease to US \$0.20 only when the amount of demand can increase to 5 billion. This problem is even more serious for the low-end product industries, such as toys and clothes, but not as serious for higher-end consumer products, such as smartphones and computers.

Beyond these obvious costs, the greater costs for RFID are the costs incurred for the necessary IT infrastructure (including servers, databases, middleware and applications). As Konsynski and Smith [64] suggest, deployment and development are likely to add significant costs to the tight implementation of RFID technology. Walker [68] also believes that optimizing processes, analyzing data, and training workers would cost companies more than the purchase of RFID technology. Moreover, the investment cost is irreversible due to the tight coupling between the technology and organization. Furthermore, most companies are uncertain as to whether the benefits derived from RFID can cover the irreversible costs of the system.

Regarding standards uncertainty, the Chinese government has decided not to use either EPC or UID; rather, it plans to develop its own RFID standard, which adds unique uncertainty to the Chinese market. Additionally, according to Lai [34], there is still the challenge of who is responsible for drafting the RFID standards. Companies that are at the assimilation stage might find it is difficult to share information and integrate with trading partners in the same industry if they use different RFID standards. These situations will undoubtedly inhibit their RFID assimilation process. Taking these factors into consideration, we posit the following hypothesis:

H8. Environmental uncertainty negatively influences RFID assimilation in China.

## 4. Methodology and data collection

In this section, we introduce the research methodology and data collection methods. The constructs are also operationalized in this section. The measures of the constructs are based on the extant literature and are revised to fit the RFID context. Finally, we report on efforts to establish the factorial validity and reliability of our measures.

## 4.1. Sample frame

We use questionnaire surveys to collect data from various supply chain participants, including manufacturers, retailers and logistics providers. Data were collected through two non-profit organizations, namely, GS1 HK and LSCM (Hong Kong R&D center for logistics and supply chain management enabling technologies). The two non-profit organizations are dedicated to helping the business community foster the adoption of international supply chain standards, technologies and practices that form the backbone of supply chain efficiency, visibility and collaboration. They helped us distribute the questionnaires to their member companies. CEOs, IT managers or CIOs who are familiar with the deployment process of RFID technology were the primary respondents. ## 5.1. Justification of the use of structural equation modeling

Currently, two SEM approaches are available for analysis: the covariance-based approach (such as LISREL, EQS, and AMOS) and the partial least squares (PLS) approach (such as PLS-Graph or Smart-PLS). We used PLS because a strong theoretical foundation is required for the covariance-based approach, whereas the PLS approach does not require such a strong theoretical foundation [67,41]. The reason for these differences is that the aim of the covariance-based SEM approach is to determine whether the covariance matrix of the theoretical model fits the empirical covariance matrix, whereas the aim of PLS is to predict and maximize the explained variance of the manifest variables [27]. Our study explores factors that influence organizational assimilation of RFID within the TOE framework and, thus, is exploratory in nature. Thus, the model does not have a sufficiently strong theoretical foundation to apply covariance-based SEM. We specifically used SmartPLS 2.0 [58] to analyze our measurement model and conduct hypothesis testing.

## 5.2. Factorial validity and reliability

Before we tested our hypotheses with PLS, we first conducted extensive pre-analysis and data validation according to the latest standards for the following three purposes [11,28,42]: (1) to establish the factorial validity of the measures through convergent and discriminant validity; (2) to check for common method bias (despite the use of a careful design to prevent it); and (3) to establish strong reliabilities.

We first established the factorial validity and reliability of our latent constructs. Factorial validity includes convergent validity and discriminant validity. According to Fornell and Larcker [24], a construct’s convergent validity is evaluated by three criteria: (1) item loadings (l) greater than 0.70; (2) composite construct reliability greater than 0.80; (3) average variance extracted (AVE) greater than 0.50. Appendix 3 depicts the item loadings of each construct; the appendix indicates that all of the constructs have item loadings greater than 0.70. Moreover, Appendix 2 indicates that all constructs have composite reliability values greater than 0.80 and AVE values greater than 0.50. Therefore, we can conclude that all of the constructs have high convergent validity.

Discriminant validity is examined by comparing the relationship between shared variances among the constructs and the value of AVE (average variance extracted). If a construct’s AVE is greater than the squared correlations with other constructs, we can conclude that it has discriminant validity. The threshold value of AVE is .5. Thus, if the AVE value is greater than .5, the construct meets discriminant validity. As summarized in Appendices 2 and 4, all of the constructs have AVE scores greater than 0.50 and the squared root of AVE for each construct is much greater than the construct’s correlation with every other construct [27], indicating that our model has sufficient discriminant validity.

Reliability measures the degree to which items are free from random error and, therefore, yield consistent results. It is evaluated by composite reliability and Cronbach’s a. If a construct has a composite reliability above 0.80 and Cronbach’s a value greater than 0.70, we can conclude that the construct has an acceptable level of reliability [24]. Again, see Appendix 2.

## 5.3. Hypothesis testing

Two steps were conducted to evaluate the research model. First, standardized path coefficients and statistical significance for the paths are estimated. Bootstrapping analysis is conducted with 400 subsamples to estimate path coefficients and statistical significance. Second, to assess the predictive power of the structural model, the coefficient of determination (R<sup>2</sup>) for endogenous variables is calculated. Through these two steps, we can test the path significance and coefficient of each variable.

In Fig. 2, we list the path coefficient and significance level of each path. The results indicate that IT infrastructure, managerial capability, absorptive capacity and environmental uncertainty significantly influence RFID assimilation in China. Of these significant paths, environmental uncertainty negatively influences RFID assimilation, while IT infrastructure, managerial capability and absorptive capacity positively influence RFID assimilation. Managerial capability is significant at the p = 0.01 level, and the other paths are significant at the $p = 0 . 0 5$ level.

## 5.4. Non-response bias

To test non-response bias, we performed a t-test to compare the demographic characteristics between early and late respondents. The results indicate that there is no significant difference between the two groups. Therefore, we conclude that there is no nonresponse bias in our survey.

## 5.5. Common method bias

Self-report surveys in which the same rater responds to items in single questionnaires at the same point in time are likely to be susceptible to CMV (common method variance). CMV is one of main sources of measurement error that can threaten the validity of conclusions of relationships between measures. To detect or assess the common method variance, we first employed the widely used method of the Harmon’s single-factor test. According to Harmon’s single-factor test, a common method is assumed to exist if (1) a single factor emerges from non-rotated factor solutions; (2) a first factor explains the majority of the variance in the variables. We used principal component analysis as the extraction method. The results indicate that only one factor is present, and the most covariance explained by one factor is 23.48%.

However, Podsakoff et al. [50] criticized this test as not sufficiently rigorous and proposed the single-common-methodfactor approach. Their approach was later implemented for PLS by Liang et al. [38] and was considered a promising approach until it was debunked as invalid and ineffectual by Chin et al. [12]. Hence, to further augment our approach of assessing CMV, we used the more useful and simple approach advocated by Pavlou et al. [47]. They note that one must simply examine a correlation matrix of the constructs and determine if any of the correlations are above 0.90, which is evidence that CMV may exist. Their fundamental reasoning is that if CMV exists in a data set, there will be extremely high correlations amongst the self-reported constructs. That is, if there are no high correlations, then the threat of any meaningful CMV is remote. Based on an examination of our measurement model statistics, we do not have inflated correlations; thus, there is no reason to believe that CMV is negatively impacting our data.

Thus, we can conclude that the likelihood of common method bias is low in our research.

## 6.1. Summary of final results

We first hypothesize that relative advantage has a significant and positive influence on the RFID assimilation process in China. However, the results indicate that the influence is not significant (H1 rejected). According to Seymour et al. [62], most organizations invest in a new technology with a short-term view to obtain a competitive advantage over competitors. However, the relative advantage might be offset by the high costs and implementation complexity of RFID technology and cannot be achieved in a short time. Indeed, some research on innovation adoption reported similar findings. According to Tornatzky and Klein’s [65] metaanalysis of innovation adoption, not all of the studies revealed that relative advantage can significantly influence innovation adoption. Another reason for this finding may be that RFID indeed plays important roles in the adoption stage, during which decision makers should make either purchasing or non-purchasing decisions based on the relative advantage of this technology; however, after adoption, relative advantage may have little influence on the adaptation and assimilation process.

In Hypothesis 2, we posit that complexity has a significant negative influence on RFID assimilation. However, the empirical result does not support our original hypothesis. This may be because technology complexity and implementation complexity are different concepts [62]. In Seymour et al.’s qualitative study of RFID adoption in South Africa, three organizations reported that the complexity of the technology implementation rather than the technology itself (configuring business rules) inhibits their RFID adoption. Thus, complexity that refers to the technology complexity in our research does not have a significant influence on the assimilation process.

Regarding Hypothesis 3, we hypothesized that IT infrastructure has a significant positive influence on RFID assimilation. The empirical results support this hypothesis. In other words, firms with mature and sophisticated IT infrastructures can assimilate RFID to a greater level than those with immature IT infrastructures. RFID can achieve its greatest benefits only through integrating with other backend systems such as enterprise resource planning (ERP), customer relationship management (CRM), and decision support systems (DSS) and sharing data and information with them [34]. Thus, physical IT assets play important roles in the integration and assimilation processes. Companies with sophisticated IT infrastructures have mature backend enterprise applications that can be integrated with RFID. Moreover, when assimilating RFID technology, companies might meet knowledge barriers and obstacles that internal IT staff must solve. Companies with mature IT infrastructure also have skilled IT staff that overcome the knowledge barriers during the implementation process. Therefore, IT infrastructure plays significant positive roles in the assimilation process of RFID technology.

In Hypothesis 4, we posit that managerial capability positively influences the RFID assimilation process in China. The results supported our hypothesis. Because RFID is a radical innovation that is resource intensive and requires substantial changes to existing work processes and organizational structure [67], organizations that have advanced managerial capabilities can overcome the managerial obstacles more successfully than those that do not have sufficient managerial capabilities.

Regarding Hypothesis 5, absorptive capacity is found to significantly influence RFID assimilation. Again, absorptive capacity refers to ‘‘a firm’s ability to recognize the value of new information, assimilate it and apply it to commercial ends’’ ([13], p. 128). It is regarded as a knowledge base, especially in terms of the extent of prior knowledge that the firm possesses [35]. Thus, firms that have strong absorptive capacity and prior experiences of using related knowledge have great capability to recognize the value of RFID technology and attempt to assimilate it and apply it to commercial ends.

In Hypothesis 6, we posit that competition intensity positively influences the RFID assimilation process, but this hypothesis is not supported by the empirical results. A possible explanation for this finding is that fierce competition drives firms to leap rapidly from one technology to another [1]. In such situations, it is difficult for firms to undergo a gradual, careful, and sustained learning-bydoing process to assimilate existing technologies [45].

In Hypothesis 7, we proposed that regulatory support has a positive influence on the RFID assimilation process of Chinese firms; however, the result was not statistically significant (H7 was not supported). As indicated by David [15], normalization and standardization procedures brought by regulations can reduce uncertainty and create network effects. These network effects can make companies aware of the new innovation and motivate them to adopt it. However, these companies’ enthusiasm will wane over time when they meet obstacles during the further assimilation process.

The different influences of regulatory support across stages can also be found in Zhu and Kraemer’s [74] investigation of e-business post-adoption. They indicate that the influence of supportive business laws is particularly important at early stages of ebusiness development compared to the later assimilation stage of e-business in an economy. Thus, regulatory support for RFID may be more important for adoption than for assimilation.

Regarding Hypothesis 8, we proposed that environmental uncertainty negatively influences RFID assimilation. The empirical results supported this hypothesis. As we mentioned in the previous chapter, there are two major uncertainties with RFID assimilation: irreversible investment costs and uncertain standards. Thus, if companies that have already entered into assimilation stages want to cancel or change their RFID implementation, they might undergo the risk of incurring irreversible costs. Moreover, companies that are at the assimilation stage might find it is difficult to share information and integrate with trading partners in the same industry because they use different RFID standards. These situations will undoubtedly inhibit their RFID assimilation process.

In conclusion, the empirical results indicate that innovation characteristics such as relative advantage and complexity do not play significant roles in the RFID assimilation process. All factors within the organizational context have a significant positive influence on RFID assimilation. Under the environmental category, environmental uncertainty has a significant negative influence on China’s RFID assimilation.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions and practical implications

While RFID has been adopted by an increasing number of Chinese companies in recent years, many of them have failed to achieve deep usage beyond initial adoption [9]. However, innovation can only generate significant business value when it is integrated into the corporate value chain [16]. Moreover, most IS innovation research focuses only on the one-shot adoption decisions, and little is known about the post-adoption stage. As indicated by Fichman [19] and Zhu et al. [77], a focused study on the post-adoption stages of assimilation is especially warranted. Thus, this research contributes to the post-adoption research, which has been much less prevalent than the one-shot adoption research.

Second, different cultural contexts and environmental settings may generate different results. We conducted this research in a new context, i.e., Chinese manufacturing industries. The results indicate the same antecedents can have reverse influences on RFID assimilation. For example, as indicated by our research, environmental uncertainty plays a significant negative role in Chinese companies’ RFID assimilation because of these companies’ riskaverse characteristics. This finding is consistent with Chau et al.’s [10] research on Internet technologies assimilation in China. They revealed that ‘‘Chinese firms are risk averse and are less likely to be bellwethers for Internet technology’’ [10]. However, this research finding is also contrary to the popular argument that in Western countries, environmental uncertainty, rather than maturity, positively influences a firm’s proactive and innovative strategies and behaviors. Because the Chinese government has maintained powerful political influence and control in the transition process, the regulatory environment has also become one of these companies’ greatest concerns. However, previous research findings based on developed economies cannot empirically support developing countries’ assimilation practices. Thus, it is necessary for us to investigate RFID assimilation in transitional economies such as China’s economy.

Third, this research investigates one significant determinant of RFID assimilation (absorptive capacity), which has seldom been investigated in the RFID adoption/assimilation literature. Based on existing literature, absorptive capacity is an important construct in the investigation of innovation adoption and assimilation, especially disruptive technologies such as RFID technology.

From an empirical implications perspective, managers can promote their RFID assimilation level by improving their managerial capability and the organization’s IT infrastructure and absorptive capacity. As indicated by the CEO of LEO Company in Hong Kong, the biggest challenge faced by RFID implementation is business process reengineering. This emphasizes the need for improved IT managerial skills for the efficient usage and assimilation of innovations. These types of skills can be obtained from RFID vendors whose interests in expanding markets are served by such transfer in China and other developing countries [36]. Firms can also obtain these skills by working with foreign multinational corporations that can benefit from transferring such skills either as a means of extending their markets or upgrading the capabilities of local suppliers in developing countries [8].

As illustrated, IT infrastructure contains three components: (1) physical assets, such as wireless network, enterprise applications including ERP (enterprise resources planning), MRP (material resource planning), DSS (decision support systems), and so on; (2) human IT resources, including technical and management skills; (3) IT-enabled intangibles, such as customer orientation, knowledge asset and synergy.

Thus, managers can improve their IT infrastructure by improving related enterprise applications and training their IT staffs on both RFID-related technical skills and management skills. They should also adopt a customer-oriented business strategy and attempt to embed the knowledge, skills and experiences of the employees into its business processes, policies and information repositories to better assimilate RFID technology. Moreover, Leung et al. [37] evaluate the use of IT by third party logistics companies in South East Asia (including Singapore and Malaysia) and indicate that the barriers of their IT adoption are a lack of software integration and logistics professionals. Accordingly, IT infrastructure can also be improved by integrating various enterprises applications and training IT staff members and equipping them with strong technical and management skills.

As indicated by Fu [25], a lack of absorptive capacity and complementary assets results in a bottleneck that inhibits innovation assimilation. This study provides important implications for firms in developing countries that are carrying out knowledge transfer. According to Lin et al. [40], developing countries lack human resources and financial resources to become R&D centers; thus, technology transfer is a key issue for these countries. To effectively transfer external technology, firms should improve their interaction mechanism, including both intra-and interorganizational interactions, to promote learning from the outside environment. Communications between employees from different divisions and backgrounds should be encouraged to improve the intra-organizational interactions. Moreover, the management of R&D activities and the construction of information collection mechanisms inside and outside of organizations are also essential.

Regarding the uncertain standards, EPCglobal only specifies an RFID standard at layer one and layer two of OSI model and leave the top layers to be defined by industries [2]. Thus, developing countries may face difficulties in harmonizing their standards at application and top layers. To address this problem, the government can make a legislation to confirm the top layer RFID standards to harmonize it with that of other countries.

At the company level, managers should also predict the uncertainty caused by the high irreversible investment costs and unconfirmed standards during the assimilation process. They should evaluate their organization’s capabilities to accept sunk costs due to the failure of an RFID project and make rationale adoption decisions. Managers should also have knowledge of the RFID standards that their trading partners’ use of these standards and attempt to use the same standards to avoid standard conflict during information and data sharing processes. This is the only method through which they can share information and data smoothly with their trading partners and achieve a high level of RFID technology assimilation.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research opportunities

Because RFID technology remains at its infancy and is not widely used in China, we were not able to achieve a large sample size. In total, we had 102 data points for the assimilation. Therefore, it is possible that some of the nonsignificant paths were caused by the small sample size rather than incorrect hypotheses. As more Chinese companies begin to use RFID technology and assimilate it into their organizations, future research involving larger sample sizes can be conducted.

When collecting data, we used a single respondent from each target company without collecting more responses from other informants in the same organization. According to Ranganathan et al. [57], random measurement error can be increased by relying on one respondent to make complicated social judgments about organizational characteristics. However, due to the costs and possibility of lower response rate, we did not collect data from multiple respondents from one organization. Future research can mitigate this problem by collecting data from multiple respondents per company to reduce the measurement errors. Further research could collect longitudinal data from multiple respondents to investigate the causal relationships between predictive vari ables and dependent variables.

Moreover, because RFID is an IOS (interorganizational information systems) innovation that requires cross-organizational cooperation to share information and business data, interorganizational factors become more important when RFID is more deeply assimilated. Importantly, our study did not generally involve organizations that had deeply assimilated RFID. Such assimilation should be the ultimate goal in automating any supply chain with RFID. Future research should thus study the influence of interorganizational factors such as supply chain integration and collaboration on the IOS assimilation processes.

Finally, we did not include top management support in our model. Other research has shown that this variable influences technology assimilation. Hence, the next version of this model and research should further consider this construct. Importantly, other related organizational context variables may also be important, such as organizational commitment or extra-role behaviors performed by IT employees (e.g., [31]), which could have significant influences on how well RFID assimilation occurs. Key differences in assimilation approaches may also be observed in different industries and national cultures and should be considered because key differences in culture have been exhibited in multiple IT use and deployment contexts (e.g., [73,43,53,44]).
