# Analytical model of adoption of item level RFID in a two-echelon supply chain with shelf-space and price-dependent demand

Joseph G. Szmerekovsky <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Vera Tilson <sup>b</sup>, Jiang Zhang <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> North Dakota State University, United States

<sup>b</sup> University of Rochester, United States

<sup>c</sup> School of Business, Adelphi University, United State

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 4 March 2011

Keywords: Item level RFID Supply chain contracts Shelf-space management

## a b s t r a c t

We investigate whether it is possible for the manufacturer as well as the retailer to derive economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from item-level RFID. We consider two supply chain contracts. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst instance the manufacturer sets the wholesale price for his product and in response the retailer sets the retail price and allocates the shelf-space. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that in this setting item-level RFID is adopted in a decentralized supply chain at the same levels of RFID costs as in an integrated supply chain with a single decision maker. The second case that we consider involves a powerful retailer setting product shelf-space prices. In this setting the manufacturer essentially rents shelf-space from the retailer. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that in this setting, the retailer may force the adoption of RFID even if it does not maximize the chain's total pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

## 1. Introduction

Studies of retail consumer behavior (e.g. [12,6]) have found that the majority of consumers do not make a substitute purchase when they do not <sup>fi</sup>nd an item for which they were looking. Further, stockouts occur with a surprising degree of frequency at retail stores [12]. Thus retail stock-outs lead to a loss of revenue and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient management of shelf-space has the potential to provide retailers with a valuable edge in their highly competitive industry. A number of retailers are implementing technological solutions such as item-level RFID (radio frequency identi<sup>fi</sup>cation) tags combined with smart shelves to reduce the incidence of stock-outs. A smart shelf is a retail shelf equipped with an antenna designed to detect the RFID tags af<sup>fi</sup>xed directly to individual items. Data gathered at the smart shelf is sent to an information system programmed to alert store employees or manufacturers when the inventory of a product falls below a predetermined level. Technical trials carried-out by major retailers like Wal-Mart have demonstrated that item level RFID and smart shelves can reduce the incidence of stock-outs by up to 30% [11].

A growing industry interest in RFID has been re<sup>fl</sup>ected in increased academic research. Ngai et al. [16] reviewed 85 RFID related papers that were published in academic journals between 1995 and 2005 with an attempt to stimulate more interest in the RFID research. Many journals such as Production and Operations Management and International Journal of Production Economics have had special issues on RFID. Despite this, while reviewing the literature on RFID Lee and Özer [14] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a credibility gap with regards to the value of RFID technology. The claimed bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of item-level RFID include lower labor costs, simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed business processes, and more accurate inventory tracking in addition to the reduced incidence of stockouts [1,5,13]. However, models which show how the additional information and visibility provided by RFID translate to these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are still needed. While the physical tagging of <sup>fi</sup>nished products with RFID tags is usually done by upstream manufacturers, many use a simplistic view of supply chain relationships to suggest that most of the economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts will accrue to the retailer [19]. Gaukler et al. [10] argued that to improve supply chain ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and stay competitive, manufacturers will “require some kind of contractual incentive to incur the tag cost, and downstream supply chain partners will need to share in the cost of the tag.” Of course this con<sup>fl</sup>ict does not occur when the manufacturer and retailer are both part of the same vertically integrated <sup>fi</sup>rm. In this context the decision to adopt item-level RFID can be based simply on a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis across the integrated supply chain. Where the retailer and manufacturer are not parts of the same <sup>fi</sup>rm, a decentralized supply chain, the adoption decision is complicated by each party having their own pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximizing objectives. Szmerekovsky and Zhang [19] show that careful analysis of supply chain dynamics is required to determine the overall effects of RFID adoption. In this paper, we investigate how economic decisions about the adoption of item-level RFID are made in both decentralized and integrated supply chains.

The model presented here weighs the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of being able to satisfy more of the customer demand against the additional costs of RFID tagging and the comparative costs of monitoring retail shelves with and without the technology. We investigate the roles price and shelf-space elasticity play in the adoption decision and <sup>fi</sup>nd that RFID technology is more likely to be adopted for products with less elastic demand. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence on RFID adoption of two different types of contractual arrangement between a manufacturer and a retailer is explored in two decentralized supply chain contexts. In one setting with a wholesale price contract, where the manufacturer is able to adjust the wholesale price to re<sup>fl</sup>ect tagging costs, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that decisions with regard to RFID adoption are the same as in an integrated supply chain. In a setting where retail space is scarce an alternative contractual form essentially entails charging the manufacturer for shelf-space. Cairns [4] pointed out that the retailer's business can be thought of as “selling to manufacturers the space at his disposal,” and some retailers have stated that they charge manufacturers for shelf-space [18]. In this version of the contractual arrangement we consider a <sup>fi</sup>xed wholesale price but permit the retailer to charge a different price for shelf-space for an RFID-enabled product. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that in such a case the conditions for RFID adoption are not the same for members in a decentralized supply chain and an integrated supply chain. Powerful retailers are inclined to pressure manufacturers to adopt item-level RFID before the costs of RFID implementation reach values that will be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the supply chain as a whole.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 explains our model. The analysis is presented in Section 3, managerial implications are discussed in Section 4, and limitations of the research are discussed in Section 5. Section 6 concludes with a summary of results and outlines future research possibilities.

## 2. Model

In this section we present our model for analysis of RFID adoption. In Section 2.1 we model the impact of RFID on costs and demand. In Section 2.2 we model the dependence of demand on retail price and the quantity of shelf-space allocated to a product. Finally, in Section 2.3 we model the decision-making dynamics in the supply chain.

## 2.1. Model of the impact of RFID technology

Introduction of item level RFID technology in a supply chain affects manufacturing costs, shelf management costs, and ability to satisfy customer demand. The two main bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of RFID which have appeared in the supply chain literature are its ability to reduce inventory inaccuracy and its ability to reduce demand distortion. Inventory inaccuracy occurs when the actual inventory available to meet demand does not match the recorded inventory in the information system. One major cause of inventory inaccuracy is inventory shrinkage, that is, the permanent loss of inventory due to theft, misplacement, obsolescence, damage, or spoilage. Demand distortion occurs when, due to poor inventory handling, inventory which is physically available in a store is not made available on the shelf for customers. Bai et al. [2] review the RFID literature on inventory inaccuracy and demand distortion and demonstrate the importance of including both effects in models of RFID adoption. Consistent with their result we model RFID's effect on both inventory inaccuracy and demand distortion. As a benchmark, we <sup>fi</sup>rst consider an integrated supply chain where a single decision-maker makes the decision about technology adoption for a single product, with the goal of maximizing the supply chain's overall pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

In our model, revenue is calculated as the product of retail price, $P ,$ and satis<sup>fi</sup>ed demand, . Satis<sup>fi</sup>ed demand is a function of the uncensored consumer demand $D ,$ the number of goods produced $Q _ { ☉ }$ and of inventory management ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. The revenue and costs of the system will depend on the level of technology adopted which can be either low technology without the use of item-level RFID or high technology that employs item-level RFID. We let $T \in \{ L , H \}$ represent the inventory management technology where L stands for low technology without the use of item-level RFID and H stands for high technology that employs item-level RFID. We assume that production cost, $C ( Q , T )$ , depends on production quantity and that the cost of managing shelf-space, $\mathcal { H } ( S , T )$ , depends on shelf-space quantity S. In an integrated supply chain we write the pro<sup>fi</sup>t as the function

$$
P \cdot \mathcal {D} (D, Q, T) - C (Q, T) - \mathcal {H} (S, T).\tag{1}
$$

We assume that the decision maker maximizes the pro<sup>fi</sup>t by selecting the retail price, the amount of product to produce, the amount of shelf-space allocated to the product, and the technology used for inventory management.

We further model the effect of technology on demand satisfaction, variable manufacturing costs, and shelf-space management costs with four parameters $\theta _ { T } , \nu _ { T } , \rho _ { T }$ and $\eta _ { T } . \Lambda s$ in Corsten and Gruen [6] we use parameter $\theta _ { T } 0 \le \theta _ { T } \le 1$ , to model the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the replenishment process with respect to satisfying consumer demand. Assuming that the retailer is not constrained by the production quantity, but only by his inventory management ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, the retailer loses $( 1 - \theta _ { T } ) D$ of the uncensored demand, due to his inability to have the product on the shelf when a customer is in the store. Hence, the retailer is only able to satisfy a fraction $\theta _ { T }$ of the uncensored demand

$$
\mathcal {D} = \theta_ {T} D.\tag{2}
$$

Gaukler et al. [10] indicated that without RFID they “expect realistic values for $\theta _ { L }$ to be around 0.9.” We de<sup>fi</sup>ne the ratio $\theta = \theta _ { L } / \theta _ { H } .$ When RFID technology is adopted we assume that the full demand D will be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed $( \theta _ { H } = 1 )$ . Note, that in practice this may not be the case, however for ease of notation we consider the uncensored demand to be scaled to that which can be achieved with RFID adoption. Given this scaling assumption we have $\theta _ { L } = \theta .$

Whether or not RFID technology is adopted will also affect manufacturing operations (e.g. tag costs). Hence, we introduce parameter $\rho _ { T }$ to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the corresponding change in manufacturing costs. Hence, production costs in an integrated supply chain are

$$
C (Q, T) = \rho_ {T} c Q\tag{3}
$$

We introduce ratio $\rho { = } \rho _ { H } / \rho _ { L }$ and without RFID we normalize the cost c so that $\rho _ { L } = 1$ , therefore $\rho _ { H } = \rho _ { \mathrm { \Gamma } }$

We also assume that the inventory is subject to shrinkage due to damage, theft, or other incidents. Essentially for units of merchandise to be available to sell to customers $v _ { T } \mathcal { D }$ units need to be produced. To simplify notation later, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the ratio: $v = v _ { H } / v _ { L } .$ With RFID we expect a reduction in shrinkage so that $ { \boldsymbol { v } } _ { H } \leq  { \boldsymbol { v } } _ { L }$ and therefore 1.

Finally we let $\eta _ { T } \mathcal { k }$ be the per unit cost of shelf-space management where the factor η accounts for the change in cost due to the level of technology employed. Again to simplify notation later we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the ratio $\eta = \eta _ { H } / \eta _ { L }$ . When RFID is not used we normalize the value of parameter h so that $\eta _ { L } = 1$ , and therefore $\eta _ { H } = \eta .$ . In general, we might expect that RFID adoption should increase the costs associated with shelf-space management due to the cost of purchasing, installing, operating and maintaining readers. However, RFID technology can also help to automate the shelf-space monitoring and replenishment processes. This automation effect could result in actual cost reductions to shelf-space management costs. Thus we make no assumption as to the value of η and write the cost of shelf-space maintenance as

$$
\mathcal {H} (S, T) = \eta_ {T} \hbar S.\tag{4}
$$

Note that in this formulation we assume that any <sup>fi</sup>xed costs associated with the installation of the equipment necessary to af<sup>fi</sup>x RFID tags in the production process or to monitor the shelves can be represented as variable costs incurred in manufacturing quantity Q and in monitoring shelves over the period of time that results in demand . That is, we amortize the <sup>fi</sup>xed costs over time as an additional per unit cost which is included in $\eta _ { H } .$ We re-write Eq. (1) as

$$
\Pi (T) = (P - v _ {T} \rho_ {T} c) \theta_ {T} D - \eta_ {T} h S.\tag{5}
$$

Our analytical model can be used to evaluate other out-of-stock inventory management technologies. For example, a recent patent entitled “Detection Of Stock-out Conditions Based On Image Processing” proposes monitoring out-of-stocks using inexpensive cameras and image processing software. Ignoring amortization of <sup>fi</sup>xed costs, such technology would imply $\rho _ { T } = 1$ , since no additional costs would be incurred in the manufacturing process, and $\nu _ { T } = 1$ since the technology is not likely to reduce shrinkage. Similar to item-level RFID, the image processing based technology is likely to reduce the costs of periodic monitoring of shelves. It would no longer be necessary to frequently send a person to check the inventory on the shelves.

## 2.2. Demand model and shelf-space management cost model

Next we assume that the retailer can control the uncensored demand D by setting the product's price and allocating shelf-space for product display. In assuming that the demand is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the shelf-space we recognize the fact that consumers can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by exposure to the product on the shelf in a way similar to how they are by other advertisement. We assume that demand is decreasing in price and increasing in shelf-space allocation:

$$
\frac {\partial D (P , S)}{\partial P} <   0, \frac {\partial D (P , S)}{\partial S} > 0\tag{6}
$$

The rate at which demand grows with a decrease in price, and an increase in shelf-space is characterized by the product's price and shelf-space demand elasticity, designated with e and γ respectively. The larger the product's demand elasticity, the larger the change in demand in response to a change in price or shelf-space allocation:

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} D (P , S ; e , \gamma)}{\partial P \partial e} <   0, \frac {\partial^ {2} D (P , S ; e , \gamma)}{\partial S \partial \gamma} > 0\tag{7}
$$

To obtain further analytical results we assume a particular tractable form of demand. We assume that all else being equal, an increase (or reduction) of x% in shelf-space allocation causes a constant increase (reduction) of γx% in demand for the product, and an increase (or reduction) in retail price by x% leads to a drop (or increase) in demand by ex%. Thus our demand model implies constant shelf-space elasticity as in Corstjens and Doyle [7] and Martinez-de-Albeniz and Roels [15] and constant price elasticity. We formulate demand D per unit time as

$$
D (P, S) = \alpha S ^ {\gamma} P ^ {- e}\tag{8}
$$

The values 0bγb1 and eN1 are appropriate elasticities, and α is a positive constant indicating the scale of demand. Practical estimates of shelf-space elasticity have been reported to be as high as 0.212 ([7,8]) and practical estimates of price elasticity have been reported as high as 2.8 (see [17] p. 113; [9] p. 101). Retail products which are likely to experience our assumptions of non-substitutability and shelf-space dependent demand include novelty items, items which are impulse buys and new product introductions. Novelty items and items purchased as impulse buys are not likely to experience any demand if they are not present and visible in the store. Hence, demand is dependent on the amount of shelf-space available and stock-outs result in no demand and hence no substitution. New product introductions are particularly dependent on shelf-space when they are introduced by a small <sup>fi</sup>rm or are otherwise limited in advertising budget. When the <sup>fi</sup>rm does not have the budget to advertise the product through media, purchases in the store will be highly dependent on the size of the display.

## 2.3. Supply chain model

We are interested in investigating how decentralization of the supply chain affects the decisions about RFID adoption. Extensive operation management and economics literature is devoted to the discussion of contracts in supply chain, and to what degree various contract types coordinate decentralized supply chains [3]. A coordinating contract means that the pro<sup>fi</sup>t in a supply chain with multiple decision makers is equal to the chain pro<sup>fi</sup>t in an integrated supply chain with a single decision maker. One of the simplest contracts employed in supply chains is a wholesale price contract where the manufacturer charges the retailer a linear tariff. It is well established that a wholesale price contract does not maximize the chain's total pro<sup>fi</sup>t due to double marginalization. However, wholesale price contracts are frequently used as they are easy to administer.

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst supply-chain model assumes that the manufacturer has pricing power. The manufacturer sets a linear wholesale price as a function of the technology required by the retailer and in response the retailer sets the retail price, allocates shelf-space, and orders the quantity of merchandise that maximizes manufacturer pro<sup>fi</sup>t. We assume that the manufacturer has full knowledge of the retailer demand function, and of parameters $\theta _ { T } , \ v _ { T }$ and $\eta _ { T } .$ In this case the retailer pro<sup>fi</sup>t is given by

$$
\pi_ {R} = \max _ {P, S, T} (P - v _ {T} W (T)) \theta_ {T} D (P, S) - \eta_ {T} h S\tag{9}
$$

while the manufacturer pro<sup>fi</sup>t is

$$
\pi_ {M} = m a x _ {W (T)} \left(W \left(T _ {R} ^ {*}\right) - c \rho_ {T _ {R} ^ {*}}\right) \cdot v _ {T _ {R} ^ {*}} \cdot \theta_ {T _ {R} ^ {*}} D \left(P _ {R} ^ {*}, S _ {R} ^ {*}\right)\tag{10}
$$

where {P \*,S \*, T \*} maximizes retailer pro<sup>fi</sup>t in Eq. (9). Since the manufacturer moves <sup>fi</sup>rst, he sets the wholesale pricing schedule W(T) so that his pro<sup>fi</sup>t in Eq. (10) is maximized. The manufacturer cannot set the price so high that the retailer will experience a <sup>fi</sup>nancial loss, nor so low that the manufacturer makes a negative pro<sup>fi</sup>t, so the two additional constraints are

$$
\pi_ {R} \geq 0, \pi_ {M} \geq 0.\tag{11}
$$

In a setting where the wholesale market is very competitive, the manufacturer has little ability to set retail price. In fact, some retail outlets charge manufacturers to display their merchandise [18]. We model this setting by assuming that the manufacturer is a price-taker. The manufacturer sells the product at the market wholesale price, and the retailer is able to charge the manufacturer for the shelf-space. The retailer charges a linear tariff and the manufacturer decides how much shelf-space to rent. We model the interaction between the manufacturer and the retailer as a leader–follower game, where the retailer offers the manufacturer two prices for shelf-space: one if the manufacturer's product is managed using item-level RFID and the other if it is not managed using item-level RFID. The manufacturer then selects both the preferred technology and the amount of shelfspace to rent. It follows that the manufacturer pro<sup>fi</sup>t as a function of the manufacturer's decision variables is

$$
\varphi_ {M} = \max _ {S, T} (W - c \rho_ {T}) v _ {T} \theta_ {T} D (P (T), S) - K (T) S\tag{12}
$$

where W is the wholesale price. The retailer pro<sup>fi</sup>t is the function of the shelf-space price schedule

$$
\varphi_ {R} = \max _ {P (T), K (T)} \left(P (T) - v _ {T _ {M} ^ {*}} W\right) \theta_ {T _ {M} ^ {*}} D \left(P (T), S _ {M} ^ {*}\right) + K \left(T _ {M} ^ {*}\right) S _ {M} ^ {*} - \eta_ {T _ {M} ^ {*}} h S _ {M} ^ {*} (1 3)
$$

where {S\* ,T \*} maximizes manufacturer pro<sup>fi</sup>t in Eq. (12). Since the retailer moves <sup>fi</sup>rst, the retailer is able to set the shelf-space and retail pricing schedule, $K ( T )$ and $P ( T )$ , so that his pro<sup>fi</sup>t in Eq. (13) is maximized. Here too we make the assumption of complete information. The manufacturer knows the retailer demand function, and the value of parameters $\theta _ { T }$ and $\nu _ { T } ,$ the retailer, on the other hand, knows the manufacturer's per item cost, $c \rho _ { T } .$ . Participation constraints imply that the retailer would not set the shelf price so low that he loses money, nor so high that the manufacturer's pro<sup>fi</sup>t is negative, therefore:

$$

## 3.1. Conditions for adoption of RFID in an integrated supply chain

As the cost of operating shelves with RFID technology decreases, it becomes pro<sup>fi</sup>table to install RFID technology for higher tagging costs and vice versa. From Fig. 1 we see that when RFID adoption is optimal with increased shelf monitoring costs (ηN1) and manufacturing costs $( \rho v > 1 )$ , then the lower the price and shelf-space elasticities the larger the range of RFID associated costs for which RFID adoption is pro<sup>fi</sup>table. If RFID adoption means decreased shelf monitoring costs $( \eta < 1 )$ , then the higher the shelf-space elasticity the higher the range of tagging costs where the adoption occurs. This occurs since with high shelf-space elasticity and low shelf monitoring costs, the retailer can increase demand for the product by allocating more shelf-space. So if shelf-space operation is expensive, the retailer will trade-off shelf-space allocation and retail price to keep the level of demand consistent.

3.2. Conditions for RFID adoption in a supply chain where the manufacturer has market power and sets the wholesale price

## 4. Managerial implications

In this section we discuss the managerial implications of the mathematical results from Section 3. From Section 3 the following results can be observed:

1. Without wholesale pricing <sup>fl</sup>exibility the retailer in a decentralized supply chain may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from RFID adoption when an integrated supply chain would not (Proposition 3).

2. With pricing <sup>fl</sup>exibility the conditions for RFID adoption to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the retailer in a decentralized supply chain, the manufacturer in a decentralized supply chain, and the integrated supply chain are all the same. Further, RFID adoption does not affect the relative distribution of pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in the decentralized supply chain (Proposition 2).

3. The shelf-space elasticity (γ) has a large impact on what cost of shelf-space (η) will result in RFID adoption but little impact on what per item manufacturing cost (ρυ) will result in RFID adoption (Fig. 1).

4. The price elasticity (e) has little impact on what cost of shelf-space (η) will result in RFID adoption but a large impact on what per item manufacturing cost (ρυ) will result in RFID adoption (Fig. 1).

5. When the increase in the cost of shelf-space is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant $( \eta = 1 )$ even large increases in the per unit manufacturing cost $( \rho v \ge 1 . 0 5 )$ can result in RFID adoption increasing supply chain pro<sup>fi</sup>ts (Fig. 1).

Results 1 and 2 address the issue of how RFID adoption can have different impacts on supply chain members and supply chains overall. Gaukler et al.'s [10] argument that the manufacturer will need to be incentivized by the retailer to incur the additional costs of RFID tagging is partly consistent with what is seen in Result 1 where products are sold through large powerful retailers with high demand for their shelf-space but where prices are not <sup>fl</sup>exible. It is consistent in that a retailer in a decentralized supply chain will pro<sup>fi</sup>t from RFID adoption at higher costs of adoption than a manufacturer in a decentralized supply chain and, hence, a retailer may need to incentive adoption for a manufacturer. However, it is inconsistent in that for our model it is not the case that a retailer pressuring a manufacturer to adopt RFID will bene<sup>fi</sup>t the supply chain as a whole. Instead a retailer may force adoption of RFID when it bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the retailer but not the supply chain as a whole. This means that large powerful retailers are inclined to pressure manufacturers to adopt item-level RFID before the costs of RFID reach values that will be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the supply chain as a whole. A possible example of this are the mandates by retailers like Wal-Mart that manufacturers adopt RFID. Our results suggest that such mandates requiring early adoption of technology may only improve performance for retailers but not supply chains as a whole.

In contrast Result 2 suggests that mandates and incentives for RFID adoption will be neither necessary nor bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the supply chain when prices are <sup>fl</sup>exible and the manufacturer is the dominant player in the supply chain. Indeed, Proposition 2 indicates that not only will the manufacturer voluntarily adopt RFID technology when it is optimal for the retailer and the supply chain as a whole, but adoption of the technology will not affect the relative distribution of pro<sup>fi</sup>ts within the supply chain. Hence, not even mechanisms to rebalance supply chain pro<sup>fi</sup>ts will be necessary. Though the proliferation of large discount retailers has reduced the commonality of price <sup>fl</sup>exibility and manufacturer dominated chains, when these conditions hold there is hope that manufacturer initiatives to adopt RFID technology will experience limited resistance from retailers and result in bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for the supply chain as a whole. What should be focused on instead are mechanisms for reducing the double marginalization which reduces the overall ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of these supply chains.

Considering that different price elasticities (e) will result in different markups in the model without price <sup>fl</sup>exibility, Results 3, 4 and 5 can be seen in Fig. 1. Note that as discussed in Section 2 Fig. 1 represents a typical range of parameter values one might expect to encounter in practice. When shelf-space elasticity is high (γ=0.2) the per unit manufacturing cost of RFID (ρυ) for which RFID adoption remains pro<sup>fi</sup>table decreases rapidly as the shelf-space cost of RFID (η) increases; but, when shelf-space elasticity is low (γ =0.1) the per unit manufacturing cost of RFID (ρυ) decreases slowly. A reverse relationship can be seen between price elasticity (e) and the two costs of RFID. These relationships are not surprising as shelf-space elasticity represents the marginal bene<sup>fi</sup>t of shelf-space and price elasticity represents the marginal bene<sup>fi</sup>t of costs. What is surprising is Result 5 which indicates that unless RFID adoption increases the cost of shelfspace then signi<sup>fi</sup>cant increases to manufacturing costs (5% or more) should not be a barrier to pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of RFID adoption when demand is shelf-space dependent. This numerical result brings into question claims that for RFID adoption to be economically feasible at the item level the cost of RFID tags and tagging must be negligible (b1%) compared to the manufacturing cost of the individual items. Indeed, our results suggest that even for large values of shelf-space elasticity (γ =0.2) such low per unit costs are only necessary if the cost of shelfspace increases by at least 60%. This suggests that a thorough analysis of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of RFID adoption must include both an analysis of the cost of shelf-space and the dependence of demand on the amount of shelf-space allocated to the product. To our knowledge we are the <sup>fi</sup>rst to explicitly consider the increased cost of shelf-space under RFID adoption with shelf-space dependent demand.

## 5. Limitations

In this paper we presented an analytical mathematical model of pro<sup>fi</sup>tability in a supply chain with RFID technology. As with any analytical model we sought to strike a balance between accuracy, generality, and tractability. To achieve this balance we chose to use a demand model that is deterministic and of a particular form and assumed costs to be linear. Therefore, our results may not hold for other models of demand or when costs of RFID implementation cannot be accurately represented as linear.

## 6. Summary and future research

Using a parsimonious mathematical model, we obtain two main results. First, if the manufacturer is able to pass on the cost of RFID tagging to the retailer through a higher wholesale price, then the interests of both retailer and manufacturer in terms of pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of RFID tags are aligned. Second, when there is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant competition for shelf-space and the manufacturer is unable to pass some of the costs of RFID tagging onto the retailer, then it is possible that while the retailer would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from RFID tags, the manufacturer will not, making additional incentives necessary for adoption.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst result should occur in situations where manufacturers have strong in<sup>fl</sup>uential positions in the supply chain and can freely adjust their wholesale prices with little interference from retailers. The second result occurs when products are sold through large powerful retailers with high demand for their shelf-space. An obvious example of this are the mandates by retailers like Wal-Mart that manufacturers adopt RFID.

In summary, our results suggest that how a supply chain member views RFID technology can differ depending on that member's power in the supply chain. Manufacturers whose supply chains are dominated by powerful retailers may <sup>fi</sup>nd that RFID is just another cost of doing business. However, powerful manufacturers who dominate their supply chains should consider RFID adoption as a bene<sup>fi</sup>t to themselves and their supply chain as a whole. Our results also show that RFID adoption may be pro<sup>fi</sup>table at unexpectedly high per unit tagging costs; but that it is vital to consider the additional cost of shelf-space associated with RFID adoption if the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of RFID adoption is to be accurately assessed.
