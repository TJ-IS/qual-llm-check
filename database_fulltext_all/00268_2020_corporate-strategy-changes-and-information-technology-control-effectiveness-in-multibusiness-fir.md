# CORPORATE STRATEGY CHANGES AND INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY CONTROL EFFECTIVENESS IN MULTIBUSINESS FIRMS<sup>1</sup>

Hüseyin Tanriverdi

Red McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin, 1 University Station, B6500, CBA 5.208, Austin, TX 78712 U.S.A. {Huseyin.Tanriverdi@mccombs.utexas.edu}

Kui Du

College of Management, University of Massachusetts Boston, 100 Morrissey Boulevard, Boston, MA 02125 U.S.A. {Kui.Du@umb.edu}

We develop a theory to explain why, how, and under what conditions corporate strategy changes negatively affect a multibusiness firm’s ability to design and effectively operate IT controls, and lead to the emergence of IT control material weaknesses (IT MW). Corporate strategy changes such as diversification, mergers and acquisitions (M&A), and divestitures alter a firm’s complicatedness by adding or removing business units. These changes can also affect the firm’s complexity by altering the degree of interrelatedness among the firm’s businesses. We hypothesize that corporate strategy changes that affect the firm’s complexity are more likely to increase IT MW than those that only affect the firm’s complicatedness. Complexity-altering corporate strategy changes are likely to disrupt all three types of IT controls: IT controls over technology, IT controls over business processes, and IT controls over people’s behaviors. We hypothesize that the changes are likely to disrupt the IT controls over people much more than IT controls over technology or business processes. Complexity-altering corporate strategy changes are also likely to affect the design effectiveness of the IT controls much more than the operating effectiveness of the IT controls. We find support for these ideas in a longitudinal study of 2,477 publicly traded U.S. firms. Results also indicate that the internal control material weaknesses (IC MW) that emerge following corporate strategy changes are primarily due to IT MW rather than non-IT MW. The proposed theory and the findings have important implications for research and practice.

Keywords: IT control effectiveness, corporate strategy, diversification, mergers and acquisitions, divestitures, complexity, complicatedness, Sarbanes–Oxley Act (SOX)

## Introduction

Firms design and operate IT controls to minimize their ITrelated risks and maximize their IT-enabled returns (Tanriverdi and Ruefli 2004). The failure to design adequate IT controls, or to effectively operate the designed IT controls, leads to IT control material weaknesses (IT MW) and inhibits firms’ ability to achieve their IT and business objectives. Relative to firms whose IT controls are effective, firms with IT MW: (1) experience more IT security incidents (Pang and Tanriverdi 2017); (2) report lower market valuations and lower accounting earnings (Stoel and Muhanna 2011); (3) fail to make accurate management forecasts (Li et al. 2012); (4) have less ability to pay their debts (Kuhn et al. 2013); (5) misstate their financials more (Klamm and Watson 2009); and (6) become more likely to terminate the employment of their CEOs and CFOs (Masli et al. 2016). Although the negative consequences of IT MW are well understood, there is a dearth of research on the antecedents of IT MW. We address this research gap.

Only two studies to date examined the potential antecedents of IT MW, as summarized in the last two rows of Table 1. Li, Lim, and Wang (2007) focused on governance and found that the lower levels of board independence and the lack of IT expertise on top management team increased the likelihood of IT MW. The authors controlled for firms’ diversification levels, but they did not find a significant effect. Haislip, Peters, and Richardson (2016) focused on the remediation of IT MW and found that switching to an external auditor who had more IT expertise helped improve the effectiveness of IT controls. These authors also controlled whether firms had M&A activity or not, but they did not find a significant effect. Studies on firms’ overall internal control material weaknesses (IC MW) also included controls for diversification and M&A. However, their results were mixed. As summarized in Table 1, some found a positive effect, some found a negative effect, and others did not find any effects. These studies alert us that corporate strategy changes could be important antecedents of IT MW. However, the lack of theorization of the links between corporate strategy changes and IT MW or IC MW; the relegation of corporate strategy variables to control variables; and the mixed findings on those control variables motivate us to theorize the link between corporate strategy and IT MW.

Strategy affects the control environment of a firm by deciding on the firm’s strategic objectives, organizational structures, process characteristics, culture, and employee traits (Cram et al. 2016a). Strategy scholars argue that a firm’s management control systems must align with the firm’s strategy (Langfield-Smith 2007; Martin 2017). Accounting scholars similarly argue that the guidance for the design and auditing of a firm’s accounting control systems should come from the firm’s strategy (Bentley-Goode et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2017). Information systems scholars have not yet focused on the relationship between strategy and IT controls (Cram et al. 2016b. However, their long quest to align business and IT strategies also implies that a firm’s IT controls ought to align with the control environment determined by the firm’s strategy.

Accounting studies recently began to theorize and test the link between business-unit level strategy and IC MW (Bentley-Goode et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2017). We seek to complement them by theorizing the link between corporate-level strategy and IT MW. Business-unit level strategy focuses on how a firm’s individual business units compete with rivals within their respective industry positions (Langfield-Smith 2007). Corporate-level strategy focuses on the firm’s choice of industry positions in which to operate its businesses, and how to structure the resource-sharing relationships of the businesses to maximize the corporate performance of the firm (Eisenhardt and Piezunka 2011).

We theoretically ground our hypotheses in complexity science. Multibusiness firms are complex adaptive systems that consist of multiple business units that can potentially interact with each other to exchange physical and informational resources to create synergies (Cilliers 1998; Eisenhardt and Piezunka 2011). Corporate strategy changes, such as diversification, M&A, and divestitures, can alter a multibusiness firm’s complicatedness by adding or removing businesses. It can also affect the firm’s complexity structure by altering the interrelatedness of the firm’s businesses. Building on complexity science, we hypothesize that corporate strategy changes that alter a firm’s complexity structure are more likely to increase IT MW than changes that only affect the firm’s complicatedness. Changing the complexity structure of the firm can change the firm’s IT contro environment by changing the cross-business IT linkages and the IT controls on those linkages. The firm may have to change its existing IT controls and design additional new IT controls at the cross-business integration and interaction points of the interrelated businesses. However, designing, effectively operating, and changing IT controls to meet the control needs of a diverse set of stakeholders from an interrelated set of businesses are highly challenging (Cram et al. 2016a). The likelihood of emergence of IT MW is high. Complexity-altering corporate strategy changes are likely to disrupt all three types of IT controls: IT controls over tech nology, IT controls over business processes, and IT controls over people behaviors. We hypothesize that the changes are likely to disrupt the IT controls over people much more than IT controls over technology or business processes. Complexity-altering corporate strategychanges are also likely to affect the design effectiveness of the IT controls much more than the operating effectiveness of the IT controls. We find support for these ideas in a longitudinal study of 2,477 publicly traded U.S. firms.

Agency theory and organizational control theory provide the theoretical foundations of internal controls (Cram et al. 2016b).

Agency theory recognizes that controlees (e.g., managers, employees) can deviate from the interests of the controllers (e.g., owners of a firm). It focuses on internal controls that can restrict such deviation (Cram et al. 2016b). Organizational control theory recognizes that managers cannot have oversight of a firm’s operations and achieve the firm’s organizational objectives unless they have access to information such as performance evaluations, monitoring and reward metrics, measurements of employee behaviors and outcomes, etc. It suggests that internal controls can enable managers to identify and resolve operational issues by responding to changing situations and guiding employee behavior (Cram et al. 2016b).

At the corporate level, an example of internal control is the establishment of an independent board of directors (BoD) to monitor and curb potentially self-serving decisions of the top management team (TMT) members (Li et al. 2007). Another example is the institution of an audit committee on the BoD to provide oversight over the firm’s internal controls over financial reporting (Daily et al. 2003). A third example is the institution of an internal audit function to oversee the firm’s risk management, governance, and control activities (Gramling et al. 2004). At the business process level, some examples of internal controls are controls over revenue cycle, expenditure cycle, claims cycle, premium cycle, inventory cycle, etc. For example, the segregation of duty (SoD) controls in these business processes aim to segregate conflicting duties so that employees cannot create fictitious transactions and conceal them to commit fraud or misappropriate assets. Another example, the “three-way match” controls, triangulate the presence of customer purchase orders, shipment notices, and payment records to cross-validate the business transactions (Tanriverdi and Du 2009). Firms often automate some of these internal controls fully or partially in IT applications that support the respective business processes.

IT controls are a subset of the internal controls. Firms design and operate IT controls to provide reasonable assurance that their IT and business objectives will be achieved. IT controls can be classified into three high-level categories along the technology-people-process triad: (1) controls over IT assets; (2) controls over people behavior around IT assets; and (3) controls over business processes supported by IT assets (IIA 2008, 2009, 2012).

IT controls over technology (Technology) refer to the internal control aspects of designing, developing, operating, using, and managing a firm’s IT infrastructure (Cram et al.

2016b). These controls aim to minimize potential hurdles to the successful development and operation of a firm’s IT infrastructure. For example, the controls over the governance of third-party IT services are examples of technology controls designed to ensure that the IT infrastructure services sourced from third parties meet the firm’s requirements (ITGI 2007). Encryption, firewalls, and intrusion detection systems (IDS) are examples of technology controls designed to ensure the secure operation of the firm’s IT infrastructure (ITGI 2007; Ransbotham and Mitra 2009).

IT controls over people behavior (Behavior) aim to minimize potential agency problems between a controller and controlees (Cram et al. 2016b). The controller designs behavior controls to minimize such deviation (Narayanaswamy et al. 2013). Behavior controls intend to regulate or adjust the behavior of the controlees around IT assets (Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003). For example, a technology committee on the BoD is a behavior control designed to ensure that IT executives make IT investments that serve the interests of the shareholders. IT executives in turn can institute behavior controls to ensure that the IT decisions of business units align with the firm’s corporate IT policies (Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999; Weill and Ross 2005). Business units can design behavior controls to ensure employee adherence to the firm’s end-user policies such as forced periodic password changes and system log-off after a period of inactivity. Behavior controls also monitor physical and logical access rights to ensure that employees do not have access to confidential data, IT systems or applications that are not required by their job duties.

IT controls over business processes (Process) aim to minimize potential risks in the data inputs, processing logic, and outputs of IT applications that support a firm’s business processes. Input controls are designed to ensure that data inputs to IT applications are valid, free of errors and omissions, and entered by authorized and qualified staff (IIA 2009). Processing controls are designed to ensure the integrity and validity of processing logic in IT applications: for example, formulas and calculations are correct; processing is authorized by appropriate users; exceptions are handled adequately; and there is an audit trail linking transactions from source to output and backward (IIA 2009). Output controls are designed to ensure that the outputs of IT applications are presented completely and accurately; unreasonable, unusual, and unexpected outcomes are investigated, checked against inputs, and reconciled with other sources; and outputs are distributed to authorized users only (IIA 2009). IT control effectiveness. A firm’s IT control effectiveness is assessed with (1) design effectiveness and (2) operating effectiveness (ITGI 2007). Design effectiveness refers to whether a firm designs the necessary IT controls; and whether the designed IT controls are adequate to provide reasonable assurance that the firm’s objectives will be achieved (ITGI 2007). Operating effectiveness refers to whether the firm operates the IT controls as designed, and whether the employees performing the IT controls have the necessary authority and expertise to perform them effectively (ITGI 2007). In the context of internal controls over financial reporting, IT MW could emerge if deficiencies in the design or operating effectiveness of IT controls result in a reasonable possibility that material errors occur in the firm’s financial statements (IIA 2008). ## Theoretical Foundations of the Independent Constructs: Corporate Strategy

Corporate strategy focuses on the strategic choices of a multibusiness firm such as (1) firm scope (i.e., industry positions in which the firm’s businesses operate) and (2) interrelatedness (i.e., the degree to which the firm’s businesses share common resources to create cross-business synergies) (Eisenhardt and Piezunka 2011; Tanriverdi and Venkatraman 2005).

Diversification is a corporate strategy change that increases the scope of a firm through entry into new businesses (Ramanujam and Varadarajan 1989). In related diversification, the newly entered and the existing businesses of a firm share common resources to create synergies and make the overall value of the corporation greater than the sum of the individual values of the businesses (Tanriverdi and Venkatraman 2005). To enable cross-business resource synergies, related diversifiers integrate business processes and IT systems across their businesses (Tanriverdi 2005, 2006). Cross-business IT inte grations create a need for new IT controls at the integration and interaction points of the interrelated businesses. In unrelated diversification, however, the business units of a diversifier are independent. These units share little or no business resources. Nevertheless, to operate under the governance of a single firm, and to comply with relevant laws and regulations, unrelated business units can also share some support functions such as HR, accounting, finance, treasury, and tax (Tanriverdi 2006).

Mergers and acquisitions (M&A) are corporate strategy changes that increase the scope or scale of a firm through the purchase of other firms (King et al. 2004). M&A could potentially change the degree of interrelatedness among a firm’s businesses, and hence, the cross-business resource sharing patterns. The addition of interrelated businesses requires cross-business IT integrations, and hence, create a need for new IT controls at the cross-business integration and interaction points (Baker and Niederman 2014; Du 2015; Henningsson and Kettinger 2016; Tanriverdi and Uysal 2011). The extent of cross-business IT integration depends on the extent of the interrelatedness between the newly acquired and the existing businesses of the firm.

Divestiture is a corporate strategy change that reduces the scope or scale of a firm through the sale of one or more business units to another firm (Brauer 2006). A firm can engage in divestitures for a variety of strategic reasons such as unlocking the value potential of a high performing business unit; or exiting a poorly performing business unit; or refocusing its business portfolio on core competencies and growth opportunities (Brauer 2006). Carving out and selling a business unit is a challenging task especially when the divested unit is interrelated with the rest of the firm’s businesses and shares common business processes and IT systems with them (Fähling et al. 2013; Smith and Watson 2013). The severance of business process and IT linkages can destroy previously optimized IT controls of the seller. To meet the control needs of its downsized business, the seller may need to design new IT controls (Fähling et al. 2013; Smith and Watson 2013; Tanriverdi and Du 2009).

## Theoretical Foundations of the Nomological Links: Complexity Science

Prior IC MW studies that account for diversification and M&A argue that adding a new business to a firm creates more internal control challenges (Ashbaugh-Skaife et al. 2007) by making the firm’s business transactions more “complicated” (Ge and McVay 2005) or more “complex” (Doyle et al. 2007b). These studies use the terms complicated and complex interchangeably and operationalize them with the same empirical measure: the count of a firm’s business segments (see Table 1).

Complexity science explains that complex is not the same as complicated (Cilliers 1998; Dekker et al. 2013). Both complicated and complex systems (e.g., firms) consist of a large number of interacting components (e.g., business units), but that is where their commonality ends (Cilliers 1998; Waldrop 1992). Complicated systems are hard to understand and control, but ultimately, they are fully knowable and controllable (Cilliers 1998). In contrast, it is infeasible to fully understand and control complex systems (Cilliers 1998).

A firm’s complexity structure is a function of both the firm’s number of business units and the degree of interrelatedness among the firm’s business units (Chung 2014; Cilliers 1998; Dekker et al. 2013; Kannampallil et al. 2011). An increasing number of business units makes a firm complicated, but not necessarily complex. It is the degree of interrelatedness among the business units that increases the firm’s complexity. Little or no interrelatedness means that the business units operate relatively independently. Business units may have a pooled dependency in that these units share the scarce financial resources of the same parent, but they do not have reciprocal dependencies. For example, these units do not exchange operational resources to create synergies. A higher degree of interrelatedness, on the other hand, means that business units have reciprocal dependencies. They exchange physical resources, customer knowledge, R&D knowledge, production knowledge, etc., to create synergies. The scope of the cross-business interactions is larger. Interactions among the interrelated business units are richer. The higher degree of interrelatedness also results in some firm-level properties that make it infeasible to fully understand and control a complex firm: non-decomposability, nonlinearity, emergence, and unpredictability (Chung 2014; Cilliers 1998; Dekker et al. 2013; Kannampallil et al. 2011).

The non-decomposability property states that a firm-level outcome such as IT control effectiveness cannot be understood by decomposing the firm into business units and then studying the respective IT control effectiveness outcomes of the business units in isolation. The firm-level outcome is not the sum of the outcomes of the individual business units. Rather, it is a function of the interactions among the interrelated business units. The interactions can generate positive (or negative) synergies that make the overall outcome of the firm more (or less) than the sum of the outcomes of the individual business units.

The nonlinearity property states that changes in inputs (e.g., adding a new business to the firm) may not produce outcomes that are proportional to the inputs, or vice versa, due to the reciprocal dependencies and interactions of the interrelated businesses (Cilliers 1998). For example, the acquisition of a small target can generate a disproportionately large outcome in the merged firm (e.g., IT MW). FedEx experienced such a phenomenon in 2018 due to its acquisition of Bongo International. Bongo’s business was to help customers with shipping calculations and currency translations. FedEx integrated Bongo into its operations and renamed it to “FedEx Cross-Border International.” It did not notice that Bongo came with an unprotected Amazon S3 server. This IT control weakness in the server remained unnoticed in FedEx’s integrated operations, and caused a major negative event in 2018 by publicly exposing the scanned passports, driver’s licenses, and other private information of more than 119,000 FedEx customers (Scipioni 2018).

The emergence property states that, without the advanced planning and intervention of a designer, interrelated businesses can interact bottom up to generate novel, unforeseen outcomes at the firm level (Cilliers 1998). In M&A, for instance, previously unplanned synergies might emerge serendipitously out of the interactions and knowledge sharing between the merging businesses (Allen et al. 2002; Colman and Lunnan 2011; Graebner 2004). Products that did not generate any material revenue in either firm could be bundled and cross-sold in innovative new ways to generate material revenues in the merged firm. This would in turn require the merged firm to institute new, previously unplanned IT controls around the bundled products and processes (Conkle et al. 2016).

Finally, the non-predictability property states that the outcomes of a non-decomposable complex firm may not be feasible to predict in advance due to the nonlinear, reciprocal interactions of the interrelated business units that produce novel, unexpected outcomes.

The distinctions between the properties and outcomes of complicated and complex firms alert us that the unexpected IT MW and IC MW outcomes of a multibusiness firm could be an artifact of the complexity structure created by the interrelatedness of business units rather than merely the complicatedness created by the count of business units.

## Hypotheses Development

For each type of corporate strategy change, we develop three hypotheses. First, we link a given type of corporate strategy change to IT MW. Second, we explain why complexityaltering corporate strategy changes are much more likely to increase IT MW than corporate strategy changes that only affect a firm’s complicatedness. Third, although all three types of IT controls are likely to be negatively affected, we explain why the complexity-altering corporate strategy changes are likely to disrupt IT controls over people much more than IT controls over technology or business processes.

## IT MW Effects of Diversification

Diversification adds new businesses to a firm’s business portfolio (Ramanujam and Varadarajan 1989). At a minimum, diversification increases the sheer count of businesses. Thus, it complicates the firm. Adding a new business expands the firm’s scope of business objectives. New business objectives expand the scope of risks faced by the firm. New IT controls are needed to address the expanded scope of risks. The need to design and operate new IT controls makes it more challenging to maintain IT control effectiveness (Ge and McVay 2005; Petrovits et al. 2011; Zhang et al. 2007).

Consider the diversification of CVS into retail health care services by opening MinuteClinic, which added 1,100 new revenue generating units to CVS. In terms of internal controls over financial reporting (ICFR), this diversification move created a new risk: not being able to collect, aggregate, and report financial statements of 1,100 MinuteClinic units accurately, and hence, failing to meet the ICFR requirements of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, including IT control requirements (Ashbaugh-Skaife et al. 2007; Ge and McVay 2005). New IT input controls were required to minimize the possibility of errors in receiving the financials from 1,100 MinuteClinic units. New IT processing controls were required to ensure that the 1,100 financial statements were aggregated correctly. Compared to a firm that does not engage in diversification, a firm that diversifies into a new business faces more challenges in designing and effectively operating IT controls, and hence, it faces higher likelihood of IT MW.

## H1: Diversification is likely to increase IT MW.

The firm faces new risks at the cross-business IT integration and interface points where data cross over boundaries into, out of, and between IT systems of different businesses (Baskerville et al. 2018). However, it is much more challenging to design, effectively operate, and update IT controls at the integration and interaction points of a set of interrelated businesses (Cram et al. 2016b). The nonlinearity of the interactions is high due to the reciprocal information and resource exchange needs of the related businesses. There is a larger number of “controlees” from different business units, whose behaviors need to be regulated at the cross-business interface points. Multiple “controllers” from the different business units have to agree on the objectives of the cross-business IT controls. Different business units may have conflicting objectives about the cross-business IT controls regarding the trade-offs among the confidentiality, integrity, availability, and appropriate use of data (Cram et al. 2016b). The possibility of emergence in the firm-level IT control effectiveness outcome is high due to the nonlinear interactions, negotiations, and power dynamics among the mutually dependent business units. The nonpredictability of firm-level IT control effectiveness is also high due to the nonlinear interactions among a mutually dependent, and dynamically interacting set of businesses.

The diversification of CVS into retailed health care services is an example of related diversification. There are resourcebased synergies between MinuteClinic and the existing pharmacy business of CVS. In complying with IT control requirements of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), MinuteClinic could leverage the existing knowledge of the pharmacy business that was already subject to HIPAA. However, the scope of the IT controls in the pharmacy business was limited to prescription related patient health information (PHI): name, birthdate, address, medication names and doses, providers’ names, and prescription numbers. MinuteClinic had to collect additional PHI such as medical history, previous treatments, current health and medical conditions, etc.<sup>2</sup> To coordinate the care of the patient, MinuteClinic also had to exchange PHI with health care organizations such as primary care physicians, local hospitals, urgent care centers, other health care providers or plans, Health Information Exchanges or similar shared electronic medical records or systems. Thus, CVS faced the dual challenges of designing and effectively operating (1) new IT controls within the MinuteClinic, and (2) new cross-business IT controls at the integration and interaction points of the MinuteClinc and the pharmacy operations. Due to the complexity dynamics of the related businesses summarized above, the overall IT control effectiveness of CVS is not simply the sum of IT control effectiveness values within MinuteClinic and its pharmacy unit; it is also a function of the crossbusiness IT controls that govern the interactions of the related businesses.

Google is another example of a complex, related diversifier across businesses such as Search, YouTube, Gmail, Google Docs, Google Maps, Google Play, Google Chrome, Google+, etc. These businesses share common resources such as customer data as well as search and advertising analytics to display ads. Due to complexity dynamics of these related businesses, developing, maintaining, and updating effective IT controls at the interface and interaction points of the businesses proved challenging. H1a: Relative to unrelated diversification, related diversification is more likely to increase IT MW.

## Effects of Related Diversification on IT Controls over Technology, People, and Process

In related diversification, businesses have mutually dependent technology, people, and business processes to coordinate and control. Among them, IT controls over people behaviors are particularly more challenging to design and operate effectively than IT controls over technology or business processes due to the adaptiveness of human behaviors (Page 2009).

Related diversifiers often seek to replicate their existing technologies, business processes, and human behaviors in the newly entered businesses (Tanriverdi 2005; Tanriverdi and Venkatraman 2005). However, cross-business replication is difficult (Winter and Szulanski 2001). Although all three elements of the technology-people-process triad are difficult to replicate, people behaviors are much more difficult to replicate than technologies and business processes (Weill et al. 2010).

Technologies and business processes are predictable and controllable. They have decomposable components, which codify a set of business logics, principles, and rules prescribed by a firm. If these technologies and processes work well in the existing businesses, a firm can clone and copy them in a related business with relatively minor modifications. Developers understand the inputs, processing logic, and outputs of each component. Nonlinearity of the interactions among the components is low. Interfaces and interaction rules of the different components are well understood and defined. The same inputs produce the same outputs in predefined ways reliably and consistently each time. The possibility of emergence of unexpected outcomes is low. Non-predictability of system-level outcomes is also low. Any deviations from the predefined behaviors of the system are exceptions that are feasible to analyze and fix (Page 2009).

In comparison, human behaviors are much less predictable and controllable. Users of technologies and business processes are diverse, intelligent, and adaptive. They learn, adapt, and change behaviors over time. There are rich, nonlinear interactions among humans: decisions and actions of people in one business unit can trigger intelligent, adaptive responses from people of interconnected business units, which in turn, can engender intelligent responses from the first business unit, which can lead to further adaptive responses from the related units. Such reciprocal, nonlinear interactions are not easily decomposable. These interactions can lead to the emergence of unexpected, unplanned outcomes at the firm level. Rules or mathematical equations cannot fully capture the workings of rich human interactions (Page 2009). The complexity dynamics created by intelligent, adaptive, and mutually dependent human interactions across related businesses makes it challenging to design and effectively operate the IT controls over people behaviors at the interface points of the related businesses. Thus:

H1b: Related diversification is more likely to increase MW in IT controls over people than MW in IT controls over technology or business processes.

## IT MW Effects of M&A

Like diversification, M&A increases a firm’s complicatedness by adding a new business to it. Compared to a firm that does not engage in M&A, a firm that grows through M&A faces greater challenges in designing and effectively operating IT controls. What is different in M&A is that the acquired business (the “target”) joins the acquirer with its own set of technologies, people, and processes, which increases the complexity level of the merged firm by increasing the differences in technologies, people, and processes (Page 2009). The acquirer faces complexity dynamics associated with the integration of two different sets of technologies, people, and processes. Figure 2 illustrates the potential scope of IT and business assets that need to be integrated in M&A.

The extent of M&A IT integration can vary across different M&A IT integration strategies (Baker and Niederman 2014; Henningsson and Kettinger 2016). However, even the least intensive and least disruptive type of M&A IT integration leads to nontrivial changes in the complexity structure of the merged firm by establishing new connections and dependencies between the technologies, people, and processes of the merging firms. Those changes are likely to disrupt some of the pre-M&A IT controls of both firms. Designing new IT controls to meet the needs of the merged firm is a challenging task. For each pair of IT applications and business processes that are being consolidated or linked, there are multiple stakeholders from the respective business and IT units of the acquirer and target, as well as stakeholders from the M&A integration teams. These stakeholders will need to jointly decide how to design the new IT controls of the consolidated applications or processes (Cram et al. 2016b). Due to the dependencies among a diverse set of stakeholders, the probability of failing to agree on the new IT controls is high. Thus, the likelihood of emergence of IT MW is also high.

## H2: M&A is likely to increase IT MW.

## IT MW Effects of Related Versus Unrelated M&A

Relative to unrelated M&A, related M&A increases the merged firm’s complexity further due to the higher degree of interrelatedness between the acquirer and target (Allen et al. 2002; Graebner 2004). Hence, the likelihood of emergence of IT MW is higher in related M&A.

Prior to an M&A transaction, non-decomposability is low as the acquirer and target are two independent firms in the market. With M&A integration, non-decomposability increases as the related businesses start fusing and developing mutual dependencies with each other. The nonlinearity of interactions also increases as the related businesses interact with each other reciprocally to discover each other’s resources and decide how to consolidate and fuse them. Some of the consolidations, integrations, and synergies are as “expected” based on the plans made during the pre-M&A planning phase. However, there are also new, serendipitously emerging, previously unexpected synergies. The interactions and knowledge sharing between the related businesses result in new, superior processes, products, and technologies in the merged firm (Colman and Lunnan 2011; Graebner 2004). The nonpredictability of the IT control effectiveness of the merged firm is high due to such complexity dynamics. IT control effectiveness is not simply the sum of the pre-M&A IT control effectiveness of the acquirer and the target. It is a function of the emerging M&A IT integrations that disrupt some of the existing IT controls and design new ones.

Figure 3 illustrates how related and unrelated M&A affect interrelatedness and complexity differently. Before M&A, Firm 1 has four business units. Then, Firm 1 acquires Firm 2 and adds it as a fifth business unit (BU5). BU5 is a related M&A. It has resource sharing linkages with BU1 and BU3. These linkages increase the complexity structure of the firm. The firm also acquires Firm 3 and adds it as a sixth business unit (BU6). However, BU6 is an unrelated M&A, which does not have any resource sharing linkages with the businesses of the acquirer, except perhaps some support services. Thus, its impact on the acquirer’s complexity structure is much less. The scope of M&A IT integration required by the related M&A (BU5) is much higher than that of the unrelated M&A (BU6). Thus, the likelihood of emergence of IT MW in related M&A is higher than in unrelated M&A.

Consider the example of Royal DSM N.V., a Netherlandsbased company, which acquired the Vitamins & Fine Chemicals Division from Roche, a Swiss-based health care group. The scope of the M&A IT integration entailed more than 360 IT integration projects. Ruud Neeskens, the senior manager of IT applications at DSM stated:

We had more than 360 projects running in parallel during the peak period [of M&A IT integration], with a lot of dependencies between them … all of this had to happen without the slightest business disruption … in 15 months …. So if you get sand in the wheels of that complex machine, then you can have the whole machine stop, then we would have had a disaster on our hands (Applegate et al. 2007, p. 7).

The M&A IT integrations can disrupt the pre-M&A IT controls of the acquirer and the target. Some IT control requirements of business processes may not be predictable in advance due to the emergence (Allen et al. 2002) and serendipity (Graebner 2004) of the M&A integrations. ## H2a: Relative to unrelated M&A, related M&A is more likely to increase IT MW.

## Effects of Related M&A on IT Controls over Technology, People, and Process

In related M&A, the acquirer seeks to integrate two sets of people, technology, and business processes that do similar jobs. The speed of integration of these resources affects acquisition performance (Homburg and Bucerius 2006). Rapid integration of people has a positive effect on performance as it reduces uncertainties about jobs, reporting relationships, job roles, job locations, etc. Rapid integration of technology and business processes, on the other hand, has negative effects on performance due to operational disruptions, customer uncertainties, and customer defections it creates (Bauer et al. 2016). Thus, acquirers often spread the integration of technology and processes over a period of years. Slow integration of technology and process minimizes disruptions to business. It also allows acquirer and target to develop mutual understanding about how to merge their technologies and processes and accomplish work in the merged business (Bauer et al. 2016). Due to the rapid integration of people, and hence IT people controls, and the slower integration of technology and business processes, we expect related M&A to disrupt IT controls over peoplebehaviors much more than IT controls over technology and business processes.

Prior to an M&A, job roles in the acquirer and target were decomposed: for example, there was a CIO in the acquirer and a CIO in the target. With a related M&A, the job roles become fused. One CIO survives the integration and assumes the CIO role of the merged businesses whereas the other CIO leaves or takes a new role in the merged IT unit. Such consolidations in people roles take place across many IT and business functions. As noted, people are highly adaptive. The threat of job or role loss increases the nonlinearity of interactions. People start engaging in sociopolitical interactions to keep their jobs, job roles, or move to more favorable job roles (LeFave et al. 2008). Such interactions increase the possibility of the emergence of unexpected outcomes as to who survive and who assume which job roles in the merged firm. The non-predictability of the outcomes is high. It is not easy for pre-M&A planners to predict in advance how sociopolitical interactions are likely to emerge and unfold. Such uncertainty also creates uncertainties in behavior controls such as logical access rights in IT systems and applications. Due to the complexity dynamics summarized, it is not feasible to predict in advance how the job roles and access rights would emerge in the merged firm. Thus, the acquirer may not be able to design in advance the new IT controls over people behaviors in the merged firm.

The acquirer also tries to identify cultural differences between the two firms and institute behavior controls to bring together the two cultures. Such top-down cultural change initiatives could face bottom up resistance, trigger adaptive behaviors by affected employees, and escalate the complexity dynamics (Cram et al. 2016b). Acquired employees often feel culture shock, loss of identity, humiliation, loss, and anger. Some of them may unexpectedly develop resistance and feelings of hostility, and attempt to bypass the IT behavior controls designed to align the two cultures (Paruchuri et al. 2006). Some employees can seek IT control workarounds to reestablish their autonomy, which would undermine the effectiveness of behavior controls (Cram et al. 2016b). During Royal DSM’s M&A IT integrations, for instance, one group of IT employees accepted the changes; another group had doubts but went along; and a third group resisted the changes. The acquirer had to take drastic action to address the emergence of unexpected resistance:

The only way to move forward was to get the reluctant employees either into another position in the organization that’s outside of ICT, or to remove them from the company completely (Applegate et al. 2007, p. 10).

The acquirer may have to terminate a large number of resisting employees. Terminations can fuel the complexity dynamics further. If terminated employees continue to access the systems, it can lead to the emergence of new security risks that were not foreseen by the acquirer. Not revoking the user IDs and passwords of terminated employees immediately is an MW in IT controls over people-behavior (IIA 2009). The IT unit also faces the challenge of onboarding all remaining employees of the acquirer and target into the integrated IT systems of the merged firm. Onboarding thousands of employees, giving them proper access rights and privileges, and segregating their duties in a controlled manner present major challenges in the compressed timeframe of M&A integration. The IT unit, which is already overwhelmed with the demands of M&A IT integration, may not have enough time and staffing to reconfigure the access rights in a controlled manner. An unintended, emergent outcome of such stressful M&A IT integration contexts is that many IT units end up rubber-stamping all user access, exception, and change requests coming from the businesses in order to meet other pressing deadlines of the M&A integrations (Roehl-Anderson 2013). Thus:

H2b: Related M&A is more likely to increase MW in IT controls over people than MW in IT controls over technology or business processes.

## IT MW Effects of Divestitures

Relative to a firm that maintains its corporate scope, a firm that reduces its corporate scope through a divestiture is more likely to experience IT MW. Divestiture seeks to decompose the seller into two legally independent businesses: DivestCo, the divested business unit, and RemainCo, the remaining businesses of the seller. The reduction in the number of businesses would make RemainCo a less complicated firm. However, the decomposition of a previously interdependent system of businesses creates complexity dynamics and leads to the loss of some important system properties (Cilliers 2000). For example, IT carve-out severs IT interlinkages between RemainCo and DivestCo, and the IT controls on them (Deloitte 2008; Kreuz and Vallerien 2004; Neely and Adolph 2007). The destruction of the previously optimized IT controls and the need to design new IT controls for the downsized RemainCo increase the likelihood of IT MW.

Figure 4 illustrates a stylized IT carve-out process of divestitures. Prior to the divestiture, the seller and the unit to-bedivested are in a parent-subsidiary relationship. Nondecomposability is high. The parent provides support services to business units: HR, payroll, accounting, finance, treasury, tax, IT, etc. (Fähling et al. 2013; Smith and Watson 2013). The extent of non-decomposability of the shared support services depends on the degree of interrelatedness among the business units. After the divestiture, RemainCo and DivestCo ought to be decomposed to become standalone, legally independent firms. Decomposing and separating DivestCo from RemainCo’s shared support environment is a major goal (Fähling et al. 2013; Smith and Watson 2013). However, IT carve-out is challenging (Fähling et al. 2013; Smith and Watson 2013). A.T. Kearney, a consulting firm that helps with divestiture transactions, states: “in nearly all of our divestiture projects, information technology has been the most complex and difficult area to separate” (Kreuz and Vallerien 2004, p. 42).

Severing IT-enabled interdependencies affects complexity dynamics by creating dangling links and holes in RemainCo’s IT environment and IT controls (Fähling et al. 2013; Smith and Watson 2013; Tanriverdi and Du 2009). RemainCo has to properly terminate or rewire the dangling IT links and redesign IT controls at the previous interface points with DivestCo. RemainCo also has to substitute any data and IT resources and services it used to receive from DivestCo. Thus, it needs to establish new links to new nodes (suppliers). At the interface points of the new nodes, RemainCo also has to institute new IT controls. These node and linkage reconfigurations increase complexity and hence the likelihood of emergence of IT MW.

H3: Divestiture is likely to increase IT MW.

## IT MW Effects of Related Versus Unrelated Divestitures

In related divestitures, many resources (e.g., customers, employees, products, brands, data, intellectual property, facilities, etc.), which used to be shared and fused across interrelated businesses, need to be decomposed, unraveled, and separated (Mankins et al. 2008). The scope of IT carveout is broad in related divestitures (Fähling et al. 2013; Smith and Watson 2013). The carve-out destroys more IT linkages and renders more pre-divestiture IT controls obsolete. Designing the new IT controls of RemainCo is more challenging due to the complexity dynamics resulting from the loss of a related business unit and associated interlinkages.

BU1 contributed to the complexity structure of the firm through its resource linkages with BU2, BU3, and BU5. The firm has to rewire the dangling links to new suppliers and institute new IT controls at the interface points with them. Thus, RemainCo faces a higher complexity level during the carve-out process. It not only has to sever existing links to DivestCo, but also has to establish new links and dependencies to alternative sources outside of the firm. Consequently, more nodes and linkages are being affected: the divested node, the links to the divested node, new nodes outside of the firm and new linkages to the outside nodes.

In comparison, BU4 was an unrelated unit. It did not contribute much to the complexity structure of the firm. Due to the lack of resource linkages with the other business units, non-decomposability, nonlinearity, emergence, and nonpredictability levels were low. Thus, the divestiture of BU4 has little or no effect on the remaining business units of the firm.

In many related divestitures, due to the complexity created by an extensive set of IT interlinkages among the interrelated businesses, IT carve-out may not be able to decompose RemainCo and DivestCo to achieve a clean break within the timeframe of the divestiture. Through a transition services agreement (TSA), RemainCo may have to continue providing IT and business process services to DivestCo (Joy 2019). Employees who depart with DivestCo may need to have continued access to RemainCo’s IT systems after the legal separation of the two firms. Such a scenario increases the security, privacy, and fraud risks, and requires the RemainCo to institute additional new IT controls to mitigate them. The new IT controls are much more complex as they need to govern the behaviors of people across two legally separate but operationally non-decomposable firms. A senior manager at IBM’s CIO office explained the IT risk and control issues of IT carve-out projects as follows:

If you don’t have a clean break [after a divestiture], people continue using your email systems, HR systems, etc. It means that you have people, within your infrastructure, who no longer work for your company. It opens up a whole bunch of security and privacy issues. It creates a lot more work and expense for IBM because anyone who remains within our infrastructure has to be cordoned off from the rest of IBM. We have to take a risk of providing them with access to general areas of IBM, and tightly control all areas that have confidential information. We have to do a soft separation. We even copy whole applications and remove all IBM information from them and provide the purchasing company access to the applications with only their pertinent information on them. We have to track duplicate applications within our infrastructure that the acquiring company is using. It makes it really complicated (Source: Authors’ interviews).

H3a: Relative to unrelated divestiture, related divestiture is more likely to increase IT MW.

## Effects of Related Divestiture on IT Controls over Technology, People, and Process

RemainCo can use a TSA to gain more time in decomposing technology and business processes of DivestCo. This can also minimize and delay disruptions to IT controls over technology and process. However, RemainCo has an urgent need to decompose human resources of the two businesses before the deal close (Joy 2019). The human resource carve-out process makes significant changes to the sheer counts, work roles, relationships, and interaction patterns of people. The complexity dynamics associated with such changes are likely to negatively affect IT controls over people behaviors.

Decomposing a complex system of interrelated businesses leads to the loss of important system properties (Cilliers 2000). For example, the decomposed businesses can unexpectedly lose their pooled negotiation and purchasing powers vis-à-vis IT vendors due to their reduced sizes. The costs of their IT services can unexpectedly go up. Their IT budgets and headcounts can go down due to the smaller sizes of the decomposed businesses. RemainCo and DivestCo used to enjoy synergies by sharing an IT unit, a CIO, and an IT workforce. After the divestiture, they lose such synergies because each business needs its own IT unit, CIO, and IT workforce. However, due to the shrinkage of business size, RemainCo cannot maintain the pre-divestiture IT budget and staffing levels (Fähling et al. 2013). RemainCo’s IT unit often has to do the same IT duties with less budget and fewer IT employees. The changes in the headcounts, work roles, and relationships of IT employees can set off complexity dynamics (Gershenson and Heylighen 2005) such as changes in roles and interaction patterns, which can in turn lead to the emergence of MW in IT controls over people behaviors.

Conflicting IT duties, which used to be segregated into multiple distinct IT employees and governed by segregation of duty (SoD) controls, may now have to be performed by a single IT employee. It would increase the likelihood of emergence of unforeseen MW in IT SoD controls. The increased workload could also lead to unexpected declines in the quality of work (Deloitte 2009). For example, RemainCo has to terminate IT network and application access of a large number of employees who depart with DivestCo. However, the reduced IT headcount may not be able to cope with the bulk access de-provisioning tasks in a timely manner. The risks of not properly terminating access rights, IDs, and passwords of departing employees could unexpectedly increase due to the reduced IT headcount. The emergence of MW in people controls such as logical access controls also becomes more likely. Furthermore, changes brought out by the IT carve-out can result in employee feelings of stress and uncertainty (Cram et al. 2016b). Less resources, higher workloads, and higher uncertainty levels can lead to the emergence of unexpected employee behaviors such as low morale, resistance to changes, withdrawal, or becoming paralyzed (Cascio 1993; Ranganathan and Outlay 2009). As employees try to meet the tight carve-out deadlines, they may be tempted to deviate from the standard work processes and IT controls designed to regulate their behavior (Cotteleer and Bendoly 2015). Thus:

H3b: Related divestiture is more likely to increase MW in IT controls over people than MW in IT controls over technology or business processes.

## Methods

## Sample and Data

Our sampling frame is the list of publicly traded firms in the U.S. stock markets. We further restrict our sample to multibusiness firms tracked in the Compustat Segments database. Longitudinal data on IT MW is available in Audit Analytics database for publicly traded U.S. firms that are subject to the Sarbanes–Oxley (SOX) Act. Section 404 of this regulation (SOX 404) requires firms to design and effectively operate IT controls as part of their overall internal controls to ensure the accuracy and reliability of their financial statements. In a SOX 404 audit, an independent auditor evaluates a firm’s IC MW and IT MW (Li et al. 2012; Masli et al. 2016). We use the “SOX 404 Internal Controls” section of the Audit Analytics database to obtain data on SOX 404 audit results of firms. If a firm restates its SOX auditing results multiple times in a given year, we use the latest available restatement. To measure IT MW and IC MW, we rely on external auditors’ independent assessments of firms’ internal controls. The timeframe of the study is from firms’ fiscal year 2004, the first year of SOX compliance, to their fiscal year 2009. Following prior accounting studies, we exclude “non-accelerated filers” (e.g., Feng et al. 2015)<sup>3</sup> and foreign firms.<sup>4</sup> We obtain and match data on independent and control variables from Compustat, SDC Platinum, SEC’s EDGAR, Bureau of Economic Analysis’ industrial IT investment database, and the Information Week 500 annual rankings. The final estimation sample consists of 2,477 firms with 10,949 observations across six years (2004-2009).<sup>5</sup>

## Dependent Variables

We use a total of eight dependent variables in our analyses: (1) IC MW, (2) non-IT MW only, (3) IT MW, (4) IT MW in technology controls, (5) IT MW in people-behavior controls, (6) IT MW in process controls, (7) MW in the design of IT controls, and (8) MW in the operation of IT controls. These variables are coded as follows: presence of MW [1] = ineffective control; absence of MW [0] = effective control.

IC of a firm is effective in a given year if there is no MW finding in its annual SOX 404 audit [IC MW=0]; and ineffective [IC MW=1] if there are MW findings. IC MW findings could be due to the presence of both IT MW and non-IT MW findings. In Audit Analytics, category #22 represents the IT MW findings; the other categories represent non-IT MW. IT MW captures whether the SOX 404 audit finds MW in IT controls [IT MW=1] or not [IT MW = 0]. It reflects the ultimate conclusion of the external auditor after an extensive review of hundreds of IT controls. Similarly, non-IT MW captures whether the SOX 404 audit finds only non-IT material weaknesses (Canada et al. 2009; Masli et al. 2016). In our sample, 163 unique firms experienced IT MW.<sup>6</sup>

Firms with MW findings have a mandate to disclose the details of the MW in their annual reports. We manually collected the disclosures from audited annual reports and coded the IT MW into further categories by following the coding procedures reported in previous studies (e.g., Li et al. 2012; Masli et al. 2016).

We first developed a coding scheme that classified IT controls into 12 categories by synthesizing the academic and practitioner literatures on IT audits and IT controls (AICPA 2004; IIA 2009, 2012; ITGI 2006, 2007). We refined the coding scheme based on intensive discussions with IT audit partners and senior managers from leading public accounting firms. Using the technology-people-process triad, we further grouped the 12 categories of IT controls into three types of IT controls: IT controls over technology, IT controls over people-behavior, and IT controls over business processes. We then calculated three binary variables to indicate the existence [1] or absence [0] of IT MWs in the three types of IT controls: Technology IT MW, Behavior IT MW, and Process IT MW. Similarly, we classified IT MWs into MWs in the design of IT controls (Design IT MW) and in the operation of IT controls (Operating IT MW). Table 2 summarizes the final coding scheme we used in classifying IT MW.

From the SEC’s EDGAR database, we collected annual filings of all firms that experienced IT MW. We excerpted the texts that described the nature of these IT MW. We then had two independent coders follow the coding scheme in Table 2 to classify the disclosed IT MW. The concordance rates between the classifications of the two coders, as measured by Cohen’s Kappa (Cohen 1960), were all over 90%, indicating the reliability of the coding scheme (Landis and Koch 1977). The two coders discussed the few discrepancies and jointly made the decision.

We classify an M&A transaction as related if the acquirer and target are in the same industry sector (two digit SIC code); and as unrelated if they are in different industry sectors. To find the intensity of related (unrelated) M&A, we sum up the values of all related (unrelated) M&A of the firm in that year and divide it by the market value of the firm’s invested capital at the end of the year.

Divestiture, Related Divestiture, Unrelated Divestiture. Building on Klein (1986), we measure the divestiture intensity of a firm in a given year as the ratio of the firm’s total value of divestiture transactions completed during that year divided by the firm’s market value of invested capital at the beginning of the year.<sup>8</sup> We further decompose the divestiture intensity measure into its related and unrelated components. We code a divestiture transaction as related if the divested unit has the same two digit SIC code as the seller’s primary two digit SIC code; and as unrelated if they have different two digit SIC codes. We find the intensity of related (unrelated) divestitures by summing up the values of all related (unrelated) divestitures of the firm in that year and dividing the sum by the market value of the firm’s invested capital at the start of the year.

## Control Variables

We include a total of 11 control variables to rule out potential alternative explanations. At the industry level, we control for environmental turbulence and munificence (Dess and Beard 1984), as well as environmental IT intensity of a firm’s businesses (McAfee and Brynjolfsson 2008). At the firm level, we control for a firm’s size, profitability, financial leverage ratio, corporate governance weakness, external auditor status, IT capability, foreign operations, and corporate restructuring activities. We also include year dummy variables to control for unobserved year fixed effects. Appendix A presents the rationales, measurement, and data sources of the controls. Table 3 presents descriptive statistics and correlations.

Reverse causality. To mitigate the potential risk of reverse causality between corporate strategy changes and IT MW, we use the following research design. First, we introduce a oneyear lag structure between the dependent and the independent variables. We measure IT MWs in year (t) and corporate strategy changes in year (t-1). We use the same lag structure for control variables (t-1) with the exception of the controls for environment variables and auditor status, which are measured in year (t). Second, in the dependent variables, we focus on the first time occurrence of an IT MW in a firm. After a firm reports an IT MW for the first time, we drop all subsequent annual observations of the firm from the sample. Third, we conduct additional analyses to explore the possibility of reverse causality by regressing the firm’s corporate strategy moves in year (t) on IT MWs of the firm in year (t-1). These analyses do not indicate any evidence of reverse causality.<sup>9</sup>

## Model Specification

Firms are at risk of experiencing MW in their internal controls during their annual SOX audits. We adopt survival analysis (Allison 2010) to estimate the effects of the hypothesized corporate strategy changes on a firm’s hazard of experiencing MW. Due to lack of a priori theory to specify the exact distribution of the hazard of a firm reporting IT MW, we use semi-parametric discrete-time Cox models that do not require the specification of the exact form of the link function between independent and dependent variables. Cox models help reduce potential biases associated with the misspecification of the statistical models. We specify the hazard of a firm i reporting IT MW at year t with the following Cox models:

$$

Cox models assume that the hazard of an event for firm i at time t is proportional to the baseline hazard, and then model how the hazard of the event per time unit varies in response to the set of time varying explanatory covariates (Cox 1972; Hosmer et al. 2008).<sup>10</sup> ë (t) is the unknown baseline hazard of the event of interest at time t (e.g., IT MW). ë(t|X ) is the hazard of the event conditional on a time varying set of explanatory variables (X ) such as Diversification, Related versus Unrelated Diversification; M&A, Related versus Unrelated M&A, Divestiture, Related versus Unrelated Divestiture, and control variables (Controls).

In our sample, a total of 83 firms experienced IT MW across multiple years. We examined the disclosure documents to find out that 13 of these firms experienced different IT MW across multiple years. We dropped these firms. The remaining 70 firms experienced the same IT MW across multiple years. We kept only the first-time occurrence of the IT MW.<sup>11</sup>

## Robustness Checks<sup>12</sup>

We check the robustness of the results with different statistical methods and sampling strategies. First, we consider paneldata models instead of Cox models. In particular, we reestimate our data by using Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) models, a specific type of generalized linear models that apply to longitudinal data analyses (Hardin and Hilbe 2003). GEE takes into account unobserved factors specific to a firm and models possible serial correlation among outcomes of the same firm in multiple years. Second, we employ rareevent logit models (King and Zeng 2001) to account for the relative rareness of firms reporting IT MW. Third, to account for the potential endogeneity of firms engaging in M&As and divestitures, we implement the Heckman two-step procedure (Heckman 1979; Shaver 1998). During the first stage of the Heckman procedure, we use the M&A and divestiture waves in a firm’s primary industry to predict the focal firm’s likelihoods of joining these bandwagons and engaging in M&A and divestiture activities. Based on the first-stage results, we compute two correction factors, for M&A and divestiture respectively, and subsequently include them in our primary estimation models at the second stage (Heckman 1979; Shaver 1998). The Heckman procedure helps correct for potential endogeneity biases that some unobserved factors could drive both a firm’s M&A or divestiture decisions and its internal control effectiveness. Fourth, as an alternative sampling strategy, we construct a matched case-control sample by matching firms reporting IT MW with firms that do not report IT MW. We use “coarsened exact matching” (Iacus et al. 2012), and apply a weighted logistic regression, as implemented by Blackwell et al. (2009), with the same set of independent variables to predict whether a firm reports IT MW. Our primary conclusions remain qualitatively unchanged in all of these robustness checks.

How do corporate strategy changes affect the design versus operating effectiveness of IT controls? In Table 4, the results in Model 7 and 8 indicate that all three corporate strategy changes lead to MW in the design effectiveness of IT controls. The hazard of an MW in the design effectiveness of IT controls increases significantly with diversification (â = .41, p-value < .05); marginally significantly with M&A (â = 1.81, p-value < .10); and significantly with divestiture (â = 2.78, p-value < .01). In Table 5, we further break down the corporate strategy changes into their related and unrelated components. The results in Models 7 and 8 of Table 5 indicate that the unrelated components do not have any significant effects on the design or the operating effectiveness of IT controls whereas the related components significantly affect at least one of them.

Related divestitures significantly increase the hazard of an MW in the design effectives of IT controls (â = 2.90, p-value < .01). They also have a marginally significant effect on the hazard of an MW in the operating effectiveness of IT controls (â = 3.01, p-value < .10). Related diversification has a marginally significant effect on the hazard of an MW in the design effectiveness of IT controls (â = 0.40, p-value < .10). These findings provide further support for our thesis that corporate strategy changes that alter the interrelatedness of business units disrupt IT controls, and require the design of new IT controls, but firms struggle to design the new IT controls.

Where do IC MW come from: IT MW or non-IT MW? Prior studies did not distinguish whether MW in the overall internal controls of firms might be due to IT MW or non-IT MW. Our results in Models 1, 2, and 3 of Table 4 allow an exploration of whether a firm’s IC MW following corporate strategy changes might be coming from the emergence of IT MW or non-IT MW. Interestingly, changes in diversification level and M&A activity, which were commonly included as control variables in prior studies (see Table 1), do not have any significant effects on non-IT MW, but they significantly increase the IT MW. These results suggest that diversification and M&A might be increasing IC MW primarily through their effects on IT MW. Furthermore, divestiture significantly increases IT MW, non-IT MW, and IC MW. These findings indicate that divestiture is more disruptive to IT and non-IT controls than diversification or M&A.

## Discussion and Conclusion

This study develops and validates a new complexity-based theory on the links between corporate strategy changes and IT control effectiveness in multibusiness firms.

## Contributions to Research

First, this study theorizes why, how, and under what conditions corporate strategy changes would affect IT MW. The study questions a taken-for-granted assumption of the prior IC MW and IT MW studies that any corporate strategy change that adds more businesses to a firm (e.g., diversification and M&A) would increase MW by making the firm more complicated and requiring more internal controls. Building on complexity science, the study makes a theoretical distinction between a firm’s complicatedness and complexity. It explains that adding new businesses to a firm would only increase MW if the new businesses are interrelated with some of the existing businesses of the firm. Indeed, the results show that the addition of unrelated businesses through diversification or M&A does not have any significant effects on IT MW. In contrast, the addition of related businesses through related diversification or related M&A significantly increases IT MW. The results support the thesis of this study that IT MW is an artifact of a firm’s complexity rather than its mere complicatedness. Adding interrelated, synergistic businesses to a firm increases the firm’s complexity structure by requiring the firm to build new cross-business IT linkages. It also requires the firm to design and effectively operate new IT controls at the cross-business integration and interaction points of the interrelated businesses. The design and operation of such controls are highly challenging due to the reciprocal dependencies among a diverse set of stakeholders from multiple interrelated businesses. In comparison, adding unrelated businesses to a firm only affects the firm’s complicatedness. Designing and effectively operating IT controls for unrelated businesses are also hard, but they are feasible to do due to the independence of the unrelated businesses.

Second, this study identifies divestiture as an important antecedent of IT MW. Prior studies did not consider the potential role of divestiture in IC MW or IT MW, as summarized in Table 1. Corporate strategy literature cites divestiture as a key element of corporate-level strategy that is used for reducing firm’s scope and complexity. Building on complexity science, this study theoretically explains why, how, and when a divestiture would lead to IT MW. Divestiture reduces complicatedness by reducing firm’s count of businesses. However, the reduction in complicatedness is not always accompanied by a reduction in complexity. Only unrelated divestiture reduces a firm’s complexity as well. Related divestiture increases complexity by decomposing an integrated firm into RemainCo and DivestCo, and requiring the reconfiguration of business interlinkages within RemainCo to fill in the voids created by the departure of DivestCo. RemainCo may also need to establish new linkages to external suppliers to substitute any data, resources, and services that it used to receive from DivestCo. Such reconfigurations in business nodes and interlinkages affect the complexity structure of RemainCo. The reconfigurations increase the likelihood of IT MW by destroying the previously optimized IT linkages and controls and requiring the design of new ones. An implication of the divestiture findings for complexity science is that taking apart a complex system of related businesses can reduce complicatedness while increasing complexity. A related implication for corporate strategy is that reductionist approaches seeking to simplify complex firms through divestitures can unexpectedly create more complexity. The focus should be on the impact of the divestitures on the interlinkages of businesses rather than the mere reduction in business counts.

Third, the study generates new theoretical insights about the relative roles of structural (technology and business process) as well as sociocultural and sociopolitical factors (people) in the emergence of IT MW. Corporate strategy changes that affect the interrelatedness of a firm’ business units lead to major structural changes in technologies and business processes of the affected businesses. These changes also affect the socio-emotional, sociocultural, and sociopolitical behaviors of people by leading to job loss, job transfers and rotations, changes in job roles, job responsibilities, culture, and political power. While all three types of IT controls (technology, people, and process) are negatively affected, controls over people-behavior are affected the most. The affected people are capable of developing smart, adaptive responses to the changes, and circumventing some of the IT controls designed to regulate their behaviors. The results indicate that firms are particularly challenged to design and effectively operate IT controls over people-behavior during corporate strategy changes.

Fourth, the study generates newtheoretical explanations about how IT MW emerge during corporate strategy changes by distinguishing between the design effectiveness and the operating effectiveness of IT controls. Corporate strategy changes that affect a firm’s complexity structure lead to IT MW primarily by creating MW in the design effectiveness of IT controls. Their effects on the operating effectiveness of the IT controls are either weak or insignificant. Firms struggle to design or change the IT controls at the integration and interaction points of the interrelated businesses affected by the corporate strategy changes.

Finally, the study breaks down IC MW into IT MW and non-IT MW to uncover that IC MW that emerge in corporate strategy changes are primarily due to the emergence of IT MW rather than non-IT MW. This finding highlights the critical role of IT controls in supporting the effectiveness of overall internal controls over financial reporting. Future studies on the antecedents and consequences of IC MW may want to include IT constructs to better understand how IT could reduce IC MW and mitigate their negative organizational consequences.

## Contributions to Practice

First, the results alert managers that corporate strategy changes they undertake to maximize their firms’ profitability and growth can also expose their firms to major risks by reducing the firms’ IT control effectiveness. As noted in the opening, ineffective IT controls have significant negative consequences for firms. Prior to undertaking the corporate strategy changes, managers may want to institute governance and change management processes to minimize the potential negative effects of the changes on IT controls. Change management controls that narrowly focus on IT controls of individual businesses are not likely to be sufficient. Diversification, M&A, and divestitures also affect the IT controls at the boundaries of multiple businesses. When cross-business dependencies are high, multiple stakeholders from the affected businesses may need to work together to agree on how cross-business IT controls need to be designed, updated, or terminated. The findings also alert external audit firms that, if an audit client has recently undertaken any corporate strategy changes, its probability of experiencing IT MW goes up significantly. Thus, external audit firms may want to increase their IT audit budgets and staffing levels to be able to discover the emerging IT MW.

Second, among the three corporate strategy changes, divestitures cause the most pervasive IT and non-IT control disruptions. This finding implies that managers and external auditors ought to be particularly vigilant about the higher likelihood of IT MW during the IT carve-out projects of divestitures.

Third, the finding that corporate strategy changes affect the IT controls over people behavior the most has implications for identity and access management (IAM) capabilities of firms. Diversification, M&A, and divestitures may require firms to onboard, offboard, internally transfer, or layoff very large numbers of employees. Each of these transactions requires the firm to provision or de-provision large numbers of user accounts, allocate new access rights and credentials, terminate old ones, and evaluate potential segregation of duty conflicts. Doing so for thousands of user accounts in a short period of time proves challenging. IAM capabilities could help firms to better cope with the logical access management challenges faced during the compressed timeframes of the M&A and divestiture transactions in particular.

## Limitations and Future Work

One limitation and a boundary condition of the proposed theory is that it focuses on multibusiness firms. The theory might not apply to single business firms. Another limitation and boundary condition is that the theory is validated with IT controls that are in scope for SOX 404 audits. While SOX 404 audits cover a broad scope of IT controls, it does not cover the full scope. The theory might also explain the emergence of control weaknesses in other IT control domains such as privacy controls and cyber security controls. Further research is required to validate this proposition. A third limitation and boundary condition is that SOX 404 mandates firms to disclose only the MW, that is, the most severe type of IT control weaknesses. Firms are not mandated to disclose IT control weaknesses in the low severity categories such as D (Deficiency) or SD (Significant Deficiency). Although the weaknesses in the S and the SD categories do not affect SOX 404 compliance, they are still important as they can increase data security, privacy, and fraud risks. The fact that the proposed theory received support with the most severe type of IT control weaknesses suggests that it might also explain the emergence of less severe IT control deficiencies. Further research is required to validate this proposition. A fourth limitation and boundary condition is that the theory is validated with a sample of publicly traded U.S. firms that were classified as “accelerated filers” by the SEC. Future research can test if the theory is valid for other types of firms such as “non-accelerated filers,” foreign firms, private firms, and notfor-profit firms. A fifth limitation is that the theory focuses only on the antecedent factors that increase IT MW. There is also need for further research on the mitigation mechanisms that can reduce the negative effects of the antecedents on IT MW.

Finally, this study’s complexity theoretic view of IT control effectiveness also has implications for future research. In an increasingly digitalized business environment, firms design and operate complex IT controls at their interface points with customers, suppliers, business partners, etc. For example, digital business platforms such as Google and Facebook rely on IT controls in their APIs to regulate the behaviors of large numbers of third party content providers, advertisers, consumers, etc. The IT controls at such interfaces entail significant dependencies among a diverse set of intelligent stakeholders. People used to be the most intelligent and adaptive element at these interfaces. Intelligent and adaptive algorithms also start to join and interact through these interfaces, increasing the complexity of IT controls further. Unexpected, unpredictable behaviors are becoming more likely at the interface points. For example, Facebook could not foresee and prevent the emergence of the privacy control failure in the Cambridge Analytica incident (Granville 2018). Google acknowledged how difficult it was to design and maintain effective controls in the APIs of Google+ (Smith 2018). Adapting a complexity theoretic view of IT controls could enable future research to better understand how IT controls can be designed, operated, and changed over time to adapt to dynamically evolving control needs at the interfaces of a diverse set of stakeholders. Studying complexity dynamics with empirical data is a challenge. This study had limitations in capturing the rich complexity dynamics unfolding during corporate strategy changes of multibusiness firms. Nevertheless, its research design was able to capture and link some of the complexity dynamics to the outcomes of interest. This research design might inspire further empirical research on complex IT control phenomena.

## Control Variables

Environmental Turbulence and Munificence levels of a firm’s businesses. Turbulence is the extent to which the environment changes frequently and unpredictably. Munificence is the extent to which the environment provides abundant resources and opportunities to suppor a firm’s sustained growth (Dess and Beard 1984). In turbulent environments, firms may need to make more corporate strategy changes to adapt to the dynamically changing environment. Firms in munificent environments often accumulate more slack resources internally, and thus, they may be buffered from immediate environmental disruptions.

We follow Dess and Beard to compute environmental turbulence and munificence. First, we measure the two variables at the level of an industry sector. For an industry sector i at year t, we calculate the average revenue of all the firms in that sector, denoted as $y _ { i t } .$ Then, for each industry sector i and year t, we regress the sector-average revenue on time in a five-year time window by estimating equations $y _ { i t } = \mathbf { a } _ { \mathrm { i t } } + \mathsf { \beta } _ { \mathrm { i t } } \mathrm { T }$ $+ \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { i t } } ,$ where T takes values of t, t-1, t-2, t-3, t-4, and t-5; ${ \bf { q } } _ { \mathrm { { i t } } }$ and $\beta _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ are parameters; and $\mathbf { \delta } \mathbf { \varepsilon } _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ is the residual. Environmental turbulence of industry sector i at year t is computed as the standard error of estimated $\beta _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ divided by the average of $\mathrm { \dot { \mathbf { y } } _ { i t } }$ in the past five years. Environmental munificence is computed as the estimated value of ${ \mathrm { { \dot { } } \beta _ { i t } } }$ divided by the average of $\mathbf { \dot { y } _ { i t } }$ in the past five years (Dess and Beard 1984). Second, we calculate the values of turbulence and munificence for a given firm by analyzing the industry sector(s) in which the firm operates. For a firm operating in only one industry sector, we apply the turbulence and munificence values of the same industry sector to the firm. For a firm operating in mul tiple industry sectors, we measure environmental turbulence and munificence of the firm as the average turbulence and munificence values across all the industry sectors in which the firm operates. The weight of a sector is the percentage of revenue the firm generates from that sector in its total revenue (Carpenter and Fredrickson 2001).

Environmental IT intensity of a firm’s businesses. Firms operating in more IT-intensive industries may face more IT-related risks and hence need more IT controls. Thus, we control for the environmental IT intensity of a firm’s businesses. We first compute the IT intensity at the level of industry sectors as the ratio of the software and hardware stock value to the total equipment stock value in a given sector (McAfee and Brynjolfsson 2008). Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) provides these industry-level IT data. Then, we compute firm-level IT intensity as the revenue-weighted average of the IT intensity levels of all industry sectors in which the firm operates in a particular year.

Following previous studies (e.g., Ashbaugh-Skaife et al. 2007; Doyle et al. 2007b; Masli et al. 2010), we also control for firms’ size, profitability, and financial leverage ratios.

Firm size. We measure firm size as the book value of the firm’s total assets [CompuStat item AT]. We log-transform this measure to reduce its skewness.

Firm profitability. We measure a firm’s profitability as the firm’s return on equity (ROE), calculated as Earnings Before Interest and Taxes (Total Assets – Total Liabilities) [CompuStat items EBIT / (AT - LT)].

Firm’s financial leverage. We measure a firm’s financial leverage as the ratio of the firm’s total liability [CompuStat item LT] to total assets [CompuStat item AT].

Corporate Governance Weaknesses. If the overall corporate governance environment of the firm is weak, IT controls could also be negatively influenced (Li et al. 2007). Thus, we control for weaknesses in the firm’s overall corporate governance. We consider the following three areas of corporate governance, including (1) whether a firm has an effective audit committee on its board of directors; (2) whether its top management team sets an appropriate, ethical tone at the top; and (3) whether the firm has an effective internal control function. We count the MW in these three areas, as reported in the Audit Analytics database as the measure of weaknesses in the firm’s overall corporate governance.<sup>14</sup>

Auditor Status. Following prior studies (e.g., Masli et al. 2010), we control for a firm’s auditor status by including a binary variable indicating whether [1] or not [0] the firm is audited by a “Big Four” accounting firm (i.e., KMPG, PricewaterhouseCoopers, Ernst & Young, and Deloitte).

IT Capability of firm. The differences in firms’ IT capabilities could potentially affect the design and operation of their IT controls. Thus, we control for firms’ IT capabilities. Following previous studies (e.g., Bharadwaj 2000), we rely on the annual IW500 rankings published by the Information Week (IW) magazine to measure a firm’s IT capability. A firm’s IT capability in a given year is coded as strong if it is selected into the IW500 list [1 = strong IT capability]; and weak [0 = weak IT capability] otherwise.

Foreign Operations. Firms operating globally face more complex competitive and regulatory environments. Following prior studies (e.g., Ashbaugh-Skaife et al. 2007; Doyle et al. 2007b), we control for firms’ foreign operations. We measure foreign operations by examining whether the firm reports [1 = Yes] foreign currency translation gain/loss [Compustat item FCA] in its firm’s financial statements or not [0 = No] in a given year.

Following prior studies (Masli et al. 2010), we code the presence [1] or absence [0] of restructuring costs [CompuStat item RCP] in a firm’s financial statement to measure whether the firm engages in significant corporate restructuring in that year.

Year Effects. We also include dummy variables to control for unobserved year fixed effects.

We performed a series of diagnostics and robustness checks to investigate the sensitivity of our main results to a few critical decisions. We performed diagnostic analyses for our use of Cox models, and conducted robustness checks based on (1) alternative model specifications; (2) alternative choices and measures of study variables; (3) alternative statistical methods; and (4) alternative sample construction approaches. The robustness tests reported below are based on the primary model reported in Model 3 of Tables 4 and 5 in the main text.

Table C1 and Table C2 present the results of the robustness tests. The results show consistent support for our hypotheses H1, H2, H3, H1a, H2a, and H3a. Based on the other results not tabulated here, we also find similarly consistent support for our hypotheses H1b, H2b, and H3b.

## Diagnostics Analyses for Cox Models

The proportionality of hazards is the key assumption underlying Cox models. We followed the recommendations of Cleves et al. (2010,Chapter 11) and conducted three diagnostic tests on the proportionality assumption based on Model 3 of Table 4 in the main text.

First, we conducted the link test as a general specification test. After estimating our primary Cox model and obtaining the estimated vector of coefficients $\hat { \beta }$ , the link test re-estimates the hazard model by using the linear predictor $X { \hat { \boldsymbol { \beta } } } $ and its squared term $\left( X { \hat { \beta } } \right) ^ { 2 }$ as independent variables. Under the assumption that $X \beta$ is the correct specification, the estimated coefficient of $X { \hat { \boldsymbol { \beta } } } $ should be significant while the estimated coefficient of $\left( X { \hat { \beta } } \right) ^ { 2 }$ should be insignificant, which we confirmed in our diagnostic test (the p-value of the estimated coefficient of $X { \hat { \boldsymbol { \beta } } } $ is less than 0.001; while the p-value of the estimated coefficient of $\left( X { \hat { \beta } } \right) ^ { 2 }$ is 0.46).

The second test is to directly check the proportional-hazard assumption of Cox models by including interactions between all the time-varying independent variables and the logarithm of survival time (Hosmer et al. 2008). Under the proportional-hazard assumption, the estimated coefficient of these interaction items should not be significant. Following the recommendations of Cleves et al. (2010) and Hosmer et al. (2008), we interacted our three primary independent variables with ln(t) and re-estimated the Cox models after including the interaction items. None of the estimated coefficients of the interaction items were statistically significant, thus supporting the use of Cox models.

The third test is to check proportional-hazard assumption based on the analyses of residuals. The specific method we adopted was developed by Therneau and Grambsch (2000); and it was described in Stata by Cleves et al. (2010). “This test is equivalent to testing that the log-hazard ratio function is constant over time” (Cleves et al. 2010, p. 207). It includes both a global test on all the independent variables and tests on individual independent variables. Our results failed to reject the proportionality assumption in both the global test and the tests on any of our three primary independent variables.

We also repeated the second and third tests on all of our control variables. In both tests, the results show that, two of the control variables, Corporate Governance Weaknesses and Auditor Status, might violate the proportional-hazard assumption, while there is no evidence of violation for the other control variables. Violating the proportional-hazard assumption means that the influence of these two variables might not be constant over time. Then, following the suggestion of Cleves et al. (2010), we checked the robustness of our results by explicitly parameterizing such change over time. Specifically, we included two interaction items of the two variables with a function of time, ln(t), in our Cox models. Our main results remained qualitatively the same at the same significance level after including the two interaction items. In addition, we checked the robustness of our results by dropping these two variables completely from the model, and our main results remained qualitatively unchanged at p < 0.10 level.

## Robustness Checks with Cox Models

## Robustness Checks with Regards to Repeated IT MW Event

In our study time window (2004–2009), out of the 317 pubic firms that reported IT MWs (Step 1 of Table B1), 83 (26%) reported IT MWs across multiple years. As reported in the paper, when we estimated our primary Cox models, we removed a firm from the risk set after it reported an IT MW for the first time. Here, we assess the robustness of the findings to not dropping the observations or conducting repeated events analyses with them.

Sometimes, a firm reports the same IT MW persistently across multiple years. Sometimes, a firm reports different types of IT MWs across multiple years (Klamm et al. 2012). Whether we should model them as single event or repeated events depends on whether the IT MW is the same or different across multiple years. To distinguish the two, we collected and read the original disclosure documents (mostly 10-K forms) of the firms that reported IT MWs across multiple years. We found out four different cases:

[Case #1] In 34 firms, the same IT MW description was repeated in all the years in which the firm reported IT MW, suggesting that the same IT MW persisted.

[Case #2] In 23 firms, the original set of IT MWs reported in the first year became partially remediated in the subsequent years; and some of the IT MWs persisted. For example, a firm reported five different IT weaknesses in year 2004; and only three of them in year 2005, suggesting that two of the original weaknesses were remediated while three of them persisted.

[Case #3] In 13 firms, the originally reported IT MW in the first year were not fully remediated, and new IT MW were found in the subsequent years. For example, a firm reported five different IT weaknesses in year 2004; and seven IT weaknesses in 2005 including some of the original five weaknesses in year 2004. Some IT MW were remediated, some IT MW persisted, and new IT MW emerged.

• [Case #4] In 13 firms, the IT MW originally reported in an earlier year were fully remediated, but new IT MW were found in subsequent years, suggesting that the firm reported different ITMW across multiple years.

In Case #1 \~ #3 (i.e., in 70 of the 83 firms, or 84.3%), at least some of the same IT control weaknesses persisted across multiple years. For these cases, it is more appropriate to treat the IT MW events as a single event that persisted rather than different events, because the reporting in subsequent years does not suggest that a new event happens to the firm in these categories. Thus, we decided to drop the subsequent obser vations after a firm reported an IT MW for the first time.

Only 13 of the firms (Case#4) qualify as truly repeated events (i.e., different IT MW events occur across different years). Even in this subgroup, we cannot rule out the possibility that an IT MW in a previous year triggered a new IT MW in the subsequent year. Two consecutive events may not be entirely independent. Because of this possibility, and due to lack of sufficient sample size (n = 13), it was infeasible to perform repeated event analyses. Thus, we decided to drop these 13 observations from the analysis. We also performed the following robustness checks.

[Robustness Check #1] We treated the 13 firms in Case #4 the same as the firms in Case #1 \~#3. That is, we kept the first IT MW event and dropped the subsequent ones. The results in Model 1 of Table C1 and Table C2 indicate that the conclusions of our primary mode remain qualitatively unchanged in this robustness check.

[Robustness Check #2] As a more conservative approach, we dropped all the observations of all 83 firms discussed above. The results in Model 2 of Table C1 and Table C2 indicate that all of the conclusions from our primary model remain qualitatively unchanged.

## Robustness Checks with Other Statistical Methods

## GEE Models

We considered panel data models as alternative statistical methods to check the robustness of our results. In selecting the most appropriate panel data model, we need to address the repeated events issues as discussed above. IT MWs reported in different years are most likely the same, persistent weaknesses instead of new ones. Thus, when organizing our data as a cross-sectional, time-series data set, there would be high seria correlations among observations from the same subject across years. We first ran the serial correlation test, as discussed by Wooldridge (2010) and implemented by Drukker (2003). The test result confirmed the existence of significant serial correlation in our data set (F-value = 39.726, p-value < .001).

We then chose to use Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) models, which allow us to directly model the possible serial correlation among the outcomes from the same subject in multiple years. GEE is a specific type of generalized linear models that apply to longitudinal data analyses (Hardin and Hilbe 2003). It utilizes both cross-sectional and time-series information of a panel data and directly accounts for the correlation structure of observations from the same subject by estimating a parameterized correlation matrix (Zeger and Liang 1986).

In specifying the GEE models, three key parameters need to be specified: (1) the distribution of the dependent variable; (2) the link function that expresses the dependent variable as a function of independent variables; and (3) the correlation structure of observations from the same subject. Because our dependent variable is binary (reporting IT MW or not), we specified its distribution as the Bernouli distribution, and the link function as the Logit function. We selected correlation structure by following the GEE model selection method developed by Pan (2001), who developed a QIC (Quasilikelihood under the Independence model Criterion) statistic that has a lower score when the model specification better fits data. With our dataset, the GEE model with an independent correlation structure had the lowest QIC score, thus preferred (Cui 2007) We then chose it when specifying our GEE model.

[Robustness Check #7] We reported the results based on the GEE model in Model 7 of Table C1 and Table C2. Our primary results remain robust when using this statistical method.

## Rare-Event Logit Models

In our sample, 1.53% observations reported IT MW. We noticed that previous studies have used Cox or other survival analyses models for events with similar or lower occurrence rates (e.g., Joseph et al. 2015; Kwon and Johnson 2014), and the absolute number of observations with IT MW (163 in the final sample) is conventionally considered sufficient. These two considerations reduce the concerns associated with the rareness of the IT MW reporting in our study. Nevertheless, we performed rare-event analyses to check the robustness of our results.

Rare-event logit models were developed in response to the concerns that estimated coefficients could be biased in regular logit models when the binary dependent variable is severely lopsided. Rare-event logit models can correct the potential biases associated with extremely lopsided binary dependent variables (King and Zeng 2001).

[Robustness Check #8] To perform rare-event logit analyses, we pooled together all the observations in which a given firm reported IT MW for the first time and all the other observations from firms that had not reported IT MW in our study period. We then ran a rare-event logi model. The results are presented in Model 8 of Table C1 and Table C2. In summary, at the significance level of .05, our primary results remain robust after accounting for the relative rarity of ITMWs in our sample.

## Heckman Two-Step Procedure

Strategic choices could be endogenous in estimating firm performance, as firms often self-select into certain choices with the expectation of certain performance outcomes (Hamilton and Nickerson 2003; Shaver 1998). In our context, it is possible that certain unobserved factors can drive both a firm’s M&A or divestiture decisions and its internal control effectiveness. Missing these unobserved factors in our estimation models, we might draw biased conclusions due to the unaddressed endogeneity problem.

Following relevant methodological guidance (e.g., Bascle 2008; Shaver 1998) and previous studies with similar research designs (e.g., Guajardo et al. 2012; Rao et al. 2001), we applied Heckman two-step procedure in combination with Cox models to account for the potential endogeneit problem. At the first step of the Heckman procedure, we used two selection equations, in which we estimated the likelihoods of a firm engaging in M&A and divestiture activities in a given year. Based on the results of this step, we calculated two Heckman correction factors as defined by Shaver (1998). Then, in the second step, we included the correction factors in our outcome equation (i.e., our primary Cox models with ITMWs as dependent variables).<sup>15</sup>

The Selection Equation. At the first step of the Heckman procedure, we made the following decisions in specifying the selection equations. First, while our original M&A (divestiture) measure is a continuous variable, for the selection equations, we converted it into a binary variable that takes on the value of [1] if a firm made at least one M&A (divestiture) transaction in a given year; or [0] otherwise. This binary variable served as the dependent variable in our first-step selection equation, as specified below.

Second, it is critical to have “exclusion restrictions” in the selection equation (Bascle 2008), that is, there should be variables that explain a firm’s M&A (or divestiture) decision but that not related to the firm’s internal control environment or ITMWs. For the selection equation, we adopted the M&A (divestiture) wave in the same industry sector as the focal firm to explain the firm’s likelihood of doing M&A (divestiture) transaction. Previous studies have shown that M&A typically happen in waves. A given firm’s decision to engage in M&A is often influenced by the M&A activities of the same-industry peers of the firm (McNamara et al. 2008). Similar bandwagon effects also exist in divestiture decisions of firms (Brauer and Wiersema 2012). However, there is no empirical evidence or plausible explanation to date that peer firms’ M&A (divestiture) decisions would directly impact the focal firm’s internal control environment or ITMWs.

Based on our review of the extant M&A and divestiture literatures (e.g., Haleblian et al. 2009; Kolev 2015), we also include five other environment-level and firm-level characteristics in the selection equation. Our final selection equations, for M&A and divestiture are specified as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Probit } (M A _ {i t}) & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} M A W a v e _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} E n v T u r b u l e n c e _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} E n v M u n i f i c e n c e _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {4} S i z e _ {i t} + \beta_ {5} P r o f i t a b i l i t y _ {i t} + \beta_ {6} L e v e r a g e _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Probit } \left(\text { Divestiture } _ {i t}\right) & = \gamma_ {=} + \gamma_ {1} \text { DivestitureWave } _ {i t} + \gamma_ {2} \text { EnvTurbulence } _ {i t} + \gamma_ {3} \text { EnvMunificence } _ {i t} \\ & + \gamma_ {4} \text { Size } _ {i t} + \gamma_ {5} \text { Profitability } _ {i t} + \gamma_ {6} \text { Leverage } _ {i t} + \mu_ {i t} \end{array}
$$

## Robustness Checks with Different Sample Construction Approaches

## Matched Treatment-Control Sample

In addition to estimate our models with full sample, we also test the robustness of our results by constructing a matched treatment-control sample. This sampling strategy can help us further address the potential issues associated with both the rarity of events (King and Zeng 2001) and with endogeneity (Reeb et al. 2012).

In particular, instead of using all the U.S. public firms as our sample, we first constructed a treatment group by including the observations in which IT MW were first reported by a given firm. Then, we constructed a control group by matching each observation in the treatment group to observations that are not in this group based on a selected set of variables as matching criteria, including (1) firm size (total asset), (2) profitability (return on equity), (3) financial leverage ratio, (4) environmental IT intensity, (5) industry sector, and (6) year. By matching, the influence of the four matching criteria variables and many other unobserved factors that are highly correlated with them can be controlled in the sample construction process.

The ideal matching technique would find exact matches for each observation in the treatment group to construct the control group. However, because the first four matching variables are continuous, it would be impossible to find exact matches based on them. Instead, we employed the Coarsened Exact Matching (CEM) procedure recently developed by Iacus et al. (2012). The CEM procedure first “coarsened” the values of a matching criteria variable of the observations in the treatment group into strata. Then, observations not in the treatment group are considered a match if their values of the same matching criteria variable fall into the same strata. Among the six matching criterion variables, we specified four variables, including firm size, return on equity, financial leverage ratio, and environmental IT intensity, for coarsened matching and two additional variables, industry sector and year, for exact matching. We also specified the use of the Sturges binning algorithm to automatically detect the cut-points to stratify the data instead of arbitrarily deciding on the cut-points by ourselves (Blackwell et al. 2009).

[Robustness Check #10] The CEM matching procedure a sample of 168 observations in the treatment group that were matched to 6,718 observations in the control group. When analyzing this matched sample, as recommended by Iacus et al. (2012), we need to weigh the observations based on the sizes of different strata. We used the Stata procedure developed by Blackwell et al. (2009) to run a weighted logistic regression with the same set of independent variables to predict whether a firm reports IT MW. The results are presented in Model 10 of Table C1 and Table C2. Our primary results remain robust to this sampling approach at the .1 significance level.

## Reverse Causality Analyses

Another potential concern for our results is reverse causality: could IT MWs be driving corporate strategy changes? To address this concern, our primary research design introduced time lag structures between our dependent (t) and independent variables (t-1). We also focused only on the first occurrence of IT MWs in a given firm and dropped subsequent observations of the firm from the sample so that, in the event that IT MWs lead to subsequent corporate strategy changes, such observations would not affect our findings.

In this section, we further explore the possibility of reverse causality. Specifically, we use ITMW in year (t-1) as an antecedent of diversification, M&A, and divestiture levels in year (t). We estimate three sets of models respectively, as reported in Panels A, B, and C of Table C3. In each panel, Model 1 uses the presence or absence of IT MW in year (t-1) as the primary independent variable and keeps the same set of control variables as in the primary models in the paper. Model 2 adds the firm’s diversification, M&A, and divestiture levels in year (t-1) as additional independent variables. Model 3 replaces IT MW in year (t-1) with the three subcategories of IT MW: technology IT MW, behavior IT MW, and process IT MW. We estimate all models as fixed-effect panel data models, with robust standard errors.

As reported in Table C3, the results do not provide any evidence that IT MW in year t-1, or any subcategories of IT MW (technology, behavior, or process) has any significant effects on the subsequent diversification, M&A, or divestiture decisions of firms in year t. The estimated coefficients of IT MW, or the three subcategories of IT MW, are all statistically insignificant (p-value > 0.10) in all nine models of Table C3. These results do not show any evidence of reverse causality.

## Comparison with Previous Accounting Studies

For the comparability of our results with those of the prior studies, we check the robustness of the results to different measures of diversification, related diversification, and unrelated diversification. As reviewed in Table 1 of the main text, previous studies on internal control (IC) MW and IT MW control for the diversification level of a firm. They measure diversification with the count of firm’s business segments. The count measure treats all business segments of the firm equally regardless of their relative size or importance in the firm’s business portfolio. In addition, it does not distinguish between the strategic relatedness or unrelatedness of the business segments. To address these issues, we used Palepu’s (1985) entropy-based measures of total diversification (DT), related diversification (DR), and unrelated diversification (DU) in our main analyses. In this Appendix, we assess if the results are robust to the use of count-based measures of diversification, related diversification, and unrelated diversification. We conduct the robustness analyses by replacing DT with the count of business segments; DR with the count of related business segments; and DU with the count of unrelated business segments; and re-running the models in Tables 4 and 5 of the paper. We present the results in Tables D1 and D2.

Following previous studies (e.g., Ashbaugh-Skaife et al. 2007; Doyle et al. 2007b; Ge and McVay 2005), we measure the overall diversification level of a firm by simply counting the number of its business segments as reported in the CompuStat Segments database. The previous studies did not break up this count measure into its related and unrelated diversification components. We count the related and unrelated business segments as follows. We take the firm’s primary industry sector (defined based on the top level NACIS code reported in Compustat) as the reference point, and compare if a given business segment of the firm shares the same industry sector with it (related); or not (unrelated). We do so for all business segments of the firm. Then, we count the number of related business segments and the number of unrelated business segments.

Comparing the results in Table D1 and D2 with the results in Table 4 and Table 5 of the main text indicates that the results remain robust to the use of count-based measures.

• Diversification (the count of business segments) increases the hazard of IT MW (â = .15, p-value < .05, Model 3 of Table D1).

Related diversification (the count of related business segments) increases the hazard of IT MW (â = .19, p-value < .01, Model 3 of Table D2).

• Unrelated diversification (the count of unrelated business segments) does not affect IT MW (â = .02, p-value > .1, Model 3 of Table D2).

Collectively, these robustness results indicate that the proposed theory is supported with the count-based measures of diversification, related diversification, and unrelated diversification as well. The novelty of the findings of this study is not due to the choice of the measures per se. Instead, the novelty is due to our complexity science-based theory. Related diversification affects a firm’s complexity by changing the degree of interrelatedness of the firm’s business units. Interrelated businesses require cross-business IT integration to share resources. This also creates need for new IT controls at the integration and interaction points. Meeting the IT control needs of an interdependent set of stakeholders from multiple businesses is very challenging. Thus, the firm is at higher risk of experiencing IT MW in related diversification. In contrast, unrelated diversification only increases a firm’s complicatedness by adding an unrelated business to the firm. It becomes harder for the firm to meet the IT support needs and institute IT controls of the new business. However, due to the lack of interdependencies with other business units, it is feasible to meet IT control needs of the new business without causing disruptions to overall IT controls of the firm.
