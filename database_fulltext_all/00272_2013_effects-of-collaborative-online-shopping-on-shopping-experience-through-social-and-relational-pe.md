# Effects of collaborative online shopping on shopping experience through social and relational perspectives

Hongki Kim <sup>a</sup>, Kil-Soo Suh <sup>b,</sup>\*, Un-Kon Lee <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia, 2053 Main Mall, Vancouver, BC, Canada

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 30 January 2012 Received in revised form 5 February 2013 Accepted 14 February 2013 Available online 24 February 2013

Keywords: eCommerce Collaborative online shopping Co-presence Shopping enjoyment Flow Embodiment Media richness

## A B S T R A C T

Collaborative online shopping refers to an activity in which a consumer shops at an eCommerce website with remotely located shopping partners such as friends or family. Although collaborative online shopping has increased with the pervasiveness of social networking, few studies have examined how to enhance this type of shopping experience. This study examines two potential design components, embodiment and media richness, that could enhance shoppers’ experiences. Based on theories of copresence and flow, we examined whether the implementation of these two features could increase co presence, flow, and the intention to use a collaborative online shopping website.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid development and pervasiveness of information technology have changed many business practices. Amazon (http://www.amazon.com/) and similar eCommerce sites, where customers buy products and pay for them on the Web, have grown rapidly and become a critical channel for reaching consumers. According to the U.S. Department of Commerce, U.S. eCommerce sales totalled \$193.8 billion in 2011, up 15.2% from 2010 and 69.2% from 2006, and accounted for 4.7% of the total retail volume in 2011 [78]. Hence, eCommerce sites, along with traditional brickand-mortar shops, have received significant attention from academic and business communities.

In an eCommerce context, many studies have focused on the impact of the design components of a website on the shopping experience and consumer behavior regarding personal, individual perspectives, as exemplified by the emphasis on building consumer trust and providing personalized services [25,36,59]. A shopping experience, however, can be influenced by social, relational, and individual perspectives [20,30,63,70,77,78]. For example, consumers can share their opinions about products, solicit various opinions from their friends, family members, and other customers, and have fun through interactions with others with similar interests [61,77]. Moreover, consumers spend more time shopping and purchase more products when shopping with family or friends [70]. In particular, Evans et al. [20] showed shopping behavior as a part of the social process of being together with family or friends and discussed the need to approach shopping experiences and consumer behavior from social and relational perspectives.

Given this context, the need for an approach based on social and relational perspectives is gradually gaining headway in the context of eCommerce. Ninety percent of the consumers who use eCommerce websites prefer contact with a human being rather than an automated mechanical entity [30]. In addition, online consumers value social relationships and enjoy communicating with others. Rayport and Jaworski [63] assert that a means of communication with other consumers is an essential element in the success of an eCommerce website and contend that social and relational perspectives are just as important online as they are offline.

In accordance with these academic points of view, a business model called social shopping is emerging. This business model is a subtype of eCommerce that uses social media to support and enhance social interaction between customers [47,58]. Many traditional eCommerce websites have tried to integrate this emerging business model into their operations. For example, eBay (http://www.ebay.com/), one of the major websites in eCommerce, has launched a function called ‘‘Help Me Shop,’’ which helps customers share their opinion of a product with their social network and solicit the opinions of its members. Another site, Groupon (http://www.groupon.com/), has purchased a social shopping platform, the Mertado, which helps subscribers create their own community and find and access offers or suggestions that correspond with their personalized requirements. In addition, traditional social network service websites such as Google Plus (plus.google.com) and Facebook (http://www.facebook.com/) are also trying to encourage users to interact within their social network when they buy a product. Thus, suggestions about the potential design components of these eCommerce sites that could influence social and relational perspectives could have theoretical and practical implications.

To the best of our knowledge, however, only a few studies have investigated which design components could influence the social and relational perspectives of online shopping. Furthermore, only very few studies have suggested possible design components to achieve these perspectives in eCommerce [87,88]. For instance, Durlach and Slater [18] and Zhao [86] have discussed the need for avatars and a communication channel such that users can become aware of each other and interact more realistically. In accordance with these studies, there has been an increased interest in avatars for eCommerce in academic and business communities [75].<sup>1</sup>

Hence, this study empirically investigated whether embodiment and media richness affect the social and relational perspectives of a shopping experience within the context of collaborative online shopping. Collaborative online shopping refers to an activity in which a consumer shops at an eCommerce website with remotely located shopping partners such as friends or family. Particularly, we examined the widely overlooked effect of co-presence on such a shopping experience and verified the process. Furthermore, by proposing and verifying two possible design components, we determined which tools are necessary to increase social and relational perspectives in a collaborative online shopping context.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses

Social and relational perspectives are based on relationships with others. To increase their interaction with others, people must perceive that they and others are present together in the same space [31]. Therefore, this study introduced the concept of copresence as a social, relational perspective that signifies mutual awareness on a collaborative online shopping website. Furthermore, the creation of hedonic value through a social, relational perspective is an important factor that produces flow [77,84], and flow is a factor that directly affects a consumer’s shopping experience [33]. Hence, this study, with a focus on co-presence and flow, proposed the research model shown in Fig. 1.

## 2.1. Social shopping

Social shopping is a subtype of eCommerce based on social, relational interaction between human relationships on the Web [47,58]. In short, social shopping is a form of eCommerce based on social relationships in which friends influence each other’s shopping experiences and purchasing behavior on the Web [44,46]. Social shopping uses technologies to mine social interactions found in malls and physical stores. For example, eBay (http:// www.ebay.com/) and Groupon (http://www.groupon.com/) help customers share their opinions of products and find offers or suggestions matched to their personalized needs in their social network. Similarly, collaborative online shopping can be considered a type of social shopping, which is defined as the activity in which a consumer shops at an eCommerce website with remotely located shopping partners such as friends or family.

The practical aspects of online shopping have drawn increased interest within academia regarding how to implement an eCommerce website that would incorporate interactions between consumers. Then, if such a website were implemented, how would it affect the interactions of shoppers using it and influence their shopping behavior. For example, numerous studies in computer science on implementing collaborative spaces across networks have been conducted [62]. Previous studies proposed certain prototypes in which users navigate together through virtual contexts and perform collaborative shopping with intelligent software agents [23,62,68]. Ye et al. [85] proposed an EasyMall system of collaborative online shopping based on intelligent agents in a virtual environment that not only imitated real interaction among customers attracted to the same products but also supported communication between multiple avatars in a 3D virtual environment.

In addition to implementation issues, interest has grown in studying the effects of collaborative online shopping on consumers’ shopping experiences and behavior. Zhu et al. [88] investigated the impacts of shared navigation and voice chat on the loss of coordination with a shopping partner in a collaborative online shopping context. Their study showed that two design components significantly enhanced the collaborative online shoppers’ performance. Notably, shared navigation was more useful with text chat than with voice chat. Similar to these previous studies, this study proposed two possible design components, embodiment and media richness, and investigated their impacts on the shopping experience through co-presence as a social, relational perspective.

## 2.2. Co-presence

Biocca et al. [3] reviewed studies that lacked consistency with regard to social presence and subsequently reconstructed and defined social presence in terms of co-presence, psychological involvement, and behavioral engagement. Co-presence refers to the degree of recognition of the presence of other users; psychological involvement refers to an observer’s understanding of the intention or thinking of another person by focusing on the other person’s emotional state; and behavioral engagement refers to the degree of perceived interdependency, connection, and responsiveness of the other person to the observer’s actions. In particular, Biocca et al. [3] contended that co-presence is the most fundamental of the three factors in creating social presence. Hence, the formation of co-presence in an online context is essential.

Even before Biocca et al. [3] established it as one aspect of social presence, co-presence, an essential factor required for the formation of social presence, had been applied in many studies. Prior studies defined co-presence as the level of sensually perceiving other individuals [28], believing that the other individual is in the same space [48,50,66], and perceiving through interaction that the perceived other is present together with the observer [17,29,31,32]. These perspectives have been applied in various studies, particularly in studies on the effects of information and communication technology [4,5,29,48,50,55–57,59,66].

Based on these studies, if co-presence can be formed in a collaborative online shopping website, we expect that the website could establish a certain degree of a social, relational shopping experience. That is, if a design component is created that allows a shopper to become fully aware of the presence of his or her shopping partner, the website can be effective (but maybe not to the same degree as brick-and-mortar malls) in establishing copresence and improving the social, relational shopping experience.

## 2.3. Embodiment and media richness

To investigate potential design components that are capable of increasing co-presence on a collaborative online shopping website, this study hypothesized that co-presence would be created if users perceive and identify each other’s presence through embodied avatars and simultaneously have access to and use of a rich communication medium.

Based on studies [27,40,67] in sociology, psychology, and social psychology, the concept of embodiment has been developed and emphasized as the means of providing users with the perception and identification of the presence of others in the same space [18,86]. Embodiment refers to the supplying of an avatar as a user’s alter ego in an online environment [86]; an avatar is defined as a visual image formed through computer technology that allows a user to form an alter ego and to identify herself or himself in an online environment [34,75]. Embodiment in an online environment creates a sense not only of the person’s own presence but also of the presence of others, as in the real world, by transmitting visual information to the other parties [1]. As a result, a degree of reality can be formed in an online environment, and co-presence with other users can be created [43,86].

This study used the concept of embodiment, defined as the use of an avatar that reflects its user in a collaborative online shopping website, as a factor to increase co-presence. Embodiment is expected to create a sense of co-presence by enabling users to express themselves online just as they do in reality, to perceive and recognize one another’s presence, and to share their environment. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H1. A shopper will perceive a higher degree of presence of a shopping partner if a collaborative online shopping website provides embodied avatars.

Durlach and Slater [18] also contended that a high degree of interaction between users is possible if an online environment has a rich, diverse, and real-time means of communication and that this interaction can create co-presence. Such a high degree of interaction is considered an important element of media richness theory, which verifies the features and effects of the means of communication; a high degree of media richness has been suggested to be an essential condition for efficient communication [15]. According to media richness theory [15], media richness increases according to the level of feedback, number of multiple cues, language variability, and personal focus that the communication medium has. A high feedback level was identified as a particularly important element in creating rich communication [74]. In addition, numerous studies of how media-rich communication influences co-presence demonstrated that co-presence increased as efficient interaction became available through an increase in media richness and consequently produced changes in individual behavior [6,39,64,69,71,73, 82]. Hence, media richness is considered an important element that affects co-presence.

Based on these studies, we expected that media richness would influence the degree of co-presence. That is, as the richness of the communication medium increases, more efficient communication will be accessible with each user’s presence clearly perceived, consequently resulting in an increase in co-presence. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2. A shopper will perceive a higher degree of presence of a shopping partner if a collaborative online shopping website provides its shopper with a richer communication channel, such as voice chat, than with a leaner communication channel such as text chat.

## 2.4. Shopping enjoyment

Although few studies have directly and empirically verified the impact of co-presence on consumers’ shopping experiences and behavior in a collaborative online shopping website, several studies have found that shopping with other people increases the enjoyment of shopping.

Tauber [77] classified motives for shopping into two categories, motives that are personal and individual and motives that are social and relational, asserting that shopping is not just a process of simply purchasing needed goods but a series of processes that allow a consumer to build relationships, create a social experience, and take pleasure in the process. Tauber [77] found that when someone shops with others, they communicate about the same areas of interest, assess goods better, and strengthen social relationships. From this perspective, Evans et al. [20] considered the process of shopping with a close friend or family member to be part of building a social relationship. Sommer et al. [70] asserted that because of the social value gained from shopping with another person, a consumer spends more time and buys more goods when shopping with others than when shopping alone. In addition, Woodruffe-Burton et al. [84] concluded that when shopping with another person, one performs one’s role within a social relationship and consequently experiences enjoyment.

The need for social and relational perspectives on online shopping behavior is also becoming recognized in studies on eCommerce websites.<sup>2</sup> In emphasizing the need for social and relational perspectives in an eCommerce context, Wolfinbarger and Gilly [83] found that online shoppers seeking hedonic value and engaging in experiential shopping value their social relationships and the hedonic experiences that they gain from such interactions. Furthermore, Rayport and Jaworski [63] concluded that the availability of a communication channel is essential to the success of an online shopping website, asserting that a systematic device that enables the formation of social relationships must be available, just as in reality.

In this study, we expected that higher levels of presence in collaborative online shopping would translate into a feeling by consumers that their shopping partners are psychologically closer and that their communication is warmer and friendlier; as a result, consumers would enjoy their collaborative shopping more [87]. Thus, the co-presence formed through a high degree of embodiment and a rich communication channel is expected to satisfy social needs and create hedonic value such as shopping enjoyment. This occurs by making it possible to increase interaction with intimate relationships and reinforce these relationships. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H3. If a shopper perceives a higher degree of presence with a shopping partner, feelings of shopping enjoyment at the collaborative online shopping website will be increased.

## 2.5. Flow

Flow has been used as an important factor in research on human–computer interaction and consumer behavior [9,13,14,26,33,54,79,81]. Through flow, various approaches have focused on topics such as online shopping website loyalty and motives for using an online shopping website [49]. Identification of the concepts of flow [45] has followed two streams of research. One stream captures flow as media experiences and reactions, and the other stream captures flow as a state of immersion. According to the portrait of media experience, flow captures the overall emotional and cognitive components through three dimensions: intrinsic enjoyment, perceived control, and concentration/attention focus [41].

Another stream defined flow as ‘‘the holistic sensation that people feel when they act with total involvement’’ [8] (p. 36). In a flow state, people ‘‘shift into a common mode of experience when they become absorbed in their activity. This mode is characterized by a narrowing of the focus of awareness, so that irrelevant perceptions and thoughts are filtered out, by loss of self-consciousness, by a responsiveness to clear goals and unambiguous feedback, and by a sense of control over the environment’’ [10] (p. 72). In this manner, Csikszentmihalyi [11] argued ‘‘when we are in flow, we do not usually feel happy, because we feel only what is relevant to the activity’’ (p. 11). From this perspective, flow has been conceptualized as a state of immersion, which is created through an experience of telepresence and intrinsic enjoyment [51].

Based on the second stream of studies of flow [8–10,12,51], this study conceptualized flow as a state of immersion to capture details of the shopping experience and investigate the interplay between shopping enjoyment and flow on a collaborative online shopping website.

Hoffman and Novak [33] suggested a conceptual framework of flow that proposed antecedents and impacts of flow in a computermediated environment. The authors argued that hedonic value as intrinsic motivation is one of the critical process characteristics influencing flow because it could create intrinsic self-relevance related to comprehension effort and attention [9,33]. In accord with their conceptual framework [33], hedonic value appears related to shopping enjoyment on the collaborative online shopping website. This apparent relationship exists because shopping pleasure based on formation and reinforcement of corelationships is one of hedonic value as an intrinsic motivation with exploratory characteristics. Numerous studies on consumer behavior in an eCommerce context have verified that when hedonic value is formed at a high level, flow, in terms of shopping, increased as suggested in flow theory [76]. Hence, we expect that shopping enjoyment formed through co-presence will produce a state of flow by focusing attention on the shopping and the goods. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H4. If a shopper feels a higher level of shopping enjoyment at a collaborative online shopping website, the shopper’s state in flow in terms of increased immersion and concentration on collaborative online shopping will be increased.

According to studies on human–computer interaction, high levels of intrinsic enjoyment and pleasure in computer-mediated environments are important factors that produce a positive attitude, increasing a user’s intention to continuously perform a task [9,72,80]. Furthermore, Gardner et al. [24] verified that a close relationship exists between a user’s attitude toward a system and his or her intention to use it. A positive subjective experience is considered a particularly important factor in influencing consumer behavior; this perspective is similar to the importance attached to a user’s attitude toward a task that forms a key variable in rational behavior theory and planned behavior theory [33].

Hence, it is expected that shopping enjoyment will increase the intention to use a collaborative online shopping website by positively affecting a user’s attitude and his or her perceived behavioral control, two elements of the planned behavior theory. Shopping enjoyment will also positively affect exploratory behavior and thus increase overall usage of the collaborative online shopping website. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H5. If a shopper feels a higher level of shopping enjoyment at the collaborative online shopping website, her or his intention to use the website will be increased.

In addition, a consumer in a state of flow is able to focus more on a given task, grasp the information related to the task, and remember it for a considerable length of time. In a number of studies on factors affecting heightened consumer learning ability [80,81], Hoffman and Novak [33] expected that as interactivity increases, a consumer explores and evaluates information more dynamically and quickly, consequently improving the degree of flow and thereby increasing learning ability. In goal-directed tasks in which extrinsic motivation exerts the prime influence, a consumer makes decisions based on information obtained through this improved learning ability. However, in exploratory tasks ruled largely by intrinsic motivation, a consumer arrives at decisions based on external information obtained from memory or word of mouth [33]. That is, as the degree of flow increases, consumers’ learning ability also increases. Hence, on a collaborative online shopping website, as the degree of flow increases, a consumer is able to evaluate products more accurately. This increase in learning ability signifies the degree to which a product’s performance or quality can be accurately evaluated through a website [38]. Jiang and Benbasat [37,38] empirically verified that a consumer is able to accurately judge a product because of increased learning ability; the authors concluded that the increased learning ability forms a positive attitude toward both the product and the website selling the product and increases as a consequence of a consumer’s intention to use the website. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H6. If a shopper is in a high state of flow of increased immersion and concentration on a collaborative online shopping website, his or her intention to use the collaborative online shopping website will be increased.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Study setting

Few commercial collaborative online shopping websites are in operation. Therefore, our investigation of whether embodiment and media richness influence a shopper’s intention to use such a website required that we develop our own websites to represent the range of variances possible with sites of varying levels of embodiment and media richness. The proposed research model was tested in a survey involving websites that used different design components: (1) text chatting without avatar support, (2) text chatting with avatar support, (3) voice chatting without avatar support, and (4) voice chatting with avatar support.

These design components were expected to yield varying degrees of embodiment and media richness. We expected that avatar support would cause variances in embodiment because subjects could identify with one another through the use of an avatar [86]. Therefore, each avatar was developed to reflect each subject’s face, height, and weight. That is, by displaying each avatar in the same space, a pair of subjects could identify each other’s avatar. We also expected voice and text chatting to each have a different degree of media richness because media richness depends on how well a communication channel conveys diverse contextual cues and how well the communicators deliver feedback to each other [74]. That is, vocal communication media could furnish verbal cues via tone, volume, and speed of speaking and provide a high level of feedback. However, text-only communication delivers only textual cues, which are incapable of conveying the various connotations of language and consequently represents a low level of feedback [27]. Subjects with voice chat were able to communicate verbally using headsets and microphones through Skype version 3.8.30.180, a third-party tool; however, subjects with text chat could only use a keyboard through Skype. Despite differences across the design components, the four conditions presented equivalent product information.

In the experiment, subjects were able to see their avatar and were allowed to zoom in, zoom out, and rotate to accurately identify the clothes that they selected. Communication and discussion about the product were permitted between the subjects. Subjects were able to see others’ avatars try on different clothes in the same space in real time as if they were in the same offline store. We provided a jacket and cotton pants because these articles of clothing have comparably few preferential differences and could be used by both genders. We displayed five different sizes of jumpers and cotton pants without any information related to their brand to eliminate brand effects. Subjects were able to select the item of their choice just as if they were in an offline store. Appendix A contains examples of what subjects saw on the experimental website.

To enhance the external validity of the results, the experimental websites were developed by professional Web designers from a commercial Web design firm. In addition, to enhance mundane realism and eliminate any potential extraneous effects in the experiment, this study selected apparel products that are sold by a real eCommerce website. We used apparel products for two reasons. First, because apparel products are experiential goods that are difficult to evaluate independently without prior experience, people tend to rely on evaluations from others, such as their friends or family members [16,52,53]. Second, because people express their personal image through their apparel, they care what others in their network think about what they wear and make their selections with this in mind. Hence, we believe that apparel fits the research objective.

The collaborative online shopping website was saved on a commercial Web server that participants accessed through the Internet with a T1 connection. IBM 1.86 GHz Pentium PCs with 2 gigabytes DDR SDRAM, the GeForce 7300 graphic cards, and 19- inch color LCD monitors were used for the experiment. Participants navigated the collaborative online website using Microsoft Internet Explorer (Version 7.0). With this experimental setting, retrieval of information and synchronous interaction between subjects were realistic.

## 3.2. Data collection procedures

To conduct the experiment, we recruited 47 undergraduate and graduate students from two large private universities who had experience in purchasing a product on the Web and asked them to invite into the experiment one of their friends (1) with whom they had bought a piece of apparel together in an offline store, (2) who had online shopping experience, (3) and who was of the same gender. In all, 94 students volunteered for the experiment. Among the participants, 14 participated in a pretest, and 80 (female, 32; male, 48) participated in the main experiment. Because most collaborative shopping occurs among close friends and family, we paired an invitee with the person extending the invitation to create 40 teams (2 subjects - 40 teams = 80 total subjects). To encourage more genuine participation, we paid each person \$30. Each pair was randomly assigned to one of four conditions to eliminate any potential extraneous effects.

To prepare for the main experiment, two operations (physical data collection and avatar creation) took place. All participants were photographed, and each subject’s height and weight was recorded. Then, each subject’s avatar was created, and the main experiment was performed one week later. Prior to the main experiment, a research assistant explained the task, procedure, and system operation to each pair of subjects. Then, each team member entered separate rooms to establish internal and external validity. Subsequently, each subject turned on his or her computer and went to the experimental website just as he or she would in shopping normally online. On the website, each subject was able to see apparel with or without his or her avatar according to the condition assigned to that specific team of shoppers. Meanwhile, subjects were allowed to share their opinions about the items in terms such as how a certain item looked and matched with the shopper. Subjects were asked to select, after thorough discussion, a piece of apparel for purchase. Following the selection, they completed a survey. All measurement items used a 7-point Likert scale (see Table 1). After finishing the questionnaires, subjects were paid for their participation, thus ending the experiment.

## 4. Analysis and results

## 4.1. Subject Information

The participants were recruited from six academic disciplines in two large private universities. Forty-eight (60%) were male, and thirty-two (40%) were female undergraduate students. The participants’ average age was 23.6, and they had averaged 2.5 offline collaborative shopping trips with a paired partner in the past year. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) further revealed no significant differences between the conditions in terms of age, number of years at the university, and the number of offline collaborative shopping trips with a paired shopping partner. In addition, none of the participants had ever purchased apparel together in a collaborative online shopping situation.

## 4.2. Examination of variation of embodiment and media richness

We were able to incorporate moderate amounts of variation of embodiment and media richness into the experimental websites by implementing two design components. Based on the scores in the survey from 80 subjects, the embodiment measures ranged from 1 to 7, with a mean of 3.4 and a variance of 1.9, and the media richness scores ranged from 1 to 7, with a mean of 5.61 and a variance of 1.1. ANOVA tests were conducted to examine whether the two design components produced different levels of embodiment and media richness across the experimental conditions. The results show that the embodiment (p < 0.01) and media richness (p < 0.01) differed statistically across the four conditions specified in the experiment. Post hoc analysis using the Sheffe tests in Table 2 shows two levels of perceptions of embodiment and media richness. Participants in voice and text chatting without avatar support perceived a low level of embodiment; however, participants in voice and text chatting with avatar support perceived a high level of embodiment. These results indicate that avatar support affects perceived embodiment without any interaction with the chatting types. The results also show that participants in voice chatting with or without avatars perceived a high level of media richness, whereas participants in text chatting with or without avatars perceived a low level of media richness. These results also indicate that the type of communication channel matters in terms of perceived media richness without reference to whether an avatar is involved. These results of the manipulation checks support our underlying assumption of the independence of embodiment and media.

## 4.3. Model testing

To analyze the research model, we selected the PLS (partial least squares) method as a type of structural equation modeling. The PLS method is mostly used when the sample size is relatively small or when explanatory research is conducted [7,21]. In this research, SmartPLS Version 2.0 [65] was used. The PLS model consists of a measurement model that measures the reliability and validity of variables and a structural model that validates the tested hypotheses.

## 4.3.1. Measurement model

We estimated the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the variables to confirm the validity of the variables. Table 3 shows the statistical results and composite reliability of the variables. As a result, we concluded that all the variables were reliable because all the composite reliability values exceeded 0.7.

Convergent reliability refers to the relatedness of variables that are theoretically correlated. This verification was arrived at through testing the reliability of each survey item, the composite reliability of each variable, and the average variance extracted (AVE) value [2,35]. The reliability of each item was assessed by examining the loadings of the measurement items on their corresponding constructs, and all item loadings should be significant and exceed 0.7. Although most items were significant at p < 0.001, items 1, 2, 4, and 8 of media richness (i.e., MR1, MR2, MR4, and MR8) showed low factor loadings and were excluded from further analysis. Likewise, the composite reliability value should exceed 0.7 [2,22], and the AVE value should be over 0.5 [35]. We confirmed that all variables were convergent and reliable because all the values of criteria surpassed the above recommended value, as shown in Table 3.

Discriminant validity represents the ability of each variable to be distinguishable enough to identify. Thus, each item is fully correlated with its variable and has no correlation with the other variables. Discriminant validity is calculated by using the square root value of AVE. If each variable’s AVE value is greater than its correlation value with respect to other variables, as shown in Table 3 [22], it is considered to be discriminately valid. Thus, we verified the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the variables.

## 4.3.2. Structural model

Based on the results of the measurement model, we performed a structural equation model analysis. To eliminate potential extraneous effects, we included the age of the subjects, their gender, and the frequency of the offline collaborative shopping by the paired subjects in the analysis as control variables.

All hypotheses were validated by the bootstrapping method in PLS. The results of the analysis are shown in Fig. 2, and the results of the hypothesis validation are shown in Table 4. As we posited in the research model and hypotheses, embodiment and media richness are positively correlated with co-presence at a statistically significant level. In addition, co-presence correlates positively with shopping enjoyment, and shopping enjoyment is positively correlated with flow. Furthermore, shopping enjoyment and flow increase a subject’s intention to use the collaborative online shopping website. However, the results did not show any statistically significant effects of the control variables. This finding suggests that social and relational perspectives could play a critical role in an increase in the intention to engage in collaborative online shopping.

## 5. Conclusion

## 5.1. Summary

The purpose of this study was to investigate whether embodiment and media richness affect one’s intention to use a collaborative online shopping website. Based on past literature and theoretical backgrounds, we proposed two potential design components, an avatar and voice chatting which would increase a shopper’s perception of embodiment and media richness in a collaborative online shopping context. We also hypothesized how the extent of embodiment and media richness affect one’s intention to use the collaborative online shopping through social and relational perspectives. In particular, this study posited that co-presence would affect the intention to use a collaborative online shopping website through shopping enjoyment and flow. To validate the research model, we developed websites with different design components, conducted lap experiments, and tested their impact through ANOVA and structural equation modeling.

The results are as follows. First, we empirically validated the effect of embodiment on co-presence and proposed an avatar as a potential design component to increase embodiment on a collaborative online shopping website. Little empirical research has been conducted on how to use avatars to create co-presence among shoppers in such a context. In this research, we found that embodiment with the support of an avatar is able to significantly increase co-presence, which in turn enhances interaction and the shopping experience between shoppers. Second, we empirically validated the effect of media richness on co-presence and proposed voice chatting as a potential design component to increase media richness in a collaborative online shopping context. Prior literature asserted that media richness could play an important role in facilitating co-presence. Likewise, the results of this research suggest that the increase in media richness by the inclusion of voice chatting can significantly influence co-presence. Therefore, we found that a high level of media richness is useful in enhancing interaction and the shopping experience between shoppers in a collaborative online shopping context. Third, through its impacts on shopping enjoyment and flow, we empirically validated the effect of co-presence on one’s intention to use a collaborative online shopping website. The majority of prior research about the intention to use an eCommerce website assumed that shoppers are motivated to shop online because doing so is useful. In short, the point of online shopping has been assumed to be the purchasing act itself. However, we suggested another point of view by empirically validating that a social, relational perspective, such as co-presence, and hedonic value, such as shopping enjoyment, could also motivate consumers to shop online. This finding suggests that social and relational perspectives and the hedonic values of shopping experience are important motivations for online shoppers in collaborative online shopping contexts.

## 5.2. Limitations and future studies

The results of this study should be interpreted considering its limitations.<sup>3</sup> First, this study investigated the effects on perceived embodiment of having an avatar rather than examining the effects of an avatar’s characteristics because extreme cases are desirable when building theories [19,42,60]. Future study should consider which characteristics of an avatar or which types of avatar could influence a user’s perception of embodiment in a collaborative online shopping context. Second, the experimental task was to identify an apparel product for purchase. Although this study chose apparel products to fit into the research context, future study should verify the proposed research model with different product types, such as electronics, to increase its generalizability. There could be contingency effects across product types. By identifying the different effects of design components from product types, a future study will show which products can be effective in a collaborative online shopping context. Third, because the research model was examined in a lab, the shoppers’ options were limited. However, in a real collaborative online shopping process, shoppers could simply use third-party tools as well as functions built into the websites to achieve equivalent or even higher degrees of social and relational perspectives. For example, if shoppers usually start collaborative online shopping with a conversation in Skype or Facebook, they may not need to exchange their tools supporting social and relational perspectives for tools provided by the collaborative online shopping websites, regardless of how rich those tools may be. Hence, by exploring how collaborative online shopping is started and processed, future studies can compare the effectiveness of integrated and separated systems supporting social and relational perspectives on a collaborative online shopping website and suggest the practical implications of such comparisons.

## 5.3. Academic and practical implications

Even with these limitations, this study has both theoretical and practical implications. From an academic point of view, we address two points that should encourage more studies about design components and their impact on shoppers’ behavior in a collaborative online shopping context. First, by proposing avatar support as one possible design component increasing perceived embodiment, we have suggested and validated a different point of view to increase the online shopping experience. More research needs to be conducted on consumers’ perception and behavior in a collaborative shopping context to further investigate novel ways to enhance their sense of embodiment on collaborative online shopping websites. In addition, by applying collaborative shopping to virtual worlds such as Second Life (secondlife.com), embodiment could be the topic of future research concerning consumer behavior in the virtual world. Second, based on our findings that social and relational perspectives can positively influence the intention to use a collaborative online shopping website, we showed that collaborative online shopping is more than just the purchasing act itself. There are social and relational values, including hedonic values, that could play critical roles in facilitating flow, which is described numerous times in the literature as a key factor in enhancing a positive shopping experience. Hence, future research on facilitating supportive tools of collaborative online shopping websites and virtual worlds and on enriching the online shopping experience can be conducted in the manner introduced above.

From a practical point of view, we advise firms and stakeholders constructing a collaborative online shopping website or a store in the virtual world to adhere to the following two points. First, we suggest that firms developing a collaborative online shopping website furnish avatars as a way to facilitate social, relational value between shopping partners and to produce hedonic value; both of these elements enhance the shopping experience through the state of immersion. The traditional design components of collaborative online shopping that are based on text chatting are ineffective at enhancing a positive shopping experience and inducing customer visits because these tools impart insufficient social, relational, and hedonic values to customers. As 3D and advanced visual effects become common on the Web through increased bandwidth, diverse design components such as avatars and voice chatting will be easily implemented in eCommerce contexts, including virtual worlds such as Second Life. Hence, we predict that the embodiment and media richness described in our study will be able to increase shoppers’ positive attitudes toward websites and consequently increase sales on collaborative online shopping websites. Second, based on our findings about the role of avatars, we believe their design and development for collaborative online websites could be a new business opportunity. We suggest that such avatars should be designed to support the facilitation of the social and relational perspectives of the customers. In addition, collaboration and integration of avatar systems between social networks and collaborative shopping websites pose the potential for increased interaction, synergy, and sales between users of social networks and online collaborative shopping websites.
