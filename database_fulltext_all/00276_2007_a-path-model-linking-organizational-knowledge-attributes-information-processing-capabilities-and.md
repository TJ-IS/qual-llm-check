Employees’ perceptions of knowledge management (KM) has been of increasing interest to organization researchers who have suggested that the understanding of organization information processing (OIP) capabilities is important in providing KM activities. Yet, there has been little research on the link between OIP capabilities and employees’ perceived usability of KM tools in an organization. We developed a path model employing several constructs: teachability, codificability, information acquisition, information dissemination, organizational memory, information integration, and perceived usability. We then explored the relationships between employees’ perception of the effectiveness of KM activities and empirically tested the model using a sample of 157 enterprises from the Taiwanese Information Service Industry. Our results showed that all proposed relationships except a link between codificability and memory capability were supported by the data. The findings and their implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Contemporary business systems are knowledge intensive, and the management of organizational knowledge is becoming critical. Knowledge management (KM) requires an environment that allows workers to create, capture, share, and leverage knowledge to improve performance. Thus, organizations’ KM strategies and initiations are strategic because they significantly help to improve long-term competitive advantage [27].

Gold et al. [9] suggested that a key to understanding the success and failure of KM is in identifying and assessing the preconditions, such as process capabilities, that are necessary for the effort to flourish. We therefore focused on organization information processing (OIP) capabilities in studying effective KM. They represent the degree of capture organizational intelligence [1], which is the capability of an organization to generate knowledge and to use it strategically in adapting to its environment. OIP attempts to explain organizational behavior by examining the information flows in and around organizations. Previous studies showed that understanding OIP capabilities is important, because it facilitates the organization’s KM activities and thus affects the organization’s strategic actions [30].

The attributes of knowledge are, of course, of critical importance in KM initiation and have been considered in many studies (e.g. [12]). Differences in their attributes affect the OIP capabilities and their impact. Yet, few studies have reported empirical tests of the links between attributes, OIP capabilities, and their effect on effective KM. We therefore decided to examine the attributes of organizational knowledge as antecedents of OIP capabilities, and thus determine the impact on employee perceptions of KM.

## 2. Theoretical development

We adopted the definition of knowledge as the capacity for effective action in a domain of human actions [34] and knowledge management as encompassing any processes and practices concerned with the creation, transfer, sharing, and use of knowledge, skills, and experience. KM draws on many disciplines; for example, organizational studies need to determine the factors that help organizations produce new knowledge (e.g. [31]), while strategic studies see knowledge as the a strategic part of a firm’s resources (e.g. [33]). Also, KM and organizational memory information systems are now an area of interest in theory and practice (e.g. [36]). Also, socio-technical studies are exploring the interrelationship between technology and organizational factors (e.g. [23]).

Ravasi and Verona [25] pointed out that a knowledge base always exists in an organization, either as individual or collective knowledge, in group routines of systems and structures, and the focus is on identifying and making use of dispersed and often tacit knowledge. Collectively, an organization’s knowledge processors are arranged into a system that amplifies the knowledge.

## 2.1. Constructs

Zander and Kogut [38] used five constructs to describe organizational knowledge: codifiability, teachability, complexity, system dependence, and product observability. Of these, codifiable and teachable knowledge can be transmitted across organizational boundaries. Therefore, we focused on these two attributes in our research model. Codifiability is the degree to which knowledge can be encoded into a set of rules and relationships. Teachability refers to the extent of easiness by which know-how can be taught to new workers. Teachability involves tacit knowledge; held informally and learned through experience [20].

Malhotra [19] hypothesized that KM embodies organizational processes that seek synergistic combination of data and information processing capacity of ITs with the creative and innovative capacity of human beings. Much of our understanding of OIP is derived from the concept of organizational learning, where the organization is seen as a learning system resulting in the accumulation of knowledge. Huber [15] recognized that organizational members acquired knowledge through observation of stimuli and interpretation of the result. Data and interpretation must then be disseminated and consensus occurs when individual differences are resolved. These shared beliefs and interpretations are termed schema.

Huber [14] identified four key leaning elements: acquisition, information distribution, information interpretation, and organization memory. Information interpretation requires the employees to recognize shared understandings and the conceptual schema of all units in the organization. To incorporate this, we focused on organizational issues, and separate information interpretation was not included. In addition, information integration occurs when complementary knowledge is combined to form new knowledge. Anand et al. [2] suggested that integration, a basic knowledge processing activity, was necessary to take advantage of complementarities among knowledge assets. Accordingly, we included information integration as one dimension of information process capabilities.

In considering the outcomes of organizational KM activities, Becerra-Fernandez and Shbherwal [4] suggested that studies should focus on perceived knowledge satisfaction rather than an objective measure of effectiveness. We therefore employed the construct perceived usability, which is the effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction with which users can achieve tasks in a particular environment [5] as a construct. Usability measures the users’ feeling on how easy, efficient and satisfied they are with the KM activities. This dimension shows whether the organization’s KM activities are implemented effectively and can be perceived by its employees rather than just setting a goal or a strategy. ## 2.2.1. Knowledge attributes, information processing, and perceived usability

Knowledge transfer can only occur if it is tacit, especially for exchange geographically [21]. If tacit knowledge is not codified, it may not be communicated and it is only acquired by sharing experiences, observation, and imitation.

Codifiability refers to the implicit knowledge extracted from people and translated into a common representation that is transmittable and useful. Schulz and Jobe [26] also suggested that codification of organization knowledge is needed to effectively manage knowledge flows, and that there is a positive relation between codification and performance. Employees’ will also perceive a higher extent of usability if knowledge has been articulated in documents.

When organizations codify their knowledge, they package it into formats that facilitate knowledge transfer. Because the organization’s members use a variety of techniques that allow external knowledge to be used in a task, we predicted that codification would facilitate information acquisition.

Hypothesis 1a. The extent of codifiability will positively relate to the perceived usability of organizational KM activities.

Hypothesis 1b. The extent of codifiability will positively relate to the organizational information acquisition.

Hypothesis 1c. The extent of codifiability will positively relate to the organizational memory.

Imitation of observable technology is easily taught, and this makes knowledge transfer easier [7]. Transfers between senior staff and new workers are, however, difficult to achieve. For example, job specialization involves jargon, assumptions, and ambiguities and nonspecialists often fail to understand what specialists are saying. Thus the higher the degree of teachability, the easier it is to meet employees’ information need and to communicate and distribute knowledge. In addition, employees’ will also perceive a higher extent of usability if it the system can be taught easily.

Hypothesis 2a. The extent of teachability will positively relate to the perceived usability of organizational KM activities.

Hypothesis 2b. The extent of teachability will positively relate to the organizational information acquisition.

Hypothesis 2c. The extent of teachability will positively relate to the organizational memory.

## 2.2.2. Information acquisition

Information acquisition is to the processes by which information is obtained, etc. [32]. It is crucial for an organization to learn faster and build competitive advantage. Enterprises interact with their environment: they absorb information, and take actions based on their experience, values, and internal rules. Information acquisition is the first step of OIP. Sinkula [28] discussed a learning organization in which three stages were followed: acquisition, dissemination, and shared interpretation. Obviously the more seamless and efficient this process, the more likely that organizational learning occurs.

Hall et al. [11] suggested that implementation of KM system should provide an organization with enhanced organizational memory. Accordingly, we expected that a high degree of information acquisition would facilitate an organization’s information dissemination, integration, and memory capabilities.

Hypothesis 3a. The extent of organizational information acquisition capability will positively relate to the organizational information dissemination capability.

Hypothesis 3b. The extent of organizational information acquisition capability will positively relate to the organizational information integration capability.

Hypothesis 3c. The extent of organizational information acquisition capability will positively relate to the organizational memory.

## 2.2.3. Organizational memory

Organizational memory is the persistent representation of knowledge and information in an organization. Walsh and Ungson [35] defined memory as stored information from the organization’s history. Together with the development and widespread availability of IT, the main issues of organizational memory involves the use of databases, knowledge bases, and the intranet. Sharing knowledge is necessary in order to make organization knowledge explicit, communicable, and integrated. Thus, we expect memory capability will have positive effects on information dissemination and integration capabilities.

Hypothesis 4a. The extent of organizational memory capability will positively relate to the organizational information integration capability.

Hypothesis 4b. The extent of organizational memory capability will positively relate to the organizational information dissemination capability.

## 2.2.4. Dissemination

Organizations can create new knowledge and learn by combining the information that is disseminated and shared by different units [29]. Therefore, information dissemination must be prior to information integration, and we expected a positive association between dissemination and integration. We also expected that greater information dissemination would lead to a higher degree of employees’ perceived usability of organizational KM activities.

Hypothesis 5a. The extent of organizational dissemination capability will positively relate to the perceived usability of organizational KM activities.

Hypothesis 5b. The extent of organizational dissemination capability will positively relate to the organizational information integration capability.

## 2.2.5. Information integration

Information integration implies that information can be exchanged, shared, evolved, refined and made available at the point of need. If organizational knowledge is not integrated, then the value of knowledge generation and codification is diminished.

It is only by combining the information from employees or groups that the enterprise can realize the full value of the knowledge. Individuals with specialized knowledge must integrate their knowledge in a group to realize its value [22]. Accordingly, we expect organizational integration capability will influence employees perceived usability of the KM activities.

Hypothesis 6. The extent of organizational integration capability will positively relate to the perceived usability of organizational KM activities.

## 3. Research method

To test the hypothesis empirically, we selected members of the Information Service Industry Association (ISIA) in Taiwan as our target population. This audience was chosen as the firms are knowledge intensive, and, as suggested by Yli-Renko et al. [37], knowledge is particularly important to them. We generated a questionnaire using a seven-point Likert scale to provide responses to the survey for all constructs. The survey instrument was pilot tested by five IS researchers working at a university in Taiwan. ISIA was founded in 1983 and is a time-honored hi-tech association. A list of 818 member corporations containing the full name, address, and corresponding manager was downloaded from the Web site of ISIA. The questionnaire is shown in Appendix A. Of the 818 surveys mailed, we received 157 usable responses, which represented a 19% response rate. The response rate is close to the recommended level for organizational surveys and similar to that obtained in similar surveys.

The possibility of non-response errors was tested by making an effort to contact sub-sample of 12 cases; however, only 8 out of the 12 were available. Several reasons for non-response were given by them: three said that only the office spokesperson could answer the question, two admitted that the topic was too confidential, one indicated that no KM material was available, and two indicated that they did not have enough knowledge to answer the questionnaire. As the study achieved a 20% response rate, this problem w2as, we feel, minimized.

## 4.1.3. Convergent and discriminant validity

Construct validity determines whether the test adequately measures the underlying construct. Convergent and discriminant validity are considered to be its subcategories or subtypes. Convergent validity is the general agreement among ratings, gathered independently when the measures should be theoretically related. Discriminate validity involves a lack of relationships among measures that should not theoretically be related. A large correlation between two constructs reflects a lack of discriminant validity. Following Anderson and Gerbing [3], we examined the parameters and their associated t-value. Parameters which had t-value greater than 1.96 were considered t significant, based on an alpha of 0.05. As shown in Table 3, t-values ranged from 6.48 to 13.96. This indicated that the observed variables were convergent in representing their underlying constructs. In terms of discriminant validity, following Fornell and Larcker’s approach, we needed to show that the average variance extracted for each construct exceeded the squared correlation between that construct and any other construct. Table 4 shows that the average variances extracted (the diagonal elements) of each pair of constructs was greater than the squared correlations (the off diagonal elements).

## 4.2. Analysis of the structural model

In addition to the fit measure, we considered the chi-square statistic, based on the work of Rai et al. [24] thus adopting measures that were less sensitive to sample or model complexity. The measures adopted included:

\- the goodness of fit index (GFI), which should be above 0.9;

\- the adjusted goodness of fit index (AGFI), which should also be above 0.9.

## 5. Discussion and implications

We hypothesized that the degree of codificability of an organization’s knowledge would affect the employees’ perceived usability of its KM activities. The results showed that an increase of codificability was likely to lead to a higher perception by the employees’ of the usability of KM activities and to enhance the organization’s information acquisition capability.

However, not all documents are useful; obviously only those that satisfy employees’ needs and preferences are useful. Perceived usability, information acquisition, and memory capabilities were significantly affected by the teachability of the process (the ease by which knowledge could be taught to new workers). Greater teachability is likely to enhance the organization’s memory capability because then techniques can be taught and this transforms them into a persistent representation of knowledge.

Information acquisition capability was found to have a positive influence on dissemination, memory, and integration capabilities. In addition, our findings suggested that the increased capability would lead to better information dissemination and integration. Our results suggested that an increased information on dissemination capability would lead to a better information integration capability and employees perceived usability of KM activities. The organization’s absorptive capacity does not simply depend on the organization’s direct interface with the external environment; it also depends on transfers of knowledge across and within subunits. Information dissemination facilitates information integration. Unless individual knowledge is shared, the knowledge will have a limited impact on organization effectiveness.

Our results suggest that an increased integration capability will lead to a better employees’ perceived usability of KM activities. Knowledge that is only in the minds of key employees or in file drawers and databases is of little value if not supplied to the right people at the right time.

## 6. Conclusion

The motivation of our study was to determine the impact of knowledge attributes and OIP ion KM activities. A theoretical model was proposed and empirical testing of the model was performed using a sample of 157 enterprises from the Taiwanese Information Service Industry. Our findings provided insights on knowledge attributes, OIP capabilities, and the employees’ perceived usability of KM activities. Codifiability and teachability were found to have a significant effect on OIP capabilities, and a strong influence on the perceived usability of the knowledge activities. One implication was that managers should pay special attention to the direct/mediating roles of OIP capabilities, and especially focus on an holistic view to integrate knowledge attributes with OIP capabilities.
