## A B S T R A C T

Assurance has different meanings, depending on the source, audience, and interpretation. We applied institutional theory and the Capability Maturity Model to conceptualize assurance: its symbolic aspects to gain social acceptance, and its substantive aspects to improve organizational capability and effectiveness in performing IS security risk management (SRM). An empirical study examined assuranceseeking behavior and outcomes for regulatory compliance. Some degree of process maturity in SRM was found necessary for producing convincing verbal accounts and compliance evidence. Findings suggest that unless an organization’s assurance claims are based on achieving Level 4 maturity, assurance will be based more on symbolism than effectiveness.

## 1. Introduction

In response to public concern about the security of sensitive organizational data deemed private or confidential to external stakeholders (e.g., customers, investors), IS security is being mandated through regulation. In fact, regulatory requirements have become the number one driver for IS security investment [7].

IS security-related regulations generally require organizations to define and implement administrative, operational, and technical controls that demonstrate ‘‘reasonable assurance’’ that security risks targeted by a regulation are managed to an acceptable level. As perfection and absolute assurance are unachievable, reasonable assurance has become a commonly stated objective in IS securityrelated industry standards and regulations. But what is assurance? How is it achieved, and what are its implications? Given the importance of assurance for regulatory compliance, and the different meanings attributed to it, it should be examined carefully.

Assurance implies a situation in which security is sound and the system provides trust and confidence in the organization’s security and privacy practices. Confidence may be based on evidence of effective security and privacy measures or on external stakeholder perceptions of the organization’s statements and observed actions. Thus, assurance may imply both formal and informal measures.

From a substantive perspective, assurance refers to the degree of confidence that security needs are satisfied. In applying the Capability Maturity Model (CMM) to systems security engineering (SSE) processes, confidence is based on validation of effective security measures. Similarly, information assurance has been characterized as effective security operations [e.g., 4]. From a U.S. Department of Defense perspective, information assurance involves operations that protect and defend information and IS by ensuring their availability, integrity, authentication, confidentiality, and nonrepudiation, including providing IS restoration by incorporating protection, detection, and reaction capabilities.

In contrast, in e-commerce contexts, assurance is mostly symbolic; it is geared to winning online consumer trust [e.g., 12]. For example, assurance services that provide trust seals to web sites assure consumers that an organization’s practices have been checked by the assurance service [2,6]. As such, a key aspect of this need for assurance is consumer (or stakeholder) perception, not necessarily effective measures. Consequently, a series of studies have examined the effects of web assurance seals on online consumer trust and intent to purchase, as opposed to investigating whether the assurance seals actually reflect effective measures for security, privacy, or integrity.

Substantive assurance is needed to ensure effective security, while symbolic assurance is needed to gain the trust and acceptance of stakeholders. COBIT, an industry framework for IT governance, describes reasonable assurance as the implementation of policies, plans, procedures, and organizational structures designed to achieve business objectives and prevent, detect, or correct undesired events. It is important to note that in a regulatory compliance context, an organization must demonstrate compliance in order to prevent any undesired event such as fines or unfavorable auditor attestations.

Despite differences between specific regulatory requirements, the need for assurance requires a general approach that shows compliance with security policies, procedures, and technologies. Process maturity in security risk management (SRM) provides a means to achieve symbolic assurance by gaining external acceptance or endorsement of SRM practices. In addition, some degree of formalization is necessary to develop and institutionalize organizational capabilities in effective SRM.

We applied institutional theory and the CMM as a way to consider assurance: the symbolic aspects of assurance to gain social acceptance, and its substantive aspects to improve organizational capability and effectiveness in performing SRM.

## 2. Assurance as organizational legitimacy

Legitimacy is a perception or assumption that actions of an organization are desirable, proper, or appropriate within some system of norms, values, beliefs, and definitions [11,15]. Assurance signals legitimacy in that it conveys the message that an organization’s practices meet some acceptable standard to an acceptable degree from a stakeholder’s perspective. The standard against which an organization is appraised is typically based on industry standards and regulatory requirements.

Evaluative legitimacy is based on stakeholder perception, while cognitive legitimacy is based on an organization’s internal ability to state why its security practices are adequate. Thus, when viewed as cognitive legitimacy, assurance inherently has two aspects: the plausibility of an organization’s assurance claims, and the actual performance of its practices.

## 2.1. Internal construction of cognitive legitimacy

Applying institutional theory, organizations achieve cognitive legitimacy by formalizing organizational activities. That is, previously informal (or non-existent) SRM practices are formalized (codified) to be more consistently and routinely performed, allowing an organization to construct good verbal accounts of their approach to managing security risks. Even if a data breach occurs, an organization can minimize claims of negligence if it can provide a credible account of its attempt to follow industry security standards.

## 2.2. Cognitive legitimacy as symbolic versus substantive management

Substantive management involves real, material change in an organization’s goals, structures or processes and has effective outcomes (e.g., improved security) as its objective. Conversely, symbolic management involves ‘‘portraying’’ change to external evaluators in order to appear consistent with social values and expectations. The objective of such ‘‘conformity’’ is external approval, not necessarily business effectiveness. Thus, from an institutional perspective, organizations are likely to formally adopt, in rule-like fashion, widely accepted external models of IS security practices for regulatory compliance in order to give the appearance of socially acceptable behavior.

While process maturity models presume substantive management and predict effective outcomes, they also provide a means of achieving cognitive legitimacy through symbolic management by formalizing organizational practices to some degree for presentation to external appraisers. Consequently, process maturity may lead to symbolic outcomes, substantive outcomes, or both.

## 2.3. The capability maturity model (CMM)

Process maturity models propose structured and industryaccepted (security) practices as an approach to assurance. The CMM was developed by the SEI at Carnegie Mellon University and provides a framework for evolving a process from an ad hoc, less organized, less effective state to a highly structured and highly effective state, as shown in Fig. 1.

## 2.4. Achieving substantive assurance from process maturity

Substantive assurance in SRM may be achieved from increased organizational capability gained as SRM processes increase in maturity. As a process becomes more mature, incremental improvements are made in its execution, and the organization gets better at its execution, i.e., its ability, competence, or proficiency to perform the process increases; thus, the terms maturity and capability are used interchangeably in the CMM. Moreover, a defined, documented, consistently performed, quantitatively managed, and continuously improved (i.e., mature) process is expected to lead to higher quality and more effective output, because a mature process is stable, repeatable, and thus, predictable. Process maturity reflects organizational capability to perform, manage, and improve a process.

## 2.5. Negotiating reasonable assurance from process maturity

The assurance-seeking behavior resulting from maturity in SRM processes allows an organization to obtain reasonable assurance from its external appraisers, as shown in Fig. 2. The higher the level of SRM process maturity, the higher the organization’s ability to manage its security and privacy risks.

Organizational reasoning and evidence are likely to improve as an organization increases its ability to manage SRM through higher levels of process maturity. Thus, cognitive legitimacy is internally constructed and reflects some combination of symbolic and substantive outcomes from its SRM compliance effort.

When a formal evaluation is performed, an auditor verifies the organization’s SRM practices against industry standards and may formally endorse the organization’s SRM practices.

Given that assurance is not absolute, it is a negotiable state, based on the evidence and reasoning that an organization presents, and the external appraiser’s perception. Finally, evaluative legitimacy may produce a formal endorsement or mere acceptance.

## 3. Assurance for regulatory compliance: a qualitative study

## 3.1. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act as our context

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX) was chosen as the study context, because U.S. companies have had to invest significant resources into compliance, thus raising the question of what value to SRM has been derived from such investment. Further, SOX is far-reaching and applies to all companies publicly traded on U.S. stock exchanges; other countries have adopted similar legislation.

SOX requires public companies traded on a U.S. stock exchange to demonstrate internal control over financial reporting<sup>1</sup>, defined as an organization’s process to provide reasonable assurance of the reliability of its financial reporting. SOX requires an external audit on IS and processes that significantly impact corporate financial reporting. Acceptance of an organization’s assurance claims is provided by the external auditor’s attestation that is published in its public financial statements.

## 3.2. Data collection

Qualitative data were collected at an organizational level by members of six companies and at an industry level by three security experts who provided advisory services to their clients.

## 3.2.1. Organizational-level data

A total of 16 key informants were interviewed in 13 interview sessions in six publicly traded companies (three interviews had two informants each). Informants with SOX experience were identified at an annual one-day symposium on information assurance and SOX compliance. These initial contacts invited additional organizational members to participate in the study, based on their roles in SOX compliance. Each company had undergone one or two annual audits at the time of the interview. The criterion for inclusion in the study sample was that informants had SOX experience in their organization. Informants were chosen from the three key roles: (1) internal auditor, (2) process owner from a functional area of business, and (3) IS manager. Depending on how an organization was structured, additional governance roles may have also participated in SOX compliance, such as risk and compliance officers. The convenience sample of 16 included six internal auditors, three IS managers, one senior IS security manager, three risk managers, and three business users from finance and accounting.

Informants were told that the purpose of the study was to examine the process their organization underwent for SOX compliance, what roles participated in the process, and outcomes related to IS security. IS professionals were asked additional questions about the concept and practice of IS security in their organization. Semi-structured interviews were conducted using guidelines from Myers and Newman [10]. No pre-conceived theoretical model existed during data collection.

## 3.2.2. Industry-level data

Three senior advisors in the consulting and applied research industries who had expertise in IT risk, IS security, and privacy, plus related regulatory compliance were also interviewed. Two of these were Principals at two different ‘‘Big 4’’ firms and worked on SOX compliance projects for clients. The third did not work on SOX compliance, but at a university applied research center specializing in enterprise IT security and advisory services for large organizational clients on multiple continents.

These three interviews provided a better understanding of the concept and practice of IS security, and of regulatory effects on IS security. Interviews with these experts were conducted prior to the analysis of the 16 organizational-level informants.

Thus, a total of 19 informants were interviewed. Each interview was recorded and lasted approximately 90 min.

## 3.3. Analysis

Interview data were analyzed by classifying segments of transcribed text into conceptual codes and short phrases that emerged from informant accounts. In an iterative process of rereading transcripts; causally connecting conceptual codes into aggregated categories; reflecting on categorization with theoretical memos; and consulting relevant literature, greater levels of abstraction were theorized by relating categories [e.g., 3, 16]. This process continued until an account formed that was solidly grounded in the interview data and theoretically informed [1] by the process maturity and institutional theory literatures.

Four levels of coding were used. Salient concepts from informant accounts emerged early during data collection as part of an open coding process. Additional instances of these initial concepts were searched in subsequent interviews; thus, data collection and analysis were interrelated. Then, similarities, differences, and relationships between the concepts were sought during a process of axial coding whereby categories were added to aggregate, decompose, or associate concepts. Selective coding aggregated code categories around a unified core category. Legitimacy emerged as the core concept to which others were related, while process maturity appeared to be the core mechanism through which it occurred. Finally, an increased level of abstraction occurred during theoretical coding that analyzed relationships between categories. Examples of conceptual coding are provided in Appendix B.

3.4. Internal construction of SRM capability in pursuit of evaluative legitimacy

## 3.4.1. Ceremonial conformance with symbolic behavior

Isomorphism (i.e., uniformity) was found to exist in the internal compliance activities across all six companies: they had implemented a similar set of sub-processes for SOX compliance which closely followed an industry standard for a risk management process<sup>2</sup>. These included processes for defining security objectives, scoping and conducting a risk assessment, designing, implementing, and testing controls, correcting controls that failed their tests, and quantifying un-remediated controls prior to an external audit. Informants commonly referenced PCAOB standards as industry frameworks to which their organizations adhered. Informants touted their organization’s conformance to these industry frameworks as assurance claims that reasonable measures were being taken to manage risk.

In addition, all six organizations claimed that SRM processes had been formalized. Though SRM process maturity increased, all six organizations focused on auditors and compliance suggesting that the need for evaluative legitimacy outweighed any desire for the capability to manage risk.

Level 2 maturity. Evidence of Level 2 maturity was found in organizations that assigned new roles and accountability to specific tasks. New departments and roles were created in many organizations, largely for compliance, rather than a belief that it was the right thing to do. For example, as part of SOX compliance efforts, one organization created an information security and three other departments. The Manager of IT Audit explained that:

. . .the auditors looked around and said, ‘Look, you don’t even have any quality control. You don’t have any security. You’re not going to pass.’ So we had to get those things established quickly.

When asked if there were any other objectives when his company created these new departments, his response was, ‘‘No, I think that’s it.’’

Another company hired an employee who had previously worked for their external auditor; the purpose of this was apparently to facilitate external audits rather than improving internal management of risk:

The lady that [the Director of Internal Audit] hired came from PWC, our auditors. So we’re using this second year as a way to streamline things: she’s bringing the PWC focus to the project. So we’re hoping that she’ll make it easier for our [external] auditors to come in and work because it will be familiar to them–in their format.

Level 3 maturity. Evidence of Level 3 maturity was found in all six organizations; SRM processes for SOX compliance were defined – thus they were documented via standardized templates and performed according to documented procedures. An IS manager at a food manufacturing company stated:

Basically our [workflow] narratives document the process. The risk and control matrix documents internal controls are being tested to ensure we’re effective and that we are compliant with SOX, and then the tests are also documented. All tests are documented. We use [software] right now that basically has all the documentation of most of the business process and the IS side.

Industry-level security experts also agreed that regulatory compliance for SOX generally increased process maturity in SRM.

Industry-level accounts of increased maturity of SRM processes provided descriptions of the risk-control matrix and the newly established ongoing monitoring of security that supported statements of organizational-level informants.

There was general consensus among informants that their SRM process maturity reached at least Level 3 maturity for SOX compliance and also evidence of symbolic management was found across companies. Though informants described risk-control matrices created for SOX as beneficial in assessing risk, matrices were not created for areas beyond SOX compliance efforts. Moreover, informants in many organizations noted that controls that were not externally audited were not regularly tested internally. One audit manager described why:

Although it’s good for business, and we probably should be doing it, it does not affect the financial statement. We’re not going to test it and don’t expect our external auditors to test it either.

Thus, companies did what was needed to pass an audit, but did not make organizational changes when external approval was not the focus. Documentation and testing are resource intensive and were not applied to controls that were not part of an external audit for regulatory compliance.

Level 4 maturity. SRM processes for SOX compliance were quantitatively managed, and thus reached Level 4 maturity; metrics of control performance were recorded and used to take corrective action when needed. Informants reported the use of two types of quantitative metrics to establish evaluative legitimacy. First, the number of control deficiencies (i.e., design or performance errors) was recorded when controls did not pass internal control testing, showing that control testing was being done. Second, the cumulative financial loss that could occur from unremediated control deficiencies was estimated, documented, and used as evidence of compliance. Thus their organizations could claim that risks were being reasonably managed.

Internal auditors at several companies recounted pressure from senior management to keep the estimated financial impact of open gaps low. One said:

We do not have a goal, but our incentive is to keep the estimated dollar loss low, because the percentage is based on pre-taxed income. If it’s too high, it’s considered a material weakness and will be published in the financial reports. So my goal is to make sure that we don’t have anything . . . Yeah, depending on our management there’s a classification of significant deficiency that must be discussed with the board of directors and the audit committee.

Thus the need to announce that deficiencies were low encouraged management to manipulate evaluation criteria to be more favorable to upper management.

## 3.4.2. Substantive outcomes from symbolic behavior

Informant accounts described process maturity at Levels 2, 3, and 4 for various aspects of SOX compliance. Though tasks that resulted in increased SRM process maturity were largely performed because of the need to achieve SOX legitimacy, several improvements occurred because of actions taken to achieve compliance.

Level 2 maturity. In the most explicit admission of symbolic management by one informant, a more structured approach to SRM resulted in effective outcomes. An IT auditor, described SOX compliance as ‘‘an unwelcomed infringement on a corporation,’’ and indicated that his senior management’s intention was to meet the minimal requirements to comply with the law. Primarily to pass their first SOX audit, the organization created four new departments and developed its first IS security policy as part of the compliance efforts. When asked if there was any side benefit, he said that fraudulent behavior had been discovered and eradicated:

Well, the action disclosed fraudulent behavior. Now, I’m not a fraud auditor, but I have heard some suggestions that the IS security manager has found strange activities that she has been able to stop.

Level 3 maturity. Documenting controls and their performance was needed for internal testing and external audits. Defined processes were standardized by performing them routinely and consistently, resulting in increased organizational capability in control performance. A technology risk officer stated that:

Now that we have SOX, the server configuration will be checked once a quarter and the requirement to do so will be formalized by our creating a test script.

Level 4 maturity. Documented quantitative results of control performance allowed an organization to make assurance claims based on evidence. Quantitative metrics also allowed organizations to manage the gap between actual and targeted results. An IS manager described how access control was quantitatively managed by tracking the number of people with inappropriate access, comparing current versus previous results, and using these metrics to manage process improvements such as deleting system access of terminated employees:

We keep metrics when we do access reviews on how many people still have access but should not; we also compare the latest with the prior review to see whether remedies had been made, etc.

Decreased control deficiencies. According to informants, a deficiency is noted in a SOX audit when there is no control in place to counter a particular risk; a control is poorly designed or is not functioning as designed. Findings supported the process maturity literature that states as maturity in SRM processes is increased, an organization’s ability to perform those processes increases, resulting in fewer errors; e.g., there were fewer cases of terminated employees with inappropriate system access. Thus, as organizations improved their maturity in SRM, control deficiencies decreased.

Improved efficiencies. Informants described several examples of improved process efficiency as process maturity increased. For example, one IT risk officer at a bank described how their control testing found errors and inefficiencies and corrected them, improving the efficiency of the approval process for IT change management:

We sometimes need up to 14 different approvers for one change (we average 6½ for one change). So if you’re the developer and you have to change a mere heading on a report, you have to get approval from about 6½ approvers. We find that the changes are not fully approved when they go into production. They may only have five and they needed six. So now our process failed. So . . . we’ve assessed our approval methodology. Should we really make them have two real approvers? We decided to assign two people and hold them accountable.

An internal auditor of another organization reported that, once existing controls were mapped to a set of control objectives, 80 out of the 500 controls were found to be unneeded and were eliminated, improving efficiency. Finally, several organizations were considering or implementing new identity and access management software to automate controls as a way to increase efficiency.

## 3.4.3. Cognitive legitimacy in SRM

Cognitive legitimacy (an organization’s ability to construct plausible, verbal accounts of its actions) may be geared toward external appraisers or the organizational policy. For example, by documenting SRM practices, and performing and testing controls on a routine basis according to documented procedures, a common set of rules emerged and SOX compliance activities became part of the company culture. Repeatable processes that were routinely and consistently performed defined a common language and became part of the company’s shared norms, as one Technology Risk Officer described:

There is now a formalized language. We did not consciously develop it but we can talk about the file permission test and people will know exactly what that is. We have our 45 people who test it every quarter. It doesn’t change every quarter, but some of the testers change throughout the year. Now it has become part of the language.

## 4. Discussion

Based on SOX compliance efforts in six companies, we found that some degree of process maturity in SRM practices was necessary for producing convincing verbal accounts and evidence of compliance. Thus, process maturity was found to be a key way of establishing assurance of regulatory compliance. Across all six companies, process maturity in SRM exhibited uniform increases in similar domains, suggesting that an acceptable industry standard had been established for compliance, and some semblance of conformance became necessary for external endorsement.

We found that control effectiveness was not understood until an organization reached Level 4 maturity with a given security control or activity. By being able to compare current with past performance, an organization could direct its resources to reach an acceptable risk tolerance level. Level 4 maturity was also found to increase efficiency. As part of increasing process maturity, the organization performs tests routinely and consistently according to standardized procedures.

## 4.1. Contributions to research

Our study provided a theoretical explanation of assurance, including its underlying meaning, how it is achieved, and its implications for IS security and privacy. Institutional theory and the CMM were integrated to model assurance as organizational legitimacy achieved through process maturity of SRM practices.

Our results suggest that organizations initially employed process maturity as a way to appease external appraisers but that increased process maturity in SRM provided substantive outcomes. While passing an external audit was important for proving evaluative legitimacy, the value-added was provided by organizational improvements gained during the preparation for external evaluation.

Security initiatives are largely driven by external factors, such as regulations, rather than holistic risk assessments [9], resulting in increased maturity in organizational SRM processes targeted by a regulation.

While top management plays a vital role in setting the tone and demanding user participation, and SRM can integrate security within business processes [13], we found that process maturity also provides a means to institutionalize SRM by defining and implementing periodically tested, repeatable processes. Furthermore, an organizational language for SRM evolved as part of the institutionalization of SRM processes. In addition to constructing assurance claims, an evolved SRM language used by organizations provided a means to more effectively discuss security risk, both internally and to external evaluators. If the extent to which security language is commonly understood enhances security awareness within an organization [8], then process maturity positively influences organizational security awareness of risks as a common language emerges.

## 4.2. Implications for practice

An organization cannot objectively know the state of its security controls on a continuous basis unless it reaches a Level 3 maturity, and it cannot know if security risks are managed to its risk tolerance level until it is at Level 4. Since the bulk of compliance costs occur in reaching maturity Levels 2 and 3 (e.g., hiring personnel, planning, and documenting policies and procedures), an organization may be cheating itself by not expending the effort to reach Level 4.

When an organization makes claims of assurance, it is important for external stakeholders to ascertain the basis of the assurance in order to gauge its value. Unless assurance claims are based on an organization reaching Level 4 maturity in its practices (i.e., routine monitoring of objective measures), assurance will be based more on symbolism than effectiveness. Level 4 maturity also encourages sustainable and more efficient SRM. Consequently, organizations are expected to increase spending on identity and access management systems from an estimated \$5.1 billion in 2010 to \$12.3 billion by the year 2014 [5].

## 4.3. Limitations

Our study focused only on one U.S. law and its consequences. However, given that laws similar to SOX have been implemented in other countries, our results should be internationally meaningful, even if regulatory effects may not be generalizable to other countries due to cultural differences. A further limitation stems from the fact that our study used a convenience sample that may have under-represented the CIO as an important role in IT governance.

## 5. Conclusion

Our study examined assurance in a regulatory context. We found that the need for external endorsement encouraged organizations to appear to be adopting sound practices, and it encouraged increased maturity in SRM. We believe that when an organization makes claims of assurance, it is important for external stakeholders to ascertain the basis of the assurance in order to gauge its value. Unless assurance claims are based on an organization reaching Level 4 maturity in its practices, assurance will be based more on symbolism than effectiveness.

## Appendix B

## Conceptual coding

Examples of salient concepts that emerged during open coding include: auditor, compliance, control testing, control remediation, various forms of documentation, measure gaps, key controls, access control, hiring, a newly established language, and SOX becoming part of the organizational culture. During axial coding, categories were added to aggregate, decompose, or associate concepts. For example, data segments coded as control testing and control remediation were aggregated to the category SRM process; documentation and measurement were aggregated to the category process maturity; culture and language were associated with institutionalization. Axial coding was also used to analyze motivations and consequences of codes, categories of codes, and relationships between code categories. As such, additional codes were developed, including organizational need for external auditor attestation; concern for negative publicity should non-compliance occur; changes to organizational structure; external auditor attestation; and claims of effective controls. During selective coding, code categories were further aggregated around a unified core category. Legitimacy emerged as the core concept to which other concepts were related, while process maturity appeared to be the core mechanism through which it occurred. Finally, during theoretical coding, in consultation with interview data and the relevant literature, additional codes were developed, such as symbolic management and substantive management, to further examine and explain the motivation for and outcomes of process maturity for regulatory compliance.
