# Do the allocation and quality of intellectual assets affect the reputation of open source software projects?

Rebeca Me´ndez-Duro´ n \*

Departament d’Economia de l’Empresa, Universitat de les Illes Balears, Carr. de Valldemossa Km 7.5, 07122 Palma de Mallorca, IB, Spain

A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 2 September 2012 Received in revised form 17 February 2013 Accepted 22 May 2013 Available online 6 June 2013

Keywords: Open source software Reputation Allocation of assets Social networks

## A B S T R A C T

We study the effect exerted by intellectual asset quality, social interaction, and the (re) allocation of intellectual assets on the reputation of open source software projects by analyzing 3196 software games over twelve months. Our main findings are as follows: (1) the aggregate performance of the individuals involved in a project increases the project’s reputation, (2) changes in individual commitment affect project reputation, (3) social interaction increases project reputation, (4) reputation is more sensitive to the performance of individuals than to social interaction, and (5) the quality of intellectual assets and the project’s social interaction both moderate the negative effect of changes in individual commitment.

## 1. Introduction

Reputation is an intangible asset that organizations rely on to gain a competitive edge in their respective markets. Reputation’s intangible nature makes it difficult to measure and quantify its effect on an organization’s success. Nonetheless, based on the organization’s reputation, one can form some expectations about its products or services. Consequently, when an organization attains a reputation, it gains legitimacy, which leads to better opportunities in the form of higher revenues, better access to inputs in the market, or higher market share [10,30].

Reputation captures quality in two ways. On the one hand, reputation depicts how consistent the organization’s performance is, and on the other hand, it shows how other actors perceive that performance [10,21,30,35]. In the first case, other organizations and consumers infer the quality of an organization by directly observing its past actions and performance [21]. Reputation differentiates top performers from underperformers [41], highquality products from low-quality products [33], and trustworthy organizations from non-trustworthy organizations. Moreover, reputation provides incentives to invest continuously to maintain an organization’s high standards. Thus, by continuously engaging in high-quality activities, actors reinforce their reputations [10,11,22,48]. In the second case, individuals indirectly infer the quality of the organization from referrals or endorsements by others [10,21]. That is, reputation is assessed by observing the organization’s social context [13,30,33,45]. Endorsements emerge from the interaction among organizations and other actors within a hierarchy, market, industry, or network. Endorsements primarily reflect interactions and efforts to prevail in other players’ minds rather than the quality of the organizations per se [13]. Overall, the information inferred either directly or indirectly from organizations’ reputations is valuable to the observant party [31].

Many scholars have focused on the determinants and processes an organization uses to attain a certain reputation level. Some scholars study the effect that interaction with other players has on reputation [33,40]. Others pay attention to the efforts the organization directs toward having an impact that is not forgotten [10]. Finally, others focus on the quality of the organization’s inputs and productive assets and its link to reputation [12,35,37]. Although theorists suggest that the quality of an organization’s assets has an influence on third parties’ perceptions of its reputation, only a few studies support this suggestion [18,35]. Moreover, it is difficult to measure the quality of assets and find a direct link between their allocation to productive activities and their effects on an organization’s reputation.

Despite the vast literature on reputation, we know very little about its effect on organizations competing in markets in which price is not a coordination mechanism between supply and demand and whose production processes rely on the voluntary contributions of individuals. Under the assumption that open source software projects are a form of knowledge-intensive organization in markets without prices and wages, in this paper, we study the relationship between the reputation of open source software projects and the quality of their intellectual assets, the allocation of these assets between different tasks, and the projects social interactions. We posit that the quality of intellectual assets, understood here as highly skilled human resources, is an important source of reputation for open source software projects. Through direct observation, third parties recognize a project’s past performance and efforts to continuously improve their processes and releases. Thus, the obtainment of human and/or intellectual assets of high quality is a method of building a reputation. We also postulate that the effect exerted by intellectual assets on reputation will depend on the allocation of these assets. The effect exerted on reputation would be stronger if intellectual assets were allocated to the most intellectually demanding activities. On the contrary, when individuals of high intellect divert their efforts toward less demanding activities, we expect this to have adverse effects on reputation. Finally, previous studies have established a connection between social interactions – as a proxy for indirect observation – and reputation [21]. We postulate that the number of connections to other projects increases a project’s reputation and consolidates its legitimacy.

The study of reputation in an open source software context is important for several reasons. First, there is significant variability in individual commitment and in the quality of software produced [9]. Second, there is an implicit norm requiring that developers receive credit for their work [43]. Third, among open source ideology principles is the belief that participating in open source software projects is a way to demonstrate individual competences and skills and gain peers’ recognition [24,44]. Finally, given that open source software has evolved into applications that are more commercial and has increasingly become an outsourcing force, the developers’ community has an obligation to their potential customers to allocate high-quality staff and reduce the turnover rates of programmers [1]; nonetheless, the voluntary nature of the projects renders these obligations a management puzzle.

In the following section, we address the theoretical framework and hypotheses. In Section 3, we describe the database, the variables, and the regression models. In Section 4, we present our results, and finally, in Section 5, we discuss our findings and discuss the study’s implications for practitioners.

## 2. Theoretical framework and hypotheses

Individuals and organizations care about their reputation if, when interacting with other actors, they foresee that their behavior may affect the outcomes of future transactions [11,42]. Thus, they commit to the use of a consistent behavior because building and maintaining a reputation is ultimately profitable [20,28,48]. Such a commitment signals their high quality [38]. Moreover, investment in reputation can lead to better opportunities in the market. Hence, an individual can increase her potential in the job market and an organization can increase its profits by charging higher prices [2,30,35] or achieving higher market shares [30]. Within the open source software context, for projects, the benefits of attaining a higher reputation are reflected in the achievement of a greater base of users and programmers [39], whereas for programmers, it is reflected in recognition from peers and a better position in the job market [24].

An organization’s reputation comprises not only current and past successes but also the quest to continuously improve products and processes. Although some scholars agree that the subjective evaluation of quality is a key determinant of an organization’s reputation [30,44], the origin of quality is not evident. Building a reputation requires investments in human, intellectual, or production assets and the involvement of every member of the organization as reputation custodians [18,19]. Quality inputs, assets, and human resources are good sources of an organization’s reputation because they actually affect the quality of products [12,35,37]. In the case of open source software projects, as knowledge-intensive organizations, their objective to assemble a pool of high-quality intangible assets is even more important [35]. Although for open source software projects, tangible assets do not create differential output values, intangible assets, such as human capital and intellectual assets, are substantial resources leading to competitive advantage.

Conversely, reputation creates beliefs about the manner in which an individual or organization will behave in the future [21,41,46]. Attaining a good reputation is costlier for poor performers than for better performers. Therefore, poor performers are expected to be less willing to pursue reputation levels that only good performers can afford to pursue [41]. Particularly, when their reliability is at risk, individuals are concerned with maintaining high performance, and hence, their reputation [28,38,49]. Therefore, individuals must sustain performance, behavior, and/or quality to maintain their reputation [7,11].

Moreover, individuals’ past behaviors convey information on their potential and generate perceptions of organizational reputation [48]. Individuals have their own reputational backgrounds, so when an open source software project receives the contribution of skilled professionals, it builds its own reputation on their individual capabilities and reputations [14,36]. These individuals constitute the intellectual assets of open source software. Therefore,

Hypothesis 1. Open source software projects endowed with highquality intellectual assets have better reputations than projects with lower-quality intellectual assets.

Organizations can develop capabilities that diminish their vulnerability to threats to their reputation [20]. One of these capabilities is the allocation of productive assets. In organizations, changes in the allocation of productive assets are common. Some examples are (a) the assignment of a group of skilled professionals to new ventures, pet projects, or alliances, (b) changes in technological processes, which can alter the quality of goods produced, (c) changes in the management team, whose decisions can affect the organization’s financial performance [33], or (d) changes to intellectual capital, which in the case of knowledgeintensive industries, can sustain or decrease product quality [32]. The reallocation of assets can affect output and, thus, perceptions of reputation positively or negatively. If an organization reallocates individuals to productive processes to which their skills are less valuable, their performance will suffer. Given that the sustainment of reputation requires continuous signals, the reallocation of intellectual assets could alter the reputation of any organization. In the case of open source software, the decision to reallocate is not usually made by the top management team, but rather by programmers. Therefore, if a highly skilled programmer performs organization and coordination activities rather than programming, the project is not taking advantages of the abilities of the programmer, even if the programmer has voluntarily decided to do so. Hence,

Hypothesis 2a. If the intellectual assets of an open source software project are dedicated to intellectually demanding activities, the project’s reputation is promoted.

Hypothesis 2b. If the intellectual assets of open source software projects are diverted from intellectually demanding activities, the project’s reputation suffers.

Regardless of the actual organization’s quality, previous research supports the notion that organizational reputation also refers to the information we can gather from peers, judges, and other stakeholders [3,13,33]. Reputation could originate from referrals or endorsements by third parties [13,30,43]. Peers, judges, acquaintances and other stakeholders are sources of information on an organization’s performance and reliability [3,13,33,35]. If the cost of gathering information on an organization is high, it is easier to base judgments on someone else’s opinion [4]. When someone’s quality is uncertain, her own acquaintances are knowledgeable informants regarding her performance [30]. In the case of open source software projects, peers’ opinions serve as a signal of quality, as to some extent, the projects can control their peers’ subjective evaluation by influencing opinion through performance. Reputation is self-reinforced through social interactions with the right actors [13,21,23,45]. Therefore, in the quest for reputation, the project members who produce high-quality software will be interested in developing links to other actors who can provide reliable information on their quality. As long as these actors have the appropriate knowledge to thoroughly understand and evaluate the project’s performance, the linkages to these actors are of more value. These social interactions with peer projects reinforce the project’s social recognition. Social recognition is positively related to the number of links that the project has within its network of peers [15]. The number of links to peer projects plays an important role in the construction of reputation, as dense networks facilitate information flows [6]. Therefore, defining the number of links to peer projects as the degree of the peer network,

Hypothesis 3. The degree of the peer network increases the reputation of an open source software project.

There is no academic consensus regarding whether direct observation or indirect referrals play a more relevant role in reputation inference. Although theoretical papers argue that previous actions and performance are valuable signals of reputation [38,41,47], most empirical studies indicate that indirect referrals in the form of connections to peers have a more significant impact on reputation than the actual quality of products and performance [3,21,30,35]. This finding suggests that it pays more to invest in social interactions than to invest in product quality or to achieve good performance, but it could also mean that the effect exerted by product quality and performance on reputation has not been correctly captured by prior research. Our proposition is that both linking to peers within a social network and investing in the quality of intellectual assets are important for building a reputation because they moderate the effects of changes in the allocation of assets. This moderation is of particular importance in the case of open source software projects. When individuals reallocate themselves to less intellectually demanding activities, social interactions within their network of peers aid in moderating the effect of such reallocation because peers are already familiar with the individual’s work and can acknowledge her quality. In parallel, if the project is allocated superior intellectual assets, inferences regarding its quality would prevail and create an overall positive effect. Therefore,

Hypothesis 4a. The quality of intellectual assets positively moderates the effect exerted by the reallocation of intellectual assets.

Hypothesis 4b. The degree of the project’s peer network positively moderates the effect exerted by the reallocation of intellectual assets.

Fig. 1 summarizes our hypotheses. We expect that the quality of intellectual assets, the degree of the peer network, and the allocation of intellectual assets to intellectually demanding activities will have a direct positive effect on the reputation of open source software projects. Moreover, we expect the quality of intellectual assets and the degree of the peer network to have an indirect positive effect through the allocation of intellectual assets.

## 3. Data and methods

We test our hypotheses using a database of open source software projects for game production. This database provides information on the popularity of each project and details on the quality of their intellectual assets, reallocation of assets, and social interactions. Our social network of open source projects meets the requirements for our testing, as it is possible to ascertain the nature of the relationships between members, the resources provided by each of the members, and the results of their interaction. The creation of software in open source mode depends on the contributions of many individual programmers, but we are able to isolate the contribution of each individual, as well as their contribution to the overall project reputation. Moreover, our data allow us to gauge the gap between reputation and actual quality; the data identify relationships within the social structure of the network and provide us with a plausible period for longitudinal analysis.

## 3.1. Data setting

The data we use in our analysis originate from SourceForge.net Research Data (Department of Computer Science and Engineering, University of Notre Dame). SourceForge.net is the largest repository of open source software. In August 2012, the repository hosted more than 324,000 projects and had more than 3.4 million registered users. SourceForge.net belongs to OSTG, Inc., which shares activity data with the University of Notre Dame for the purpose of academic research on open source software under the condition of sharing data with other researchers interested in the open source software phenomenon [5].

Fig. 1. Theoretical model

As a repository, Sourceforge.net has several characteristics that promote the simultaneous production of code throughout a network. The purpose of the repository is to provide a platform for software development over a World Wide Web infrastructure in which many individuals interact and exchange knowledge. They host projects and provide tools that allow for asynchronous communication, store people’s contributions, and screen software for redundant knowledge. Project creation includes the following steps: An initiator registers the project in the repository, declares the project’s purpose, objectives, goals, target markets, technical characteristics, and team workforce, and asks for programming contributions. Sourceforge.net administrators then authorize the creation of a space to host the project if it satisfies the main requirements for the repository. Sourceforge.net has some parallelisms with a marketplace where projects needing intellectual capital meet their intellectual investors. These intellectual investors are programmers who are willing to devote their effort and knowledge to achieve the project’s goals. Once the repository hosts the project, it records and controls all activities. With every instance of project activity, the repository electronically archives information. These activities may include communications among members, forum posts, and more importantly, the artefacts – or modules of software code – produced by people engaged in programming activities. An important feature of the repository is its open access. Everybody on the World Wide Web, whether registered on Sourceforge.net or not, may view the project, download it, examine the code, recognize the authors, and make a contribution unless the authors state otherwise. The repository records the changes in the team workforce, so it is possible to know whether there are new members or whether team members have left the project. As the repository tracks all artifacts, we can identify which members have contributed to the development of their projects, how often they do so, and to what extent do they contribute to other projects. In addition, the projects on Sourceforge.net can be downloaded for free. Therefore, the popularity of a project is a direct measure of its reputation because the preferences of software users are not distorted by the software’s price.

We used a sub-sample of 3196 valid observations over twelve months of projects aimed toward the development of game software. To obtain this sample, we first examined monthly data dumps and selected all of the projects in the GNU Public License (GPL) category. GPL grants the programmers and users of software the freedom to distribute and modify copies of the software and transfer those privileges to further developments [9]. We restricted ourselves to GPL-licensed projects to avoid any copying, adoption, or distribution restrictions that may bias our sample. Therefore, all of the projects in our sample have the potential to reach the same audience. Second, we limited the sample to games in the general category. We chose this particular sub-sample because game software can attract a mass of non-skilled persons, and therefore, their market is not restricted to the programming skills and abilities of end users, as is the case with many other software projects, such as server, database and programming language projects. Third, we restricted the sample to the projects that were ‘alive’ during the entire period. A project is ‘alive’ as long as the repository hosts the project, and it is ‘dead’ when it leaves the repository. Given the impossibility to distinguish the reasons for leaving the repository, we focused on ‘alive’ projects. On

Sourceforge.net, it is possible to develop projects in a closed mode, meaning that no other programmers within or outside the community have access to them. We picked only those projects that were open to the community and general public to obtain a homogeneous sample in terms of openness. Finally, we examined projects that provided readily available information on downloading activity. The source of information is a relational database; some observations of the matching process could be missing. We followed this procedure over twelve months, from February 2005 to January 2006, to build an unbalanced panel of 25,491 total observations.

## 3.2. Variables

On Sourceforge.net, programmers freely contribute to one or several projects without receiving compensation or salaries. Software production requires only the intellectual capital of programmers. Moreover, consumers do not need to pay to acquire the software, which in some sense, eliminates the distortion in game downloading caused by prices [17]. Consumers need only browse through the available software and download the ones that meet their preferences. At the same time, reputation does not allow projects to charge higher prices, as is the case in other markets. Nonetheless, reputation can be translated into greater recognition among end users and the network community. The variables in our analysis, described below, consider the particularities of the setting.

## 3.2.1. Dependent variable

3.2.1.1. Variation in project reputation (D Project Reputation). Our proxy for reputation is based on number of downloads per month. A download occurs when one user retrieves the executable files of the software for her private use. Thus, the number of downloads is a measure of popularity among a mass of users. Although popularity is slightly different than quality and reputation, it inherently assigns value to the potential to achieve prominent results [34]. Therefore, a mass of users download software because they believe it has some potential to meet their expectations. These expectations are fulfilled only if the software is high quality. Moreover, the number of downloads is consistent with the nature of software games. Software games are digital goods that can be accessed over the Internet, and downloading is the most common way to access them. Furthermore, downloads are a common indicator of performance for open source software projects [8,16] and experiential goods [17,27]. Our variable is measured by the difference in the number of downloads between two consecutive months. We use D Project Reputation to denote this variable.

## 3.2.2. Independent variables

3.2.2.1. Quality of intellectual assets (Asset Quality). An open source software project’s main productive asset is its intellectual capital [32]. Intellectual capital is the economic value of the intellectual assets in transforming knowledge into output [29]. One likely indicator of the quality of intellectual assets is the output already produced. Therefore, the individual performance of project members is a good proxy for their economic value, and hence, their quality. Concretely, we use the individual performance of all project members to measure the quality of intellectual assets. For each period, we compute the number of artifacts (pieces of code) that a member has sent to the focal project and any other projects within the network, and we then calculate a sum for all of the focal project members. The consideration of Asset Quality as a linear function of each member’s contributions is a strong assumption. Nonetheless, the variable is a proxy for programmers’ ability to detect and solve programming puzzles. We use Assets Quality to denote the quality of intellectual assets.

3.2.2.2. Project’s links to peers (Degree). Prior studies have shown that there is a strong relationship between the number of direct ties to other projects and number of project downloads [25]. This finding suggests that the establishment of links and the generation of knowledge flows with peer projects facilitates focal project success. A project’s network degree is the number of connections a project has to other projects through its members. Any single programmer could be simultaneously enrolled in other projects and thus interact with other people throughout the network. Interaction serves as a social device that increases project reputation because it increases the visibility of the project. Degree denotes the number of other projects to which the focal project is connected through its members. That is, when a member of the focal project is simultaneously involved in another project, the focal project creates a link to that project. We disregard redundant links; that is, when different members of the focal project simultaneously join the same other project, Degree only increases by one unit.

3.2.2.3. Reallocation of intellectual assets (Commit ). We want to analyze whether the reallocation of intellectual assets may affect the reputation of a project. In our setting, each individual is a source of intellectual assets, and changes in their commitment to the project represent reallocation. Therefore, we want to study how changes in the individual’s commitment to programming activities affect project reputation. In our setting, we can distinguish between two types of individuals according to their affiliation with the project. On the one hand, there are individuals who are affiliated with the project or who have requested affiliation with the project manager. We denote these individuals as ‘members’. On the other hand, there are individuals who are not affiliated with the project but randomly contribute by sending their contributions to the repository. We denote these individuals as ‘non-affiliated contributors’. We focus on ‘members’ because during the period of analysis, their names or nicknames appear on the front page for each project, whereas non-affiliated contributors are more difficult for the audience to identify.

Affiliated individuals can choose their level of commitment to the project. As there is no penalty or explicit pressure to adopt a certain level of commitment, affiliated individuals can freely choose one of the following four alternatives: (a) they can be active, exclusive contributors to the project, (b) they can be active contributors to the project and to other projects, (c) they can be active contributors solely to other projects, and (d) they cannot contribute to any project. A member contributes when she performs programming activity and sends an artifact. Our measure indicates the number of individuals who fall into each category and checks for changes with respect to the preceding period. We use the notation Commit , for which N, which ranges from 1 to 4, identifies one of the four levels of commitment previously described. Thus, Commit accounts for the number of members who contribute exclusively to the focal project, Commit accounts for the number of individuals who simultaneously contribute to the focal project and other projects within the network, Commit accounts for the number of members of the focal project who contribute solely to other projects, and Commit accounts for the number of focal project members who do not contribute at all. It is important to discern that when a member contributes to the focal project and to any other project, the focal project still retains the individual’s abilities, and in addition, the member expands the focal project’s social network. Thus, the effect of Commit will capture, to some degree, the joint effect of the quality of the focal project’s intellectual assets and its links to peer projects. Focal project members, who only contribute to other projects, simply extend the focal project’s social network. Hence, the effect of Commit will capture, to some degree, a pure social interactions effect. When focal project members do not contribute to any project, the project receives no apparent benefit from their membership. To eliminate collinearity among these variables, Commit will not be used in the analysis.

We use two complementary measures that proxy for the project’s ability to attract the interest of other programmers and the project’s quality.

3.2.2.4. Non-affiliated contributors (Non-Affiliated Contributors). Even when a project creates a broad network for social interaction and a reliable pool of intellectual assets and obtains persistent commitment from its members, it must produce highquality products that actually sustain its reputation. A reasonable measure for quality is the interest that the project arouses among non-members. The project’s ability to attract the interest of other programmers explains the user’s interest in a project [39]. We expect interesting, relevant, challenging, or valuable projects to receive more contributions from non-affiliated programmers in the network and therefore increase in quality. We use Non-Affiliated Contributors to denote the number of outsiders who participate in the project. Non-affiliated contributors are those who send artifacts to the focal project but are not listed as members on the front cover for the project.

3.2.2.5. Artifact growth (Artifact Growth). A project’s quality could also be measured by the project’s data processing efficiency. We examine the number of pieces of code or artifacts submitted for a given project. The number of artifacts a project receives during a given period is highly related to its life cycle and is dependent on a project’s schedule, if any. When a project is in the early stages, it requires strong programming activity for its main features to be developed. As the project evolves, the requirement for new artifacts diminishes. Moreover, as the project enters the later stages, if the software is reliable and efficient, the number of potential artifacts will decrease because new artifacts could be corrections of prior bugs. Therefore, of two similar projects in the later stages of development and with similar timelines, the one that requires more artifacts is more likely to be less efficient. It could be argued that the number of artifacts in absolute terms simply proxies for complexity. Hence, we use the growth in the number of artifacts from one period to another as a proxy. Therefore, if we control for life cycle, higher growth in the number of artifacts from period to period signals poorer quality. We use Artifact Growth to denote the difference in the number of artifacts that a project receives between two consecutive periods.

## 3.2.3. Control variables

In our empirical analysis, we use some characteristics of the focal project as control variables. These variables are mainly demographic issues, such as the size and stage of a project, but also include technical peculiarities that could make the project more attractive for both programmers and users. Our control variables are as follows.

3.2.3.1. Reputation during previous period (Previous Reputation). We control for previous changes in downloads by including the first lag of the dependent variable in our empirical model (D Project Reputation ). We use Previous Reputation as a notation.

3.2.3.2. Project size (Size). Project size is the number of focal project members. By including this variable, we seek to control for the higher variance in the dependent variable observed among the largest projects and to be consistent with the previous literature [39]. We use Size as a notation.

3.2.3.3. Project stage (Stage). Open source software projects included in our database could be at any of the seven stages of development: planning, pre-alpha, alpha, beta, production, mature, and inactive. As previously discussed, the stage of the project and the number of downloads are significantly correlated. In the early stages, the core task is to create an initial system that evolves over time, generating lower user demand. In the final stages, the core task is the diffusion of the product, so the user demand is higher [39]. The project stage is important because each stage may require a different knowledge base. We use Stage as a notation.

3.2.3.4. Other project characteristics. We control for other project characteristics such as programming language, operating system, user interface, intended audience, and the game’s speaking language.

## 3.3. Method of analysis

To test our hypotheses in a dynamic context, we use a linear regression model to panel data analysis with fixed effects (within) using Stata 11. We regress the dependent variable, D Project Reputation, on the independent variables in first differences as well, with the exception of the variables Commit and Artifact Growth, which are already defined in first differences. Therefore, we regress the variation in the number of downloads per month on the variation in the independent variables and controls per month, controlling for the project’s fixed effects throughout the period of study. The use of this type of regression method meets our goal of testing how differential rates of intellectual asset quality affect actual project reputation. This dynamic estimator allows us to isolate the effect exerted on reputation by the particular characteristics of a project that make it more attractive to programmers and users. Our approach is a consistent transformation of Pareto distributions, as is the case with our dependent variable, for example. A Pareto distribution is the statistical representation of what Merton [26] defined as the ‘‘Matthew effect’’: those who already have will be given more. In the setting of our analysis, very few projects are profusely downloaded and many others never attract a single user. Our results are robust against collinearity problems. The results of the collinearity tests are available from the authors upon request.

## 4. Results

In Table 1, we report descriptive statistics on the variables in levels, and in Table 2, we present the correlation matrix of the variables in levels. In Table 3, we provide the results of our estimations.

Table 1 shows that the dependent variable, D Project Reputation, that is, the average variation in number of downloads, has a mean of 18. Nonetheless, its standard deviation is approximately 2700 downloads. We also observe that the average Size of a project is 1.93 members, with a standard deviation of 2.24. This result implies that most of the projects are very small; indeed, most of them have only 1 member, or at most, 2 members. Table 1 shows that the average Degree is 0.2, with a standard deviation of 1.6. This result reveals that projects have little connection to other projects, and in many cases, they are isolates. In Table 1, we also show that the average of Asset Quality is 0.42, with a standard deviation of 5.52, suggesting that for every two members, on average, approximately one artifact is submitted. The average values and the standard deviations of the Commit variables are very small. Therefore, members seldom change their level of commitment. However, the descriptive statistics reveal how sensitive to variation in commitment the projects are. If one of the members changes their level of commitment, there is no programming activity for the project.

Table 2 reports the values of the correlation matrix of the main variables in the model. We can emphasize that the correlation coefficient for D Project Reputation and Asset Quality is 0.032 and is statistically significant at 5%, which is consistent with our hypotheses. The correlation between D Project Reputation and Artifact Growth is -0.049, and it is statistically significant, which in a sense, confirms our presumption. Most of the coefficients, although small, appear consistent with the general hypotheses and provide support for the chosen proxies.

Model 1 in Table 3 is our simplest specification. In this model, we regress D Project Reputation over the control variables. The first remarkable finding in Model 1 is the negative first order autocorrelation in D Project Reputation. A change in reputation at time t - 1, measured by Previous Reputation, has a negative and significant effect on the dependent variable, the change in reputation at time t, measured by D Project Reputation. This result is a common pattern in all of the models we include in Table 3. The estimated coefficient is, in absolute terms, less than one (0.674). Therefore, an exogenous shock to project reputation progressively vanishes over time. Artifact Growth has a significant and negative effect on D Project Reputation, meaning that when the number of artifacts drawn by the project increases, its reputation decreases. Other things being equal, projects that receive more pieces of code still have many programming issues to solve. Therefore, potential users consider the project to be of lower quality. In other words, the projects that experience higher increases in the number of downloads are those that experience a higher decrease in the number of artifacts uploaded. We also find that the difference in Non-Affiliated Contributors has no significant effect on D Project Reputation. Thus, projects that attract new non-affiliated contributors experience no increase in downloads. Coefficient values, signs, and levels of significance remain consistent and stable throughout all regressions.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

Creating and sustaining a reputation is not an easy task for organizations of any type. This process requires commitment to sustain performance, produce high-quality goods, and distinguish organizations from their competitors. In this paper, we argue that in the particular case of open source software projects, reputation can be favored by obtaining the commitment of high-quality intellectual assets and the creation of a network of peer organizations that can endorse them. We also argue that reputation can be affected by subtle reallocations of intellectual assets. We use data from Sourceforge.net to test our hypotheses.

We proxy reputation as the number of times the project has been downloaded, and we proxy the quality of intellectual assets as the individual performance of the projects’ members. In our particular setting, we measured individual performance internally and externally for each particular project. Project members’ abilities and skills for solving tasks within and/or outside the focal project provide a good reference with regard to the quality of its intellectual assets. Additionally, people within the network are aware of these abilities and skills. We use the focal project’s number of connections to other projects as a proxy for its connectedness within the network. In addition, our proxy for changes in the allocation of intellectual assets is the number of project members who engage in programming activities at different commitment levels.

We provide empirical evidence supporting our hypotheses that the quality of intellectual assets, the number of connections to peer projects and changes in the allocation of intellectual assets all affect the reputation of open source software projects. Our findings suggest that open source software projects, which attempt to differentiate themselves from others by obtaining high-quality intellectual assets, will be rewarded. In addition, we provide evidence regarding the relative importance that the quality of intellectual assets versus being well connected have for the project’s reputation. We conclude that is important to have connections to gain reputation but that dependence on a robust set of intellectual assets is even more important to sustaining that reputation.

The allocation of productive assets has always been an important issue in management research. In this study, we progress beyond traditional statements by claiming that the reputation of open source software projects is sensitive to the reallocation of intellectual assets. Our findings show that it is harmful for a project’s reputation to ‘‘(re)allocate’’ intellectual assets to less intellectually demanding activities. When an individual changes her level of commitment to the project in terms of dedication but not membership and begins to send artifacts to other projects, the project suffers important losses in reputation. This problem diminishes if the project possesses important intellectual asset resources and connections to peer projects.

Our study reveals how the quality of intellectual assets moderate the effects of reallocating the people engaged in programming activities. There are some possible explanations for these results. First, when more people are dedicated to programming activities, the quality of intellectual assets increases the project’s reputation because it has gained some prestige through its ability to engage more programmers. Second, when more people are exclusively dedicated to programming activities for other projects but the project has high intellectual asset quality, it increases the project’s reputation because the pool and the quality of intellectual assets can replace members’ dedication to other projects. Third, when more people are dedicated to programming activities in focal and other projects simultaneously, the quality of intellectual assets decreases the project’s reputation because it implies that the intellectual assets are not fully devoted to the project, and it is likely that the quality of the project will diminish.

The strength of our setting is that we can identify how projects interrelate through their members, evaluate the quality of their intellectual assets, and isolate the effects of both variables. We measure the project’s reputation through its ability to reach a greater audience, that is, the number of downloads. In our setting, in which exchanges are not based on prices, downloads reveal the authentic individual’s preferences. Individuals base their downloading decisions on the quality of the software, which is either directly observed or indirectly perceived. Although this situation is not common, it is equivalent to other circumstances in which competition is based on quality rather than on price.

Our study has limitations. First, we recognize that measuring reputation according to downloads includes other factors that influence popularity but are not related strictly to quality, such as the fun experienced by users, appearance, or current fads. This measure also excludes characteristics that could be related to quality but that the user could find more difficult to distinguish, such as design complexity or code efficiency. There could be alternative measures for reputation in the world of open source software, such as project code that becomes part of standard modules in other software. Unfortunately, this information is not present in our dataset. However, we constrained our sample to include only software in which many of the good features of code can be translated into characteristics that users value and embrace. Second, we have measured the focal project’s connections through its members. We cannot, however, evaluate the quality of such connections. Most studies on reputation through social interaction acknowledge that both the quality of the relationship and the quality of the partner in the relationship will affect reputation level. Our proxy treats all links as equal. Nonetheless, the positive effect we report for our proxy indicates that being connected is important in terms of reputation. Third, we do not differentiate between the number of contributions and the actual quality of contributions because we linearly aggregate individual perfor mance to proxy for the quality of intellectual assets. Usually, projects receive many artifacts that are insufficient for resolving bugs, and one artifact will suddenly trigger a solution. Weighing the number of contributions by their quality would be a more accurate proxy for intellectual asset quality. Unfortunately, such a proxy cannot be calculated because of data limitations. Another possibility would be assuming decreasing or increasing returns to scale or rectangular or triangular functions, but these would all be mere assumptions that are more difficult to test. For the sake of parsimony, we chose the linear function. In our favor, we argue that even when programmers and their contributions may differ in quality, their aggregated output is a valuable signal of quality for the individuals within the network and the potential users of the project. We recognize that it would be interesting to measure the effect of disaggregate individual performances, as particular individuals could be steering a project’s quality, and ultimately, the project’s reputation. Fourth, in a dynamic environment such as the software industry, it is of extreme importance to improve versions in a timely manner. Therefore, the project timeline and its realization play an important role in the gain (or loss) of reputation. Timely versions gain more adepts and generate some sort of first mover advantage over competing projects, whereas delays disappoint users and other programmers. Unfortunately, we do not have information on project schedules and whether deadlines have been met. Finally, another limitation is the setting itself. In the world of videogames, there are very few instances in which potential users do not consider price when determining whether to buy a product. Therefore, our results are restricted to open source videogames and do not account for what would be of importance for (close-) licensed videogames.

Notwithstanding these limitations, we believe that our findings provide useful insights into the processes that open source software projects may use to create and sustain their reputation. Although reputation is a somewhat complex construct, recognizing its processes and mechanisms facilitates the development of a strategy for its creation and reinforcement over time.

## 5.1. Implications for practitioners

The implications of our findings are relevant to the management of open source software for several reasons. First, the voluntary nature of open source activity makes it more difficult to obtain the commitment of programmers. Therefore, the ability of project submitters to disclose the project’s goals, characteristics, and challenges on its front page could be an important factor attracting the interest of other programmers, as their involvement is usually related to the significance of the project [50]. Second, whenever a programmer asks to be a member of a project, the project manager might be tempted to consider admitting only those programmers of recognized quality if the main objective is to rapidly gain the attention of others. However, by selecting only widely reputable programmers, the project manager could be wasting an opportunity to admit potentially good –but lesserknown– programmers, who would fully deploy their skills by programming for the project and finally earn their own reputations. Moreover, disregarding the application of a programmer because she is not recognized could delay the production of code. As we have used an aggregate measure for intellectual asset quality, our findings reveal that total number of artifacts is important, regardless of how many persons have produced them. Therefore, the admission of more programmers exhibiting lower artifact production could overcome the lack of recognition. It is also important for the project to list them as members rather than characterize them as non-affiliated contributors because increasing the number of the latter is not relevant to increases in downloads, all else being equal. Third, the open environment could produce mismatches between the members and the tasks they choose, leading to the waste of programming resources in other non-core activities. Hence, it is important for project managers to lead project members to choose the activities that are best suited to their skills, and therefore, the necessities of the projects. Nonetheless, if project members choose to devote their time to non-programming activities, project managers should ensure that programming activities do not experience a significant decrease. Last, project managers should not disregard the connections between their project and other projects because the number of projects in which their members also participate is important to the diffusion of the project’s outcomes.
