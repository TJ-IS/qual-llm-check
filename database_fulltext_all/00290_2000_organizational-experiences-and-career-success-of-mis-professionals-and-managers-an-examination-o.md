## Abstract

The role that race plays in influencing the work experiences and career outcomes of MIS personnel has been neglected in the MIS human resource management literature to date, despite the significant demographic changes in the work force projected by the year 2000 and beyond. Thus, the need to understand and eliminate any barriers to advancement for various subgroups is essential if employers are to continue attracting the most qualified employees. This study examines relationships among race, organizational experiences, job performance evaluation, and career outcomes of black and white MIS employees in a large, multi-national telecommunications company. Direct and indirect effects of race on job performance evaluations and career outcomes (i.e., advancement prospects, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment) are examined. Compared to white MIS employees, black MIS employees reported less job discretion, less career support, and lower levels of met expectations. In addition, blacks received lower job performance ratings and were less satisfied with their careers than whites. Among other things, it is recommended that organizations be sensitive to and work to prevent the disparate treatment of black and white employees in all areas of the company, especially by supervisors, which negatively affects black employees' opportunities for promotion and advancement and the quality of their experiences on the job. Employers cannot assume automatically that black and white employees, or other subgroups, have the same experiences on the job or that their perceptions of their experiences are the same. Suggestions for additional research are offered and human resource management implications are identified.

Keywords: MIS management, MIS personnel, career management, race differences

## Introduction

An important issue that has been neglected in the MIS human resource management literature is the role of race in influencing the work experiences and career outcomes of MIS personnel. Today, blacks comprise 7 percent of MIS professionals including programmers, systems analysts, operations specialists, and managers (Bozman, 1988), which is similar to the representation of blacks in the overall population of managers and professionals, estimated at 6 percent in 1986 (Williams, 1987). Although blacks have gained entry into the MIS field, they have found it difficult to advance professionally and managerially within their organizations (Bozman, 1988; Wilson, 1990). Perhaps, as the management literature suggests, this is because minorities may experience considerable discrimination on their jobs, which lowers their performance and ultimately impedes their career advancement.

Understanding and eliminating any barriers to advancement for various subgroups in the workplace should be of increasing concern to the MIS field as a whole. Utilizing all human resources in the MIS function more effectively, rising personnel costs relative to hardware costs, and the high rates of turnover among this group of employees (Lockwood and Sobol, 1989) are all very important in meeting these concerns. Even more important are the significant demographic changes in the work force projected by the year 2000. The Hudson Institute, in its report for the U.S. Department of Labor (Johnston, 1987), estimates that by the end of the next decade only 15 percent of new job entrants will be U.S. born white males, while 20 percent will be U.S. born non-whites, 42 percent will be U.S. born white females, and 23 percent will be foreign born immigrants (Johnston, 1987).

In a comprehensive study of race, organizational experiences, and career outcomes among a heterogeneous group of 828 professionals and managers, Greenhaus, et al. (1990) found that black managers felt less accepted by their organizations, perceived less job discretion, and were rated lower by their supervisors on job performance and advancement prospects than their white counterparts. Moreover, blacks were more likely to have reached a career plateau and experienced lower levels of career satisfaction than whites.

There is some evidence that MIS personnel differ from employees in other fields on a number of personality and motivational factors such as need for achievement, need for growth, and social needs. This has implications for the management of such employees (Couger, 1988; 1990; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Lucas, 1989; Woodruff, 1980); other research indicates minimal differences in the sources of motivation (Ferratt and Short, 1986; 1988). There are also reports that MIS personnel face limited advancement prospects, especially into senior general management positions within their organizations, and generally exhibit little loyalty to their organizations (Tanniru, 1983; Woodruff, 1980). These differential characteristics, experiences, and attitudes of MIS personnel highlight the need to examine the work experiences and career outcomes of professionals and managers specifically employed in the MIS field.

However, because there is almost no empirical research that has examined the impact of race on the careers of MIS employees, it is not known whether the kinds of race differences reported by Greenhaus, et al. (1990) for the overall group of managers and professionals, hold true for the subset of individuals employed in the MIS area. The purpose of this study is to address this gap in the literature by investigating race differences in the organizational experiences and career success outcomes of MIS managers and professionals. Perhaps an even more important aspect of this study is that the results, if different for blacks and whites, would suggest the need to examine the organizational experiences and career success of other subgroups of individuals who will comprise a significant share of the work force in the future, particularly women and immigrants, in order to look for similar differences. Our purpose, then, is to explore several questions relative to the organizational experiences and career success outcomes of black MIS managers and professionals and to discuss the human resource implications of the findings.

The model proposed by Greenhaus, et al. (1990) was modified in this study in order to incorporate variables and linkages thought to be especially important to the careers of MIS employees. These include technical training and management development programs, and the importance of organizational commitment and its determinants among MIS employees. Thus, this study sought to extend the previous findings by undertaking a more fine-grained analysis of the relationships among race, organizational experiences, and career success specifically among MIS personnel. The study, which is exploratory in nature, represents a first step in examining a minority group in the MIS profession. It can determine the presence or absence of race differences in MIS employees' organizational experiences and career success outcomes. The variables included in the model and the proposed relationships among these variables are discussed in the following sections.

## A Model of Career Experiences

The model of career experiences examined in this study (presented in Figure 1) includes three sets of variables: (1) the race of the MIS employee; (2) five organizational experiences: feelings of acceptance by the organization, job discretion, met expectations, career support received from one's supervisor, and participation in technical training and management development programs; and (3) four indicators of career success: job performance, future advancement prospects, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment. Based on the findings of Greenhaus, et al. (1990), which suggest that race has no effects on sponsorship, career support, or career strategies, we decided to exclude sponsorship and career strategies from our model. However, given the importance of supervisory support among MIS employees (Couger, 1990; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Ferratt and Short, 1986; Turner and Baroudi, 1986) and the significant effect of race on supervisory support among MIS employees, we decided to retain it in our model. Furthermore, career plateau status was excluded from the model for three reasons: (1) Greenhaus, et al. (1990) explains only 6 percent of the variance of career plateau status; (2) career plateau status is a dichotomous variable that produces meaningless and uninterpretable results; and (3) the majority of MIS employees (91 percent) reported that they are considered non-plateaued. In addition, because of their importance among MIS employees (Bartol and Martin, 1982; Couger, 1990; Kirkley, 1988; Lucas, 1989), training programs and met expectations were included in the revised model. Since organizational commitment has been found to be one of the most important factors affecting whether MIS employees leave an organization (Baroudi, 1985; Bartol, 1983; Igbaria, 1991), it was also included in the study.

## Race and organizational experiences

Employment discrimination can take two forms—access discrimination and treatment discrimination. Access discrimination refers to the systematic exclusion of members of a subgroup (e.g., blacks) from entry into an organization. Treatment discrimination, on the other hand, occurs when subgroup members already employed in an organization receive fewer resources or rewards than they deserve (Levitin, et al., 1971). Discriminatory treatment may include reduced access to training programs, low salary increases, restricted advancement opportunities, exclusion from the organization's social networks, and inadequate support from others in the organization (Ilgen and Youtz, 1986). These five organizational experiences are examined in this study and represent a potential indicator or symptom of treatment discrimination.

Hypothesis 1 proposes a series of relationships between race and organizational experiences. The proposed relationships are based on the literature regarding race effects in organizational settings.

H1: Blacks confront more negative organizational experiences than whites in several respects.

## Acceptance by the Organization

Acceptance into an organization's informal social networks is an important contributor to an employee's career growth. Inclusion into informal organizational relationships can provide information, support, and social contacts. Although MIS employees have been reported to have lower social needs than non-MIS employees, feelings of acceptance by the organization are still likely to be important to MIS employees, who are often alienated from the mainstream of the organization (Bartol and Martin, 1982; Lucas, 1989).

Minorities are often poorly integrated into the informal networks of their organizations, since they may be ascribed outgroup status by the dominant group in the organization (llgen and Youtz, 1986). In fact, there is some evidence that blacks tend to feel excluded or alienated from informal groups within the organization (Fernandez, 1981; Nixon, 1985a). Within the MIS field, blacks tend to have few opportunities for professional networking (Bozman, 1988), an observation consistent with the view that blacks are less likely to be accepted by their organization than whites.

H1A: Black MIS employees feel less accepted by their organization than white MIS employees.

## Job Discretion

The amount of discretion or autonomy present in a job can reflect the potential for power within an organization (Kanter, 1979). Job discretion may be especially important for MIS people, who seem to possess high needs for autonomy and dominance (Couger, 1990; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Lucas, 1989). Minorities may experience limited job discretion or influence because of the outgroup status they occupy with respect to their supervisors (Ilgen and Youtz, 1986). Consistent with this view, there is some evidence that black managers possess less autonomy in their jobs than white managers (Fernandez, 1975; 1981).

H1B: Black MIS employees experience less discretion in their jobs than white MIS employees.

## Met Expectations

This concept is designed to measure the extent to which one's initial expectations when taking a job have been met, relative to such experiences as the rate of promotion, career opportunities, salary increases, etc. The role of met expectations as a source of satisfaction and commitment has received considerable support (Wanous, 1980), especially if the expected outcomes are also valued by the employees (Greenhaus, et al., 1983). Met expectations may be particularly important for MIS people, who, because of their relative shortage in the labor market, tend to have higher expectations than other employee groups (Couger, 1990) and report lower levels of satisfaction than accountants and engineers (Woodruff, 1980).

Although no prior research has examined race differences in met expectations, there is reason to believe that blacks may be less likely than whites to have their expectations fulfilled on the job. It is possible that black candidates for MIS positions are promised so much during recruiting that their actual job experiences fall short of their initial expectations. Alternatively, it is possible that blacks and whites hold similar initial expectations, but that the career advancement opportunities faced by blacks (Brown and Ford, 1977; Fernandez, 1975) markedly departed from their earlier expectations.

H1C: Black MIS employees experience lower levels of met expectations than white MIS employees.

## Career Support

Employees' careers are likely to be enriched by supportive relationships with their supervisors. Such support may take the form of career enhancing functions such as providing challenging assignments, visibility, and sponsorship, as well as psychosocial functions such as counseling, acceptance, and friendship (Baird and Kram, 1983; Greenhaus, 1987). The importance of career support as a determinant of employee work motivation has been consistently emphasized in the MIS literature (Couger, 1990; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Ferratt and Short, 1986; Turner and Baroudi, 1986).

Several prior studies suggest the possibility of race differences in the degree of career support experienced by managers and professionals. Jones (1986) found that only 15 percent of the black managers in his sample perceived their organizations as supportive of black managers. Additionally, black managers are less likely to receive career guidance and information than white managers (Alderfer, et al., 1980; Fernandez, 1981). Moreover, Thomas' (1986) findings imply that black managers may receive inadequate support from their immediate supervisor.

H1D: Black MIS employees receive less extensive career support from their supervisors than white MIS employees.

## Participation in Technical Training and Management Development Programs

Access to formal and informal training and development activities represents one aspect of the opportunity structure of organizations that fosters skills growth and is associated with career success (Kanter, 1979). Moreover, rapid technological changes require ongoing technical updating on the part of MIS people (Lucas, 1989). In addition to technical training, many MIS employees feel the need to acquire broader business-oriented knowledge and additional managerial skills (Bartol and Martin, 1982; Kirkley, 1988; Lucas, 1989), especially if they desire to move into managerial positions. No prior research, to our knowledge, has examined race differences in managers' participation in technical training or management development programs. It might be speculated, however, that if organizations hold negative stereotypes of minorities (llgen and Youtz, 1986), they may be unwilling to invest substantial time and money in the training and development of minority managers and professionals.

H1E: Black MIS employees participate less extensively in technical and management training activities than white MIS employees.

## Organizational experiences and career success outcomes

Although career success may be judged from a number of different perspectives, Schein's (1978) distinction between external and internal careers provides a useful framework for selecting multiple indicators of career success. From an objective, external perspective, two key indicators of career success are the organization's assessment of an employee's performance on his or her current job and the employee's future advancement opportunities. Indeed, effective role performance and continued vertical mobility with increasing levels of responsibility have been identified as indicators of a successful career from the organization's point of view. In contrast, from a subjective, internal perspective, favorable attitudes such as job or career satisfaction and organizational commitment may be important reflections of career success.

This research examines both external (objective) and internal (subjective) indicators of career success. Organizationally defined external career success outcomes include supervisory ratings of the employee's job performance and the supervisor's assessment of the employee's advancement prospects. Subjectively defined internal career success outcomes include the employee's level of career satisfaction and the employee's commitment to his or her organization.

Hypothesis 2 proposes relationships between the five organizational experiences and job performance.

H2: Favorable organizational experiences are associated with high levels of job performance.

Specifically, it is predicted that:

## H2A: Feelings of acceptance by the organization are positively related to job performance.

It is expected that acceptance by (and inclusion into) the formal and informal aspects of organizational life will enhance job performance. Acceptance and inclusion can increase the power, contacts, and resources required for effective job performance. In addition, feelings of acceptance by the organization may induce an employee to work harder on behalf of the organization, thereby performing more effectively on the job (Rifkin, 1985; Woodruff, 1980).

## H2B: Job discretion is positively related to job performance.

The amount of discretion present on the job is expected to increase the quality of one's job performance. Job discretion or autonomy motivates employees to expend high levels of effort (Hackman and Oldham, 1976) and provides opportunities to exercise decision-making skills that can enhance the effectiveness of one's job performance.

## H2C: Met expectations are positively related to job performance.

Little, if any, research has examined the relationship between met expectations and job performance. However, it is plausible to expect that employees whose initial expectations have been met or exceeded would be more highly motivated to perform effectively than employees whose expectations have been unfulfilled. Unmet expectations may produce feelings of unfairness or inequity (Adams, 1963; Couger, 1988; 1990; Couger and Zawacki, 1980), and employees may choose to reduce effort and performance as a reaction against the inequity that is experienced.

## H2D: Career support is positively related to job performance.

Supervisors can provide career support by offering constructive performance feedback, disseminating important career-related information and advice and assigning challenging, visible tasks and projects. It is expected that these elements of support can promote effective job performance (Berlew and Hall, 1966; Couger, 1988; 1990; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Ferratt and Short, 1986; Latham and Wexley, 1981) either by improving job-related skills or by increasing the level of motivation.

## H2E: Participation in technical and management training activities is positively related to job performance.

Training and continuing education are critical factors in the development and retention of highly skilled MIS employees (Borovits, 1984). Training programs are designed to increase employees' job-related abilities and knowledge. Because ability is a major determinant of job performance (Porter and Lawler, 1968), participation in training activities would be expected to facilitate effective job performance.

## Relationships among career success outcomes

A positive relationship between employees' job performance and the favorability of their career advancement prospects is predicted in Figure 1. This prediction is based on the observation that the appraisal of current job performance often plays a significant role in the organization's assessment of an employee's promotability (Igbaria, 1991; Stumpf and London, 1981). Employees who are performing poorly in their current positions are rarely seen as capable of succeeding in more responsible positions, whereas effective performance in the current position is viewed as a necessary precondition for success in other organizational roles.

H3: Job performance evaluations are positively related to employees' career advancement prospects.

As indicated in Figure 1, it is expected that advancement prospects are related to an employee's level of career satisfaction. Employees whose future is held in high regard by their supervisors may receive more challenging assignments, larger salary increments, and greater recognition, all of which can promote a high level of career satisfaction. Indeed, a lack of promotional opportunities has been cited as a major source of dissatisfaction among MIS personnel (Compton, 1987; Woodruff, 1980).

H4: MIS employees whose advancement prospects are perceived to be favorable are more satisfied with their careers than MIS employees whose advancement prospects are perceived to be unfavorable.

There has been considerable research linking job satisfaction to organizational commitment (Baroudi, 1985; Bartol, 1983; Parasuraman and Alutto, 1984). It is also reasonable to expect that high levels of career satisfaction would enhance organizational commitment, since employees who are satisfied with the direction their careers have taken should perceive greater benefits in retaining membership in their organizations than employees whose careers have been less gratifying.

H5: There is a positive relationship between career satisfaction and organizational commitment.

## Method

## Procedure and sample

This present research is part of a comprehensive study of the career experiences of black and white professionals and managers (Greenhaus, et al., 1990). Participants in the larger study were employees of three companies with extensive operations in the eastern portion of the United States, in the communications, banking, and electronics industries. Within each company, all black managers and professionals were identified and matched as closely as possible with a white manager or professional, on the basis of age, organizational tenure, job function, and organizational level. This ensured that any observed race differences in the variables under investigation could not be attributed to differences in the background characteristics of the black and white managers and professionals. A Career Development Survey was distributed to each employee selected to participate in the study. In addition, the supervisor of each individual selected received a separate Supervisor Survey, cover letter, and return envelope. Identical code numbers were affixed to the two surveys so that subordinates' and supervisors' responses could be linked. A number of the supervisors received multiple copies of the supervisor survey, one for each of their subordinates included in the study.

Although the sample from the larger study consisted of 828 employees and their supervisors (representing a 50.8 percent response rate from three companies and a 52 percent from the communication company), the sample for this study included 138 employees who held professional and managerial positions in the MIS field from just one company, which employed considerably more MIS employees than the other two participating companies. Only 10 MIS employees were identified in the banking firm, and 5 MIS employees were identified from the electronics company. Given the dominant representation of MIS employees from the communications company, the respondents from the banking and electronics companies were eliminated so that conclusions regarding differences between black and white MIS employees would not be confounded by differences in the companies with regard to industry, mission, size, and structure.

The organization studied is a large, multi-national company with gross revenues in excess of \$10 billion and more than 70,000 employees worldwide. It is an industry leader in the technology field and invests substantial resources in staff development and training. The IS budget was approximately 5 percent of total revenues.

## Characteristics of the sample

Of these 138 employees, 69 (50 percent) were black and 69 (50 percent) were white. Their mean age was 37.30 years (S.D. = 7.13), and 72 (52 percent) of the employees were women. Tenure in the organization averaged 14.75 years (S.D. = 7.16). Length of tenure in the current job averaged 4.10 years (S.D. = 2.54). Approximately 80 percent of the sample had attended college, and

28 percent of the participants were college graduates (see Table 1).

A wide variety of job functions within MIS was represented in the sample. The researchers examined employee job titles as well as additional information obtained from the company to classify employees' positions as either managerial or professional. Positions were classified as managerial (coded 2) when the job title suggested managerial, supervisory or coordinating responsibility, or when the hierarchical level indicated manager status. Among MIS personnel, for example, the managerial employees held such positions as project leaders, senior programmers/analysts, shift supervisors, senior applications or senior systems programmers, and MIS managers. Positions were classified professional in nature (coded 1) when the job title suggested technical responsibilities and the hierarchical level indicated professional status. The MIS professionals included systems analysts and designers, applications programmers, analysts, and systems programmers.

Eighty-two (59 percent) of the MIS participants held managerial positions, and the remaining 56 participants held professional positions without supervisory responsibilities. Among the MIS employees who held managerial positions, 12 were project leaders, 14 were senior programmers and analysts, 15 were senior computer operators, shift supervisors, or supervisors of production and I/O control, 20 were lead and senior applications programmers, senior system programmers, or database administrators, and the remaining 21 were in charge of different administrative staff, such as training. Among the professionals without supervisory responsibilities, 33 were systems analysts and designers, five were programmers, and 18 were analysts and systems programmers.

As indicated earlier, the sampling procedure was designed to produce groups of blacks and whites comparable in age, organizational tenure, job function, and organizational level. The success of the sampling procedure is reflected by the non-significant differences between blacks and whites on age, education, organizational tenure, job tenure, marital status, and organizational level, and by the similarity of the jobs (all were in MIS) held by the black and white employees. Further analyses indicated that blacks and whites did not differ in the level of education attained or in the managerial versus non-managerial nature of their position within MIS. The only background variable on which blacks and whites differed significantly was gender ( $X^{2} = 3.71, p < .05$ ); a greater number of whites than blacks were women (40 vs. 29).

## Measures

## Organizational Experiences

Perceived acceptance by the organization was assessed with eight items (e.g., “I feel isolated from others in my work group,” reverse scored) taken from a longer “corporate fit” scale developed by Nixon (1985a). Responses to the eight items (each made on a five-point scale from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree”) were averaged to produce a total acceptance score (alpha = .80).

Job discretion was measured with five items (e.g., "I have considerable decision-making power on my job") taken from a longer "job power" scale developed by Nixon (1985b). Responses to the five items (each made on a five-point scale from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree”) were averaged to produce a total job discretion score (alpha = .76).

Met expectations were assessed with a four-item scale developed for this study (see the Appendix for these items). The four areas (rate of promotion, salary increases, current career opportunities, and training and development experiences) were selected because it was felt that minority employees would be more concerned about their progress in these. Therefore, the expectations of minorities in these areas may be more unrealistic, as a result of being heavily recruited by a company. For each of four outcomes employees indicated whether their experiences were much more, somewhat more, about the same, somewhat less, or much less than what they had expected when they began employment with the company. For each item, the higher the score the greater the extent to which the outcome met or exceeded initial expectations. A factor analysis (with varimax rotation) produced a single factor solution, with an eigenvalue of 2.25, which accounted for 56.2 percent of the explained variance. Therefore, responses were averaged across the four items (alpha = .74).

Career support was measured by a scale developed expressly for the comprehensive study (Greenhaus, et al., 1990). In that study the internal consistency of the career/supervisory support scale was .93. In this study, employees indicated their agreement or disagreement (on a five-point scale) with nine items concerning the degree of career development support they received from their supervisor (e.g., “My supervisor takes the time to learn about my career goals and aspirations”). All de novo scales were subjected to a factor analysis to assess the dimensionality of the measure, and coefficient alphas (Cronbach, 1951) were calculated to estimate the internal consistency reliability of all the measures as used in this study. The factor analysis of the career support scale (with varimax rotation) produced a single factor solution, with an eigenvalue of 6.07, which accounted for 67.4 percent of the explained variance. Therefore, responses to all nine items were averaged to produce a total support scale (alpha = .94).

Participation in technical training and management development programs, respectively, was measured by individual items on the Career Development Survey dealing with participation in training and development activities. As part of this section, employees were given one-paragraph definitions of technical training and management development respectively and were asked to indicate the year in which they most recently participated in each activity (see the Appendix for the texts of these items). Those employees who reported participation in technical training (n = 118) were coded 1, and those who did not report participation in technical training were coded 0. Similarly, employees reporting participation in management development (n = 91) were coded 1, while those who did not report participation in such an activity were coded 0. Because of the common element of technical training and management development programs and the significant correlation between the two items (r = .25, p ≤ .01), we created a combined variable called training programs. MIS employees who reported experience in both technical training and management development programs were coded 2; those who reported experience in one of the programs were coded 1; and those who having experience in neither program were coded 0.

## Career Success Outcomes

Employees' job performance was assessed with a performance rating scale (Touliatos, et al., 1984) included on the Supervisor Survey. Supervisors rated the employee (on a seven-point scale with anchors of "unsatisfactory" and "excellent") on such characteristics as ability, cooperation, job knowledge, and quality of work. Greenhaus, et al. (1990) reported the results of a factor analysis on the total sample that produced two interpretable factors, one tapping the "relationship" component of performance (e.g., loyalty, attitude, cooperation, punctuality, attendance), and the other assessing the "task" component of performance (e.g., ability, job knowledge, creativity, judgment, accuracy). However, since a second-order factor analysis on the MIS employees revealed that one global performance factor explained 91.5 percent of the variance, the ratings on the entire set of 23 items were averaged to produce a total job performance scale (alpha = .97).

In order to assess employee's advancement prospects, supervisors responded to the following item: "What is the likelihood that the employee will be promoted to a higher position sometime during his or her career with the company?" Responses to this item were made on a four-point scale with the following anchors: high likelihood; moderate likelihood; low likelihood; and no likelihood. This item was also used for the larger study (Greenhaus, et al., 1990) and by Igbaria (1991).

Career satisfaction was measured with five items developed expressly for the larger study (Greenhaus, et al., 1990). The scale was also retested by Igbaria (1991) and found to be reliable. Individuals were asked to indicate their agreement or disagreement with each statement on a five-point scale ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree. A factor analysis (with varimax rotation) produced a single factor solution, with an eigenvalue of 3.30, which accounted for 66 percent of the explained variance. Therefore, the five items were averaged to create a career satisfaction score (alpha = .87).

Organizational commitment was measured by a five-item scale based on Alutto, et al. (1973). Each item required the respondent to indicate the probability of leaving the organization for an alternative job given slight increases in selected inducements (e.g., status) on a five-point scale ranging from (1) yes, I would definitely leave to (5) no, definitely would not leave. Responses to the five items were averaged to produce a total organizational commitment score (alpha = .86).

## Data analyses

As indicated earlier, there was a significant relationship between race and gender among the respondents. Therefore, it was necessary to have controls for gender in all analyses so that conclusions regarding race differences were not confounded by gender differences between black and white employees. Race differences in the set of variables under investigation were examined using a multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) in which race was the independent variable, gender was the covariate, and the five organizational experiences and four career success outcomes were the dependent variables. The technique of path analysis using least squares multiple regression was used to determine whether the observed pattern of relationships among the variables was consistent with the causal model presented in Figure 1. The choice of path analysis over structural equation modeling techniques such as LISREL was based on the small sample size. However, several steps were taken to check for possible violations of the assumptions underlying path analysis (Billings and Wroten, 1978; Heise, 1968). First examination of the alpha coefficients indicated satisfactory levels of internal consistency reliability among all the multi-item scales (alpha coefficients ranged from .74 to .97). Furthermore, the intercorrelations among the study variables were also examined. This examination, reported in Table 2, revealed no evidence of extreme multicollinearity (i.e., r's ≥ .80). The correlations among the variables ranged from -.18 to .60, and the median intercorrelation was .18. In addition, the residuals of the endogenous variables were tested for autocorrelation using the Durbin-Watson "d" test (Dillon and Goldstein, 1984). The results indicated the absence of correlated residuals.

Hierarchical multiple regression analyses (Cohen and Cohen, 1983) were used to test the relationships proposed in Figure 1. In order to test Hypothesis 1, each organizational experience was regressed on gender and race. Hypothesis 2 was examined by regressing job performance on gender, race, and organizational experiences. Hypothesis 3 was tested by regressing advancement prospects on gender, race, organizational experiences, and job performance. Hypothesis 4 was tested by regressing career satisfaction on gender, race, organizational experiences, job performance, and advancement prospects. Finally, Hypothesis 5 was tested by regressing organizational commitment on gender, race, organizational experiences, job performance, advancement prospects, and career satisfaction.

In each analysis, the significance of the beta weight for the hypothesized independent variable was examined to determine support for the hypothesis. In addition, the significance of the beta weight for race was examined to determine whether there was an initial race effect on the dependent variable, and the recalculated beta weight for race in the last step of the analysis was examined to determine whether the initial race effect (if there was one) persisted after controlling for subsequent variables in Figure 1.

## Results

The intercorrelations among the study variables are shown in Table 2, and the results of the multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) are presented in Table 3. The MANCOVA revealed a significant $F=2.30,\ p<.05$ multivariate effect of race on the set of dependent variables under investigation. The univariate analyses showed that compared to blacks, whites perceived more discretion in their jobs, experienced a higher level of met expectations, perceived a greater degree of career support, had higher job performance ratings, and reported higher levels of career satisfaction.

The results of the hierarchical multiple regression analyses predicting the five organizational experiences are presented in Table 4. Consistent with Hypothesis 1, whites reported more job discretion, a greater fulfillment of expectations, and greater career support than did blacks. However, blacks and whites showed no differences in feelings of acceptance and participation in training programs.

The results of the analyses predicting job performance are shown in Table 5. There was a significant race effect for job performance, in which whites received a higher performance rating than blacks. In support of Hypothesis 2, organizational experiences explained 22 percent of the variance in job performance. Job discretion, career support, and participation in training programs were all positively related to job performance ratings. Moreover, the race effect on performance remained significant after controlling for the organizational experiences ( $\beta = .22$ , $p \leq .01$ ), indicating that the initial race differences in job performance could not be explained by the organizational experiences of blacks and whites.

Table 5 also presents the results of the analyses predicting advancement prospects. Only job performance explained significant portions of the variance in this category. Employees with high job performance ratings were perceived to have more favorable advancement prospects than employees with low job performance ratings. It should be noted that gender, race, and organizational experiences were found to be unrelated to advancement prospects. On the other hand, job performance, as hypothesized, was found to be related to career advancement prospects.

Table 6 presents the results of the analyses predicting career satisfaction and organizational commitment. Consistent with Hypothesis 4, advancement prospects were related to career satisfaction. In addition, there was a significant race effect, in which whites expressed higher career satisfaction than blacks. It can also be

seen that met expectations and career support were associated with high levels of career satisfaction. Note that the initial race effect on career satisfaction was no longer significant ( $\beta = .07$ , NS) after controlling for the subsequent variables in Figure 1. In contrast to Hypothesis 5, career satisfaction was unrelated to organizational commitment. However, organizational commitment was stronger for employees who felt accepted by the organization, who experienced high levels of met expectations, who received supervisory support, and who were perceived to have favorable advancement prospects. Race was unrelated to organizational commitment. Table 7 summarizes the results of our hypotheses. Eight of the 13 hypotheses and subhypotheses were statistically significant. In all analyses there were controls for gender.

## Discussion

This study examines the role of race in the career experiences of MIS employees. Significant race differences were found for a number of organizational experiences and career success outcomes, and, without exception, those differences favored white employees over black employees. In this section, these findings are discussed in relation to the prior literature, areas for additional research are suggested, and some of the human resource management implications of the data are identified. Because this study's sample of MIS employees was drawn from a more diverse group of managers and professionals, comparisons are drawn between our findings and the findings of Greenhaus, et al. (1990) when appropriate.

This study indicates that blacks in professional and managerial positions in MIS perceive less discretion and autonomy on their jobs than whites in similar positions. This finding is consistent with the speculations of Ilgen and Youtz (1986) and Kanter (1979), as well as with Greenhaus, et al.'s (1990) finding that used a more heterogeneous group of managers and professionals. Apparently, either black employees are provided with relatively little job discretion by their supervisors, or they seek few opportunities to exercise discretion in their jobs.

Consistent with the first explanation, black MIS employees reported receiving less career support from their supervisors than did white MIS employees, a finding that supports the earlier work of Jones (1986) and Fernandez (1981). Since one form of career support is the assignment of challenging, autonomous tasks (note the positive correlation between job discretion and career support in Table 2), it is plausible that the relatively weak career support provided by supervisors of black employees is responsible for the restricted level of job discretion perceived by the blacks. It is interesting that a significant relationship between race and career support was not obtained among the more diverse group of managers and professionals studied by Greenhaus, et al. (1990). It is not clear whether race differences in career support are more prevalent among professionals and managers in MIS positions, or whether the results were influenced by the culture of the specific company under investigation.

Black MIS employees reported a lower level of met expectations than white MIS employees. It is impossible to determine from this study whether blacks' initial expectations were more inflated than the expectations of whites, or whether the actual work experiences of blacks were more negative than the work experiences of whites. The numerous race differences in career success outcomes (e.g., job performance, career satisfaction) suggest the plausibility of the latter explanation, but additional research is clearly required in this area.

Although blacks and whites did perceive differences in three of the organizational experiences examined in this study, there were no race differences in feelings of acceptance by the organization, or participation in technical and management training programs. This suggests that differential treatment of blacks and whites in MIS positions does not necessarily extend to all areas of organizational life. Note that Greenhaus, et al. (1990) found that black managers and professionals reported lower feelings of acceptance than white professionals and managers. Future research should examine the factors (e.g., organizational culture, leadership processes) that explain why differential treatment is reflected in certain organizational experiences but not in others. In addition, future research should also examine whether the non-significant effect of race on acceptance is influenced by the professions or community or by the culture of the specific industry under investigation.

Hypothesis 2, which predicted relationships between the five organizational experiences and job performance, was partially supported. MIS employees who participated in training programs performed more effectively on their jobs than employees who had not participated in such activities. This finding reinforces the importance of formal training and development programs, and highlights the utility of technical updating in the MIS field, where technological changes are commonplace (Lucas, 1989).

Job discretion and career support were also positively related to job performance ratings. The organizational behavior literature has emphasized the importance of both job discretion (Hackman and Oldham, 1976) and career support (Greenhaus, 1987), and the present data indicate that these can be significant experiences for MIS personnel. Since Greenhaus, et al. (1990) found that discretion and support promoted job performance for a diverse sample of managers and professionals, the importance of these experiences is evidently not unique to MIS employees. Nevertheless, this does not detract from the important role that job discretion and career support can play in the work lives of MIS employees (Couger, 1988; 1990; Couger and Zawacki, 1980).

In this research, blacks received lower ratings on job performance than whites. Moreover, although it has been suggested that race differences in performance may be attributed to differences in the organizational experiences of blacks and whites (llgen and Youtz, 1986), the race differences remained significant even after controlling for organizational experiences. Note that race differences in job performance were largely unexplained by exposure to different organizational experiences. Although there were race differences in job discretion and career support by the supervisor, these experiences did not substantially account for the race differences in job performance ratings. The race differences in job performance found in this study are consistent with the findings of Greenhaus, et al. (1990). Although this could reflect true differences in blacks' and whites' job performance, there was no evidence of differential "qualifications" in terms of education, experience, participation in training and development activities, etc. Instead, the lower assessment of blacks' performance could have resulted from rater bias or other biases in the rating process. Since race explained a significant portion (9 percent) of the variance in job performance evaluations, it is possible that the predominantly white raters (in this study, 93.9 percent of MIS managers and professionals were evaluated by white raters) used race rather than work-related cues in evaluating their subordinates. Because an employee's subsequent advancement opportunities are so heavily dependent on the supervisor's assessment of his or her job performance, any bias in the performance appraisal process inevitably will produce bias in promotion decisions over time. Therefore, the results of this study indicate the need to monitor more carefully the performance rating process within the MIS function. Future research should examine the impact of rater bias on the performance of subordinates and should also incorporate objective measures of job performance to reduce the bias on job performance evaluations.

In support of Hypothesis 3, the most powerful predictor of advancement prospects was an employee's level of rated job performance, a finding similar to the results reported by Stumpf and London (1981). MIS employees were seen as having relatively restricted promotion opportunities because they received lower job performance ratings. Although this finding is not unique to MIS employees (Greenhaus, et al., 1990), it does apply to the MIS field and warrants the serious attention of MIS employers of MIS professionals and managers. The effect of race on advancement prospects was insignificant, which is inconsistent with the findings of Greenhaus, et al. (1990). Future research should examine whether the results were influenced by the industry or by the profession.

Consistent with the predictions of Hypothesis 4, advancement prospects were related to career satisfaction. These results indicate that current and future advancement opportunities play an important role in determining the level of career satisfaction for managers and professionals in the MIS field. In addition, the career satisfaction of MIS employees seems to be enhanced by satisfactory levels of career support and by work experiences that meet prior expectations. These findings are consistent with the importance attached to support in the MIS literature (Couger, 1988; 1990; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Ferratt and Short, 1986; Turner and Baroudi, 1986), as well as with the significance of met expectations in employees' affective reactions to work (Wanous, 1980).

The correlational data indicated that blacks expressed a lower level of career satisfaction than whites. Moreover, the path analysis revealed that the effect of race on career satisfaction was largely indirect, operating through met expectations, career support, and advancement prospects. Specifically, blacks' relative dissatisfaction with their careers can be explained by the lack of career support they receive and by the unmet expectations they experience. This finding reinforces the importance of understanding the source of race differences in career support and met expectations among MIS employees so that policies and practices to eliminate differential treatment in these areas can be developed.

Although much has been written about the lack of commitment that MIS employees hold toward their employer (Baroudi, 1985; Bartol, 1983; Bartol and Martin, 1982; Lucas, 1989; Turner and Baroudi, 1986), the average level of commitment in this sample (3.72 on a five-point scale) was not particularly low. Moreover, the data revealed that organizational commitment was highest for employees who felt accepted by the organization, whose expectations had been met on the job, and whose advancement prospects were seen as favorable. Acceptance may be a critical determinant of commitment for MIS employees because of the status they occupy as staff experts in a service function that is poorly understood. Given their uncertain status, the more likely MIS employees will be included in the formal and informal social networks of an organization, the greater loyalty and commitment they display toward the organization.

In a similar vein, MIS employees whose initial expectations have been fulfilled may develop trust, affection, and commitment toward the organization. Finally, the importance of advancement opportunities to MIS employees has been suggested frequently in the literature (Bartol and Martin 1982; Couger, 1988; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Mason, 1987; Reed, 1987). It is not surprising, therefore, that MIS employees who are thought to have favorable advancement prospects are more committed to the organization than those whose advancement opportunities seem more limited. Perhaps MIS employees who are seen as upwardly mobile are given more challenging assignments and more sizable rewards, thereby binding them strongly to the organization.

Inconsistent with Hypothesis 5, career satisfaction did not explain a significant portion of the variance in organizational commitment. Although there was a positive correlation between career satisfaction and organizational commitment (r = .26, p ≤ .01), career satisfaction was also related to acceptance (r = .22, p ≤ .01) and met expectations (r = .60, p ≤ .001), both of which had entered the regression analysis prior to career satisfaction. Thus, it is not career satisfaction per se that enhances feelings of commitment to the organization, but rather the factors that promote career satisfaction such as feelings of acceptance and met expectations.

Given the numerous race differences observed in this study, it is somewhat surprising that blacks were as committed to the organization as whites. Upon reflection, however, it is apparent that two of the three variables that predicted commitment (acceptance and advancement prospects) did not themselves exhibit race differences, whereas most of the variables on which there were race differences (job discretion, job performance, and career satisfaction) were unrelated to organizational commitment. This finding suggests that the presence or absence of a race difference in a particular career outcome (such as organizational commitment) depends very specifically on whether blacks and whites differ on those conditions that determine the outcome. In this case, black and white MIS employees were not substantially different on two of the organizational experiences (acceptance and advancement prospects) that strongly influenced commitment.

Results of this study show that job discretion, career support, and training programs mediated the effect of race on job performance evaluations, and job performance evaluations mediated the effect of race on advancement prospects. The effect of race on job performance evaluations was largely direct. In contrast, the effect of race on career satisfaction was largely indirect, mainly, through organizational experiences (e.g., met expectations, career support). Although the magnitude of race effects was modest, the consistent direction of race differences should caution employers to be vigilant in their attempts to assure equal opportunity. Moreover, the study results should encourage scholars to conduct programmatic research on the careers of black and white professionals and managers among MIS employees in order to identify other relevant variables that impinge on the job performance evaluations and career success of both groups. Future research should also examine other minority groups such as women, Asians, and Hispanics, and identify the problems they face and the obstacles that prevent them from advancing into senior positions within the MIS area. It is necessary to understand the experiences of different minority groups in the MIS profession.

In summary, the results of this study provide moderate support for the proposed model among MIS employees. It appears that the relationships among the study variables are more complex than we initially proposed them to be. Future research should include revising the model to incorporate additional organizational experiences such as receiving challenging task assignments with high visibility, other job characteristics such as skill variety, autonomy, and feedback from the job itself, and role stressors. Those variables and others may be relevant to understanding the job performance and career outcomes of members of different subgroups. Because the data used in this study came from one large communications company, the findings may not be generalized to a broader cross-section of organizations. Therefore, additional research should also examine race differences for a variety of companies with MIS employees and confirm the generalizability of these findings to other companies and industries.

Also, our findings may have resulted from the culture of the particular company, rather than the culture of the MIS profession, and should be considered in this light. Moreover, the sample consisted of predominantly lower and middle-level MIS managers and professionals. Therefore, blacks and whites representing a broad range of organizational levels should be represented in future studies, and the generalizability of the findings to upper-level executives and professionals must be established. Further, different methodologies, mainly qualitative methods such as the use of interviews, will be required to greatly enhance understanding of the relationships found. Nevertheless, this study represents an early step in examining minority groups in the MIS field. We hope that the diverse findings will stimulate additional research in field settings on the similar and unique career experiences of minorities and contribute to a deeper understanding of the dynamics of race and gender in the career development of MIS managers and professionals.

## Human Resource Implications

In this study, blacks reported less job discretion and lower career support than whites, which suggests that organizations need to be sensitive to the possibility that black and white employees in the MIS field are treated differently by their companies. Supervisors of MIS employees need to be especially sensitive. They should be aware of the importance of discretion and support to a subordinate's career, should be trained in the techniques required to provide satisfactory levels of discretion and support to their subordinates, and should be vigilant in assuring that a subordinate's minority status does not detract from the amount of discretion and support that is provided.

Black employees also reported lower levels of met expectations than white employees, which suggests that it is important to examine the recruiting process to ensure that the expectations of black candidates for MIS positions are not increased unrealistically. In attempting to attract qualified black employees, an organization may unwittingly produce overly inflated expectations that it cannot meet. On the other hand, the expectations of black employees may be realistic, but the organization may be overly cautious in allowing them to demonstrate their ability to perform well. Monitoring the expectations of newly hired black and white MIS employees may prove helpful in determining the source of employees' expectations and in setting them at more realistic levels. Also, the need to provide adequate discretion and support for black employees to succeed in challenging assignments has already been noted.

Although there were no race differences in feelings of acceptance by the organization, acceptance was a critical determinant of the organizational commitment of MIS employees. Therefore, efforts should be aimed at facilitating the acceptance of MIS employees by others in the organization. Such activities might include (1) reducing the physical isolation of the MIS function by increasing the interaction, both formal and informal, of MIS employees with other areas; (2) improving horizontal and vertical communications; (3) building cohesive work groups; (4) rewarding the accomplishments of MIS personnel; and (5) increasing the understanding of and appreciation for the MIS area throughout the organization as a whole.

This study shows that the most substantial portion of the race effect on job performance evaluations was direct. This may be due to the presence of bias in supervisory ratings of job performance. Organizations should examine their performance appraisal systems on a regular basis. If there are race differences in performance ratings, it is necessary to determine whether they are due to race differences in actual job performance, or whether there is bias in the rating process. If it is determined that blacks do perform less effectively on the job than whites, it is important to understand why these performance differences exist and to develop programs (e.g., coaching, training and development, visible job assignments) that equalize opportunities for both groups. If bias is discovered in the rating process, then efforts must be directed toward the development of more valid performance appraisal systems. Because the supervisor's assessment of a subordinate's job performance plays such a prominent role in forming opinions about the subordinate's advancement prospects, any bias in the performance appraisal process inevitably will produce bias in promotion decisions over time.

Minority groups should require formal review processes. We suggest that supervisors review their subordinates' performance quarterly and their advancement potential yearly. Blacks should require career guidance in terms that they understand with respect to performance and career opportunities. Blacks, as well as whites, should insist that they have systematic performance appraisals and discuss their advancement potential and career-planning needs with their supervisors. They should ask for ongoing feedback regarding their performance as well as counsel about their career options within the organization. If they feel they are being treated inequitably and if dual standards for performance evaluations are being used, they should pursue it vigorously up the hierarchy. Blacks should recognize that performance is assessed based on the amount of job discretion and supervisory support they have. Furthermore, they should act decisively and actively participate in both formal and informal work groups. Blacks should also insist on participating in training programs in order to keep abreast of new concepts, strategies, plans, and technologies.

It is possible that other organizational experiences, unexamined in this study, were responsible for the race differences in job performance evaluations. The effect of race on job performance evaluations may reflect actual performance differences between blacks and whites, and these differences may be due to differential treatment and experiences. Organizations should examine a number of other potential experiences that may impede the performance of black professionals and managers and should identify those circumstances (e.g., economic conditions, organizational culture) under which various forms of treatment discrimination are most likely to occur.

The results indicate that black employees were more likely than white employees to be dissatisfied with their career. Organizations should determine whether there is a race difference in career satisfaction. If a race difference is found, it is essential that organizations understand the reasons behind the disparity and act to assure equal advancement opportunities for all groups. An examination of the career paths of black and white employees within MIS, a review of the criteria used to make advancement or other mobility decisions, and an assessment of the fairness of the performance evaluation system would represent important steps in eliminating bias in the advancement opportunities for MIS employees and would eventually increase the level of career satisfaction and organizational commitment among black professionals and managers.

Organizations must recognize the heterogeneous groups within the company and that diversity is an important aspect of the global economy. In conclusion, we recommend that organizations do the following:

1. Initiate strategies to recruit, train, and develop black employees, utilizing the same criteria that they use for their white employees. Such strategies should also incorporate clearly defined and communicated career and promotion policies and practices for all employees.

2. The quality of first line supervision is critical to the success or failure of all employees, but especially to minorities and women. Therefore, organizations should

a. help their supervisors recognize their own subjectivity and take steps to ensure that rewards and promotions are delivered in as fair a manner as possible;

b. Develop training programs to give supervisors effective and bias-free' tools to better evaluate their subordinates' performance and advancement potential, and to prepare them to deal with their subordinates' career planning and development needs;

c. Supervisors should be encouraged to include minorities in their informal job-related social interactions, where important information is dispensed about the organization's activities.

3. Develop concrete performance measurement criteria, preferably objective, to evaluate all the subordinates.

4. Increase commitment to AA/EEO throughout the company's existing functions, in all departments and at all levels—specifically, develop and implement educational and training activities in managing diversity.

Implementation of these recommendations will help to create a hospitable environment in which every employee is empowered to achieve his or her maximum level of productivity.

## Participation in Technical Training and Management Development Programs

Employees were asked to indicate whether they participated in technical training and management development programs. Technical training programs were defined as “programs designed to teach specific job-related information and skills (e.g., programming).” Management development programs were defined as “programs designed to teach broad managerial skills such as supervision and coaching, management decision making, strategic policy making.”
