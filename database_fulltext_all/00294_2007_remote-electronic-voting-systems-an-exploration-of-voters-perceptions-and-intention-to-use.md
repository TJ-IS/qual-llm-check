# Remote electronic voting systems: an exploration of voters’ perceptions and intention to use

Yurong Yao<sup>1</sup> and Lisa Murphy<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Information Systems & Operations Management, Suffolk University, Boston, MA, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>WestWind Technologies, Inc., Huntsville, AL, U.S.A.

Correspondence:

Y. Yao, Information Systems & Operations Management, Suffolk University, 8 Ashburton Place, Boston, MA 02108, U.S.A. Tel: 617-573-8356; E-mail: yyao@suffolk.edu

## Abstract

Coupling telephone and web interfaces with computers for balloting outside the polling place, remote electronic voting systems (REVS) give the voter a choice: polling booth, absentee ballot, or remote voting. Not only does this e-Government technology raises issues such as security, voter participation, and accessibility, REVS technologies themselves differ in features and enabling conditions. How users (voters) perceive REVS’s availability, mobility, accuracy, privacy protection, and ease of use, is likely to affect their use intention. Intention to use or not to use a voting technology can translate into a decision to vote or not – and there are no ‘do-overs’. We develop a model and report on a survey of potential voters – people waiting to be impaneled on a jury – in regard to the impact of REVS characteristics on voting intentions and how the two most discussed REVS technologies of telephone- and web-based interfaces are perceived.

## Introduction

The controversy surrounding the Florida paper ‘hanging chad’ ballets in the 2000 United States (U.S.) presidential election stimulated practical and theoretical research on technologies to support voting. By the next U.S. presidential election in 2004, more electronic voting systems (EVS) using technologies such as touch screens were adopted across the United States and its 200,000 voting booths (Zetter, 2004). In 2006 mid-term election, Department of Defense offered voting by e-mail to all oversea soldiers by using IVAS system (Hertzberg, 2006). Yet, concerns over the reliability of voting persist; as a report by coalition of voter action groups identified usability issues, delays, failures in retrieving results, and other problems for on-site voting in the November, 2007 U.S. congressional and gubernatorial elections (Voters Unite, 2007). However, efforts to support voting for Iraqis displaced by war and New Orleaneans displaced by Hurricane Katrina have increased interest in reliable mechanisms for supporting voting away from traditional polling places (e.g., Whoriskey, 2006). A special class of voting technologies – remote electronic voting systems (REVS) – offers the potential to reduce concerns over mistallying while increasing rates of voter participation, another concern of researchers and observers.

The U.S. Census Bureau (2005) reports that the participation rate in U.S. presidential elections declined from 63% in 1960 to 55% in 2004. While many factors may contribute to trends in voter participation, the inconvenience of the traditional voting process – identifying and traveling to the correct polling location to wait in line for a few minutes in a booth – likely plays an important role. In the 2004 U.S. presidential election, 50% of registered voters who did not vote gave scheduling conflicts as a reason (Census, 2005). The U.K. also has similar concerns: the participation rate of 61% in 2005 general election was down from 71% in 1997; among nonvoters in 2001, 37% claimed time conflicts or location inconvenience (LGA, 2002) and in 2005, 33% cited that some circumstance of that day prevented voting (Sanders et al., 2005).

Absentee voting – receiving and returning ballots by mail – is an increasingly popular alternative; after absentee voting began in California in 1978, the state experienced a 20% increase in voter participation (Patterson and Caldeira, 1985). In 2000, Oregon conducted its presidential voting solely by mail and obtained a 3.5% increase in turnout over 1996 compared to a nationwide increase of 2.1% in the same period (CalTech-MIT, 2001). Oregon further experienced a record votingage-eligible population turnout of 70.6% in the U.S. 2004 presidential election, compared to 58% turnout nationally. Turnout of the registered voters in Oregon was even 86% (Vote by mail act, 2006). In 2005 U.K. general election, there also was a noticeable increase of postal voters (Sanders et al., 2005). But because absentee voting requires advanced planning and is subject to varying local restrictions, it does not address last minute scheduling conflicts or delays and problems arising at physical polling places such as overcrowding, staffing difficulties, or technical failure (as illustrated in Voters Unite, 2007).

Voting technologies have not remained static: handmarked paper ballots have been replaced by punch-outs, optical scanners, mechanical lever machines, and direct recording electronic machine (CalTech-MIT, 2001). As a natural extension of EVS, REVS are an entirely automated electronic voting environment using computers and telecommunication technologies for remote access. REVS also eliminate manual registration verification (a timeconsuming component of most voting experiences), facilitate monitoring, voting and tallying, and give accurate and immediate results. REVS place the voter in a position of choice: the traditional polling booth, an absentee ballot, or remote voting. REVS may improve participation via increased voter convenience and by saving travel time, reducing waiting, and lessening congestion. While no one technology can solve all the problems facing election administrators, REVS overcome some of the limitations of prevailing election technologies, and not surprisingly, introduce new issues.

The debate on the pros and cons of remote electronic voting has raised flags of security, feasibility, privacy protection, voter accessibility, and turnout (e.g., Mohen and Gildden, 2001; Rubin, 2002). Despite these concerns, several American states and the U.S. government are exploring REVS via the secure electronic registration and voting experiment (SERVE) (Jefferson et al., 2004). In the U.K., more than 50 pilot tests using various election technologies have been conducted since 2000 (Electoral Commission, 2003); for national elections, the U.K. has identified electronic voting to aid in their goal of increasing voter participation (Henry, 2003). Brazil has already held an all-electronic national election (Riebeek, 2002); and the Netherlands successfully used the Internet in an election with 70,000 voters (Pieters and Kiniry, 2005).

Research on electronic voting spans a number of disciplines. Table 1 summarizes work by computer science (system design and security protocols), psychology (voter motivation), political science (influence of voting mechanisms on participation), economics (influence of economic status on voter power), ethical science (voting equity), and decision sciences (voting analysis methods). Although many EVS have been developed – one such being the Accuvote-TS direct record system, which displays the ballot and feedback on-screen (Wikipedia, 2006) – robustness, correctness, and security remain major challenges (e.g., Kohno et al., 2004).

What is missing from the existing e-government research is the fundamental question of whether voters would like to use the REVS and what characteristics are valued in REVS. Noted technology observer Peter Neumann (2005) suggests that human behavior issues will prove critical to the success of these sensitive technologies: voters’ attitudes towards REVS and election participation via REVS will have decisive impact on acceptance and use. Information Systems (IS) research suggests that if REVS are like other technologies, characteristics of REVS technologies in general, and particularly those that differ from those traditional in-booth voting machines (whether mechanical or electronic), are likely to have an impact on voters’ decisions to participate in an election (Orlikowski and Iacono, 2001). This perspective is endorsed by the findings of the U.K. from its many pilot tests, that the key issue is the willingness of citizens to use voting technologies (LGA, 2002).

While remote voting brings increased convenience, voters may have new concerns about privacy protection, counting accuracy, and the resources and tools needed to access and use REVS. Telephone and web-based REVS are two of the most likely media to host remote voting (LGA, 2002); each has its own strengths and drawbacks. We expect that voters will value some features over others and make their participation decisions accordingly.

In this study, we explore the potential for REVS characteristics to influence voters’ acceptance of REVS and election participation decisions. Our research questions are: Which characteristics of REVS might affect voter’s participation intention? Which characteristics are likely to impact their preferences for different voting mechanisms? We hope to provide insights to practitioners (e.g., election administrators, policy makers, and vendors) for REVS design and implementation. Because little research has been done from a voter-technology usage perspective, this study will address a gap in e-voting system research and address the relevance of models of user’s acceptance of technology to a technology setting with complex power and choice relationships.

## Theoretical foundation and research model

Our model – the REVS participation intention model – draws from two literature streams: information technology adoption and EVS design.

## Individual technology acceptance

Determinants of an IS’s acceptance and use have been addressed for technologies ranging from personal e-mail to enterprise resource planning systems (e.g., Davis, 1989; Taylor and Todd, 1995; Hwang, 2005). Of the many models to study adoption of information technology, the Davis (1989) technology adoption model (TAM) is the most widely applied. Because REVS are a new application of information technology subject to individual choice by users (i.e., voters), TAM is a sound choice to begin to understand voters’ technology adoption decisions. A useful resource is the comprehensive review by Venkatesh et al. (2003) of an extensive body of individual information technology acceptance research; the result is the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), which incorporates significant determinants from studies based on TAM and seven other adoption models. Empirical comparisons between UTAUT and eight other models in the technology adoption stream showed that UTAUT can explain about 70% of variance in behavior intention and 48% of the variance in system use.

In UTAUT, three factors – performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence – directly impact an individual’s behavioral intention to use a technology. Performance expectancy is ‘the degree to which an individual believes that using the system will help him or her to attain gains in job performance’ (Venkatesh et al., 2003, p. 447). Effort expectancy refers to the degree of ease associated with the use of the system. Social influence is the degree to which an individual perceives that important others believe that he or she should use the new system. Gender and age moderate these effects on adoption as men and younger workers tend to care more about whether the system can improve the performance when they consider using the system, while women and older workers are more concerned about how much effort a new system will require. In addition, Venkatesh et al. (2003) found that facilitating conditions – the degree to which an individual believes that an organizational and technical infrastructure exists to support the use of the system – will significantly impact usage.

In the context of remote voting, performance expectancy can be represented by specific features of REVS, such as accurate tallying and privacy protection. Effort expectancy, also called ‘ease of use’ in TAM, is included as a core construct. Facilitating conditions can be reflected by the accessibility of REVS. At this point in our understanding, we exclude social influence from our model based on the primarily individual nature of the decision to use REVS or not; this is consistent with the finding of Carter and Be´langer (2005) that social impact does not have a significant impact on a citizen’s intention to use e-government services.

## E-voting design considerations

While election administrators make the formal adoption decision about which technologies to buy and implement, their decisions are heavily scrutinized (Solop, 2001). Whether on-site or remote, individual voters still control their use of e-voting technologies; this is reflected in practices such as the requirement that polling places accommodate requests to vote using a paper ballot. In a governmental election setting, requirements for remote e-voting might differ from those for less sensitive or narrower-scoped balloting such as election of a corporate board or a local school board. Hiller and Be´langer (2001) note that the design and development of REVS have particular legal issues not faced by similar technologies such as e-commerce. A case in point is the first online election in Arizona that was nearly cancelled due to concerns over equal accessibility to all voters based on knowledge requirements and physical access to voting devices (Solop, 2001).

As technologies evolve, specific mechanisms and design choices are likely to alleviate some concerns and raise the visibility of others. Because election administrators make decisions about voting technologies in advance of a voter’s individual decision about whether and how to participate, perceptions of voters about these technologies should be central to REVS design and implementation choices.

Several well-known vendors have worked on the development of EVS, such as EDS, Global Voting Systems, and Diebold. As early as 1997, AT&T’s research lab was involved in the design of a REVS for Costa Rica. Dr. Lorrie Cranor, who is now considered as an expert in e-voting technology design, actively participated in the project and intensively studied EVS and their use in governmental elections (Cypertimes, 1997). Drawing on these experiences, Cranor and Cytron (1997) and Cranor (2000) identified key design criteria for EVS: accuracy, democracy, privacy, verifiability, convenience, flexibility, and mobility.

Cranor’s design criteria and the work on technology adoption overlap on several counts: accuracy, referring to the accurate ballot storage, transfer and count, corresponds to ‘performance expectancy’ in adoption theory. Convenience refers to the minimal equipment and skills required to exercise voting rights with REVS and corresponds to the technology adoption concepts of ease of use and availability, which also correlates to ‘effort expectancy’ and ‘facility condition’ in UTAUT. Flexibility in Cranor’s list addresses voting technology design to allow open-end questions, a somewhat advanced feature beyond this study’s scope. Mobility highlights the effect of loosening location and schedule requirements but has no direct correlation to a UTAUT construct.

Cranor’s other design criteria are specific to voting as they address the basic requirements of any election: democracy, privacy, and verifiability. Democracy as Cranor conceives it refers to assuring that only eligible people can vote and they vote only once. Privacy refers to maintaining the sanctity of one’s vote, and verifiability addresses confidence in the tallying of results. Privacy also corresponds to ‘performance expectancy’ in UTAUT. Verifiability includes how to verify ballots are valid, prevent voter fraud, and assure that each vote is counted but counted only once. In remote voting, meeting standards of democracy may be aided by technologies such as passwords or smart cards; verifiability is not a new issue and it is not clear if remote voting raises new challenges in this area. We do think that unsupervised remote voting with its huge variation in physical setting (e.g., from private home or office to public library or school computer lab and to cell phone or PDA at the coffee house) raises the criticality of privacy over a traditional booth (whether the act is paper- or electronic-based).

Our review suggests that users will be influenced directly by features reflecting Cranor’s design criteria for accuracy, privacy, convenience, and mobility. Convenience is further represented by ease of use and availability. It is our assessment that the design criteria of democracy, verifiability, and flexibility are beyond the scope of current REVS technologies and therefore we have excluded them from this study.

## REVS participation intention model

The model combines technology adoption and e-voting design by proposing that an individual’s perception of the above five characteristics of REVS will affect his/her intention to participate in an election in which REVS are available. The basic model (Figure 1) suggests that the five characteristics independently influence intention. To keep the initial model simple, we do not include voter characteristics or task (i.e., election type) in the model. Each element in the model is defined below and is followed by our hypotheses.

Participation intention is the extent of the voter’s intention to participate in a particular public election using REVS. Prior research suggests that strong intention is very likely to result in actual behavior (e.g., Taylor and Todd, 1995; Venkatesh et al., 2003). We think that the perceived characteristics of REVS will impact voter’s intention to participate in any given election.

Availability means that REVS should ensure that each eligible voter has equal and convenient physical access to the voting system. In UTAUT terms, availability corresponds to a facilitating condition as identified by Venkatesh et al. (2003). In the U.S., equal access for all citizens to exercise their voting rights has been protected by law going back to 1870 and the adoption of the 15th Amendment (Weedon, 2004). Availability of REVS is an important factor impacting the feasibility of voting – lack of access to the technology is an absolute barrier to participation via REVS and therefore is seen as a barrier to assuring equal access. In Arizona’s recent online election, efforts to promote access included driving a van equipped with laptops around residential communities to reach voters without computers (Solop, 2001). It is no stretch to posit that perceptions of accessibility will influence REVS participation intention.

Ease of use is the degree to which a person believes that it is easy to understand and potentially use REVS to execute his/her vote. It requires that the interface of REVS should be easy enough for all eligible persons to understand and operate the systems correctly without special technical skills, prior experience, or more than cursory instruction. Perceived ease of use has been widely confirmed as a key determinant for technology acceptance (e.g., Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Venkatesh et al. (2003) found that ease of use measured as effort expectancy significantly affects users’ acceptance under both voluntary and mandatory use contexts. Thus, when it comes to REVS, ease of use should impact voters’ projection of their own future behavior in regard to that technology.

Mobility refers to the degree of flexibility the voters can achieve by eliminating location and time or schedule restrictions. Compared with the traditional voting booth setting, mobility is perhaps the key advantage associated with remote voting. In the U.S. election history, the attractiveness of absentee voting – the first form of remote voting – has demonstrated its ability to influence voter participation (Patterson and Caldeira, 1985). Compared with paper absentee ballots, REVS can provide equal or greater flexibility and while supporting last minute changes in voting plans. A U.S. Census Bureau (2005) study showed that around half of registered voters did not vote in 2004 presidential election due to factors associated with mobility: bad weather, health issues, transportation problems, or tight schedules. Reed’s (2000) research in Thurston County, Oregon, supported this premise with 91.5% of the trial voters stating that they would choose to vote online. More realistically, Done (2002) showed that 62% of registered voters in Arizona expressed their willingness to vote online. Outside of the U.S., the U.K. has looked to technologies such as mobile phones, Internet voting, and electronic voting kiosks in over 50 council elections to motivate voters and turnaround the decline in voter turnout (Xenakis and Macintosh, 2004). A survey conducted right after the 2001 general election in the U.K. also found that 66% of the nonvoters said they would have been more likely to vote if they could have had the choice to use phone or mobile phone (LGA, 2002). We propose that remote voting technologies will be perceived as having higher mobility and be associated with increased intention to vote.

Privacy in a REVS setting is more complex than in some others that sound similar such as e-commerce: who is registered to vote is public information along with the information that someone voted, but not how they voted (Hiller and Be´langer, 2001). Thus REVS must ensure that the voter is correctly registered, assure that the one remotely voting is that person, accurately capture her/his vote, but not link any particular vote to a particular voter. Potential threats to voter privacy may arouse strong negative voter reactions (Larsen, 1999). For remote voting in general, there is no minimum level of privacy assured (as compared to a booth at a polling place); in some cases, an individual’s vote may become ‘family voting’ (LGA, 2002). Citizens’ perceptions of privacy protection offered by REVS are a clear driver for individual’s use choice.

Accuracy refers to the degree of correctness that REVS can guarantee in ballot storage, transportation, and counting. In an ideal situation, REVS would guarantee that all validated ballots will be counted only once without any change or elimination (Cranor, 2000). The fundamental function of voting machines is to accurately record and count ballots; in the 2000 presidential election, miscounting of African-American ballots in Palm Beach County resulted in strong protests and numerous recounts (Solop, 2001). Insecure electronic voting created problems for Vivendi Universal SA’s shareholder meeting when the electronic vote was distorted via abnormal amounts of absentee voting (Sayer, 2002). In line with Venkatesh et al. (2003) who found that performance expectancy significantly impacts user’s intention to use a system, accuracy is a key performance expectancy of REVS and thus will affect a REVS participation intention.

Based on the above discussion, our hypotheses all associate higher intention to participate with the existence or extent of five characteristics of REVS. Read the following as beginning with ‘For elections in which REVS are offered, a REVS perceived to have y

H1 y higher ease of use will be associated with higher intention to participate.

H2 y higher availability will be associated with higher intention to participate.

H3 y higher mobility will be associated with higher intention to participate.

H4 y higher privacy will be associated with higher intention to participate.

H5 y higher accuracy will be associated with higher intention to participate.

Remote voting must rely on reaching voters through widespread, publicly available communication media; two such mechanisms are telephone- and web-based Internet. The wide distribution of these two media was also confirmed in the U.K.’s pilot testing (LGA, 2002). The cost and accessibility issues associated with choosing one REVS mechanism over another are significant, not the least of which is because the voters have a say in which technology they use. To help us understand how voters assess the attributes of REVS technological foundations, we explore how perceptions of the five attributes relate to the two leading candidate mechanisms but do not offer a specific hypothesis of one over the other.

## Research design

Survey, as one of quantitative methods, emphasizes on hypothesis testing and generalization of findings (Denzin and Lincoln, 1998). It is a realistic and efficient way to assess developed research models in a large sample in order to reach a certain level of statistical power and increase the accuracy of findings. Hence, this study utilized a self-report paper-based survey to examine REVS participation intention model among prospective voters. The instrument was developed, pretested, and piloted before being implemented among U.S. citizens assembled in a waiting area pending assignment to a jury.

## Item development

Based on Arizona online voting studies (Solop, 2001; Done, 2002) and the definition of REVS characteristics, a survey questionnaire was developed. As few empirical studies existed, most questions had to be developed to measure REVS characteristics as perceptions (of privacy, mobility, accessibility, and accuracy) and perceived participation intention. Ease of use was borrowed from previously tested survey instruments (Davis, 1989) with wording modified to suit the context of this study. A five-point Likert-scale was used to measure all the perception items where 1 is ‘strongly disagree’ to 5 is ‘strongly agree’.

## Pretest

Four IS faculties and four Ph.D. students participated in a pretest. Seven of the pretesters were American citizens with experiences of participating in U.S. local and national elections. Several were familiar with e-voting and adoption research and they all had expertise in developing survey questions. Based on their comments, the questionnaire was significantly revised to improve face validity.

## Pilot test

A pilot test was conducted using 137 MBA students. After excluding 16 international students and 14 incomplete responses, 107 valid surveys were used for analysis. At that point, we only had five constructs, privacy, accessibility, mobility, accuracy, and perceived participation intention. Exploratory factor analysis showed that participants had a hard time understanding accessibility (the degree of difficulty to which the voters are able to access and use REVS without any additional knowledge). We modified the questions and replaced the one construct with two – ‘availability’ and ‘ease of use’ – to clarify the distinction between degree of difficulty in access and degree of difficulty in use (once access is assured). The revised questionnaire was further tested among 297 voters participating in state elections; 283 complete surveys were analyzed by using exploratory factor analysis and structural equation modeling. The final version of the questionnaire (appears as Appendix D) was reviewed by four IS scholars who suggested only slight revisions, lending confidence to the face validity, construct validity, and reliability of this instrument.

## Study execution and results

## Subjects and setting

The survey was conducted among people called for jury duty in a major southern city in the U.S. Any U.S. citizen over the age of 18 can be called for jury duty, which provides a ready-made cross-section of people with time on their hands. The questionnaire packet contained information on the research project and brief description of the general operation of the two REVS mechanisms (web- and telephone-based).

While waiting in a library, all entering subjects were handed the survey regardless of socio-economic status, education, or age; while no records were kept, study administrators reported that the refusal rate was low. Over a 1-month period, 476 subjects took the survey; questionnaires with more than 25% missing values were removed resulting in 453 valid responses. Appendix A provides demographics of the subjects compared to the U.S. November 2004 election participants. Compared with national figures, we noted the lower frequency of 18–24 year olds and 55 þ year olds, and somewhat different distribution of lowest and highest educational categories. The sample has a wide distribution among age, education, and gender, and the racial composition is representative of the region (mid-south U.S.) in which the study was conducted and in which about 25% of U.S. voters reside. We conclude that this sample is suitable for examining voter’s perceptions of voting technologies.

## Measurement model

Several measurement models were tested (Table 5). After dropping two items each for privacy and accuracy, all the factor loadings were above 0.7, showing items loaded well on the construct they were intended to measure. This is consistent with all Cronbach’s alpha scores being above 0.8. Discriminant validity was assessed using average variance extracted (AVE) analysis; the square root of each construct’s AVE as higher than any correlation between that construct and other items. The results of the measurement model indicate sufficiently high reliability, construct validity, and discriminant validity to proceed with the structural assessment.

## Structural model

The structural model (Figure 2) for REVS participation intention was used to test the hypothesized path relationship between the constructs. GFI and CFI were both above 0.9, reflecting a good fit between the specified model and the study data set (Hair et al., 1998). The correlation coefficients – four of which are significant – are specified along each path. The five REVS characteristic constructs explain 42% of the variance in REVS participation intention. The positive impacts of Ease of Use (H2), Mobility (H3), Privacy (H4), and Accuracy (H5) on REVS participation intention were significant. However, the impact of Availability on REVS participation intention was not significant; H1 was not supported. We also ran several alternative models by specifying the relationship among the characteristics, such as Mobility and Availability. The coefficient significances between five characteristics and participation intention remain almost the same with worse absolute fit measures and change of parsimonious normed fit index (PNFI) less than 0.01. Hence, with theoretical justifications, we are confident that our proposed model has a good fit with the data.

UTAUT (Venkatesh et al., 2003) tells us that we can expect to see differences based on individual characteristics of the adopter. A post hoc analysis (see Appendix B) shows that the perception of REVS characteristics by different age groups; the highest and lowest perception scores for any characteristics vary by about 0.5 (on a 1–5 scale). Consistent with prior findings (Solop, 2001; Venkatesh et al., 2003), one age group showed a significant difference: older people (over 64) reported lower agreement (higher level of concern) across all the characteristics.

## Gender differences

Gender has commonly played a role in the acceptance of new technologies, and it has the significant moderating impact on effort expectation and performance expectation (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2003; Taylor, 2004). Figure 3 shows that for the female subjects, CFI and GFI both were above 0.90, showing a good fit between the model and the data set. Four factors – Availability, Ease of Use, Mobility, and Accuracy – were about equally important influencers on women’s perceived participation using REVS. The bold font on Privacy indicates that this item, while not significant for females, was significant for males.

Likewise, the model for the men (Figure 4) had high CFI and GFI scores showing a good fit between the model and the data set. Privacy, Mobility, and Accuracy significantly impact men’s perceived participation using REVS, with Accuracy’s coefficient value somewhat higher than the other two. The bold font on Availability and Ease of Use indicates that these items, while not significant for males, were significant for females.

The differences between female and male perceptions of REVS and the factors that would influence their use are compelling: three out of five characteristics differ between men and women. While Mobility is very similar in both models in terms of coefficient size and significance, Accuracy is significant to both, but has double the coefficient value for males: men think it is much more important for a REVS to be accurate as women do. The model can explain 42% of variance of women’s REVS participation intention and 48% of variance of men’s REVS participation intention.

## Discussion

This study examines the impacts of REVS characteristics on voters’ perceived voting behaviors and their preferred mechanisms for remote e-voting. Privacy, Ease of use, Mobility, and Accuracy are important correlates of REVS participation intention while availability was only meaningful for women.

The study reveals that increased availability cannot improve either telephone- or web-based REVS participation intention. This reflects the perception among at least some voters that the infrastructure enabling REVS may have sufficiently wide distribution and accessibility. In the U.S., penetration of Internet reaches 73% (using Internet somewhere) and household telephone reaches 96% (Pew Internet Project, 2006). This result parallels findings in some non-governmental elections; for instance, of all the votes processed by ADP during the third quarter in 2000, almost 40% were processed via the Internet compared to 20% by paper and 5% by telephone (Associated Press, 2001). Since the U.K. shares similar technology penetration to the U.S. – 93% of household have fixed telephone and 53% have Internet access (LGA, 2002), the U.K. government determined that media access was sufficient for remote election pilots. Note that while concerns over equality of access do not appear to be an individual preference issue, they are likely to remain a significant factor in public discussion and institutional acceptance of any voting technology.

A post hoc analysis found that among the participants 74% report feeling comfortable using a computer, but only 68% of them can easily access the Internet. In contrast, 87% can conveniently use telephone. This result is consistent with Carter and Be´langer’s (2005) finding about significant impact of ease of use on online e-Government services usage. Efforts directed at careful designing of a voting website interface may pay off but only if users can be exposed to online voting procedures before they make their access/participation decision.

As expected, increased mobility of REVS increases participation intention. The implication is that voters do appreciate the time and location choice flexibility features of REVS. Advice to e-Government practitioners is to emphasize the remote feature of REVS as a part of a detailed plan to convince voters the feasibility of remote voting processes.

Accuracy in the context of REVS conveys a form of ‘usefulness’ since it represents a core function that a voting technology must deliver. Its significant impact on REVS participation intention is consistent with Venkatesh et al.’s (2003) finding about impact of ‘performance expectancy’ in adoption of a wide range of technologies. What would convince voters of accuracy for REVS is not revealed by our study; its high importance is a reason that computer scientists advocate maintaining a paper trail (Rebecca and Neumann, 2002).

Our findings show that privacy significantly impacts REVS participation intention. Like accuracy, how much privacy, and what it would take to convince users is not revealed in this study. This finding implies that government should educate voters how to protect privacy in remote voting. It is promising that in the U.K. voting pilot studies, 71% of voters and 61% of nonvoters found the new methods (e.g., online and mobile phones) provided sufficient privacy (Xenakis and Macintosh, 2004).

Demographic differences could benefit from additional attention. Our findings support practitioner concerns related to age and gender. Older people are significantly less accepting of REVS. More detailed analysis on gender show three out of five factors differ across the sexes. Privacy is a factor significantly impacting men’s REVS participation intention but not women’s. Perhaps women are more likely to share information on their voting inclinations with others already, or believe that the legal context provides them with sufficient assurance of privacy. Physical availability and ease of use strongly influence women’s participation intention, but not men’s. We interpret this to be consistent with Venkatesh et al.’s (2003) finding that generally men are more technically oriented than women and are more likely to try new technologies; perhaps due to historically existing lifestyle and cultural differences, the difference may disappear as the new generation grows up. One conclusion is that assuring equal access at election time may require unequal training and communication access before the election.

## Contribution and limitations

By investigating the impact of REVS characteristics on the REVS participation intention and the choice of REVS mechanism, this study contributes to both research and practice. Based on this study, we can point to the relevance of key constructs from the long stream of technology adoption literature (TAM and UTAUT) to questions of the characteristics of remote voting technologies. E-voting has some particular requirements that can illuminate the sources of variation in adoption intentions considered in UTAUT and other models. While limited in scope – this study does not tell us the source of perceptions and differences in privacy, ease of use, and availability or the role of technology in assuaging voter’s concerns, these results do indicate that findings from technology adoption studies in other contexts are worth heeding. From a theoretical perspective, issues about differences in users often seem to lead to no particular insight as functional capability (usefulness) generally trumps other factors. In other words, why bother if the application does not deliver the needed functionality? Given similar levels of functionality, REVS adoption is the result of a kind of hybrid individual–collective choice: an individual’s choice to use, but collective decision to make such a choice available because users cannot independently adopt REVS without the initiative of their election administrators. Consequences of such a joint decision is more or fewer people (or people with different characteristics) voting – which is more than a case of wasted funds, higher service delivery costs, or slower service (as with other types of e-Government) – it is a matter of core democratic function. By looking at the voter’s part of the adoption decision via their perceptions of key attributes and technology options, this paper reduces a gap left by previous studies.

While the study showed a high degree of validity and reliability, limitations can be identified. Concerns about generalizability arise from the locale (a southern state). Interestingly, this state is a leader in non-REVS, which may somehow affect voters’ perceptions of traditional vs new voting technologies. This may be offset by a lower statewide Internet access rate compared to national averages (Pew Internet Project, 2006). Because this sample is perhaps more familiar with EVS use (in a booth setting) than average, their intentions to adopt REVS may be more meaningful than the perceptions of a sample with less voting technology experience, given both groups would lack real exposure to actual remote voting technologies. Voters represent an extremely broad population with a wide variety of experiences, and voting is a sensitive topic; the choice of a paper, self-administered questionnaire in a jury waiting area captured a crosssection of possible voters but provided a limited exposure to the specifics of the technologies. A chance to capture richer, more nuanced reactions to specifically enacted technologies would be valuable.

## Conclusions

In conclusion, we would expect that users will provide a more accurate assessment of their future intentions to use a system when they have more information about it; this is reflected in the finding that prior use is a major predictor of future use (Taylor and Todd, 1995). Yet, when it comes to voting, the adoption decision is fixed in space and time and users have no practical way to experience the technology except by using in an irreversible context – there are no ‘do overs’ in an election. In effect, it becomes vote our way or do not vote – a choice setting that places a premium on what users think they know about a technology. Thus, the adoption decision by voters may indeed be made based on weakly founded perceptions of the actual technologies similar to what subjects in this study experienced.

This study shows that while respondents prefer booth voting, the percentages of voters indicating a willingness to use REVS technologies is attractive for those concerned with dropping participation rates. ## REVS definition and descriptions of REVS technologies provided to survey participants

A Remote Electronic Voting System (REVS) is an entirely automated electronic voting environment that enables remote voting, eliminates manual registration verification, facilitates monitoring, voting and tallying, and gives accurate and immediate results.

## 1. Telephone-based REVS

A telephone-based REVS is an interactive voice response system. The voting process is similar to accessing your checking account by telephone. Voters call a pre-assigned number using a touch-tone telephone and input some code assigned by the government (e.g., social security number) for identification check. Then voters follow detailed instructions to vote. The system will confirm each choice and require the voters to explicitly push a button for vote finalization. If the phone call cuts off for any reason, the voter will have to start over again. After the fina confirmation, the vote is submitted to central databases. Voters cannot access to their votes any longer.

## 2. Web-based REVS

A web-based REVS operates on the Internet. The voting process is similar to accessing your checking account online. Voters will access specific voting website and enter certain ID and PIN assigned by the government. After a successful log-on, voters can read instructions and fill in an electronic ballot using a mouse. The system will confirm each vote and the final ballot. If the process is incomplete for any reason, the ballot wil be reset. After voting is completed and ballot is submitted, the window will automatically close.
