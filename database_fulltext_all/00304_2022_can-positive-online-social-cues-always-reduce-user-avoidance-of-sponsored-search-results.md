# CAN POSITIVE ONLINE SOCIAL CUES ALWAYS REDUCE USER AVOIDANCE OF SPONSORED SEARCH RESULTS?<sup>1</sup>

Honglin Deng Advanced Institute of Business, Tongji University, Siping Road, Shanghai, CHINA {denghonglin@tongji.edu.cn}

Weiquan Wang College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon, Hong Kong SAR, CHINA {weiquan@cityu.edu.hk}

Siyuan Li Raymond A. Mason School of Business, William & Mary, Williamsburg, VA 23187 U.S.A. {siyuan.li@mason.wm.edu}

Kai H. Lim College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon, Hong Kong SAR, CHINA {iskl@cityu.edu.hk}

Online social cues that utilize user-generated data, such as user reviews and product ratings, have become one of the key factors influencing online user behavior and decisions. Online users who shared their reviews and ratings about a product (or a seller) become an abstract reference group to a focal user interested in the same product. This study focuses on sponsored search results (SSRs), a type of unsolicited information that matches users’ search queries and receives high evaluations from prior consumers. We investigate the effects of positive social cues on alleviating users’ avoidance responses toward an encountered SSR when searching for a product in a C2C e-commerce context. We synthesize the avoidance literature and identify three forms of SSR avoidance, namely, cognitive, behavioral, and affective avoidance. We apply users’ implicit concerns on SSRs to explain users’ avoidance of an encountered SSR. In addition, we extend social influence theory to online settings where abstract reference groups are posited to trigger social influence. We examine how and under what conditions the three forms of SSR avoidance can be reduced by various positive online social cues (i.e., product- and seller-related). We conduct three laboratory experiments. Results attest to users’ implicit concerns on SSRs and their avoidance of SSRs and reveal different effects of various social cues on reducing the three forms of SSR avoidance. This study uncovers the theoretical mechanisms of social influence on reducing SSR avoidance in online settings. It also offers practical implications for online search service providers to help online users’ decision making in their search process.

Keywords: Sponsored search, user avoidance, social influence, abstract reference group, internalization, implicit concern, laboratory experiment

## Introduction

With the development of the Internet, online search has become an inevitable part of users’ daily lives (e.g., Im et al. 2016; Li et al. 2017). When users conduct a product search online, they usually receive two types of search results: organic and sponsored (Animesh et al. 2011). Users generally prefer organic results over sponsored search results (SSRs) even if most SSRs are generated based on users’ search keywords (Jansen et al. 2007), presumably because users often consider SSRs as unsolicited promotion information (Fitzsimons and Lehmann 2004).<sup>2</sup> Figure 1 shows an SSR by Amazon when users search for “iPhone 11 pro max case.”<sup>3</sup> This SSR includes information of a sponsored product (i.e., a phone case), the product seller, and an aggregated product rating from previous buyers. In this example, this SSR well matches users’ search keywords, and the associated product is highly rated by others (an average five-star rating). If users neglect this product simply because it is an SSR, they may miss a good product alternative. Given the limited screen size of computer monitors and mobile devices, many good product choices may not be able to make it to the top few positions of the organic rankings. Therefore, sellers have vast incentive to work with online platforms to include their products in SSRs. How to effectively bring users’ attention to SSRs and invite users to include the SSRs in their consideration set then becomes a pressing issue that matters to both online users and service providers. To date, presenting positive user ratings and reviews alongside SSRs is a common strategy implemented by online platforms to approach this issue.

In the Information Systems (IS) literature, positive user ratings/reviews have been empirically shown to be effective in increasing users’ intention to adopt an IT because they serve as users’ decisional reference and exert social influence (e.g., Goh et al. 2013; Li et al. 2019; Yi et al. 2019; Zhao et al. 2018). These cues are also referred to as social cues. For example, when a user is considering buying an online game, a positive social cue like “rating: 9.5/10” can increase the user’s purchase intention of the game (Zhu and Zhang 2010). Such social cues are pervasive in online settings; some cues are about products, whereas others are about sellers (Dewan et al. 2017). However, for unsolicited information, anecdotal evidence indicates that presenting positive social cues alongside SSRs does not always increase their click-through rate (CTR).<sup>4</sup> This suggests that the effect of positive social cues on users’ responses toward unsolicited information may be more complicated than what we understand in the current literature. Despite having been theoretically and empirically shown to work in various contexts across disciplines, including psychology, marketing, and IS, positive social cues may not always be effective in an SSR context. Thus, understanding the underlying reason is of theoretical and practical importance.

This study begins the quest by drawing upon the literature on social influence and unsolicited information in online settings. We aim to examine how different types of positive social cues can increase user receptiveness toward unsolicited online information under different situations. Although positive social cues have been reported effective in many offline and online contexts, two unique characteristics of SSR context may render these cues ineffective under the following situations. First, when unsolicited SSRs are presented together with organic search results, users are inclined to prefer organic search results and likely to view SSRs with doubts and concerns (Jansen et al. 2007). This inclination is because SSRs act in sellers’ interest and may conceal possible disadvantages of a sponsored product and/or seller (Goldman 2006; Kim, Barasz, and John 2019). For instance, some users may doubt a sponsored product’s quality, whereas others may be concerned about a seller’s credibility. In line with the marketing and psychology literature, these concerns/doubts are referred to as users’ implicit concerns on SSRs (also called implicit theories or naïve theories in the literature), which are users’ preexisting assumptions or stereotypical biases about SSRs (Deval et al. 2013; Gawronski et al. 2003). Changing users’ responses toward a product formed based on stereotypical biases is difficult. A key premise of this research is that these concerns must be addressed to improve users responses toward SSRs.

Second, online social cues exert influence differently from those in offline contexts. Offline social influence occurs when people engage in recurring interactions with their local peers (Venkatesh and Morris 2000). By contrast, online social cues are provided by other users who share the same product interests but have no prior interactions with a focal user (Yan et al. 2019). These cue providers may not even be identifiable as online ratings are often aggregated (as shown in Figure 1) (Zhao et al. 2018). Thus, a focal user has no compelling reason to blindly follow other users’ opinions, which is a type of social influence often seen offline (Wang et al. 2013).

These two contextual characteristics of SSRs highlight a need for a nuanced theorization on a contingency approach to matching different positive social cues with distinct implicit concerns of users on SSRs. Therefore, this study’s first objective is to develop a fine-grained theory that delineates how different online social cues can be used to address users’ implicit concerns and thus improve users’ response toward an encountered SSR.

On top of the two aforementioned contextual characteristics, a close examination of the literature on user response toward online advertising reveals that users take various forms of response to avoid an SSR, collectively termed as SSR avoidance (e.g., Fossen and Schweidel 2019; Shin and Lee 2016).<sup>5</sup> A user may intentionally ignore (cognitive avoidance), dislike (affective avoidance), or simply not click an SSR (behavioral avoidance). These different forms of avoidance may not always be in concordance with one another. For instance, in Figure 1, the social cue may direct a user’s attention on the SSR (i.e., altered the cognitive response), but it may not necessarily be able to lead the user to click on the SSR (i.e., unchanged behavioral response). To develop a theory on reducing SSR avoidance, various forms of SSR avoidance should be distinguished so that effective mechanisms can be deduced for each avoidance form. This aspect is important yet largely neglected in the literature. Therefore, this study’s second objective is to examine the effects of positive social cues on different forms of SSR avoidance.

Based on our contingency approach, we theorize and develop a set of hypotheses pertaining to reducing various forms of SSR avoidance by matching positive social cues with different types of users’ implicit concerns about SSRs. To test our hypotheses, we conduct three laboratory experiments in a C2C e-commerce setting wherein SSRs and social cues are widely used. This study contributes to the IS literature by contextualizing social influence research to an understudied IS research domain of unsolicited information in online settings. We provide a nuanced theoretical understanding of the effects of positive social cues on reducing different forms of users’ SSR avoidance. Findings from this study also inform online search service providers on how to present suitable social cues alongside SSRs so that users would not miss good product alternatives in their online product search process.

This study proceeds as follows. The next section reviews the theoretical foundation of this study, followed by the development of our hypotheses. The three experiments are then presented. Finally, we discuss the findings from the experiments and the implications.

## Theoretical Background

## Social Influence: From Offline to Online Settings

Social influence theory was originally developed to investigate whether and how individuals are influenced by others (Kelman 1958). Traditional social influence research focuses mainly on local social networks. For example, Venkatesh and Davis (2000) apply social influence theory to explain how peers affect users’ intention to adopt information systems in workplaces.

With the wide spread of Web 2.0, social influence in online settings reaches far beyond local communities and prevails among online users who may not even know one another but share certain assumed/inferred commonality, such as users who are interested in the same product (Dahl et al. 2013; He and Bond 2015). This phenomenon occurs because people have the need to interact socially when making purchase decisions (Godes et al. 2005). In online settings, given that a focal user’s local friends’ or peers’ opinions are not always available, other online users with common interests, who have publicly shared their opinions (i.e., electronic word-of-mouth —eWOM) and are accessible anytime and anywhere, come in handy as the focal user’s decisional reference (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). These online decisional references are known as the abstract reference group (Sridhar and Srinivasan 2012). Acquiring and referring to the opinions of an abstract reference group compose a basic part of online social interactions and a foundation of online social influence (Chen et al. 2011).

Referring to the opinions of an abstract reference group may not always change users’ decisions or attitudes. In offline social influence studies, members of an individual’s reference group are usually local friends or workplace peers with a longitudinal relationship (e.g., Venkatesh and Morris 2000). Therefore, people often feel obligated to follow their peers advice to meet their expectations (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004). An online abstract reference group, however, is conceived temporarily upon a user’s search for a product (Dahl et al. 2013). The user does not have a concrete idea of the exact identities of other members in the abstract reference group, as their identities are normally disguised or not disclosed (Zhao et al. 2018). Thus, the user may not feel a strong obligation to follow others or blindly follow the opinions from the abstract reference group. Instead, the user will carefully assess the group’s opinions and adopt the ones that are helpful for her product decision, and her product attitude will be affected by these adopted opinions (Li et al. 2017). As such, opinions from an abstract reference group may not always be influential in online settings, which motivated us to investigate the circumstances in which opinions from abstract reference groups are influential.

## User Avoidance of Unsolicited SSRs

Extant literature classifies avoidance into three forms, namely, cognitive, behavioral, and affective (Cho and Cheon 2004;

Kelly et al. 2010), which correspond to the three basic attitudinal responses of consumers toward marketing stimuli (Bagozzi et al. 1979; Vaughn 1980). This study adopts this categorization of avoidance and defines SSR avoidance as users’ cognitive, behavioral, and affective responses to prevent the acquisition of an unsolicited SSR.

Cognitive avoidance of an SSR occurs when users intentionally ignore an SSR (Cho and Cheon 2004; Golman et al. 2017). This response emphasizes that despite having noticed the existence of an SSR, users intentionally avert their attention from it (Krohne 1993; Lavy and van den Hout 1994; Van Reijmersdal et al. 2016). Although sometimes cognitive avoidance of an SSR may be associated with muscle-driven responses (e.g., users avert their eyesight from the SSR zone), it highlights that users intentionally do not attend to the SSR and leave the SSR on the screen (Golman et al. 2017). A lack of user attention is an extensively adopted indicator for cognitive avoidance in the avoidance literature (e.g., Chatterjee 2008; Shin and Rhee 2017).

Behavioral avoidance in online advertising contexts involves refraining from performing a seller’s anticipated action (e.g., clicking banner advertisements) (Burke et al. 2005; Cho and Cheon 2004; Golman et al. 2017).<sup>6</sup> A low CTR of banner advertisements is considered an indicator of behavioral avoidance in the literature (Burke et al. 2005). Accordingly, in this study, behavioral avoidance of an SSR is reflected by not clicking on an SSR.

Affective avoidance of an SSR is associated with users’ feelings or emotions toward an SSR (Cho and Cheon 2004; Howell et al. 2014). This form of avoidance is usually related to dislike (or even hate and disgust) (Crites et al. 1994; Westbrook 1987) or an overall bad/unpleasant feeling toward a target (De Jans et al. 2020; Kirmani 1997). In our study, affective avoidance of an SSR is indicated by the extent to which users dislike the SSR (Baek and Morimoto 2012).

Each form of SSR avoidance has its unique characteristics. Cognitive avoidance emphasizes that users intentionally do not attend to or spend cognitive effort to process an SSR (averting attention); behavioral avoidance highlights that users are not taking action to consciously adopt an SSR (e.g., no click-through); and affective avoidance focuses on users feelings (e.g., dislike) toward an SSR. Reducing cognitive avoidance does not necessarily lead to a reduction in behavioral or affective avoidance (Turner 1991). A user may not click an SSR (i.e., high behavioral avoidance) or still dislike the SSR (i.e., high affective avoidance) even when the user attends to the SSR (i.e., low cognitive avoidance). Such a response may happen if, for example, a positive social cue is presented alongside an SSR, which attracts the user’s attention, but the user has concerns on the SSR that cannot be resolved by the social cue presented.

## Reasons for SSR Avoidance: Users’ Implicit Concerns on SSRs

To reduce user avoidance of SSRs, we need to identify the underlying reasons for such avoidance. Drawing upon the literature on consumer skepticism of advertising, we argue that users’ SSR avoidance arises from their preexisting assumptions or biases about SSRs, which are called users implicit concerns on SSRs (Deval et al. 2013). Although SSRs and organic search results usually do not differ in their relevance to users’ queries, users prefer organic search results to SSRs (Kim, Maslowska, and Tamaddoni 2019; Yu and Marakas 2019). This is because when SSRs are co-presented with organic search results, users may doubt the motivation of SSRs’ sponsorship (Boerman et al. 2017; Cho and Cheon 2004; Obermiller et al. 2005). Being sponsored by sellers, SSRs understandably act on sellers’ interest and may conceal possible drawbacks of a sponsored product and/or seller (Kim, Barasz, and John 2019). Therefore, users may perceive that SSRs have disadvantages as compared with organic results and believe that without sponsorship, sponsored products would not be ranked as high as organic results (Wang and Wang 2019; White and Horvitz 2015).

The e-commerce literature has suggested that users’ evaluations about a product and those about the seller are the two major factors in their decision making (Dimoka et al. 2012; Ghose 2009; Pavlou et al. 2007). We reviewed the marketing and IS literature on consumers’ skepticism, bias, concerns, and evaluations toward advertising (see Table E1 in Appendix E for a summary). Our review revealed that consumers’ concerns on advertising center on the advertised product and the advertiser (e.g., Darke and Ritchie 2007; Herbst et al. 2011; Yi et al. 2019). Accordingly, in our SSR context, users have two broad categories of implicit concerns on SSRs: product- and seller-related concerns. Among these concerns, product popularity, product quality, and advertiser credibility are among the most important aspects of consumers’ evaluations of advertisements (e.g., Isaac and Grayson 2017; Simonson et al. 1994). Therefore, this study focuses on three key implicit concerns: popularity and quality concerns about products and credibility concerns about sellers. We deem these implicit concerns as the primary reasons for users’ SSR avoidance in the e-commerce context.<sup>7</sup>

Regarding the popularity concern, previous research finds that users generally think that sellers promote their products through advertising “because [the product] is unpopular” (Lichtenstein et al. 1989, p. 166). For the quality concern, the literature finds that consumers perceive an advertised product as low quality (Lichtenstein et al. 1989). Compared with other products in organic search results, users presuppose that sellers may reduce their investment in product quality to compensate for the high expenditure on sponsorship (Kirmani 1990).

In addition to product-related concerns, users may avoid an SSR because of their implicit concerns on seller credibility. This concern is rooted in users’ skepticism regarding an unfamiliar seller or sponsor (Obermiller and Spangenberg 1998). Given that sellers in a C2C e-commerce context are largely unknown, users tend to question their credibility (Ou and Davison 2009).

## Hypothesis Development

Figure 2 depicts our overall research model, which shows the effects of positive social cues on reducing users’ cognitive, behavioral, and affective avoidance of an SSR. In this section, we first theorize the effects of social cues on cognitive avoidance. Next, we uncover the mechanism and the conditions under which positive social cues can improve users perception of an encountered SSR, which consequently reduces their behavioral and affective avoidance of the SSR. Corresponding hypotheses are developed and stated accordingly.

As users have different implicit concerns on SSRs, different social cues may be required to address these concerns. To address the three major implicit concerns related to sponsored products (i.e., popularity and quality concerns) and sellers (i.e., credibility concern), we identify three corresponding types of positive social cues. Two cues are related to products (i.e., product cues): sales volume cues and quality rating cues. Sales volume cues refer to information cues that suggest a high sales volume for a sponsored product, such as “803 users have purchased this deal” (Kuan et al. 2014; Luo et al. 2014). Quality rating cues indicate high ratings regarding the quality of a sponsored product (Li and Hitt 2010). The third type of social cue, credibility rating cues, is related to sellers and presents the accumulated positive evaluations on the focal seller’s overall credibility (Metzger and Flanagin 2013; Sundar et al. 2009).

## Effects of Social Cues on Cognitive Avoidance

We posit that a positive social cue reduces users’ cognitive avoidance of an SSR, regardless of the type of social cue presented. Cognitive avoidance occurs when users avert their attention from an SSR despite their awareness of the SSR. To reduce cognitive avoidance of an SSR, a social cue needs to (1) attract a user’s attention to the cue and (2) lead the user to exert effort to process the associated SSR.

When a user is facing high uncertainties in decision making (e.g., numerous product alternatives in the search results), a reference group’s opinions and decisions are helpful for uncertainty reduction, because the user can learn from the purchase and consumption experience of the reference group (Awad and Ragowsky 2008). A social cue makes the opinions of the abstract reference group accessible for the user’s reference (Rhue and Sundararajan 2019), and these opinions carry much weight in the user’s attention allocation (Cheung and Lee 2012).

More importantly, other users’ opinions from an abstract reference group are generally considered trustworthy (Chen et al. 2015), because the motivation of online users to share their comments and ratings is perceived as altruistic without selfinterest or personal gain (Amodio and Devine 2006). In an SSR context, a positive social cue prompts a user that other members in the abstract reference group purchased or positively commented on a product (or seller) presented in an SSR. This specific SSR, albeit unsolicited, is relevant to the user’s search, and the social cue can help the user evaluate the SSR. Consequently, the user will allocate cognitive resources (i.e., attention) to process the social cue and the target of the cue (the SSR), which leads to a reduction of cognitive avoidance of the SSR. Therefore, we posit that positive social cues can reduce users’ cognitive avoidance of an SSR.

H1: A user’s cognitive avoidance of an SSR is lower when a social cue is presented alongside the SSR than that when no social cue is provided.

## Effects of Social Cues on Behavioral and Affective Avoidance: Internalization

Extant research has identified internalization as a key mechanism through which social influence imposes its effects (e.g., Cheung et al. 2009; Kelman 1958; Trenz et al. 2018).<sup>8</sup> Internalization has been applied in social influence research to explain how an individual forms or changes attitudes toward an object (Wood 2000). Acceptance of a reference group’s opinion and changes in users’ evaluation of a target are considered an indicator of internalization (Eagly and Chaiken 1993; Zhao et al. 2018). In this study, we apply the mechanism of internalization to theorize the effects of social cues on reducing behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR. We elaborate on the process of internalization of social cues and conditions under which internalization occurs in the following subsections.

## Internalization: Improving Users’ Perception of an SSR Related to an Active Implicit Concern

We argue that the effects of a positive social cue on reducing users’ behavioral and affective avoidance are achieved through users’ internalization of the cue. That is, internalization mediates the relationship between the presence of a social cue and the reduction of behavioral/affective avoidance. In the process of internalizing a positive social cue (e.g., a high rating on product quality), a user revises her initial evaluation of an encountered SSR and rectifies her evaluation in accordance with a reference group’s opinions inferred from the cue (e.g., the perceived quality of the sponsored product is improved) (Mannes 2009; Zhao et al. 2018). The user then applies her rectified evaluation on the SSR to decide her behavioral and affective responses toward the SSR (Flanagin and Metzger 2013). Figure 3 shows the internalization of a social cue.

We argue that not all opinions from a user’s abstract reference group will be internalized in an SSR context, which renders some positive social cues ineffective in reducing behavioral and affective avoidance. The psychology literature suggests that for internalization to occur, an individual must perceive that the reference group’s opinion is of value to her in improving her decision quality (e.g., Bagozzi and Lee 2002; Kelman 1958) and is “relevant to (addressing) her own concern” (Kelman 1961, p. 72). In an SSR context, for a positive social cue to be internalized by a user, the cue should address the concern that the user has when evaluating an SSR.

As we reviewed previously, a person may have multiple implicit concerns about SSRs. However, the extant literature finds that usually only one active implicit concern matters at the time of judgment (Campbell and Kirmiani 2000). Users apply this active implicit concern (instead of other inactive ones) to make a decision, especially when they need to decide quickly, during which they normally do not allocate much time and effort (e.g., a decision on whether or not to avoid an SSR) (Dou et al. 2010).

In an SSR context, a user applies an active implicit concern on SSRs at the time of judgment to form the key perception of the sponsored product or the seller involved in an encountered SSR (Campbell and Kirmiani 2008). This perception reflects the user’s evaluation of the SSR and leads to SSR avoidance. Taking the popularity concern on SSRs as an example, an active popularity concern on SSRs implies that (1) the popularity of a product is the most critical attribute/concern in a user’s product choice and (2) the user implicitly assumes that the specific sponsored product has low popularity (Deval et al. 2013). With this initial perception in mind, the user is less likely to click or like the SSR. The same logic applies to the active quality/credibility concern. Thus, to reduce the behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR, a positive social cue needs to improve the user’s key perception of the SSR.

We propose users’ perceptions on the SSR related to the active implicit concern as a specific set of mediators between positive social cues and users’ behavioral and affective avoidance. This set of mediators serves as a manifestation of internalization in our SSR context. These mediators include users’ (1) perceived popularity of a sponsored product when the popularity concern is active, (2) perceived quality of a sponsored product when the quality concern is active, and (3) perceived credibility of a seller when the credibility concern is active. We next elaborate on the effects of social cues and the role of these mediators.

## Matching Effects of Product Cues

When a positive social cue about an SSR can address a user’s active implicit concern at the time of judgment (see the upper path of Figure 3), this cue triggers internalization, as manifested by improving the user’s evaluation of the SSR, which ultimately reduces her behavioral and affective avoidance of the SSR.<sup>9</sup> We refer to this effect as a matching effect of a social cue, and the conditions under which this effect occurs as matched conditions. Two matched conditions related to product cues are as follow:

(1) Sales Volume Cue—Popularity Concern: a sales volume cue, as an indicator of product popularity, is presented alongside an SSR when a user’s popularity concern is active.

(2) Quality Rating Cue—Quality Concern: a quality rating cue is presented alongside an SSR when a user’s quality concern is active.

We assert that compared with the condition where no social cue is provided, users’ affective and behavioral avoidance can be reduced by a social cue in a matched condition (i.e., the matching effect) but not in a mismatched condition (i.e., Sales Volume Cue—Quality Concern and Quality Rating Cue— Popularity Concern). Internalization of a social cue will only occur in matched conditions for two main reasons.

First, under matched conditions, a social cue and a user’s active implicit concern involve the same product attribute (i.e., product popularity or quality). This congruence can increase a user’s psychological engagement with the abstract reference group given the shared product concern (Bargh and McKenna 2004; Li et al. 2021). Such engagement can help maximize the user’s value in product selection. In the consumer decision literature, consumers’ value maximization means achieving an optimal decision by selecting a product that is superior to what the consumer values as important product attributes (e.g., Morandini et al. 2015; Simonson 1989). For instance, when a user has a concern about product quality, the user may select a product with the highest rated quality by engaging with an abstract reference group, because this product choice is optimal for the user (i.e., maximizing the user’s value). Given that the product quality rating is provided by a reference group that values the same product attribute (i.e., quality) as the user, she is likely to take the quality social cue seriously and refer to it for decision making (Kumar and Benbasat 2002).

Second, members in an abstract reference group are believed to be knowledgeable and credible about the sponsored product, because they have purchased and consumed the product (Pavlou and Dimoka 2006). A user will believe that the choices of the abstract reference group (SSRs) should not be missed, and including them in her consideration set can optimize her decision outcome. As such, the presented social cues (i.e., abstract reference group’s opinions) will encourage the user to constructively reconsider and rectify her initial evaluations on the sponsored product (Flanagin and Metzger 2013).

Taken together, under matched conditions, because users perceive the abstract reference group as a credible source and believe that including SSRs in their consideration set enhances their decision quality, they will internalize the presented social cues and related SSRs (Kelman 1961). Such internalization is manifested by improving users’ perceived popularity (quality) of the sponsored product (Weber and Crocker 1983). Through improving their perception of the SSR (mediation effect), users’ behavioral and affective avoidance of the SSR will be reduced.

On the contrary, under mismatched conditions, a social cue does not directly address the active concern at the time of judgment. The social cue is not considered valuable to the focal decision task and cannot improve users’ perception of the key product attribute of their concern (i.e., no internalization occurs). Thus, the initial perception of a sponsored product is not improved, and a user’s behavioral and affective avoidance will not be reduced. That is, the effects of positive product cues on a user’s behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR are contingent upon the match between the product cue and the user’s active implicit concern. Thus, we posit the following hypotheses:

H2a: When a presented product cue matches a user’s active implicit concern, the user’s behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR will be lower than those when no social cue is provided.

H2b: When a presented product cue does not match a user’s active implicit concern, the user’s behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR will not be lower than those when no social cue is provided.

H3: The effects of product cues on a user’s behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR under matched conditions are mediated by the user’s perception of the sponsored product related to the user’s active implicit concern.

## Matching and Spillover Effects of a Seller Cue

The same logic and arguments described above for the matching effects of product cues apply to the matching condition of a seller cue (i.e., Credibility Rating Cue— Credibility Concern). By improving a user’s perception of seller credibility (via internalization), a high credibility rating cue will reduce the user’s behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR. We posit the following hypotheses:

H4a: When a positive credibility rating cue is presented and a user’s credibility concern is active, the user’s behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR will be lower than those when no social cue is provided.

H4b: The effects of the credibility rating cue on a user’s behavioral and affective avoidance of the SSR are mediated by the user’s perception of seller credibility when the user’s credibility concern on SSRs is active.

In addition, unlike the effects of product cues in mismatched conditions, we expect that the presence of a positive credibility rating cue can reduce a user’s behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR in mismatched conditions where the popularity or quality concern is active (this effect is referred to as a spillover effect hereafter).<sup>10</sup> The primary premise for the spillover effect is that a credibility rating cue influences a user’s general perception of the seller, and this effect spills over to perceptions related to product attributes (e.g., popularity or quality) (Dimoka et al. 2012). A key difference between product- and seller-related concerns is that a user would perceive seller’s credibility as a general-level attribute and would use it to infer product-level attributes (e.g., Keller and Swaminathan 2008; Peter et al. 1999). The scope of judging a seller’s credibility is broader than that of judging a specific product attribute (Goldsmith et al. 2000). Forming beliefs on a seller’s credibility relies on an aggregation of many pieces of information, including product and service quality and market share (e.g., Fombrun 1996; Shapiro 1983).

For instance, Amazon uses the label “Amazon’s Choice” to indicate that a seller has high credibility; the Amazon’s Choice is labeled based on a variety of factors, including (product) rating to shipping speed. Therefore, users may assume the product quality to be good when they see a high rating on seller credibility.

Previous studies have also shown that individuals’ evaluation of a company’s credibility (seller credibility in our C2C e-commerce context) affects their perceptions of the company’s products in terms of various product attributes (e.g., Dimoka et al. 2012; Fombrun 1996; Goldsmith et al. 2000). When internalizing a positive credibility rating cue, a user may infer that the seller’s products should be popular and of high quality, because high product popularity and quality are essential parts that constitute a seller’s high credibility (Fombrun 1996). When a user notices a credibility cue that contradicts her initial concern on a particular product attribute, this cue can also improve her product evaluations along that attribute. Under this type of mismatched condition, the credibility rating cue remains effective in reducing behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR.

H5a: When a positive credibility rating cue is presented and a user’s popularity concern or quality concern is active, the user’s behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR will be lower than those when no social cue is provided.

H5b: The effects of the credibility rating cue on a user’s behavioral and affective avoidance of the SSR are mediated by the user’s perception related to the user’s active implicit concern.

Product cues are not expected to be effective when users credibility concern is active because building high credibility requires a seller to cover many aspects in addition to product attributes (Jarvenpaa et al. 1999; Utz et al. 2012). A single product attribute cannot reflect the seller’s overall credibility (Kazmi and Batra 2009). For instance, a knock-off product may have a high sales volume because of its low price, but users may still perceive the seller with low credibility (Tan et al. 2014). Therefore, high performance in a single product attribute is insufficient to improve a user’s low credibility perception of a seller.

## Methodology and Results

We conducted three separate experiments to verify our theoretical grounds and test our hypotheses. The three experiments were conducted in a laboratory in a university in Hong Kong. Experiment 1 verified our theoretical grounds of implicit concerns, users’ perceptions of an SSR, and their SSR avoidance. Experiment 2 examined the effects of the two product cues on the three forms of SSR avoidance and the mediation effects of users’ perception related to an active implicit concern on SSRs (i.e., H1–H3). Experiment 3 examined the effects of a positive seller cue on users’ SSR avoidance (i.e., H4 and H5).

## Experiment 1

Experiment 1 employed a between-subjects design (social cue: no cue versus sales volume cue versus quality rating cue versus credibility rating cue) (see Table 1). In this experiment, we presented a search results page (with an SSR) to participants and recorded their responses toward the SSR. Our purposes were to verify (1) whether users avoid an encountered SSR; (2) whether users’ SSR avoidance is driven by their implicit concerns on product popularity, product quality, and seller credibility; and (3) whether social cues can improve users’ perceptions of an SSR. For experimental purposes, we chose taobao.com as the experimental platform. The website is currently the largest C2C e-commerce website in China and one of the most popular e-commerce websites worldwide. We simulated the search results page to that of taobao.com.

## Experimental Tasks and Procedures

Experiment 1 involved 80 participants, with 32 males and 48 females, aged between 19 and 32. All participants had more than 4 years of Internet usage experience. They shopped at least three times on taobao.com in the last 12 months. Thus, we considered them all qualified to perform our online shopping and search tasks.

Upon arrival at the laboratory, participants were briefly oriented on the experiment and were informed that the study investigates “how users choose products from taobao.com.” Participants watched a short introductory video about the task they would undertake in the experiment. They were then asked to complete the following tasks in sequence.

(1) Eye-tracking task: Participants were equipped with an eye-tracking device.<sup>11</sup> The purpose of this task was to record participants’ gaze data as a measurement of cognitive avoidance. A set of calibrations was conducted in which participants were asked to look at a group of 16 dots on the screen sequentially. A research assistant (RA) immediately corrected any calibration error whenever it occurred. Then, participants were asked to browse a piece of sports news that was irrelevant to the experiment for five minutes to help them get used to wearing the device. Following the training task, participants entered a product-search task while wearing the eye-tracking device, and their visual attention on the search results page was recorded.<sup>12</sup>

(2) Product-search task: Participants were asked to imagine that they have an iPhone 5s and are searching for an iPhone 5s case on taobao.com for personal use.<sup>13</sup> We presented them with a search results page, which contained a sponsored result (i.e., the SSR) followed by organic results. All products in the search results matched the search query (i.e., iPhone 5s cases), and these products came from various sellers. Participants could freely browse the search results page without time constraints. They could click on any product (i.e., the SSR and/or any of the organic results) whenever they wanted to read detailed product information, in the same way that they would usually browse the search results page on taobao.com. Participants were informed that they would not be provided with detailed product information immediately. When a product was clicked, a new webpage was opened in a background tab of the multi-tab Internet browser. The RA told participants that the webpage in the background tab involved detailed product information. However, we instructed participants, for experimental purposes, not to switch to that page but to stay on the search results page until they finished browsing the search results page and answered a posttask questionnaire. This instruction aimed to minimize potential confounding effects of different product information presented. All click behaviors were recorded by a computer server that hosted the experimental website.

## Discussion of Experiment 1

The results of Experiment 1 provide compelling evidence for our theoretical ground. First, we verified that most participants avoided unsolicited SSRs. Second, we confirmed that the three implicit concerns were applied by participants when encountering SSRs. Third, the positive social cues in our experiment improved participants’ perceptions of the SSR as we expected.

## Experiment 2

## Experimental Design

We conducted Experiment 2 to test the posited matching effects of product cues and the mediating role of participants’ perception of an SSR related to an active implicit concern (i.e., H1–H3). We employed a 2 (active implicit concern: a popularity concern versus a quality concern) × 3 (social cue presented: no cue versus a sales volume cue versus a quality rating cue) between-subjects design (Table 2). A control group, wherein participants did not receive implicit concern activation manipulation, and no social cue was presented on the search results page, was also included. The purpose of the control group was to check the success of our manipulation of active implicit concern. Each group had 28 participants, with a half assigned to an eye-tracking condition (except for the control group).

## Experimental Tasks and Procedures

We recruited 196 participants for Experiment 2. Participants had similar demographic backgrounds (e.g., age and Internet usage experience) to those of Experiment 1. The experi mental tasks (i.e., an eye-tracking task and a product-search task), procedures employed, and the search results page were similar to those in Experiment 1, except that we added an implicit-concern-activation task (omitted for participants assigned to the control group) before the eye-tracking task (or before product-search task for participants in non-eye-tracking conditions). The objective of this additional task was to activate a specific implicit concern about SSRs.

Users may have various implicit concerns on SSRs in general but apply the active implicit concern to form their perception of the sponsored product or the seller (Campbell and Kirmiani

2008). To operationalize an active implicit concern in laboratory settings, scholars from the marketing and psychology fields apply a priming method to activate a specific implicit concern; this activation brings an implicit concern to a user’s working memory and enhances its accessibility during the process of decision making for an experimental task (Kan et al. 2014). A common method of priming that e-commerce studies apply is to expose participants to a cognitive stimulus, such as reading an article related to an implicit concern (e.g., Dou et al. 2010; Mandel and Johnson 2002).

We also adopted priming to activate an implicit concern in an SSR context. Specifically, in the implicit-concern-activation task, we asked participants to read an implicit-concernactivation article as elaborated in the following paragraph.

Implicit-concern-activation Article. The article, which was written for our experimental purposes, mimicked a recent marketing survey report by a world-famous consulting company that focused on the phenomenon of sponsored search on taobao.com. The article mainly addresses the question: For what kinds of products do sellers tend to use sponsoring on taobao.com? For the condition in which a popularity concern was active, the article concluded that “86% of the sellers who are using sponsoring are doing so because of their lack of confidence in their products’ popularity.” For the condition in which a quality concern was active, the article concluded that “86% of the sellers who are using sponsoring are doing so because of their lack of confidence in users’ acceptance in the quality of their products.” The article then provided justification for the conclusion that “product popularity (quality) has become one of the important factors that affect the ranking in the search results pages. Products lacking popularity (of low quality) are placed after the first 10 pages. However, users rarely browse search results that are listed after the first 10 pages.” Except for the aforementioned arguments, all other content of the articles were identical across different conditions, including a brief introduction of sponsored search on taobao.com and a description of the consulting company.

Measurements. Three sets of measurements were used to evaluate the three forms of SSR avoidance. First, a participant’s cognitive avoidance of the SSR was captured by three self-reported items adapted from the literature (Cho and Cheon 2004) as well as the two objective measures from the participant’s gaze data (i.e., percentage of fixation on the SSR and percentage of time spent on viewing the SSR) (Cyr et al. 2009; Duchowski 2007). Second, a participant’s actual click on the SSR was used as an indicator of behavioral avoidance of the SSR (a binary measurement of whether the participant clicked the SSR or not). Third, affective SSR avoidance was measured by two self-reported items adapted from prior studies on advertising avoidance (Cho and Cheon 2004). Measurements for perceived popularity and perceived quality of the sponsored product were identical to those used in Experiment 1. Appendix D shows all the measurement items. In addition to the aforementioned key variables, three control variables were measured, namely, participants’ (1) familiarity with the online shopping platform in the experiment (i.e., www.taobao.com) (Bhattacherjee 2002), (2) familiarity with the search product in the experimental task (i.e., mobile phone cases) (Kent and Allen 1994), and (3) interest in the search product (Bloch and Richins 1983).

## Results of Experiment 2

Manipulation Checks. Table A3 in Appendix A presents the descriptive statistics of Experiment 2. We first checked the success of our manipulations to activate the two productrelated implicit concerns. The results indicate that both manipulations were successful. Participants’ perceived popularity of the sponsored product was significantly lower upon reading the popularity-concern-activation article than that of participants under the control condition (p < 0.05). Similarly, participants’ perceived quality of the sponsored product was lower for those assigned to the quality-concern-active condition than those under the control condition (p < 0.01). We also asked participants to rate their perceived importance of product popularity and quality in their product evaluations (sevenpoint scale). As expected, participants’ self-reported importance of product popularity (quality) in the popularity-(quality-) concern-active condition was higher than that of participants under the control condition (both p < 0.001). These results show that after participants read the popularity-(quality-) concern-activation article, product popularity (quality) became salient when they evaluated the product, and the sponsored product was perceived to have low popularity (quality). Thus, our manipulations to activate the two product-related implicit concerns were successful.<sup>18</sup>

During the product-search task, half of the participants of each treatment group were equipped with an eye-tracking device to capture their visual attention. The data were used to check whether participants actually noticed the SSR and the social cue. For the total time spent viewing the search results page, participants allocated 13.65% of their time on the SSR zone on average. As for participants’ visual attention on social cues, the average viewing time of the social cue was 3.18 seconds (ranging from 1.34 to 4.83 seconds). These results suggest that participants successfully noticed the SSR and the social cue. We also checked the perceived helpfulness of the sales volume and quality rating cues and confirmed that participants considered the sales volume (M = 5.44 / 7) and quality rating (M = 5.67 / 7) useful.

Effects of Product Cues on SSR Avoidance (H1, H2a, and H2b). To verify the effects of a product cue on participants cognitive avoidance of the experimental SSR, we conducted three ANCOVAs to compare groups with a product cue to those without a cue. We used the following three variables as DV in the three ANCOVAs: (1) self-reported cognitive avoidance, (2) percentage of fixations on the SSR, and (3) percentage of time on viewing the SSR. The results reveal significant effects on cognitive avoidance (all $p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Post hoc comparisons show that regardless of the activation of a popularity concern or a quality concern, compared with the no-cue condition, a product cue (sales volume cue or quality rating cue) significantly decreased participants’ self-reported cognitive avoidance and increased their percentage of fixations and time on viewing the SSR (all $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Thus, H1 was supported.

Figure 4 depicts the number of participants that clicked the experimental SSR (i.e., behavioral avoidance) under various conditions and the averages of their self-reported affective avoidance. A larger number of clicks of a group reflected lower behavioral avoidance. We ran logistic regressions to test the main effects of product cues on behavioral SSR avoidance.<sup>19</sup> In the conditions where popularity concern was active, the number of participants who clicked the SSR in the matched condition (i.e., the condition of Sales Volume Cue— Popularity Concern: 18 out of 28 participants clicked the SSR) was significantly higher (â = 2.38, p < 0.001) than that in the control condition (i.e., the no-social-cue condition: 4 out of 28 participants clicked the SSR). However, the number of participants who clicked the SSR in mismatched condition (i.e., the condition of Quality Rating Cue—Popularity Concern: 9 out of 28 participants clicked the SSR) was not significantly larger than that in the control condition (â = 1.04, p > 0.1). A similar pattern of matching effect was observed in conditions where a quality concern was active. Only the quality rating cue but not the sales volume cue reduced behavioral avoidance. Thus, the matching effects posited in H2a and H2b were supported for behavioral avoidance.

Regarding participants’ affective avoidance, the results also reveal a significant matching effect. In conditions where a popularity concern was active, the positive sales volume cue significantly decreased participants’ affective avoidance compared with participants in the conditions without a social cue (mean difference = 1.27, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). By contrast, when the quality rating cue was presented (i.e., a mismatched condition), affective avoidance was not significantly lower than that under the no-cue condition (mean difference = 0.30, p > 0.1). A similar matching effect was observed in conditions where a quality concern was active. That is, the quality rating cue (mean difference = 0.78, $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ rather than the sales volume cue (mean difference = 0.17, p > 0.1) could reduce affective SSR avoidance.<sup>20</sup> These results support the matching effects posited in H2a and H2b with respect to affective avoidance.

Test Results of the Mediation Hypothesis (H3). To test the posited mediation role of perceptions on the experimental SSR related to an active implicit concern, we first conducted Sobel tests (Sobel 1982). The results show that when a popularity concern was active, the effects of social cues on behavioral and affective avoidance were mediated by participants’ perceived popularity of the sponsored product (both $p < 0 . 0 5$ for behavioral and affective avoidance) but not by the perceived quality of the sponsored product (both $p > 0 . 1$ for behavioral and affective avoidance). By contrast, when a quality concern was active, the effects of social cues were mediated by participants’ perceived quality of the sponsored product (both p < 0.05 for behavioral and affective avoidance) but not by the perceived popularity of the product (both $p >$ 0.1 for behavioral and affective avoidance).

We also performed bootstrapping to construct the confidence intervals (CIs) of the mediation effects to assess whether the direct effects of the IV on the DV still exist after controlling for the effects of the mediators (see Figure 5) (Hayes 2013; Hayes and Preacher 2014). We calculated bias-corrected and accelerated CIs across 5,000 bootstrap resamples and specified a 95% CI. The test shows similar results to those of the Sobel tests but further reveals that social cues’ effect on participants’ behavioral avoidance was partially mediated by the perception related to the active implicit concern. That is, a significant direct effect of social cues on behavioral avoidance was observed. By contrast, the effect of social cues on affective avoidance of the SSR was fully mediated by the perception related to the active implicit concern. Thus, H3 was partially supported. ## Discussion of Experiment 2

Direct Effect of Social Cues on Behavioral Avoidance. Experiment 2 supports our posited effects of product cues on cognitive avoidance (H1) and the matching effects of product cues on behavioral and affective avoidance (H2a and H2b). However, the in-depth analyses on the results for H3 show interesting yet unexpected results. Specifically, our mediation tests show that social cues under matched conditions exerted a direct effect on behavioral avoidance after controlling for the mediated effects. One possible explanation for this direct effect is that when individuals’ implicit judgments are violated by a positive social cue, they may become curious about the cue and deliberately seek additional information to figure out the discrepancy by clicking the SSR (Meyers-Levy and Tybout 1989).

## Experiment 3

Regarding the seller cue, we posited a spillover effect for a credibility rating cue (i.e., H5a and H5b) in addition to its matching effect (i.e., H4a and H4b). Experiment 3 tested the effects of a credibility rating cue on users’ SSR avoidance.

## Experimental Design

We employed a 3 (active implicit concern: a popularity concern versus a quality concern versus a credibility concern) × 2 (social cue: no cue versus a credibility rating cue) betweensubjects design (Table 3). In addition, a control group (no implicit concern activation manipulation and no social cue presented) and two supplementary groups (with an active credibility concern and either a sales volume cue or a quality rating cue presented) were included. The supplementary groups were utilized to verify our arguments that product cues will be ineffective in reducing behavioral or affective avoidance when the user’s credibility concern is active. Each group had 32 participants. Among the 288 participants recruited from a Hong Kong university, 126 were male and 162 were female. They had similar demographic backgrounds to those in Experiments 1 and 2.

The experimental tasks and procedures were similar to those in Experiment 2. For the conditions in which a credibility concern was active, the implicit-concern-activation article stated that “86% of the sellers who are using sponsoring are doing so because of their lack of confidence in users’ recognition of their credibility.” The justification used in the article for this conclusion was that “seller credibility has become one of the important factors that affect the ranking in the search results pages. Sellers with low credibility will be placed after the first 10 pages.” Other contents were identical to the articles used for the conditions in which popularity and quality concerns were active.

## Discussion of Experiment 3

Experiment 3 provides compelling evidence for our hypothesized effects of a credibility rating cue about a seller. First, in support of H5, we verified that this seller cue could spill over its effects to users’ perceptions on the sponsored product, thereby reducing their SSR avoidance. Second, replicating the finding from Experiment 2, we confirmed the mediating role of participants’ perceptions on the SSR related to the active implicit concern (H5b) and the matching effect between a credibility rating cue and the active credibility concern (H4).

## General Discussion

This study focuses on online social influence in the context of SSR avoidance in C2C e-commerce. Online users avoid unsolicited SSRs due to their implicit concerns, even when these SSRs fit users’ search queries and are highly evaluated by prior consumers. Positive social cues can reduce users’ SSR avoidance. We examine three forms of SSR avoidance (i.e., cognitive, behavioral, and affective avoidance) and theorize the different effects of product- and seller-related social cues on these forms of avoidance. Although all these online social cues can reduce cognitive avoidance of SSRs, we uncover their sophisticated effects on users’ behavioral and affective avoidance when users have different implicit concerns. We attest to a matching effect of a particular product-related cue with a specific active implicit concern of users on reducing their behavioral and affective avoidance of an SSR. Specifically, to reduce behavioral and affective avoidance, a social cue needs to improve users’ perception toward the SSR by addressing their active implicit concern on the SSR. We also reveal a spillover effect of a seller-related social cue, but not a product-related cue, on behavioral and affective avoidance. A seller cue can reduce behavioral and affective avoidance, even when users’ active implicit concerns are related to the sponsored product.

## Theoretical Contributions

Our study offers several theoretical contributions to IS research on social influence and user response to unsolicited online information. First, we extend the application scope of social influence research by bringing it to an understudied IS research domain—unsolicited information in online settings, which has been created and fueled by the advent of Web 2.0.

Extant IS studies on social influence are primarily conducted in offline contexts where users’ adoption of information systems is influenced by their peers (e.g., Venkatesh and Morris 2000). Under such contexts, influencing peers are usually co-workers or offline acquaintances, who are identifiable and known to users. Moreover, in the IS field, social influence studies that examine the effects of social cues, either from friend endorsements or online crowd aggregated ratings, have concluded that these cues are effective in confirming users’ initial adoption of an IT artifact (e.g., Dewan et al. 2017). Such contexts differ from ours, in which the information presented to users is unsolicited and the source of information is unknown and likely unidentifiable to users (i.e., the user’s reference group is “abstract” in online settings). Such unsolicited information may not always be perceived positively by users and may likely be viewed with skepticism and concern (Fitzsimons and Lehmann 2004). Our contextualization of social influence research on this new IS research domain provides an in-depth understanding of online social cues’ effects on users’ response toward unsolicited information. To our knowledge, we are the first to bring social influence research to this nascent research domain with the perspective of an abstract reference group. Our theorization can serve as a foundation for future research in this area.

Second, we advance the theoretical understanding of condi tions under which positive social cues are effective in encouraging user receptiveness of unsolicited online information. We apply users’ implicit concerns to explain users’ avoidance of such information and investigate how social cues from an abstract reference group can be effectively deployed to improve users’ responses toward unsolicited information. Despite their importance in users’ decision-making, implicit concerns are largely neglected in the IS and social influence literature (Campbell and Kirmiani 2008). Implicit concerns of a target are users’ preexisting beliefs/biases, that is, their stereotypical association with the target (e.g., Herz and Diamantopoulos 2013; Wan et al. 2017). Changing users’ responses toward a product formed based on a stereotypical association is challenging. By applying the lens of implicit concerns, we provide a theoretical understanding of why the current use of positive social cues is sometimes ineffective in C2C e-commerce environments. Our investigation provides a new approach for future studies to examine online social cues’ effects in influencing user receptiveness of unsolicited information.

Third, this study advances research on online social influence by uncovering the subtle inner workings of internalization through the matching and spillover effects. We conceptualize users’ internalization of a social cue as the extent to improve users’ perception of an encountered SSR. Previous research on the general effects of online social influence either overestimate or underestimate the actual effectiveness of productrelated social cues when data are aggregated across conditions where the cues match and mismatch users’ active implicit concern during decision making. To our knowledge, this study is among the first few that look into specific underlying internalization mechanisms of social influence in online settings. The unknown yet complicated nuances of internalization in online settings are revealed. In particular, the matching effect revealed in this study indicates that not all positive social cues can trigger internalization. An accurate assessment of the effects of these cues on users’ decision making requires researchers to identify the conditions under which internalization occurs in an online setting. Our study provides a step toward this direction.

Fourth, the spillover effect examined in this study prompts indepth explorations on the occurrence of internalization in an SSR context. In line with the importance of user feedback on seller credibility and reputation (Pavlou and Dimoka 2006), the present study offers a theoretical explanation and empirical evidence of the spillover effect in users’ internalization of a social cue. Extant literature has considered an online seller’s credibility as an indicator of the validity of the information provided about the seller’s products (Dimoka et al. 2012). We extend that line of research by arguing that users’ perceived seller credibility is formed by their perceptions on product attributes, and that online reviews on seller credibility can affect users’ product-level perceptions (e.g., product popularity and product quality) (Fombrun 1996). This contention explains why a seller-related social cue can spill over to users with product-related implicit concerns, but not vice versa (i.e., from a product-related cue to users with sellerrelated concerns). Such a sophisticated effect indicates that rather than taking social cues as a one-size-fits-all solution, the nature of different types of social cues (e.g., productversus seller-related) must be carefully considered, and their specific underlying mechanisms should be examined.

Finally, this study contributes to the IS literature by delineating how to address different user avoidance forms of unsolicited information through social cues. The extant IS literature focuses on scenarios whereby users intend to adopt an IT artifact but does not capture another side of users’ initial tendency: avoidance (e.g., Dewan et al. 2017). Influencing online users becomes complicated when investigating different forms of users’ avoidance of unsolicited information. For certain forms of user response (e.g., cognitive avoidance), social cues always have a positive, direct effect. By contrast, to alleviate users’ affective avoidance, social cues need to rectify their evaluations of the encountered SSR. This nuanced understanding of reducing different forms of SSR avoidance may serve as a base for future studies on users responses toward unsolicited online messages.

## Practical Contributions

Users tend to avoid SSRs when searching for information and products online, even if these SSRs are valuable to users (e.g., matching users’ search queries and being evaluated highly by prior consumers). This study contributes to practice by revealing the reasons for such avoidance and examining how to improve users’ receptiveness toward SSRs by presenting different positive social cues alongside the SSRs.

First, this study shows that users’ implicit concerns are a primary reason for their SSR avoidance and reveals how positive social cues address those concerns. Our study suggests that online platforms need to strategically provide different social cues to maximize the cues’ effectiveness. Among the three types of social cues examined in this study, seller credibility cues are the most effective cue as they can address users’ product- and seller-related concerns. However, for new and unknown sellers on C2C platforms, seller-related cues may not be readily available; search service providers need to take initiatives to motivate users (i.e., buyers) to provide seller-related ratings and comments so that these seller-related cues can be accumulated and presented alongside SSRs (e.g., Lawrence and Valsiner 1993).

Although seller credibility cues are ideal, product-related cues should not be neglected. They are also effective in reducing users’ SSR avoidance if presented in such a way that they directly address users’ concerns. When users have concerns about SSR’s product quality, platforms need to present product cues that reflect the SSR’s high quality (such as high product quality ratings). However, when users have concerns about SSR’s product popularity, platforms need to present product cues that show the SSR’s popularity (such as the total sales or the popularity ranking of the sponsored product).

In online environments, product-related cues are easy to collect as platforms usually keep detailed transaction data, which contain product sales volume (popularity cue). Ratings on the product (quality cue) can also be collected from other platforms. Search service providers may want to identify users’ concerns on the promoted contents (e.g., through reviewing their online comments). Identifying such concerns will enable the platforms to ensure that the presented product cues match and address users’ concerns. Accordingly, users will internalize the matched social cues, and their attitude toward the sponsored content will be enhanced.

Second, this study identifies three types of SSR avoidance and reveals that a different approach needs to be utilized to reduce each of the three types of avoidance. If the purpose of an SSR is to catch users’ attention and to create product or brand awareness (i.e., reduce cognitive avoidance), presenting a social cue, no matter the type of cue, is sufficient. However, if the purpose of the SSR is to invite the user to take the focal product into full consideration in their decision-making process, platforms need to be selective when implementing social cues. They can provide either seller-related cues and/or product-related cues that match users’ concerns. Mismatched product cues are ineffective in reducing users’ behavioral or affective avoidance of SSRs. Indeed, the current way of implementing online social cues in practice is not always effective. The low click-through rate of SSRs with social cues presented testifies to this (e.g., the rate of Google was only 3.17% in 2019). We need to consider the type of user avoidance to be reduced (i.e., cognitive versus behavioral/affective avoidance) as well as the concern in the user’s mind when we present social cues along with sponsored content.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has a few limitations. First, we adopted laboratory experiment designs, a technique that has high internal validity but sacrifices realism and generalizability (Shadish et al. 2002). For instance, we instructed our participants that they could click on any product. However, to gain experimental control, they were not provided with product details in the experiment; the specific format of social cues may differ across different websites; and other potential factors (e.g., product risk and type) were overlooked. To increase realism and generalizability, future studies are encouraged to validate our findings in field settings.

Second, we activated only one implicit concern during users’ decision making to examine our posited matching effects of social cues. Different implicit concerns may co-exist especially when users make decisions that require much time and effort. Future studies can extend our work by examining the contexts where users apply multiple implicit concerns simultaneously and whether a specific social cue or multiple cues are needed to reduce users’ avoidance.

Third, we did not directly measure users’ internalization of social cues in our experiments. We drilled down to the specific mechanism of internalization in our SSR context and considered users’ perceptions on an SSR and the seller as the manifestations of their internalization of related social cues. Future studies can directly measure users’ internalization of the cues when investigating the effects of social cues.

Fourth, avoidance is opposite to the concept of approach, which refers to users’ intentional seeking of certain contents or objects. Future studies may extend our work by investigating potential strategies to increase users’ approach to advertised content in online settings. Despite many doubts and concerns about SSRs, some users may intentionally look for good deals from advertised content. We offer this suggestion because reducing users’ avoidance may not be equivalent to an increase in approach (Knowles and Linn 2004).

## Conclusion

Online social cues have become one of the key factors influencing user behavior and decisions. By integrating social influence theory and the perspective of users’ implicit concerns in an SSR context, we investigate the underlying mechanisms of the effects of social cues on reducing users cognitive, behavioral, and affective avoidance of an SSR. Positive social cues can address users’ implicit concerns on SSRs during decision making, therebyincreasing user likeness and CTR of an SSR. In addition, these social cues are generally effective in reducing users’ cognitive avoidance of SSRs. This study contributes to research and practice by opening the black box of the theoretical mechanisms of social influence in online settings, particularly in an SSR context. We provide a deeper understanding of the various forms of SSR avoidance as different types of user responses toward unsolicited online information. Our work can serve as a foundation for future research to investigate social cues’ effects when users encounter unsolicited commercial messages in online settings.

The purpose of including a control group was to ensure that the intended implicit concern, but not others, was activated in the experiment (i.e., a manipulation check). Some variables had no difference among the control group and other treatment groups. For example, the difference in perceived quality of the sponsored product between the control group (Group 7, M = 4.46) and the matched condition “Quality Rating Cue—Quality Concern” (i.e., Group 6, M = 4.54) was not statistically significant (p > 0.1). This result was because participants in the matched condition received two manipulations. One manipulation (i.e., the implicit-concern-activation article) decreased participants’ perceived sponsored product quality when the quality concern was active. The other manipulation was the social cue that increased such a perception. Taking these two manipulations together, no difference should be observed in terms of participants’ perceived product quality between the control condition and the matched condition. A second example is that no significance was noted in terms of the affective avoidance between the control group (Group 7, M = 3.84) and the group under a mismatched condition (e.g., the “Quality Rating Cue—Popularity Concern” condition, Group 3, M = 3.93). The social cues under mismatched conditions could not effectively reduce the affective avoidance. Thus, affective SSR avoidance of participants under mismatched conditions was similar to that of the control group.

## Appendix E

Literature Review of Consumers’ Implicit Concerns on Advertising and Promotion

To decide appropriate implicit concerns for our research context, we conducted a systematic literature review about users’ schemas and implicit bias toward advertisements (Webster and Watson 2002). We searched the top-tier marketing and IS journals by applying keywords such as “consumer schema of advertising,” “consumer bias on advertising,” “consumer concerns on advertising,” and “consumer evaluations on advertising.” Table E1 provides a summary of the review results. The results confirm that product popularity, product quality, and advertiser credibility are among the most important aspects of consumers’ evaluations of advertisements.
