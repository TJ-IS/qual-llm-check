## Abstract

Post-acceptance IS use is the key to leveraging value from IS investments. However, it also poses many demands on the user. Drawing on the challenge-hindrance stressor framework, this study develops a theory to explain how and why IS use stressors influence post-acceptance use. We identify two different types of IS use stressors: challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors. We hypothesize that they are appraised through challenge IS use appraisal and hindrance IS use appraisal, respectively, through which they influence routine use and innovative use. We evaluate our hypotheses by surveying 178 users working in one organization and analyze the data collected using consistent partial least square (PLSc). We find that challenge IS use stressors positively influence routine use and innovative use via challenge IS use appraisal. Hindrance IS use stressors negatively influence routine use via hindrance IS use appraisal. We then dive deeper into these findings using a two-step fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), identifying the presence of challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal as necessary conditions for high innovative use. We also reveal that the presence of hindrance IS use stressors and hindrance IS use appraisal only influences routine use and innovative use in the absence of challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal. We discuss the practical relevance and transferability of our findings based on a comprehensive applicability check. Our findings advance IS scholarship of IS use stress and post-acceptance use by showing how routine use and innovative use emanate from IS use stressors.

Keywords: Routine Use, Innovative Use, Challenge IS Use Stressors, Hindrance IS Use Stressors, Technostress, Appraisal, Mixed-Methods, Fuzzy Set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA), Two-Step QCA, Applicability Check

## 1 Introduction

The ongoing and everyday post-acceptance use of implemented information systems (IS) by employees is key to leveraging value from IS investments (Jasperson et al., 2005; Li et al., 2013). Literature on workplace IS use shows that IS use can be routine or innovative (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013). Both types can improve a user’s performance. Routine use is exploitative in nature. It implies that the IS is used in a predictable and standardized way to accomplish work (Li et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2019). It increases efficiency and reduces susceptibility to errors. Innovative use is when users discover new ways of using an IS to accomplish work in novel ways. It is explorative in nature (Li et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2019), such that users accomplish tasks more creatively and effectively. Thus, organizations benefit from users using IS routinely and innovatively. Both use types are central for organizations to translate IS investments into business value (Devaraj & Kohli, 2003). Even though both routine use and innovative use can be beneficial, little is known about when, why, and how they emerge.

Recent research shows that psychological factors relating to individual motivation can explain why some users use IS more routinely or more innovatively than others (Li et al., 2013). A further important research finding is that users’ perceptions of their IS use is shaped by IS use environments (Jasperson et al., 2005) such as the workplace because of the demands that emerge from them (Eckhardt et al., 2013). Most workplace IS use environments make significant IS use demands on users, including the need to deal with system breakdowns and requiring users to constantly learn to use new IS and work with IS under time pressure. Users are inevitably confronted with such demands because working without any IS use is almost impossible in most lines of work, as IS use has become an integral part of organizational processes and workflows.

Extant research into IS use demands provides two useful concepts on how IS use stressors shape IS use. First, there is substantial evidence that IS use demands burden users. Such perceived demands, which we refer to here as IS use stressors, are appraised as negative factors that hinder and adversely affect IS use and job-related outcomes (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Tarafdar et al., 2020). Second, and diametrically opposed, other studies indicate that users sometimes appraise certain IS use stressors as challenging in a positive way, such that IS use stressors stimulate them to increase their performance by seeking new ways of working by using IS innovatively (Eckhardt et al., 2013).

Both concepts indicate that IS use stressors are connected to users’ IS use, either in a hindering or challenging way. In order to provide an understanding of how IS use stressors shape IS use in terms of the two important use behaviors—namely, routine use and innovative use—we draw on the challenge-hindrance stressor framework (LePine et al., 2005), a prominent theoretical framework from organizational behavior literature. Drawing on this framework, we develop theory that links IS use stressors with routine and innovative use. Specifically, we conceptualize two different types of IS use stressors. Challenge IS use stressors reflect IS use demands that present the potential for users’ personal growth, development, reward, or learning, such as working with

IS under pressure. Hindrance IS use stressors are IS use demands that carry the potential for users’ loss, constraint, or harm—for example, system breakdowns. We examine how users perceive and appraise challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors and investigate how both types of IS use stressors and their appraisal influence routine and innovative IS use, as formulated by our research question:

How do challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors influence routine use and innovative use?

Based on the challenge-hindrance stressor framework (LePine et al., 2005) we conceptualize and define four new constructs. We argue that the influence on routine innovative use depends on how challenge and hindrance IS use stressors are appraised (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; LePine et al., 2016). As such, challenge IS use appraisal refers to users’ subjective interpretation that IS use demands have the potential to benefit them in terms of personal growth, development, rewards, or learning. In contrast, hindrance IS use appraisal refers to users subjective interpretation that IS use demands have the potential to affect them adversely in terms of loss, constraint, or harm. We draw on these two separate appraisal processes to develop our research hypotheses, suggesting that the interplay between challenge and hindrance IS use stressors and their respective appraisals influences routine and innovative use.

Our methodological approach is to evaluate our research hypotheses based on data collected in a single organization (N=178) taking a structural equation modeling (SEM) approach. Employing the consistent PLS (PLSc) method, we found empirical support for three out of four hypotheses. Our findings suggest that challenge IS use stressors have a positive influence on routine and innovative use via challenge IS use appraisal. Our analysis also indicates that hindrance IS use stressors have a negative effect on routine use via hindrance IS use appraisal. However, we did not find statistical evidence suggesting that hindrance IS use stressors influence innovative use via hindrance IS use appraisal.

To better understand the nonsignificant relationship between hindrance IS use appraisal and innovative use, we performed a post hoc analysis to expand our results and complemented the variable-focused SEM method with a case-focused method in terms of a two-step fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), which allowed us to complement the symmetric test (through SEM) with an asymmetric test (through fsQCA). Symmetric tests study the accuracy in high values of an antecedent condition indicating high/low<sup>1</sup> values of an outcome condition and low values of the antecedent condition indicating low/high<sup>1</sup> values of the outcome condition. In contrast, asymmetric tests <sup>2</sup> study the accuracy of a specific value (i.e., high or low) of an antecedent condition, indicating a specific value (i.e., high or low) of an outcome condition without predicting how the inverse value of the antecedent condition relates to values of the outcome condition. Thus, asymmetric tests such as fsQCA offer a means to analyze contrarian associations. It is assumed that even for large effect sizes, 10% to 20% of the cases in a data set display contrarian associations (Spivack & Woodside, 2019). Given that the SEM method did not identify a symmetric association between hindrance IS use appraisal and innovative use, this percentage of contrarian associations is likely to be even higher in our case and may hence offer an explanation for the nonsignificance.

To analyze this, we investigated configurations of antecedent conditions that contribute to innovative use. Further, for the sake of holistic analysis, we also did the same for routine use. Results of the two-step fsQCA confirm the existence of contrarian associations in the following two aspects. The first is causal asymmetry, meaning that different and not inverse antecedent conditions contribute to high routine or high innovative use rather than low routine or low innovative use. The second is causal complexity, meaning that more than one antecedent condition contributes to routine and innovative use and that a mixture of the presence or absence of challenge and hindrance IS use stressors— via the mediating impact of challenge and hindrance IS use appraisal—contributes to whether users engage in high or low routine and innovative use. These insights resulting from cases in the data set that follow contrarian associations provide rich theoretical insights into how challenge IS use stressors, hindrance IS use stressors, challenge IS use appraisal, and hindrance IS use appraisal contribute to routine use and innovative use. In a final methodological step, we assessed the practical value of our findings by conducting an applicability check (Rosemann & Vessey 2008) of our results in postsurvey interviews.

Our first contribution is offering a theoretical and empirical understanding of how routine use and innovative use emerge from the user’s appraisal of IS use stressors in their IS use environment. We conceptually extend the literature considering internal motivational factors as antecedents of routine and innovative use by showing that demands appraised in the external environment shape these use behaviors.

Second, we extend IS use stress research by conceptualizing two different types of IS use stressors (challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors) and appraisals (challenge IS use appraisal and hindrance IS use appraisal) and empirically validate their linkages. Overall, by integrating IS use stress and post-acceptance use research, we offer a novel theoretical perspective on how users respond to ongoing IS use demands. Finally, we contribute by using an innovative methodological approach that integrates SEM analysis with a two-step fsQCA analysis to reveal contrarian associations, followed by an applicability check.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. After reviewing the research discussing postacceptance IS use behavior and IS use stress research, we explain the challenge-hindrance stressor framework that guides our theorization. Based on that, we conceptualize the new constructs, which are then used to develop our research model and evaluate it using quantitative survey data applying SEM. Our results are complemented by post hoc analyses based on a two-step fsQCA that also considers cases in the data set that follow a contrarian association. Next, we provide an applicability check demonstrating that our results are valuable for and applicable to managerial practice. Finally, we summarize our contributions and recommend potential areas of future research.

## 2 Literature Review on Post-Acceptance IS Use and IS Use Stress

Our study explains the influences of different IS use demands on routine and innovative use. The literature review situates it at the intersection of the research strands of post-acceptance IS use and IS use stress research.

## 2.1 Post-Acceptance IS Use

The large body of research on post-acceptance IS use (Burton-Jones et al., 2017) reveals insights into antecedent factors of post-acceptance IS use (Bhattacherjee 2001; Bhattacherjee & Lin, 2014; Jasperson et al., 2005; Thatcher et al., 2018). However, research on specific postacceptance IS use behaviors remains scant (Carter et al., 2012). Broadly, research posits that there are two central types of post-acceptance IS use behaviors: routine use and innovative use (Table 1).

Routine use is defined as a standardized way of utilizing IS to support one’s work. It is characterized as exploitative use with a large degree of routinization (Li et al., 2013; Roberts et al., 2016). To a large extent, routine use reflects that the use of IS is considered a normal part of everyday work (Saga & Zmud, 1994; Sundaram et al., 2007). One implication of routine use of an IS is that fewer variations can be observed in the user’s use patterns (Saga & Zmud 1994). Its exploitative characterization is reflected in the notion that the IS is used in a standardized and predictable way and users become more efficient at appropriating benefits from it (Sun et al., 2019).

Innovative use is when a user discovers new ways of working with IS to support their work. It is characterized as explorative, emergent, and extended use (Li et al., 2013; Roberts et al., 2016). To a large extent, innovative use indicates that a user tries to innovate by discovering new ways of using IS (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Jasperson et al., 2005; Sundaram et al., 2007). Innovative users have a high willingness to apply the IS in new ways and areas (Nambisan et al., 1999) to achieve results not feasible without such use (Nambisan et al., 1999; Saga & Zmud, 1994). Innovative use is explorative when users actively attempt to experiment in their use of IS to do their work (Sun et al., 2019). Routine use usually precedes innovative use but both can occur simultaneously (Li et al., 2013).

It is widely acknowledged that IS use is key to transforming IS investments into business value (Devaraj & Kohli, 2003). Thus, both routine use and innovative use are crucial for organizations. Indeed, research shows that both positively influence task productivity, task innovation, and management control, thus enhancing users’ task performance (Sun et al., 2019). Both are also associated with the volume and diversity of managers’ ideas for organizational innovation (Roberts et al., 2016).

Extant research on the antecedents of routine and innovative use has focused primarily on motivation. Research distinguishes between extrinsic motivational factors, such as perceived usefulness, and intrinsic motivational factors, such as the intrinsic motivation to know, accomplish or experience stimulation. Both sets of factors drive routine usage, while only intrinsic motivational factors drive innovative use (Li et al., 2013). For routine use, the relative effect of extrinsic motivation has been shown to be greater than the relative effect of intrinsic motivation, and the reverse holds true for innovative use (Li et al., 2013).

In parallel, an emerging research strand also considers the potential effects of IS use environments and the emerging IS use demands on IS use (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Maier et al., 2015b; Tarafdar et al., 2020). The notion pervading this strand is that IS use is influenced by IS use stressors. In the workplace environment, for example, too many work demands can burden the user, which might inhibit them from being innovative in IS use. While there is some research on factors inhibiting IS use in general (Cenfetelli 2004; Cenfetelli & Schwarz, 2011), to date, little is understood about how IS use demands are related to routine and innovative use. The literature on IS use demands focuses on stress relating to the use of IT in organizations, which we address next.

## 2.2 IS Use Stress

The majority of research on IS use stress relies on the transactional model of stress (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984) to describe the overall stress process relating to IS use, including causes and consequences (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Tarafdar et al., 2019). Specifically, research has focused on five IS use stressors (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tarafdar et al., 2010): technoinvasion, techno-complexity, techno-overload, technoinsecurity, and techno-uncertainty. Other research approaches indicate that technological characteristics within the workplace environment might cause IS use stressors, including job insecurity, role ambiguity, work-home conflict, invasion of privacy, and work overload (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Maier et al., 2015a), or suggest that there is a close correlation between personality traits and stress from IS use (Pflügner et al., 2021a; 2021b).

IS research further demonstrates that IS use stressors evoke behavioral outcomes (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tarafdar et al., 2010; Tarafdar et al., 2015). It has been shown that IS use stressors can, for example, lead to quitting a job, reduced user performance, and less innovativeness (Maier et al., 2015a; Maier et al., 2019; Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008). Research has also shown that users adapt their IS use behavior in stressful situations (Maier et al., 2015b; Tarafdar et al., 2020). Indeed, emerging concepts indicate that IS use stressors that are perceived as positive by the user are not expected to lead to negative outcomes (Tarafdar et al., 2019). Thus, there are research opportunities to understand how IS use stressors and IS use appraisal can together shape post-acceptance IS use in terms of routine and innovative use.

## 2.3 Summary and Research Objective

Our review of the literature implies several things. First, post-acceptance IS use research has noted the importance of both routine and innovative use and has examined motivational antecedents, indicating that IS use environments and emerging demands can shape IS use. Second, research on IS use stress has focused on demands that burden the user and create stress, i.e., IS use stressors. However, such research has focused more on IS use stressors that hinder users and lead to negative effects (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008) than on the potentially positive challenging influence of IS use stressors on IS use. Our research objective extends these two literature streams by studying how the perception of IS use demands appraised in the IS use environment can influence both routine and innovative use. We, thus, examine how IS use demands that are negatively appraised hinder the user and how IS use demands that are positively appraised challenge the user.

## 3 Theoretical Background: Challenge-Hindrance Stressor Framework

The challenge-hindrance stressor framework from the organizational behavior literature (Cavanaugh et al., 2000; LePine et al., 2005) is a theoretical lens that explains individuals’ behavior in response to stressors and their appraisal. The stressor represents the perception of the presence of the demand and it is suggested that a given stressor is either a challenge or a hindrance (LePine et al., 2016). Challenge stressors are demands that present the potential for personal growth and rewards. Hindrance stressors are demands that present the potential for personal loss, constraint, or harm. Appraisal is the subjective evaluation of the meaning of a demanding stimulus (LePine et al., 2016). Thus, a challenge appraisal implies that demands are appraised as beneficial for the individual, e.g., in terms of personal growth. Hindrance appraisal means that demands are appraised as adverse for the individual, e.g., in terms of personal loss. Given these two types of stressors and appraisals, the challenge-hindrance stressor framework proposes that each type of stressor is appraised separately—challenge stressors are generally appraised as challenging and hindrance stressors as hindering (Cavanaugh et al., 2000; LePine et al., 2005).

Given that understanding, the challenge-hindrance stressor framework argues that challenge and hindrance stressors and appraisals influence behavior. That is, both the type of the stressor and the corresponding appraisal of the stressor together have positive or negative effects on individuals (LePine et al., 2016) in terms of emotions, psychological states, or behavior (Ohly & Fritz, 2010; Webster et al., 2011). This implies that the positive influence of challenge stressors on behavior is only realized if these stressors are also appraised as positive—that is, as providing an opportunity. Likewise, the negative influence of hindrance stressors on behavior is only realized if they are appraised as hindering, i.e, as producing a threat. Among other effects, hindrance appraisals may result in emotional exhaustion, job dissatisfaction, or turnover intentions (Webster et al., 2011), whereas challenge appraisals may increase creativity or lead individuals to behave proactively (Ohly & Fritz, 2010).

## 4 Conceptualization of Challenge and Hindrance IS Use Stressors and Appraisals

In the context of IS use as examined behavior, we draw from the challenge-hindrance stressor framework (Cavanaugh et al., 2000; Crawford et al., 2010; LePine et al., 2005) to distinguish between and define challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal, as well as hindrance IS use stressors and hindrance IS use appraisal, as shown in Table 2.

## 4.1 Two Types of IS Use Stressors

Based on the related management literature we suggest that there are two types of stressors in the IS use context in terms of challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors.

We define challenge IS use stressors as IS use demands that present the potential for users’ personal growth, development, rewards, or learning. Such challenge IS use stressors include, for instance, having high levels of responsibility that involve IS use, having a large workload that requires IS use, or having to perform complex tasks using IS (Eckhardt et al., 2013). These stressors have the potential to result in positive outcomes for the user, as the stressor is the perception of the presence of the demand. However, such stressors still need to be subjectively interpreted before they can influence the outcome.

In contrast, hindrance IS use stressors refer to IS use demands that present the potential for users’ loss, constraint, or harm. For example, it has been shown that computers freezing (Weinert et al., 2020), security issues (D’Arcy et al., 2014), and intrusive IS (Ayyagari et al., 2011) are hindrance IS use stressors in everyday work. These stressors have then the potential to result in negative outcomes for the user.

## 4.2 Two Associated Appraisals of IS Use Stressors<sup>3</sup>

We conceptualize two types of IS use appraisal. Challenge IS use appraisal refers to a user’s subjective interpretation that IS use demands have the potential to benefit them in terms of personal growth, development, rewards, or learning (Tarafdar et al 2019), meaning that the user expects that dealing with such demands will be beneficial and rewarding, even though it requires time and effort. Therefore, the appraisal is grounded in the evaluation of the IS use demand in the IS use environment (Jasperson et al., 2005). Working with IS under pressure might be evaluated by the user as, for example, an opportunity to find new ways of performing a task, an opportunity to be recognized by others, or as an opportunity to discover ways of saving time on similar tasks in the future or simply a means of improving general efficiency (Eckhardt et al., 2013).

In contrast, hindrance IS use appraisal refers to a user’s subjective interpretation that IS use demands have the potential to affect them adversely in terms of loss, constraint, or harm. This means that users may interpret dealing with stressors in the IS use environment (Jasperson et al., 2005) as requiring time and effort that will not be rewarded. For example, users may perceive a computer freezing as costing time and effort to fix the issue without furthering the goal of completing the intended task (Weinert et al., 2020). Similarly, IS security issues may also be perceived as costing time and effort without delivering rewards for users (D’Arcy et al., 2014). Thus, users typically evaluate such problems as hindering.

## 4.3 Summary

Based on these definitions, it is clear that both types of stressors stem from IS use demands (Tarafdar et al., 2019) in the IS use environment (Jasperson et al., 2005). While making appraisals, users evaluate the present IS use stressors based on their interpretation of whether the invested time and effort will be rewarded. We next develop our hypotheses explaining how challenge and hindrance IS use stressors mediated by challenge and hindrance IS use appraisal influence routine and innovative use.

## 5.1 Challenge IS Use Stressors and Challenge IS Use Appraisal

Challenge IS use stressors include things like high workloads that require the use of IS, complex tasks that are executed through IS, working with an IS to meet deadlines under time pressure, and the necessity of using broad IS-related skills and abilities. To understand how users react to the presence of challenge IS use stressors, we focus on how users evaluate them in the workplace environment and assess whether the stressors benefit the user, e.g., by enabling them to get more work done when using an IS and therefore being more productive. The presence of challenge IS use stressors is thus evaluated in the broader work context and in terms of whether such stressors provide opportunities for work-related personal development and accomplishments. For example, when users perceive high workloads that require the use of an IS, they assess whether this will provide rewards when done successfully. We suggest that challenge IS use appraisal is particularly likely when users believe that their time and energy investments will be rewarded in the demanding workplace environment (Crawford et al., 2010; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). We posit that when the presence of a challenge IS use stressor is appraised as such, and thus evaluated as an opportunity for the user in the workplace environment, it will influence postacceptance IS use behavior in two ways.

In the first scenario (H1), users consider using IS as a normal part of their work (Schweiger & DeNisi, 1991) and thus integrate it into their work routines (Laumer et al., 2016; Sundaram et al., 2007). When a challenge IS use stressor is present and users evaluate that the stressor will be beneficial to them, they will tend to use the IS in a routine way (Li et al., 2013). For example, when users experience ongoing high workloads that require the use of an IS, they incorporate the associated use of IS into their work routine, thus engaging in routine use.

In the second scenario (H2), users may expect to use the IS more innovatively to achieve their work tasks more quickly, efficiently, effectively, or enjoyably (Saga & Zmud, 1994)—for example, by finding new uses for the IS (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005) or by exploring the IS and identifying new benefits of using it (Nambisan et al., 1999). For instance, when working with an IS under time pressure, users might identify innovative ways of using IS to accomplish tasks, e.g., by finding new features enabling them to quickly complete their work (Eckhardt et al., 2013). Hence, based on the presence of the challenge IS use stressor and the evaluation of the stressor as an opportunity, innovative use can occur, such that the IS is used in ways that go beyond the original use intention (Jasperson et al., 2005; Sun, 2012). We, therefore, hypothesize that:

H1: Challenge IS use stressors have a positive mediated effect on routine use via challenge IS use appraisal.

H2: Challenge IS use stressors have a positive mediated effect on innovative use via challenge IS use appraisal.

## 5.2 Hindrance IS Use Stressors and Hindrance IS Use Appraisal

Hindrance IS use stressors include daily hassles such as IS breakdowns that make it more difficult or timeconsuming to complete work, unclear instructions on how to use an IS, or IS that are inadequate for accomplishing specific tasks (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Tarafdar et al., 2010). When users are confronted with hindrance IS use stressors, they evaluate their potential impact on their work as obstacles that prevent the accomplishment of work tasks because they require users to invest effort and time in handling them, which distracts them from the regular work tasks. Hence, the IS use stressor is evaluated as having the potential for a negative impact. We theorize that hindrance IS use appraisal is particularly likely when it is difficult or impossible for the user to determine whether their investment of time and energy will be rewarded in a demanding work environment (Crawford et al., 2010; Lazarus and Folkman 1984). This can lead to two scenarios.

In the first scenario (H3), users engage in workarounds to avoid the use of the IS (Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006; Laumer et al., 2017), demonstrating less willingness to integrate the IS associated with the IS use stressor into their work routines (Schwarz et al., 2014). In this case, they are not able to settle into routine use behaviors.

In the second scenario (H4), users are not motivated to find the time or make the effort to explore new or innovative potential uses of the IS to support work tasks (Nambisan et al., 1999). Thus, in this scenario, they are then less likely to use the IS in an innovative manner. We, therefore, hypothesize that:

H3: Hindrance IS use stressors have a negative mediated effect on routine use via hindrance IS use appraisal.

H4: Hindrance IS use stressors have a negative mediated effect on innovative use via hindrance IS use appraisal.

## 6 Methodological Approach: Quantitative Survey Study

To test the hypotheses, we adopted a quantitative research approach and collected survey data from IS users in an organization.

## 6.1 Organizational Characteristics and Sample Characteristics

Our empirical site was an organization that produces drivetrain and brake system applications for the automotive industry. At the time of study, it had more than 3,500 employees and an annual sales volume of around €500 million. The organization is headquartered in the EU and has factories around the world, including the United States, Germany, and China. Employees use general office IS applications for day-to-day work, such as ERP software, Microsoft Office, an email client, and other technologies relevant to IS use stress.

We collaborated with the organization’s CEO office to select potential participants. The number of invitations was limited by the CEO’s office and employees were selected randomly. In total, we invited 200 employees to participate in the survey and 178 employees responded. All of them worked in the same branch. Regarding the data sample (see Table 3), 59% of participants were male; the average participant was 37.5 years old and used IS (ERP software, Microsoft Office, email, etc.) for 256.6 minutes per day for work purposes.

## 6.2 Measures

The survey measured the relevant constructs including challenge IS use stressors, hindrance IS use stressors, challenge IS use appraisal, hindrance IS use appraisal, routine use, and innovative use. We also measured four control variables that included intrinsic motivation toward accomplishment, intrinsic motivation to know, intrinsic motivation to experience stimulation, and extrinsic motivation.

As far as possible, our survey items were based on measures validated in previous research (see Appendix A). For challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors, we adapted measures from previous scales based on organizational behavior research (LePine et al., 2016) to develop new scales for both (see Appendix A for more details). Challenge IS use appraisal and hindrance IS use appraisal were measured with three items each, which we adapted to the IS use context from previous research (LePine et al., 2016). Routine use and innovative use were both measured as suggested in previous research (Li et al., 2013), as were control variables (i.e., intrinsic motivation toward accomplishment, intrinsic motivation to know, intrinsic motivation to experience stimulation, extrinsic motivation) (Li et al., 2013). While the survey was administered in Germany, we followed Brislin’s (1970) recommendations and the translation / backtranslation procedure to ensure equivalence. We first established the items in English, then carefully translated the items into German using three native speakers. Based on those translations, we discussed differences until we reached a consensus on the items. Finally, a bilingual native speaker of English, who was not involved in the first steps translated the items back into English. After reworking minor differences, the final formulations are presented in Appendix A.

## 7 Results of Hypothesis Testing

Our empirical analysis adopts the consistent partial least squares path modeling (PLSc) method (Dijkstra & Henseler, 2015) and SmartPLS 3.2.7 (Ringle et al., 2014). Based on the widely accepted “rule of ten”, as well as suggestions that a given level of power depends on a wide range of different aspects (Kim 2005), our sample size exceeds the requirements of Steiger’s gamma (114.7) and RMSEA (101.0) (Appendix B). Moreover, we determined the extent of common method bias (CMB) with four different tests and concluded that CMB does not present an issue for the results (Appendix B).

## 7.1 Measurement Model

Each construct was measured by reflective indicators. We validated the measurement model by ensuring content validity, indicator reliability, construct reliability, and discriminant validity (Bagozzi, 1979). To ensure content validity, we either adapted items from prior research to the IS use context or developed new items for constructs that have not previously been used (see Appendix A). Indicator reliability indicates the rate of variance of an indicator that has origins in the latent variables. To explain 50% of the variance of a latent variable by the indicators, the factor loading must exceed the recommended threshold of 0.707 (Carmines & Zeller, 2008). This was fulfilled for all indicators and the loadings were confirmed to be highly significant (Appendix A) using the bootstrap method. Finally, we performed an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Our findings reveal six separate constructs: challenge IS use stressors, hindrance IS use stressors, challenge IS use appraisal, hindrance IS use appraisal, routine use, and innovative use. We next calculated composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) (Fornell & Larcker, 1981), and compared it to the recommended criteria, whereby AVE should be higher than 0.5 and CR higher than 0.7. As illustrated in Appendix B, both criteria were fulfilled. Discriminant validity describes the extent to which measurement items differ from each other (Campell & Fiske, 1959). As illustrated in the diagonal of the bivariate correlations in Appendix B, these square root values are greater than the corresponding construct correlations (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hulland 1999), thus fulfilling the requirement. We used the heterotraitmonotrait (HTMT) ratio of correlations criterion to assess discriminant validity because it is considered more reliable than the Fornell-Larcker criterion (Henseler et al., 2015). Using the absolute HTMT<sub>0.85</sub> criterion, we determined that discriminant validity is not an issue for our research, meaning that the measurement model is valid.

We also tested for multicollinearity. As indicated by the variance inflation factor (VIF) indicator, each VIF value was lower than the recommended maximum VIF value of 5 (Rogerson, 2001), with the highest value being 2.995 between innovative use and intrinsic motivation to know.

## 7.2 Structural Model

We used the coefficient of determination (R²), the significance levels of each path coefficient, the mediation effects, the effect sizes, and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) to evaluate the structural model. As illustrated in Figure 2, the coefficient of determination indicates that we explained 60.9% of the variance of routine use and 56.8% of the variance of innovative use. Based on path coefficients and significance tests, we identified one path as nonsignificant. Specifically, our results reveal that challenge IS use stressors significantly influence challenge IS use appraisal and hindrance IS use stressors significantly influence hindrance IS use appraisal. Moreover, our results show that challenge IS use appraisal positively influences routine use and innovative use and hindrance IS use appraisal negatively influences routine use. However, our data indicate that hindrance IS use appraisal does not significantly influence innovative use. Moreover, our results reveal that, of the control variables, only extrinsic motivation influences routine use and innovative use significantly.

To specifically test our mediation hypotheses, we applied a bootstrapping technique, confirming three out of four mediation effects. Specifically, our findings show that challenge IS use stressors have a positive mediated effect on both routine use (H1 supported; 0.128, $p < 0 . 0 2 5 )$ and innovative use (H2 supported; 0.139, $p < 0 . 0 0 5 )$ via challenge IS use appraisal. Hindrance IS use stressors have a negative mediated effect on routine use (H3 supported; -0.099; $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) via hindrance IS use appraisal. However, we found no negative mediated effect of hindrance IS use stressors on innovative use via hindrance IS use appraisal (H4 not supported; 0.040, $p > 0 . 2 0 0 )$ . We confirmed these results using other approaches (Baron & Kenny, 1986; Preacher & Hayes, 2004). We also compared the effect sizes of our control variables, which have been studied in previous research as antecedents of routine use and innovative use, with our target variables. The results indicate that only extrinsic motivation has a significant impact on routine use and innovative use.

Our results indicate that the control variables have a weak strength of effect for routine (0.118) and innovative use (0.186), while challenge IS use stressors, hindrance IS use stressors, challenge IS use appraisal, and hindrance IS use appraisal have a stronger effect for routine (0.463) and innovative use (0.294). Finally, Henseler et al., (2014) propose using the SRMR. The value of 0.06, which is far less than the recommended maximum value of 0.10 or 0.08 (Hu & Bentler, 1999), indicates a good fit.

## 8 Post Hoc Analyses: Qualitative Comparative Analysis

The preceding analysis supports three of the four hypotheses (i.e., H1, H2, and H3). However, the lack of support for H4 suggests that hindrance IS use stressors have no negative mediating influence on innovative use via hindrance IS use appraisal. This finding is contrary to what was expected and warrants further investigation. To explore possible reasons for this non-significant effect, we performed a post hoc analysis via an expansion approach (Venkatesh et al., 2016). SEM is a variable-focused method that specifically identifies symmetric associations. However, it is known that, even in symmetric associations with large effect sizes (e.g., as reported above for challenge IS use stressors, hindrance IS use stressors, challenge IS use appraisal and hindrance IS use appraisal on routine use [0.463] or innovative use [0.294]), 10% to 20% of the cases in a data set follow other, contrarian associations (Spivack & Woodside, 2019). A contrarian association concerns a situation in which a high/low value of an antecedent condition indicates a specific value of an outcome condition without suggesting that the inverse value of that antecedent condition is also related to values of the outcome condition, which might, in turn, be a reason for nonsignificant SEM relationships (see Figure 3, right side and red quadrants). Nevertheless, we observed a potential pattern in these data (e.g., see the similar pattern in both diagrams highlighted in the oval pattern), but determining this requires using a casefocused method, such as a two-step fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), which allows for the analysis of contrarian associations.

## 8.1 Two-Step fsQCA Analysis

We first display the cases in crosstabs for the two variables hindrance IS use appraisal and innovative use, which have a nonsignificant relationship in SEM. Using a 5-point scale for hindrance IS use appraisal and a 7-point scale for innovative use, we are able to display the cases with 35 cells (Figure 4).

Notably, Figure 4 highlights that most cases lie on the diagonal, as expected. Interestingly, the majority of cases are in the quadrant of low hindrance IS use appraisal and high innovative use (top left). However, in the bottom-right quadrant (high hindrance IS use appraisal and low innovative use) there is exactly the same number of cases as in the top-right quadrant (high hindrance IS use appraisal and high innovative use) and in the bottom-left quadrant (low hindrance IS use appraisal and low innovative use). This finding indicates a contrarian association because, with a symmetric relationship, most cases would be in the top-left and bottom-right quadrants. This suggests the need to perform a two-step fsQCA analysis to consider cases beyond symmetric associations.

We offer two potential explanations, which are indicatively illustrated in Figure 4.

First, the nonsignificant relationship between hindrance IS use appraisal and innovative use could be grounded in causal asymmetry. Causal asymmetry means that the causes for the occurrence of an outcome (e.g., innovative use) may be different and not the inverse of causes contributing to the nonoccurrence of that behavior (Cenfetelli, 2004). This typically happens when cases in the data set have contrarian associations. This cannot be captured by a variablefocused method such as SEM because of the underlying assumption that the inverse causes for high levels of innovative use contribute to low levels of innovative use (see Figures 3 and 4).

Second, the non-significance between hindrance IS use appraisal and innovative use could be grounded in the causal complexity of innovative use, which is typically characterized by conjunctural causality and asymmetrical causality (Misangyi et al., 2017). On the one hand, this means that users’ joint perception of challenge IS use stressors, hindrance IS use stressors, challenge IS use appraisal, and hindrance IS use appraisal contributes to high innovative use. This refers to conjunctural causality, meaning that an outcome (here: innovative use) does not have a single cause (here: both types of IS use stressors and IS use appraisals). Instead, an outcome “rather result[s] from the interdependence of multiple conditions” (Misangyi et al., 2017 p. 256). On the other hand, high innovative use might only occur when challenge IS use stressors are present while hindrance IS use stressors are absent. This refers to asymmetrical causality, meaning that the presence and/or absence of multiple conditions (here: challenge IS use stressors, hindrance IS use stressors, challenge IS use appraisal, hindrance IS use appraisal) may contribute to innovative use, depending on the presence or absence of the other conditions. Either of these potential explanations could underlie the nonsignificant SEM result (Figure 2) discussed above. Either situation could occur when there are cases in the data set that follow contrarian associations (i.e., between hindrance IS use appraisal and innovative use). Thus, we are interested in accounting for contrarian associations that do not become visible when using SEM with its assumption of symmetric and linear causality between variables and the outcome.

To study contrarian associations as well as causal asymmetry and causal complexity (i.e., conjunctural causality and asymmetrical causality), we used twostep fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA). QCA supplemented our SEM findings. Even though the main objective was to better understand the nonsignificant relationship of hindrance IS use appraisal on innovative use, we performed the two-step fsQCA on both IS use behaviors and considered both types of IS use stressors and the associated appraisals. This ensured a holistic perspective and offered insights into the characteristics of the influence of the two types of IS use stressors and IS use appraisals on routine and innovative use. In more detail, we identified whether any of these antecedent factors are necessary conditions or contributing factors for a sufficient configuration for high routine or innovative use.

## 8.2 fsQCA and Rationale for Using Two-Step QCA

We used fsQCA to explain how the presence and/or absence of multiple independent variables, which are represented as mathematical sets and called conditions, simultaneously influence the high or low level of an outcome (Ragin, 2000; Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). The simultaneous presence or absence of multiple conditions is called configuration (Fiss, 2011; Ragin 2000) and fsQCA enables the identification of configurations that are sufficient for the high or low level of an outcome. When a sufficient configuration is present, the outcome needs to be present as well. Furthermore, fsQCA also reveals whether certain conditions are necessary for an outcome. A necessary condition is a variable that is always present if the outcome is present but whose presence alone does not cause an outcome. In our analysis, we focused on identifying both the sufficient configurations and necessary conditions for high and low routine and innovative use. To analyze a mediating research model with independent and dependent variables as well as multiple mediating variables, scholars recommend a two-step fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) (Schneider & Wagemann, 2006). For more detailed information about two-step QCA and fsQCA, please see Appendix D. This approach uses three kinds of variables: (1) independent variables, also known as distant conditions (here: challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors), (2) mediating variables, also known as close conditions (here: challenge IS use appraisal and hindrance IS use appraisal), and (3) dependent variables, also known as outcome condition (here: routine use and innovative use). Using these variables, we followed a two-step approach. In a first step, we analyzed what configurations of independent variables influence the dependent variables. In a second step, for each configuration found in the first step, we analyzed what configurations of the mediating variables influence the dependent variable. These results were then combined (see Appendix D).

## 8.3 Data Analysis with Two-Step fsQCA

To calibrate and analyze the data, we used fsQCA software (Ragin 2006), which has been recently employed in IS research to study user behavior (Mattke et al., 2021; in press). To calibrate the data into fuzzy sets, we used the direct method of calibration with the maximum Likert value as the fully-in anchor, the median value as the point of maximum ambiguity, and the minimum value as the fully-out anchor (Ragin, 2008). To avoid fuzzy values with the value of 0.5, which would result in a dropout of the configuration, we followed previous recommendations and added a constant of 0.001 (Maier et al., 2021). Based on the resulting truth tables (see Appendix D), we conducted four separate fsQCAs. The first fsQCA evaluated the necessary conditions and sufficient configurations for high routine use and the second fsQCA evaluated the same for high innovative use. The third and fourth fsQCA evaluated the same for low routine use and low innovative use.

We tested for sufficient configuration using a consistency threshold of 0.80, which is above the minimum consistency of 0.75 (Ragin, 2008) and increases the overall reliability of the results (Fiss, 2011; Mattke et al., 2021). We also followed recommendations to use a frequency threshold of nine, as a higher sample size requires a higher frequency threshold (Rihoux & Ragin, 2009). We tested for necessary conditions using the recommended consistency threshold of 0.90 (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012), indicating the degree to which the cases with the same conditions display the same outcome. Finally, we set the coverage threshold to 0.60 to avoid trivial necessary conditions (Ragin, 2006).

## 8.4 Results and Interpretation of the Two-Step fsQCA

For high routine use, the presence of challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal constitute a sufficient configuration. For low routine use, the absence of challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal combined with the presence of hindrance IS use appraisal and hindrance IS use appraisal constitute a sufficient configuration. No necessary conditions exist for high or low routine use (see Figure 5).

For high innovative use, the results show that the presence of challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal constitutes a sufficient configuration. In addition, the presence of challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal are necessary conditions for innovative use. For low innovative use, the presence of hindrance IS use stressors, the absence of challenge IS use stressors, the presence of hindrance IS use appraisal, and the absence of challenge IS use appraisal constitute a sufficient configuration (see Figure 5).

In addition, the two-step fsQCA results show that the sufficient configurations for low routine use and innovative use are different and not the inverse of the configurations for the high level of these behaviors. Moreover, the coexistence of the absence of challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal—for low routine use and innovative use—thereby highlights the causal complexity (in terms of asymmetrical and conjunctural causality) because the presence of hindrance IS use stressors and hindrance IS use appraisal only lead to low routine use and innovative use if challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal are also absent.

As discussed above, our SEM analysis does not support H4. This additional two-step fsQCA analysis enriches and expands the SEM analysis results by identifying cases following contrarian associations implying causal complexity and causal asymmetry as one cause for the nonsignificance revealed by SEM.

## 8.5 Sensitivity Analysis

Sufficient configurations are highly sensitive and depend, among other things, on the conditions included in the analysis or the selection of the type of QCA. Accordingly, we reanalyzed our data in two respects. First, even though previous research argues that “the notion of ‘controls’ is usually not part of the analysis” (Fiss et al., 2013 p. 195), we included the controls of the SEM study in our two-step fsQCA. The results confirmed our initial findings, as the analysis continued to report four sufficient configurations with the presence of challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal as a necessary condition for high innovative use. In addition, each configuration also included the controls, meaning that the three intrinsic motivations and the extrinsic motivation— which we also used in the SEM study (see Figure 2) and which have also been examined in previous IS research (Li et al., 2013)—are also present for high routine use and high innovative use. Second, even though a two-step QCA approach is superior to a onestep QCA for analyzing mediating relationships, we nonetheless reanalyzed the data with a one-step fsQCA. For high innovative use and high routine use, our findings provide two sufficient configurations in each case. In both cases, the main sufficient configuration (with the highest empirical relevance represented by coverage values) is identical to the findings reported in this paper. In addition, there is a niche configuration in each case (unique coverages < 0.035), which includes the hindrance IS use stressors and both types of appraisal. The very low coverage values indicate that these configurations are rare and not as central as the other two sufficient configurations (Ragin, 2006), as shown in Figure 5. In both cases, the reported sufficient configurations of the two-step QCA were also included when reanalyzing the data with controls or with a one-step QCA.

## 9 Research Relevance and Transferability to Practice

In addition to the empirical results, we are also interested in how practitioners can utilize our results. As recommended by other scholars (Rosemann & Vessey, 2008), we performed an applicability check of our results to demonstrate the relevance of our results and their importance, accessibility, and suitability for practice (Gill & Bhattacherjee, 2009; Te’eni et al., 2018).

We also used the interviews to gain further practical insights into the nonsignificant hypothesis H4 and the fsQCA results. Applicability checks are instruments used to discuss theoretical and empirical findings with practitioners (Maier et al., 2021). The data collection of the applicability check was performed with five employees (see Table 4). We conducted interviews using a semistructured interview guideline protocol (see Appendix E). Each interview lasted between 20 and 30 minutes. Overall, the interviewees confirmed our findings and reported experiencing them at work. They also provided comments and perspectives about the implications of our findings for them and their organizations (see Table 4).

Our interview partners discussed the organizational and individual importance of the topics of this research study. They underscored the need to understand how routine and innovative use is grounded in IS use stress, as exemplified by the following interviewee statements:

We know from our daily practice that a routine handling of IS is important in order to work error-free and time-efficiently. ... We also encourage our employees to use IS innovatively, especially to optimize work processes.

While the use of IS is typically considered as negative stress hindering employees, it seems highly relevant to me to understand stress from a negative and positive side.

In terms of the accessibility of the results, several interviewees commented that the configurations were easy to understand. The following statement about the combination of these two analyses, in particular, is typical of all interviewees: “The combination of identifying ways to increase routine use and innovative use is transparent and plausible.”

The suitability of our results was discussed by all interviewees, who saw different implications of the results for their daily practice. We identify four main perspectives discussed by our interviewees (see Table 4). First, they discussed the implications for their general view of IS that IS use stressors are not per se a negative consequence of using IS. Second, they discussed the implications for working with IS, agreeing that they only innovatively use IS if they are challenged by using an IS, concluding that fostering challenge IS use stressors is important for enabling innovative use. Third, they identified implications for IS training and the importance of users evaluating IS use stressors as a challenge rather than a hindrance to foster routine use and innovative use. Fourth, they highlighted implications for organizational IS management policies and the importance of providing guidance for hindrance IS use stressors and to support users in appraising IS use stressors as a challenge.

We also gleaned further insights into the influence of hindrance IS use stressors and hindrance IS use appraisal on innovative use. While the SEM results do not show a mediated effect of hindrance IS use stressors to influence innovative use via hindrance IS use appraisal, the twostep fsQCA results indicate that low and high innovative use are grounded in different factors. Our interviews provided further insight regarding these results.

Interviewees agreed that dealing with hindrance IS use stressors is common and habitual for them, as summarized by one interviewee:

It seems really common and habitual for employees to deal with—how do you call it— hindrance IS use stressors. … I would guess that most employees have given up thinking about that, so confrontation with those stressors is habitual and meaningful. (Deputy CIO of a business consultancy)

Recent research confirms this observation that employees might consider hindrance IS use stressors habitual, pointing to a possible habituation effect, which is also relevant to IS use stress. If this effect also applies to users confronted with hindrance IS use stressors, users may get so used to the presence of hindrance IS use stressors that they less frequently appraise them as hindering, thus diminishing their influence on innovative use.

Workarounds are mentioned as a strategy to deal with hindrance IS use stressors, as summarized in the following statement by a non-IS employee:

Oh, I would be rather surprised if [hindrance IS use] stress had no negative effect on whether I use IS innovatively … but, in my experience, system breakdowns are rather common and our ERP system is often unresponsive, so I have developed workarounds and I am really good and fast at performing them, so I am experienced and prepared for that hindering stuff.

Previous research has identified many reasons for developing workarounds. Generally, workarounds are used to avoid using an IS and instead follow non-IS routines to deal with hindrance IS use stressors appraised as hindering. When workarounds are used in response to hindrance IS use stressors, it may mitigate the hindrance IS use stressors’ negative influence on innovative use. In other words, practitioners have approached the non-significant effect of the relationship implied by H4 by offering an explanation in terms of the absence of effective workarounds.

## 10 Discussion and Implications

Productivity gains from ongoing digitalization depend in part on whether IS are used in both routinized and innovative ways (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013; Roberts et al., 2016). At the same time, users are confronted with various IS use demands grounded in their ongoing use of IS on an everyday basis within their workplace environment, which can influence both routine use and innovative use. The present study contributes to the research streams of post-acceptance IS use and IS use stress by investigating the relationship between IS use demands and routine use and innovative use.

## 10.1 Summary and Integration of Results

In our study, we combined quantitative and qualitative approaches complementing each other to examine how challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors influence routine use and innovative use. The SEM findings show that challenge IS use stressors have a positive mediated effect on routine and innovative use via challenge IS use appraisal and that hindrance IS use stressors have a negative mediating influence on routine use via hindrance IS use appraisal. Expanding the SEM results with the QCA method, we also examined contrarian associations and found that hindrance IS use stressors and appraisal are only relevant in the absence of challenge IS use stressors and appraisal since under this condition they cause both low routine use and low innovative use. In the presence of challenge IS use stressors and appraisal, it does not matter whether hindrance IS use stressors and appraisal are absent or present since under this condition high routine use and high innovative use will be observed.

We also reveal that the presence of challenge IS use stressors and appraisal are imperative (necessary condition and sufficient configuration) for high innovative use. Put simply, challenge is a must for high innovative use. The post-survey applicability check confirmed the practical relevance of these findings and provides further qualitative explanations of the quantitative results. Together these three sets of analyses generate rich insights regarding how challenge and hindrance IS stressors stemming from demands in the IS use environment can influence routine and innovative use (see Table 5). For further details of the combination of methods, the derived inferences, and validation see Appendix F.

## 10.2 Implications for IS Theory

This study contributes to the literature in the stream of post-acceptance IS use research by investigating the antecedents of routine use and innovative use. We develop theory explaining that challenge IS use stressors appraised as such are the basis for routine use. The literature makes it clear that using IS for a certain task causes the development of routines (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013; Polites & Karahanna, 2013). This includes, among other things, simple (nonchallenging) repeated IS use over time (Agarwal & Prasad, 1999; Polites & Karahanna, 2013). Building on this idea, we show that routine use might also stem from the presence of challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal, where these stressors drive the user to IS use and hence facilitate routine development. This is a novel approach to looking at routine use. We also demonstrate that users only pursue innovative use in the presence of challenge IS use stressors that they appraise accordingly. Previous research has posited that users are shackled to the status quo when using IS (Laumer et al., 2015; Lee & Joshi, 2017) and avoid investing time and effort in leaving the status quo (Maier et al., 2015b; Mattke et al., 2018). Such a status quo bias implies that users might use IS habitually without innovatively seeking novel uses. However, we know that disruptions can lead users to rethink wellestablished work processes (Polites & Karahanna, 2013), which can lead to innovative use. Indeed, it has been argued that users only adapt to new IS use behaviors when confronted with new challenges (Sun, 2012). Such challenges may be embodied in challenge IS use stressors appraised as such. Without a challenge IS use appraisal, the user might continue to use the IS without innovation. Overall, our study thus establishes a set of novel theoretical antecedental processes for routine use and innovative use in terms of mediating influences of challenge IS use stressors via challenge IS use appraisal, and hindrance IS use stressors via hindrance IS use appraisal.

IS use research into inhibiting and enabling factors (Cenfetelli, 2004) shows that the presence of hindrance IS use stressors might discourage IS use. For example, we know that uncertainty, intrusiveness, being overloaded by information, and irrelevant requests discourage IS use, reducing the chances of developing routines or identifying innovative ways of using an IS (Cenfetelli & Schwarz, 2011). However, our research demonstrates that hindrance IS use stressors rule out both routine and innovative use, but only when challenge IS use stressors and appraisal are absent. In other words, when a user is confronted with hindrance IS use stressors and appraises them as a hindrance, they will neither develop a routine way of working with the IS nor identify innovative ways of using the IS unless they also perceive the challenge IS use stressors and appraise them as challenging. We thus show that enablers and inhibitors influence routine use and innovative use together, and that this combined influence offers more insights into the interplay of enablers and inhibitors than studying enablers or inhibitors separately.

We also contribute to IS use stress literature. Current empirical IS use stress research primarily focuses on negative effects (e.g., Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008), arguing that IS use stressors overwhelmingly lead to perceived exhaustion (Ayyagari et al., 2011) or to IS use discontinuation (Maier et al., 2015b), among other things. We extend this understanding by demonstrating that challenge IS use stressors can have positive consequences. We theorize and validate that—in addition to hindrance IS use stressors— there are challenge IS use stressors. Recognizing this difference offers a more nuanced and complex understanding of user behavior in relation to IS use stress. Further, the IS use stress literature has focused, so far, on IS use stressors and their influence on users’ psychological and behavioral reactions (Tarafdar et al., 2010). We conceptually extend this literature by demonstrating the role of appraisal. Appraisal vis-à-vis stress from the use of IT is an emerging topic with little existing research (Stich et al., 2019; Tarafdar et al., 2019). We suggest that post-acceptance IS use behavior (in this case routine use and innovative use) is influenced by IS use stressors only in the presence of the relevant appraisal (Appendix C).

Finally, we theoretically integrate the two streams of post-acceptance IS use and IS use stress research by demonstrating that the antecedents of routine use and innovative use emerge from IS use demands. Research shows that user motivation guides post-acceptance IS use behavior (Li et al., 2013) and that IS use stress causes IS discontinuation (Maier et al., 2015b). We extend this understanding by demonstrating that the appraisal of ongoing IS use stressors influences routine use and innovative use. In this new theoretical direction, we consider IS use stressors as antecedents of routine use and innovative use. We explain that both challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors influence post-acceptan0ce IS use via appraisal. We offer insights into what factors influence post-acceptance IS use behavior (challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors), how these factors yield influence (via challenge IS use appraisal and hindrance IS use appraisal), the conditions necessary for highly innovative use (challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal), and why this occurs (i.e., the independent appraisal of challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors influence routine use and innovative use). With these insights, we offer a theoretical approach that uses IS use stress to explain routine and innovative use.

## 10.3 Implications for IS Research Methods

Our research follows a novel three-step research design including SEM analysis, QCA analysis, and an applicability check. First, we used SEM analysis to test our research hypotheses based on survey data. Second, we extended the SEM analysis by undertaking a twostep fsQCA, which investigated cases in the data set that follow contrarian associations, and in doing so generated two important insights. One, we identified causal asymmetry between the antecedent conditions influencing low and high routine use and innovative use. Two, we identified causal complexity, demonstrating that routine use and innovative use are caused by more than one condition. With this combination of two methods, we identified symmetric associations (through the variable-focused method SEM) as well as contrarian associations (through the case-focused method QCA). To our knowledge, this is the first study in IS to complement SEM with two-step fsQCA to reveal a richer and wider set of unexpected insights than SEM analysis alone (see Table 5). In a third step, our applicability check helped us evaluate the practical value of our study (Gill & Bhattacherjee, 2009; Te’eni et al., 2018). Rosemann and Vessey (2008) emphasized that it is important to illustrate the importance, accessibility, and suitability of research results to support the transfer to practice.

With our approach of mixing methods, we follow recent suggestions (Leidner, 2020) that combining particular methods (in this case SEM and two-step QCA) represents a specific form of methods contribution, particularly when it generates novel theoretical insights and is a temporal forerunner.

## 10.4 Implications for IS Practice

Our results are also valuable for and accessible to the practice of managing IS. First, our study can help organizations understand that the presence of IS use stressors can be challenging in a positive way. Accordingly, conditions such as heavy workload that requires the use of an IS or working with an IS under time pressure can be beneficial in terms of enhancing both routine use and innovative use of IS. Second, organizations should focus on reducing hindrance IS use stressors such as system breakdowns or unclear IS instructions. Since such stressors may be unavoidable in everyday work with IS, organizations should support users in dealing with them effectively. Third, organizations should also foster challenge IS use stressors by, for example, actively motivating users to leverage IS to help them deal with heavy workloads, meet pressing deadlines, and perform complex but important tasks. Indeed, our results indicate that the presence of such stressors and their appraisal as challenges are necessary for innovative use. Fourth, organizational IS management policies should acknowledge and reflect the possibility of both challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors may coexist. Accordingly, IS use policies such as shutting down email servers in the evening and restrictive bring-your-own-device policies (Valta et al., 2021) should be reevaluated in terms of their impact on both negative hindrance and positive challenge IS use stressors.

## 10.5 Limitations and Future Research

This research contains several limitations. First, since we collected data within a single organization, participants came from the same technology environment and organizational culture. It is possible that the effects of challenge and hindrance IS use stressors are different for different organizations. This limitation may affect the generalizability of our findings.

There is room for future research to study the relationship between IS use stressors, appraisals, and behaviors. General work stress research (Bakker et al., 2010) and IS use stress research (Maier et al., 2019) have demonstrated that users appraise stressors differently depending on their personality. Future research could consider the impact of user personality on the appraisal of stressors and on routine and innovative use. There is also a substantial percentage of users who report perceiving challenge or hindrance IS use stressors but do not appraise them accordingly (Figure C1, Appendix C). Exploring why different individuals appraise the same stressors differently would be an interesting future research avenue. Based on previous research (LePine et al., 2016), we made an ex ante classification of challenge IS use stressors, hindrance IS use stressors, challenge IS use appraisal, and hindrance IS use appraisal. However, it is possible that some users may appraise certain use stressors as challenging while others may appraise the same as stressor as hindering depending on their experience, training, profession, personality type, or corporate culture, among other traits—which would be a worthy topic for future research.

## 11 Conclusion

Routine use and innovative use are two important facets of post-acceptance IS use. IS use stressors represent important IS-related demands in the work environment. This study integrates these two issues by investigating two kinds of IS use stressors—challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors—as antecedents of routine use and innovative use. We find that challenge IS use stressors appraised as challenges can foster both routine and innovative use, while hindrance IS use stressors appraised as hindrances inhibit routine and innovative use. We further suggest that users will only innovatively use IS if they experience challenge IS use stressors and appraise them as challenges, irrespective of the presence of hindrance IS use stressors.

## Appendix A: Classification, Scale Development, and Measures

Previous research provides no IS-specific measures for challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors. However, as items for challenge and hindrance IS use stressors are related to previous general (LePine et al., 2016) and IS-related stress research (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008), we used items from those measures to develop an initial pool of items for challenge and hindrance IS use stressors (Chau, 1999). We then discussed the items within our research team and with employees working for the organization in which we performed our survey study. ## Appendix B: Sample Size, CMB, Measurement Model

Required sample size: Within our research model, routine use and innovative use have both the highest number of antecedents. Both have six antecedents in terms of challenge IS use appraisal and hindrance IS use appraisal as well as the four control variables. Thus, to technically use the PLS algorithm, we needed a sample size higher than 60 (multiplication of: 6 (six antecedents) \* 10). We also followed Kim’s (2005) suggestion that a given level of power depends on (a) number of variables / degrees of freedom, (b) the relation among the variables, (c) choice of fit index and (d) the value of the fit index and proposes a range of different fit indexes. Using Steiger’s gamma (with $\gamma = 0 . 9 5 ;$ α = 0.05; Power = 0.90), the proposed sample size is 114.7 and using RMSEA $( \mathbf { a } = 0 . 0 5 ;$ ; Power = 0.90) reveals a minimum proposed sample size of 101.0 for the proposed research model. The sample size of our study meets all of these conditions and the sample size of 178 participants is larger than the required sample size.

Common method bias: Empirical research must consider common method bias (CMB) in self-reported data (Podsakoff et al., 2003). To determine the extent of CMB, we ran four different tests. The results of the first test, Harman’s single factor test, which indicates whether the majority of the variance can be explained by one single factor, show that only 24.7% of the variance of the data is explained by one factor. Second, we included a marker variable (“Coffee is important in my $l i f e ^ { \prime \prime } )$ in our research model. We expected this variable to not be highly correlated with other constructs considered in our study. Following the technique suggested by Lindell and Whitney (2001) and the recommendation of Chin et al. (2012) to a priori choice of a theoretical unrelated marker variable, we used the secondsmallest positive correlation among the constructs measured and the marker variable (0.02 for both studies). We then developed a CMB-adjusted correlation matrix to examine the structural relationships in the research model (Malhotra et al., 2006). Our results indicate that the directions and significance levels of the paths remained unchanged. Third, we used the procedure of examining the correlation matrix as specified by Pavlou et al. (2007). Extremely high correlations $( \mathbf { r } > 0 . 9 0 )$ are an indicator of CMB but our correlation matrix did not indicate such high correlations. The fourth test was proposed by Williams et al. (2003), who suggested determining the extent of CMB with the help of PLS by including a CMB factor into the model. All remaining factors were transformed into several single-item constructs and the ratio of R² with a CMB factor is compared to R² without a CMB factor. Comparing the average R² without a CMB factor and the delta R² that could be explained with the CMB factor resulted in a ratio of 1:193, i.e., no observable signs of CMB influence. In addition, the path coefficients from the CMB factor and the original constructs had a ratio of 1:252. Finally, our results reveal that only one path coefficient from the CMB factor to the single-item constructs was significant. Comparing this with prior results indicates that CMB is unlikely to have distorted the results.

## Appendix D: Types of QCA, the Two-step QCA, and Performing the Two-Step fsQCA

## Three Types of QCA

There are three main types of QCA used in research: crisp set QCA (csQCA), multi-value QCA (mvQCA) and fuzzy set QCA (fsQCA). These types of QCA differ in terms of how conditions are coded. The csQCA codes conditions in the two binary values “0” or “1.” The mvQCA usually uses more than two values to code conditions. The fsQCA allows a condition to have values from “0” to “1” (Schneider and Wagemann 2012). In this study, we used fsQCA because the nature of our outcome variables (routine use and innovative use) can have continuous variables, meaning that the extent of routine use and innovative use can be low, medium, high, and all values in-between.

## The Two-Step QCA Approach

In Step 1 of the two-step QCA approach, the distant conditions, contextualized here as challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors, are analyzed to identify sufficient configurations of distant conditions or (outcome-enabling configurations) which lead to the outcome. In Step 2, the close conditions, contextualized here as challenge IS use appraisal and hindrance IS use appraisal, are analyzed to identify sufficient configurations of close conditions that lead to the outcome within the outcome-enabling configurations identified in Step 1. Using separated data sheets for each outcome-enabling configuration from Step 1, we added only configurations of close conditions in which the configuration of distant conditions equals this specific outcome-enabling configuration. We then used these new data sheets to analyze sufficient configurations of close conditions. Thus, there were as many analyses of sufficient configurations of close conditions as there were outcome-enabling configurations (Schneider & Wagemann, 2006)

Let us illustrate the two-step process using an example (Figure D1). If there are three distant conditions (DC1, DC2 and DC3) and Step 1 (thus the first QCA) reveals two sufficient configurations leading to the outcome (Outcome-Enabling Configuration 1 and 2), two separated datasheets are created. The first data sheet contains only observations, matching Outcome-Enabling Configuration 1 and the second datasheet contains all observations matching Outcome-Enabling Configuration 2. Then an analysis of sufficient configurations of close conditions is conducted for each of the outcome-enabling configurations. In our example, the analysis for sufficient configurations of close conditions (CC1, CC2, CC3) is conducted for Outcome-Enabling Configuration 1 (second QCA) and for Outcome-Enabling Configuration 2 (third QCA). This results in different sufficient configurations of close conditions leading to the outcome for each outcome-enabling configuration. For instance, the analysis within Outcome-Enabling Configuration 1 reveals two sufficient configurations of close condition (1a and 1b), and the analysis within Outcome-Enabling Configuration 2 reveals one sufficient configuration of Close Condition 2a. Finally, each outcome-enabling configuration from Step 1 is combined with the revealed sufficient configurations of close conditions from Step 2, resulting in combined configurations. In the example, this results in three combined configurations.

## Appendix F: Integration and Validation of Combined Results

Following the examples of prior research (Venkatesh et al., 2013), we leverage the combined results of our thre complementing studies to deduce a deeper understanding of how challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors influence routine use and innovative use. This requires, first, the deduction of complementary knowledge gained from the combination of the three studies and, second, the validation of the individual and combined results.

## Complementary Knowledge Gained from All Three Studies

The SEM results indicate that challenge IS use stressors have a positive mediating influence on routine use and innovative use through challenge IS use appraisal. This is consistent with the QCA and interview results, showing that challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal need to be present for a high routine and innovative use. The QCA results complement these insights by identifying that challenge IS use stressors and appraisal are also necessary for high innovative use. Therefore, we conclude:

Challenge IS use stressors foster routine use and innovative use through challenge IS use appraisal, and challenge IS use stressors and challenge IS use appraisal are also necessary for innovative use.

Further, our SEM results indicate that hindrance IS use stressors have a negative mediating influence on routine use through hindrance IS use appraisal but no influence on innovative use. While the QCA results support the influence on routine use, they show that hindrance IS use stressors can result in low innovative use through hindrance IS use appraisal, if challenge IS use stressors and appraisal are absent. The interviews further indicate that hindrance IS use stressors have a negative effect on innovative use. Thus, we conclude:

Hindrance IS use stressors hinder routine use and innovative use through hindrance IS use appraisal if challenge IS use stressors and appraisal are absent.

## Validation of Results

Design quality: The purpose of combining different research methods is clearly stated: The QCA results expand the results of our SEM analysis and the post hoc applicability check offers complementary knowledge on the practical value of our theorization. Following our research model explaining how challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors influence routine use and innovative use, we set up our study following the needs of the model. A pure quantitative approach based on SEM cannot deliver the necessary insights on the role of hindrance IS use stressors. Therefore, we chose to combine and expand the results with a QCA approach and to complement our theoretical findings with insights from practitioners’ perspectives adding more richness to our understanding. The sample selection for all three studies is appropriate and large enough (Collins et al., 2006; Marx, 2006). The measurement items that serve as input for the SEM and QCA analysis were either based on prior research or validated with Q-sorting (see Appendix A). The interview data are representative for offering practical insights and we were sensitive to the principles of flexibility, nondirection, specificity, and range while conducting the interviews (Sarker et al., 2018). The overall reliability and validity of the measurement model are clear and there is no evidence for common method bias (see Appendix B). The QCA results are robust to changes in the used thresholds and the applied calibration process (see Appendix D). We recorded and transcribed the interviews following recommendations (Myers 2009). The analysis was iteratively performed with two co-authors until we reached theoretical saturation. The interrater reliability was 0.91, indicating strong reliability and plausibility of the results.

Explanation quality: The SEM results are largely consistent with what we expected, based on prior research. Further, those results are plausible and of high practical value, as confirmed by the qualitative study. The QCA results offer complementary knowledge on the role of hindrance IS use stressors and shed light on causal asymmetry and the equifinality of results (Misangyi et al., 2017), which allow us to better understand the underlying reasons for the SEM results. We integrated all types of inferences to come up with a holistic theorization of how challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors influence routine use and innovative use. The theorization is transferable to other stress contexts and was only possible through the combination of multiple methods. Therefore, the combination of methods was necessary to overcome methodological barriers and offer rich insights into how challenge IS use stressors and hindrance IS use stressors influence routine and innovative use.
