# B2B integration in global supply chains: An identification of technical integration scenarios

## a b s t r a c t

The competitiveness of businesses is increasingly dependent on their electronic networks with customers, suppliers, and partners. While the strategic and operational impact of external integration and IOS adoption has been extensively studied, much less attention has been paid to the organizational and technical design of electronic relationships. The objective of our longitudinal research project is the development of a framework for understanding and explaining B2B integration. Drawing on existing literature and empirical cases we present a reference model (a classification scheme for B2B Integration). The reference model comprises technical, organizational, and institutional levels to reflect the multiple facets of B2B integration. In this paper we investigate the current state of electronic collaboration in global supply chains focussing on the technical view. Using an indepth case analysis we identify five integration scenarios. In the subsequent confirmatory phase of the research we analyse 112 real-world company cases to validate these five integration scenarios. Our research advances and deepens existing studies by developing a B2B reference model, which reflects the current state of practice and is independent of specific implementation technologies. In the next stage of the research the emerging reference model will be extended to create an assessment model for analysing the maturity level of a given company in a specific supply chain.

## 1. Introduction

The ongoing specialization and globalization in today’s economies have led companies to adjust their business scope and to rethink their boundaries (Jacobides, 2005; Santos and Eisenhardt, 2005). While concentrating on core competencies, they increasingly rely on their supply chain partners for complementary activities. Inter-organizational electronic interfaces (Chatterjee et al., 2006; Newell et al., 2001) play an important role in coordinating and streamlining these distributed supply chains.

Along with this intensified electronic interaction comes an increase in requirements for infrastructure and technology. The first wave of ERP (enterprise resource planning) systems introduced internal information integration into the company by provid ing cross-functional data integration and process support. Once internal information integration was established, companies started to seek external integration with their suppliers, customers and service providers. ERP systems were equipped with interfaces for electronic data interchange (EDI) and electronic linkages were setup to seamlessly support business processes between firms and to realize additional operational and strategic benefits of vertical integration (Mukhopadhyay and Kekre,

2002; Saeed et al., 2005; Zhu et al., 2004). Information technology infrastructures and components, such as electronic data interchange (EDI), e-business and e-commerce solutions or ERP II that complement enterprise systems with interorganizational integration capabilities are important enablers of external integration.

Several streams of research have investigated the phenomena related to external integration, among them inter-organizational systems (IOS) (Barrett and Konsynski, 1982; Hong, 2002; Johnston and Vitale, 1988), e-commerce and e-business (Gosain et al., 2004; Kalakota and Robinson, 2000), business networking (Alt and Fleisch, 2000; Fleisch, 2001; Fleisch and Österle, 2000) and collaborative business (Wölfle and Schubert, 2008). The existing body of research has mainly examined the strategic and operational impact of external integration and analyzed IOS adoption (cf. Robey et al., 2008; Wareham et al., 2005). However, much less attention has been paid to the design of electronic linkages with external parties and the role of enterprise systems, two topics that are discussed in this article. Two research questions are of particular interest:

(1) How can we classify existing B2B integration approaches? In view of the manifold technological options for establishing inter-organizational linkages, a technology-agnostic conceptualization is required to synthesize the commonalities and differences between the different B2B implementation technologies.

(2) How are organizational and technical B2B integration approaches interrelated? In order to address this question, the interplay between organizational and technical integration needed to be further examined (Gurbaxani and Whang, 1991; Snow et al., 1992; Wigand et al., 1997).

These two questions motivated the authors to embark on a collaborative longitudinal research proiect on supply chair integration. The objective is the development of a reference model for B2B integration, which considers technical forms of integration and their interplay with organizational integration. We were inspired by the ideas of the Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI) developed by the Software Engineering Institute (Chrissis et al., 2006) and used a similar logic in conceptualizing our model. To test and validate our framework, we selected 112 suitable real-world B2B integration projects from a pool of over 500 case studies that were collected using a uniform taxonomy, the so-called eXperience methodology (Schubert and Wölfle, 2007). The results of our exploratory and confirmatory research reveal typical patterns in the technical integration of external partners which we consolidated into five technical integration scenarios.

## 2. Literature and research gap

The following sections give an overview of current literature in the field and discuss the identified research gap that we are addressing with our research.

## 2.1. E-business and e-commerce

Electronic collaboration has been intensified by the emergence of the Internet as a widely-accepted global communication platform and the widespread availability of e-commerce and e-business applications. The enormous effects of the Internet on selling and buving transactions are reflected in the creation of online channels (e.g. Madlberger. 2006: Subramaniam et al., 2000) and the emergence of online intermediaries (Bakos, 1997; Day et al., 2003; Giaglis et al., 2002). Although many authors do not clearly distinguish between e-commerce and e-business, in the narrow sense, e-commerce focuses on online buying-and-selling transactions, whereas e-business is used as a broader term for conducting business over electronic communication channels (Papazoglou and Ribbers, 2006). On the supplier side, e-business includes activities such as e-procurement (Mukhopadhyay and Kekre, 2002; Subramaniam and Shaw, 2002; Tanner et al., 2008), which involves electronic tendering and ordering via electronic catalogs, as well as the more general activities of supply chain integration (Gunasekarana et al., 2008; Nurmilaakso, 2008). On the customer side, e-commerce and e-business have significantly altered customer relationships (Otim and Grover, 2006). Many authors argue that companies that use electronic customer touchpoints to effectively collect knowledge for, from, or about customers, and are able to improve customer relationships throughout the customer life cycle (Alt et al., 2005; Kolbe and Meyer, 2005; Salomann et al., 2005; Stone et al., 2002). On the supplier and customer side, shared information platforms and online intermediaries enable electronic information sharing and transactions within a business network or industry (Baida et al., 2008; Hirnle and Hess, 2007; Giaglis et al., 2002)

Several frameworks and taxonomies have been suggested to classify and describe e-business scenarios (Dorn et al., 2009). The more business-oriented ones, such as the e<sup>3</sup>value methodology (Gordijn and Akkermans, 2001; Gordijn and Tan, 2005; Kartseva et al., 2005), the Multi-Party Business Collaboration Modeling Language (Xu, 2007) or the e-business model ontology (Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2002) describe the logic of e-business value generation. These frameworks and taxonomies improve our understanding of the business rationale and application scenarios for e-business. Another stream of research developed reference architectures that support e-business implementations by outlining architecture blueprints. Among them are ebXML and the UN/CEFACTs Modeling Methodology (UMM) (Hofreiter et al, 2006), and several B2B architecture frameworks (Al-Naeem et al., 2004; Baghdadi, 2004; Rittgen, 2008). Their main contributions are general architecture conceptualizations and modeling notations. However, most of the reference architectures are generic models and do not reflect the characteristics of actual B2B integration scenarios.

While the e-commerce and e-business literature started as dedicated streams of research, it is increasingly acknowledged that the phenomena related to the adoption, use and impact of Internet-based channels have much in common with the ones studied for prior IOS technologies (Reimers et al., 2010; Stelze et al., 2006). Consequently, e-commerce and e-business research increasingly converges with the existing research stream on inter-organizational systems (IOS)

## 2.2. Inter-organizational integration technologies

The current wide range of different forms of inter-organizational integration reflects the technology evolution from the first EDI-based systems established in the 1960s with proprietary technologies through to the Internet as a global communication network that gave rise to the concepts of e-commerce and e-business, and, more recently, to Web Services and service-oriented architectures (Nurmilaakso and Kotinurmi, 2004).

Electronic data interchange (EDI) has long been in active use as the means of bridging the boundaries between different companies and industry sectors. UN/EDIFACT as one of the first worldwide standards provided a practical solution to the electronic exchange of business documents (Reimers, 2001). In the 1970s and 1980s, industry associations developed standardized data exchange formats, such as SWIFT for financial institutions or ODETTE for the automotive industry. This subsequently led to international, industry-spanning standardizations, such as ANSI X.12 and EDIFACT. Since the mid 1990s, inter-organizational systems technology has been completely overhauled by the emergence of the Internet as global communication infrastructure offering more advanced integration capabilities. Compared to previous forms of electronic data interchange which built on proprietary technologies, the Internet provides a more flexible and much cheaper platform for external integration (Bussler, 2003; Christiaanse et al., 2004; Gosain et al., 2003; Linthicum, 2001; Picot, 1982). Hence, modern forms of B2B integration build on Web architectures and use the Extensible Markup Language (XML) for electronic messages. Web Services and service-oriented architectures (SOA) further enhance well-established Internet infrastructures and introduce the next paradigm shift (Alonso et al., 2003; Mueller et al., 2010; Zhao et al., 2008). By establishing service-based communication across heterogeneous platforms and among enterprises the document-centric approach to B2B integration is replaced by a process-centric approach (Bussler, 2003; Dorn et al., 2009). ## 2.3. Levels of inter-organizational integration

A vast body of literature has focused on the adoption, use and impact of IOS. It emphasizes the multiple facets of interorganizational integration. At the most basic level, IOS automate an existing communication process, thereby reducing manual data processing and improving the reliability as well as the timeliness of information (Hoogewegen and Wagenaar, 1996). As firms progress towards using IOS to closely couple business processes between firms, they are able to realize the additional operational and strategic benefits of vertical integration (Mukhopadhyay and Kekre, 2002; Saeed et al., 2005; Zhu et al., 2004). Hence, the use of IOS is considered most beneficial if applied in cooperative relationships (Chatfield and Yetton, 2000; Johnston and Vitale, 1988) and accompanied by process innovations such as vendor-managed inventory or continuous replenishment in the retail and consumer goods industries (Clark and Stoddard, 1996; Riggins and Mukhopadhyay, 1994).

In order to describe the interplay between B2B integration technology and the context in which it is applied, existing literature has distinguished different levels of inter-organizational integration. Three main perspectives on inter-organizational integration are most commonly identified: technical, organizational, and institutional integration. These are discussed in detail in, for example, Bauer and Stickel (1998), Österle et al. (2001), Wölfle (2007), and Grant and Tu (2005).

1. Technical integration describes how information is processed and shared electronically within and across organizations. It comprises the enabling information technologies and systems presented in Table 1.

2. Organizational integration refers to the type of inter-organizational interactions that are enabled by B2B integration technologies. It comprises the organizational structures and processes which are put in place to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of the supply chain.

3. Institutional integration describes the different actors and the formal and informal agreements which govern inter-organizational relationships.

The three levels describe the main aspects of inter-organizational integration. There are other possible views, for example, social and/or cultural integration as discussed by Kumar and van Dissel (1996) or by Brown and Duquid (2000). This work draws attention to the fact that information acquires meaning only through social context. This poses an obvious problem to the use of information in computer systems as the recipient of the information may be completely unaware of the contextual interpretation of the sender. In the context of inter-organizational business information exchange this requires exchanging parties to agree on exchange standards and provide detailed descriptions about the meaning of fields (semantics) and the response patterns to process the received information (pragmatics). This view is incorporated into the institutional view where the negotiation, agreement and setting of community level information interchange standards is undertaken (Scifleet et al., 2007).

A noteworthy finding from the existing IOS research is that sharing ‘‘more’’ information is not necessarily ‘‘better’’ (Gosain et al., 2004; Premkumar, 2000). Although companies seek broader information from their partners, ensuring the quality of information exchange is equally important. In addition, external information is often only valuable if it is highly time specific and specific to the information requirements of an enterprise. Further, companies have to be in a position to process external information, otherwise they will not be able to obtain value from that information. In this regard, organizational information processing theory (Galbraith, 1973) provides a valuable theoretical lens for analyzing B2B integration. According to Bensaou and Venkatraman (1996) and Kim et al. (2006), information processing capabilities need to fit a number of contextual factors, which are summarized as information processing needs. They argue that uncertainty in the supply chain, e.g. in the case of fluctuating demand patterns, are the contextual factors that give rise to information processing needs. Since companies are seeking optimal performance, they will generate appropriate information processing capabilities. They take decisions on the structural design of the supply channel which create capabilities for effective information processing such as collection of appropriate information, its timely movement, and transmission without distortion. It can be concluded that close collaborative relationship such as vendor-managed inventory (VMI) or efficient consumer response (ECR) are associated with stronger information processing needs than the traditional order-based relationship.

## 2.4. Research gap

From an academic perspective, e-business and JOS research have mainly dealt with exploring the strategic and operational impact of external integration and analyzing IOS adoption (cf. Robey et al., 2008; Wareham et al., 2005). This is underpinned by a recent review of 51 theory-based empirical studies of IOS (Robey et al., 2008), which classifies the main themes of this research into adoption, governance and organizational consequences of IOS. However, much less attention has been paid to the design of inter-organizational linkages. In view of the manifold forms of B2B integration – such as e-mail communication, customer or supplier portals, the exchange of EDIFACT or XML documents, and, more recently. Web Services, we argue that classification models are needed to describe and analyze the different forms of B2B integration in a technologyagnostic way. Further research is also necessary to investigate and understand the effectiveness of specific B2B integration technologies in a particular context.

For studying the design of B2B integration linkages, we draw on information processing theory, which postulates that the ‘‘fit’’ between information processing capabilities and information processing needs is a strong determinant of task effectiveness and performance (Galbraith, 1973; Bensaou and Venkatraman, 1996). Applied to B2B integration, we argue that the organizational level, notably the collaborative business processes, defines the information processing requirements, whereas the information processing capabilities are a result of B2B integration technology implementation. This calls for a congruence of the technical integration and the organizational context in which B2B integration is applied.

## 3. Research objectives and method

The objective of our longitudinal research project is to develop a comprehensive assessment model for B2B integration. Stimulated by the ideas of existing maturity models such as the CMMI, we plan to build an instrument that allows companies to describe, analyze and evaluate their own B2B integration capabilities. In order to do so, a reference model is required to describe the B2B integration capabilities and requirements of a given company. This model needs to operationalize the three main aspects of inter-organizational integration described above (technical, organizational, and institutional integration).

Once fully developed, the reference model can be further used to assess and compare B2B integration capabilities. As a basis for our study, we have created a database of case material describing the current state of the art of B2B integration in real-world companies To date, we have classified and filed 112 company cases. The database will continue to be extended in the future as more case studies are developed. Based on this rich empirical dataset, the data analysis is likely to reveal typical patterns of B2B integration, such as the current practice for given company profiles (industry, supply chain role, size, number of suppliers/customers, and so forth). The final objective, however, is an assessment model for B2B integration that draws a comparison between the company’s current B2B integration capabilities and the patterns typical of companies in similar industries or contexts. The resulting ‘‘rating’’ will enable us to identify, for example, whether a company is a leader, a follower or just an average user of integration technology and enable the identification of areas for improvement.

We use an interpretive research approach. ‘‘It acknowl edges that the understanding held by the human subjects in the researcher’s field of study (i.e. the subjective understanding) is part and parcel of the overall subject matter that the researcher is studying, and therefore requires observation and data collection’’ (Lee and Hubona, 2009, p. 238). As described above, we extracted the three basic integration levels from the research literature. For a holistic classification scheme we needed to explore the three levels in detail and operationalize them with the help of appropriate dimensions and values. In this paper we illustrate a part of the entire process focusing on the technical view. In a future step we will develop the reference model further into an assessment model. The assessment model uses the levels and properties of the reference model and assigns assessment values to each of the dimension. To be useful the data for the assessment model must be derived from real world transactional logs of companies with a large amount of direct B2B document exchange or B2B service providers (intermediaries).

\- A B2B Integration Reference Model (classification from the literature of supply chain integration, model built from a data base of real-world cases ? data exploration).

\- A derived B2B Integration Assessment Model (model built from real world transactional data enabling companies to assess their own capabilities and the ranking of their value chain integration ? analysis of current state).

\- A B2B Integration Development Guide (developed from research literature and analysis of best practice ? prescription explaining ways of bridging the gap between the current state and an ‘ideal’ state).

## 4. Research objectives related to technical integration

In this article we focus our attention on the first element, the classification scheme which defines the B2B Integration Reference Model. It is the basis for all subsequent research objectives and the thorough development and validation are pivotal to our future research. 112 company case studies were analyzed to develop the technical level of the B2B Integration Model. The empirical data basis, the methodology used and the specific research steps are described in this section.

## 4.1. Data collection and analysis

Case studies are particularly suitable for understanding phenomena within their organizational context (Yin. 2003), In this study we make extensive use of cases to develop the classification scheme. We first developed an ‘‘a priori model’’ with a selected group of 14 cases. To validate the model we applied it to a further number of 98 cases (112 in total). By doing this, we used the possibilities of case study analysis identified by Bonoma (1985) who describes two application areas of case study research in the social sciences: (1) building theory by using inductive principles and (2) validating existing theories and thus deducing empirical consequences (see also Eisenhardt, 1989).

The source of the cases is a publicly available database, the so-called eXperience case study database. A longitudinal research project underway since 1999 in a partner network among Swiss and German Universities (the eXperience initiative, Schubert and Wölfle, 2007), has developed more than 500 case studies of real-world IS implementations. Today, the eXperience case study database is the largest case study platform in the German speaking area. The majority of these cases deal with projects on enterprise systems implementations. About 25% of the case descriptions contain information about the extended supply chain, including business partners, business processes and technical integration. The term ‘‘eXperience’ stands for the method of gathering and documenting authentic knowledge on the use of information technology. The eXperience case (research case) includes an in-depth description of an existing enterprise system solution and respective practices in an organization. It contains

\- a description of the organizations and actors involved;

\- details of specific national regulations;

\- the business scenario, partners, and company strategy;

\- the project objectives, expectations, and desired benefits;

\- the actual outcome of the project (enterprise system solution);

\- the advantages achieved and the shortcomings observed (learning).

The cases are written on the basis of interviews with key informants, such as the responsible managers or project leaders in the portrayed company. Before being published, all case studies go through a rigorous editorial process to ensure veracity and quality. With the help of a common template and the use of a uniform terminology, the editorial team ensures that the case studies are comparable and can be cross-analyzed. As a result, the eXperience database provides an increasingly vast, empirically derived dataset for case study research which can be accessed free of charge by the community of IS researchers

As of May 2010, the eXperience database comprised 407 case studies in German, 80 in English, and 14 in French which are available online (http://www.experience-online.eu). We identified a sub-sample of cases that focus on B2B integration projects. From the uniform template used in the eXperience methodology and its standard chapters, we selected those chapters describing B2B integration aspects (see Fig. 2) and used them for cross-comparison of the case studies. The technical implementation of B2B integration scenarios is described as part of the application view (Section 5.3.3 of the case studies). Additionally, all cases were profiled according to industry, size, role in the supply chain, target group, type of implementation, etc. described in the case. Based on the case descriptions, we used a comparative analysis (cross case analysis) to reveal typical patterns and current practices. The following sections outline the details of our research process.

## 4.2. Research process

The development of the reference model was carried out in three main steps (cf. Fig. 3).

(1) Initialization phase: The first step consisted of a comprehensive literature analysis which followed established approaches as described by Webster and Watson (2002) and Fettke (2006). A part of the literature review has been presented above. The literature analysis helped in the identification of the basic research questions. It also confirmed the abovementioned lack of a holistic reference model for B2B integration. However, we noted that the existing reference models (e.g. Williams, 1997; Fleisch and Österle, 2000; Hong, 2002; Giaglis et al., 2002; Otto and Wäsch, 2003; Stelze et al., 2006; Reimers et al., 2010) show similarities in the contextual levels, which need to be taken into consideration when designing a B2B integration solution. It is thus the case that almost all the previous research literature contains references to the three levels (1) technical, (2) organizational and (3) institutional. These three levels were identified for inclusion in the ‘‘a priori reference model’’. At the end of the initialization phase we had thus identified our three basic levels and set the focus on the technical view for the consecutive step. For the following exploration phase, the model was further developed by the additions of classification criteria; these represent the dimensions of the model. The criteria served as the basis for a preliminary coding scheme, which was discussed among the members of the research team (including the coders) and agreed upon before the actual coding started.

(2) Exploration phase: For the exploratory phase we used a cyclical process of data reduction and coding. We performed an in-depth content analysis applying techniques described by Miles and Huberman (1994) and Saldaña (2009). In this paper we focus on the identification of the dimensions of the technical level with the help an interpretive case analysis. However, the data analysis was performed for all three levels of B2B integration. For the development of the holistic a priori model a set of cases that have a special focus on supply chain integration (14 cases) was selected.

Sampling: We used sampling strategies suggested by Yin (2003) for the selection of representative cases. In order to build a selection of companies that cover a broad range of contextual issues, we included variation in industry sectors (e.g. public administration, food industry, medical technology, consumer goods), roles in the supply chain (manufacturer, distributor, service providers, wholesale, retail), customer types (B2B, B2C, A2A, A2B), integrated business processes and sizes (very small to large). The integration solutions in the cases had to include at least two parties that had established a technical integration. The detail level of the description had to be sufficient for the coding. Coding process: Two independent researchers (also referred to as ‘‘judges’’ in the literature, cf. Lombard et al., 2002) used the preliminary coding scheme for the coding of the integration projects described in the cases. The two researchers each started with a blank Excel sheet containing the names of the companies and choice boxes containing the pre-defined codes. They both independently coded the 14 selected cases. In addition to pre-defined codes they were encouraged to attribute new codes wherever necessary (open coding). In doing so, we interpreted the described case descriptions and (as is typical for interpretive research) ‘‘develop(ed) an interpretive understanding of the subjective understanding’’ (Lee and Hubona, 2009, p. 238). The cases were homogenous in the use of technical and business vocabulary and presented a valuable source of interpretation for the coders

1. Companybackground: General description of the company (e.g. turnover, industry sector, target group, number of employees, etc.).

2. Project management: aspects describing the circumstances of the integration project (e.g. installation costs, reasons for investment, IT partners, etc.).

3. Technical integration: Standards used (e.g. document standards, diversity (as e.g. discussed by Massetti and Zmud, 1996) process standards), integration layer (client, application, database), type of link between information systems (central system or two/more systems), etc.

4. Organizational integration: Characteristic properties of business management aspects, e.g. integration reach (internal, with external party), economic effects (project cost), enterprise benefits (Williams and Schubert, 2010), etc.

5. Institutional integration: Supply chain role (e.g. manufacturer, retailer (SCC, 2001)), project role (initiating partner, connected partner, intermediary), involved processes (e.g. primary and secondary (Porter, 1985)), direction of the integration (horizontal, vertical, diagonal (Ruhnke, 1966)), governance and power structures (Weill and Ross, 2004), dependency (Thompson, 1967), etc.

The objective of the exploration phase was the identification of groups of codes, i.e. codes that were typically found together for an individual case. We hoped to find patterns in the codes that could lead us to ‘‘typical forms of integration’’.

Intercoder reliability: The intercoder agreement in our initial content analysis was high; eventually 100% agreement was reached. Whilst such a high initial agreement is unusual, the case descriptions we were working from were highly structured and unambiguous and codes could clearly be assigned to each of the cases.

Data analysis: The coding results showed that three of the codes, namely (1) integration reach, (2) project role, and (3) link (marked in bold in the above list), always appeared in five typical combinations. These combinations represent the five ‘‘integration scenarios’’ which are discussed in further detail below.

(3) Validation phase: In the following validation phase the two researchers applied the coding scheme to another 98 cases (112 in total). In this phase they also eliminated redundant or added new descriptive criteria to the scheme. The intercoder reliability in this step was close to 90%. In approximately 10% of the cases the help of the third researcher was required to provide a degree of impartiality, to mediate and resolve any coding conflicts and to reach an agreement on the coding of a case. The final result was an Excel sheet with more than 30 codes (classification criteria). This procedure provided a degree of triangulation and interpretive validity (Miles and Huberman, 1994, p. 266) and was used to improve the quality of the interpretation of the empirical data.

In this process, we tested the validity of the identified five technical integration scenarios of our a priori model. All five integration scenarios could be successfully assigned to each of the 98 cases. The dimensions of the scenarios were thus added to our proposed B2B Integration Model. The contents of the framework are described in the next section.

Third-party validation of the classification scheme was conducted as follows. Two independent researchers were given the scheme and sample cases to code. They applied the classification scheme consistently and were able to identify no additional codes. The preliminary classification has also been presented at conferences and workshops in order to gather discipline feedback. As discussed above the cases were homogenous in the use of technical and business vocabulary and thus relatively clear and straightforward to analyze.

## 5. The development of the a priori model

## 5.1. Conceptualization of the technical integration

Technical integration describes how information is processed and shared electronically within and across organizations. In order to differentiate how enterprise systems are interconnected, we distinguish the following three forms of integration (c.f. Al-Naeem et al., 2004; Bussler, 2003):

1. In the case of client integration, external users are gaining direct access to data and application by means of Web frontends or portals.

2. Application (functional) integration denotes the machine-to-machine coupling of applications. It can be achieved by exchanging electronic messages via remote method invocation or service calls, but may also be associated with asynchronous communication.

3. Data integration describes a direct link on the database level. The most popular forms of data integration are data synchronization or replication, and file copy.

Fig. 4. Generic model of technical integration.

This classification scheme (shown in Fig. 4) is applied to the case material and allows us to classify and cross compare the cases.

## 5.2. Conceptualization of organizational integration

For the purpose of conceptualizing the organizational context, we classified every case according to its industry sector, its position in the supply chain, and the direction of integration. For the position in the supply chain, we used the roles described by the SCOR model. These are: manufacturer, distributor, wholesaler, retail, service provider and intermediary.

The direction of integration can be differentiated according to the position in the supply chain (Ruhnke, 1966; Robertson and Langlois, 1995; Hong, 2002):

1. Vertical integration: The partners are in the same industry sector but at different positions in the supply chain. This is typically the case of supplier or customer integration in supply chains.

2. Horizontal integration: The partners are in the same industry sector and at the same position in the supply chain. Since the partners share a common role, they collaborate to create an augmented output for their suppliers or customers.

3. Diagonal integration: The partners are in different industry sector and at different position in the supply chain. Typical examples are shared services or outsourcing scenarios.

The following section describes the results of the analysis of the first set of case studies.

## 5.3. The a priori B2B integration model

The 14 initial cases show two characteristic forms of integration. Firstly, there are companies in which the involved parties deploy different information systems for the collaborative process. Secondly, solutions exist in which the integration is realized through a joint software system.

The comparison of the 14 solutions resulted in five identifiable integration scenarios (integration processing capabilities) (Schubert, 2008):

\- Scenario 1: Parallel use of different information systems with manual system access.

\- Scenario 2: Parallel use of different information systems with data interchange and direct connection.

\- Scenario 3: Parallel use of different information systems with data interchange provided by an intermediary.

\- Scenario 4: Joint use of a self-operated, central ERP system.

\- Scenario 5: Joint use of a central system operated by an intermediary.

In the following sections, the five integration scenarios are described in generic terms using a graphical representation. Each scenario is then elaborated using a vignette to explain how a company would typically deploy this scenario. Finally, we provide illustrative examples to explain each scenario using data drawn from the case companies presented in Table 2.

## 5.3.1. Scenario 1: Parallel use of different IS with manual system access

Scenario 1 describes a situation in which a company has a powerful internal information system, e.g. an ERP system, and wants to extend access to this system to partner companies. Two parties are involved in this scenario, (1) the initiating partner and (2) the connected partner (the party using the electronic connection channel to manually access the integration platform). As indicated in Fig. 5, the integration is realized with the help of an access gateway (a portal) that is internally connected to the backbone information system. This way the company does not directly open its critical ERP system to the partner(s) but makes a dedicated mediating platform available. In this scenario the connected partner(s) may or may not operate their own ERP system, which is not directly connected to the mediating platform (the portal). As a result, partners must perform a separate data management process using their own information systems (if necessary).

![](/api/attachments/8F534M4P/fulltext/images/cc326e519f68656d29ff21edcd57f19143d82312d7317113f807da3232616195.jpg)  
Fig. 5. Scenario 1: Heterogeneous ERP Systems, External System Access.

Vignette: Company A, a subsidiary of a large domestic conglomerate, produces chocolate and chewing gum products for the domestic market and for export. In order to concentrate more on their core competencies, Company A introduced a vendor managed inventory (VMI) solution. With this solution suppliers can independently monitor resources and needs of Company A and place orders. With VMI, the supplier is responsible for ensuring the optimum inventory level in the customer’s warehouse. Through the portal, the suppliers have access to pre-defined areas of the customer’s ERP system. The benefits of this VMI solution are reductions of inventory, lower transaction cost as well as a stronger and more reliable relationship between supplier and customer. The basic idea is that the suppliers themselves constantly monitor resources and needs of the articles they provide. Company A can now concentrate more on their core competencies and gives the suppliers more freedom in the handling of their master contracts.

The software application is an installation of SAP Enterprise Portal. The system provides warehouse and order processing functionality and generates reports and evaluations. The portal which is necessary for displaying VMI information to suppliers is based on SAP Enterprise Portal, a component of SAP NetWeaver 04. It is used for authentication of users and the provision of individual access rights to functions and data of the ERP system. The portal stores the account information, role definitions and the user mappings in a separate database. The suppliers only need a Web browser to access the portal server of Company A. Using a Web browser has the advantage that no additional programs need to be installed and maintained and any Internet-enabled computer can be used. Through the portal solution the supplier communicates directly with the database of the ERP system of Company A. No data is stored on the system of the supplier. If needed, such information has to be keyed in again or has to be exported from the ERP system of Company A and imported into the supplier’s ERP system.

Fig. 5 shows the schematic description of such a portal solution.

The following two examples further illustrate collaboration solutions with the help of a portal, which is internally integrated with the ERP system of the initiating party. In one case study a service portal for different target groups (named POLY-COM) is described, by which both the unstructured communication processes (in a ‘‘project room‘‘) as well as the structured process for repair orders are supported. The portal is integrated through an internal interface with the ERP system of the initiating institution RUAG. In a second case, the company Chocolat Frey operates an ‘‘Enterprise Portal’’ for external access to requirements and stock data for suppliers. The ERP systems of the suppliers are not integrated with the portal. With the help of the portal system, the business partners are able to operate a Vendor Managed Inventory for Chocolat Frey.

## 5.3.2. Scenario 2: Parallel use of different IS with data interchange and direct connection

The majority of the case studies describe an electronic document exchange between the ERP systems of different partners. The variety of different implementation approaches used here is remarkable. In some cases the integration of the ERP systems is realized with a direct connection (scenario 2); in other cases this is done through an intermediary (scenario 3).

In the case of direct integration (scenario 2), the ‘‘initiating partner’’ takes control of the conception and the operation of the integration infrastructure (gateway). The connected party can either operate its own integration gateway or use a direct link to the integration gateway of the initiating partner (see Fig. 6). If the connected partner operates its own integration gateway it can act as the initiating partner on its part and encourage its own customers/suppliers to connect through this gateway. Today, integration gateways are in most cases ready-made components of standard enterprise systems and can be ‘‘bought’’ together with the ERP system. Large ERP vendors such as SAP and Microsoft offer specialized integration solutions (e.g. MS BizTalk Server and SAP NetWeaver).

Vignette: All logistics processes of Company B were to be outsourced to the specialist logistics Company L. In the course of this project, an interface between the ERP system at Company B and the warehouse management system (WMS) at Company L were to be implemented. The integration of the two systems included the following main processes and functionalities:

\- Order processing: Picking and shipping by the logistics service provider Company L.

\- Delivery of purchased merchandise directly to Company L.

![](/api/attachments/8F534M4P/fulltext/images/56c85ee833cba1eba4c6f55c8d9fb4b645c1cb247f41dca5ec90c305c4352125.jpg)  
Fig. 6. Scenario 2: Heterogeneous ERP Systems, EDI, Direct Partner Integration

\- Inventory balance between ERP system of Company B and Logistics System at Company L.

\- Update and maintenance of master data in both systems.

Incoming customer orders are received at Company B mainly by phone, fax, email or EDI and entered into the sales module of the ERP system. The order data is transferred to the warehouse management system. On the basis of the order data the allocation for shipping the products to the respective customer is made. In batch operation (4–5 times a day), the data is sent via an interface to the WMS of Company L where the allocation of freight forwarders and the picking process is prepared.

The central element of the solution is the ERP system Semiramis 4.2, which is based entirely on Java. Relevant to the collaboration processes are in particular the features of order processing, production planning, purchasing and warehousing, as these require the active integration and the exchange of data between the ERP system at Company B and the WMS of Company L. The integration gateway is written in Java and controls communication with the interface at logistics service provider Company L.

As shown in Fig. 6, scenario 2 is the one that was most frequently encountered in the cases. Further examples include e.g. the company Candulor that uses the particular import/export capabilities of its standard ERP system Simultan ERP and creates the capability for their customers to send orders to their ERP system electronically via a specially formatted Excel file. This enables validity checks at the collection point of customer orders. Alongside this, an interface was set up for the syn chronization of management of goods between the parent company and subsidiaries.

At INTERSPORT Switzerland SAP Exchange Infrastructure, or short SAP XI, an integration component provided by SAP, enables the direct electronic exchange of structured messages between the parent company, subsidiary and independent (specialist) trade partners in the area of order processing and stock management of goods.

For a smooth integration with their logistics service provider, the firm Sonax developed a special integration interface between their ERP system and the logistics system of their service provider. With the help of self-defined ‘‘telegrams’’ (exchange documents), 166,000 structured messages between manufacturer and logistics service provider are exchanged monthly for the processes of order processing, management of goods and warehouse management.

The company RUTRONIK receives orders, consignment removals, forecasts, delivery notes, and invoices from 300 customers via a specially programmed e-mail gateway. In this process the customers send e-mails with attachments containing specified document types. The simplicity of the system ensured that even larger RUTRONIK customers, who have their own EDI systems at their disposal, do not use their own infrastructure but conduct their connection via the e-mail gateway instead.

## 5.3.3. Scenario 3: Parallel use of different IS with data interchange provided by an intermediary

Scenario 3 describes a situation in which the business partners each operate their own information systems that are electronically connected through the infrastructure of an intermediary (see Fig. 7). This scenario represents the case of traditional electronic data interchange (EDI) through the infrastructure of value added networks (VANs). These VANs provide gateway services to other VANs and electronically connect businesses worldwide. Some are specialized on industry sectors (e.g. SupplyOn for manufacturing) or business documents (e.g. PayNet for invoices). Other providers, such as Crossgate or GXS, support multiple industry sectors and document types.

Vignette: Company C is a small company that produces syrups, spreads, dessert sauces, as well as finished products for bakeries. With the help of an ERP standard software for SMEs and a connected e-business platform they managed to fulfill the requirements for electronic document exchange of one of their most important customers, Company Z. Company C uses the order processing module of Abacus, its ERP system, to process incoming orders for goods. The exchange of electronic orders and delivery notes between Company C and Company Z is realized using a specialized EDI network provided by the ERP vendor. The network provider also facilitates the transmission of invoices. The bills are digitally signed by a specialized financial gateway provider. For the VAT-compliant archiving of electronic invoices Company C uses the online archive of the financial gateway provider. Archiving is thus outsourced. Auditors can access the stored invoices with a Web browser in the online archive.

The important customer, Company Z, uses SAP for their internal operations. When Company Z sends an order to Company C it is first sent out as an iDoc, a proprietary SAP format using the network services of the intermediary BIS (Seeburger) for the data transfer. The document is then relayed to AbaNet (another intermediary) where it is converted to ABADOC, the internal Abacus format. Company C finally receives the order through a special e-business communicator that facilitates the import into the Abacus software where it can be accessed from any PC workstation in the order processing module

![](/api/attachments/8F534M4P/fulltext/images/663b2afa1fdd8a753ed5dc200418cbf644b69b02935675af4bb73b34756c19c9.jpg)  
Fig. 7. Scenario 3: Heterogeneous ERP Systems, EDI, Connection to Intermediaries

Two exemplary companies use the services of a specialized intermediary for the exchange of structured business documents. The company tts Global Logistics uses the Business Integration Platform (BIP) of the VAN provider Crossgate both for the internal integration of two information systems as well as for external data exchange with customers. With this connection, tts can send transfer orders, order status, stock movement and delivery notes in tts format to Crossgate. Crossgate collects these business documents and forwards them in the format favored by the customer. Via the B.I.P., tts exchanges 80,000 documents monthly with their customers.

The German retailer EDEKA receives invoices via the platform of its provider 1stbp. 1stbp offers a process in which a message in EANCOM format is created from a print data stream and forwarded to the corresponding recipient (similar to the process of printing to a pdf file). At EDEKA, around 440,000 annual routine internal invoices can be electronically received and automatically processed with this possibility. Additionally, the portal also offers access to the status of the invoice and therefore provides a transparent invoice processing between the involved parties.

## 5.3.4. Scenario 4: Joint use of a self-operated, central ERP system

In this scenario a central ERP system facilitates direct access for external parties without the need of an intermediating platform. In this scenario the initiating partner operates the central system and provides various parties with access to it by means of terminal emulation software or a Web interface. This integration solution is typically used for internal integration with subsidiaries (different locations) or an external sales force. However, it can also be used to connect to external partners (e.g. a service partner or customers).

Vignette: Company D uses the ERP system Microsoft Dynamics AX. The operation of the ERP system is outsourced to a hosting partner. Access to the system is available for all users via a Citrix Presentation Server, both for the employees at the various locations of Company D as well as external partners, such as the logistics service provider. At two locations Company D produces roof tiles in a wet process. The manufacturing process is characterized by a very intense use of the manufacturing facilities. Production planning is key to the company’s success. Company D pursues a consistent outsourcing of its software applications. The company uses ‘‘thin clients’’ with no local software installed except for the operating systems and the Citrix terminal software. Even the use of CD drives or the possibility of access via the USB interface is omitted. The integration of data and processes is realized through one central ERP system with remote access for authorized users (internal as well as external).

Fig. 8 shows the multiple possibilities of the cross-site integration via a central ERP system.

Two exemplary companies illustrate the solution of an integrating, central ERP system for business collaboration. The company Musik Hug has a similar approach as Company D. The ERP system of the music store allows the construction of a ‘‘virtual warehouse’’, which unites the article master of all branches and makes possible external access via a terminal server/clients or rather via a Web shop for branches and customers. In this way, a cross-site stock management and article search is possible. The wine merchant Vinothek Brancaia enables its on-site staff and sales representatives a VPN access to the central ERP system. This leads to a central order entry and to an improved analysis via Business Intelligence Functions.

## 5.3.5. Scenario 5: Joint use of a central system operated by an intermediary

Scenario 5 describes an integration solution in which a central integration platform is used to facilitate business processes and data interchange between interests groups (in the same industry sector). The central system is typically run by one of the involved parties or by an independent intermediary (see Fig. 9). The connected parties are independent in the operation of their own information systems. As in scenario 1, there is a certain degree of data redundancy because the connected parties contribute to the central system but also need to maintain their own internal databases. This solution is typical for industries with a strong need for the standardization of data exchange, as for example the finance industry. The SWIFT network is an excellent example for this scenario. SWIFT (an association founded by financial institutions) acts as a service provider mandated to run an intermediating networking service.

Vignette: Company E acts as an intermediary for a whole supply chain. The company developed a coordination platform for the timber industry that connects many small suppliers (forest owners, forestry companies, forestry companies, shippers and timber marketing companies) and a few large companies (e.g. sawmills, paper and energy producers). Timber comes in different forms and can be used for different purposes ranging from firewood for the generation of energy to wood for high quality furniture. Company E built up an electronic coordination platform where available timber can be registered, offered, purchased and marketed. The platform supports a smooth and transparent process for all connected parties. It frees each of the parties from the need of buying their own individual software solution. All functions can be accessed using a Web browser. The platform is financed by member fees. The software was developed in cooperation with a specialized software vendor who now owns shares in the platform delivery Company E.

The consulting firm Swisscanto Real Estate Management is another illustrative case from the sample providing a sector based solution. The platform VIAM enables access for external management companies and service providers and conducts processes for order processing centrally, with finalizing invoice issue via another integrated IT provider for all participants.

## 7. Conclusions and future research

The findings documented in this paper are drawn from a long-term research project in the area of B2B integration. From the literature we identified the need for a technology-agnostic reference model that allows us to classify and compare different B2B integration approaches among supply chain partners. While our long-term goal is to develop an assessment model for B2B integration, this paper focuses on the development of the basic reference model, most importantly the technical integration aspects.

The main contribution of this research is the identification of the five integration scenarios, which were derived from an initial set of 14 cases and validated using a total of 112 real-world company projects. These B2B integration scenarios represent (in a technology-agnostic way) how companies establish electronic links between internal information systems and those of external partners. The scenarios thereby extend existing research, which is largely limited to studies of specific B2B integration technologies, such as EDI or XML. We introduce a general classification scheme, which was developed from the research literature and an investigation of real-world cases. This scheme not only allows for the evaluation of B2B integration capabilities independently from the specific implementation technology but can also be used for comparing the effectiveness of different technologies in realizing a specific integration scenario. On closer examination, the case studies show a variety of different implementation approaches for each B2B integration scenario. This heterogeneity is a result of (1) the different information systems used by the involved parties, (2) the message content which is mostly displayed in a proprietary, vendor-specific format, and (3) the many different ways of exchanging electronic documents. This raises the question of why today, more than 20 years after the introduction of off-the-shelf ERP software, B2B integration architectures are still ‘‘tailor-made’’ and implement proprietary instead of standard interfaces. One could expect that nowadays every information system that has an interface for the reception and dispatch of structured business documents would be based on widely accepted message content and transmission standards. The analyzed cases show that this is not the case. Today’s companies appear, as ever, compelled to reinvent the wheel and to develop their own solutions and interfaces, tailored ideally to the needs of their internal value chain as well as their supply chain (Schubert, 2008). The result is a heterogeneity of integration solutions, which will be difficult to manage with the increasing diffusion of electronic networking and, if left unsolved, will present a significant problem in the future.

While our study provides interesting insights into B2B integration, its results must be viewed in light of some limitations. As discussed earlier, we observe a large heterogeneity of B2B integration technologies. However, our study does not answer the question why this heterogeneity exists and why industry standards have not reached widespread adoption. Drawing on other studies (Zhu et al., 2004), it could be argued that path-dependency or the significant costs of standards implementation are possible reasons. Another limitation of the study may be its focus on German-speaking countries. We are currently extending the research study and adding new cases from other European countries. As described in the beginning of the paper, the long-term goal of this research is the development of an assessment model for B2B integration which will enable us to study B2B adoption patterns, network effects, and the cost/benefit ratios for electronically connected companies.

Another interesting topic for future research is the design of the next generation of B2B integration architectures. In order to face heterogeneity, networked businesses could use services of specialized intermediaries who construct closed systems for their customers and offer gateways to third-party networks. The rationale for using intermediaries can be bound in network effects and the fact that every single party connecting to a network can reach all other participants at an instance. From a technical perspective, it can be assumed that the broad dissemination of service-oriented architectures (SOA) will facilitate connections between different information systems. The increasing use of Web Services for internal as well as external access to information systems supports this assumption. However, the 112 cases used in this study show little, if any, evidence for this trend towards Web Services.

A possible solution to the challenge of global electronic networking is the creation of ERP systems with standardized interfaces and the use of worldwide directories for the identification of market participants (e.g. D-U-N-S or Global Location Number from GS1). In the case of telephony (and post) worldwide valid telephone numbers (and addresses) have been established, by which business partners can be found in the network. Another desirable solution in the area of B2B integration would be the development of a globally recognized business partner directory for electronic data exchange.
