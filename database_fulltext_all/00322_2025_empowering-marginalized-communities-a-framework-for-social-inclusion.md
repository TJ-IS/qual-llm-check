# Empowering Marginalized Communities: A Framework for Social Inclusion

Israr Qureshi,<sup>1</sup> Babita Bhatt,<sup>2</sup> Shena Shaikh<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Australian National University, Australia / Queen’s University Belfast, UK, i.qureshi@qub.ac.uk <sup>2</sup>Australian National University, Australia / Queen’s University Belfast, UK, b.bhatt@qub.ac.uk <sup>3</sup>Indian Institute of Technology, India, la20resch01001@iith.ac.in

## Abstract

Social inclusion—the ability to participate fully in one’s social world—is gaining importance in policy and academic circles. Information systems research has shown how addressing digital divides and expanding individual capabilities could increase the inclusion of marginalized groups. Yet while these contributions are notable, much of early research often overlooked the deep-seated power relations embedded in social structures—organized patterns of relationships, norms, and institutions that perpetuate inequalities and hierarchies based on gender, race, ethnicity, and caste. However, the field has evolved to bring a more nuanced understanding of how social inclusion can be achieved during the implementation of digital projects. Building on these emerging insights, in this paper, we explore how a social infomediary—an intermediary addressing social issues through information provision to marginalized communities—uses a digitally enabled agriculture extension project to build social inclusion in communities. Drawing on a qualitative case study of a social intermediary in India, our research highlights the role of social context in facilitating and constraining social inclusion efforts. Based on our findings, we develop a 4R social inclusion framework for digital development projects that shows the importance of recognition, reposition, representation, and reciprocation in fostering social inclusion. We also identify corresponding processes: transformative narratives and dialogues, empathic scaffolding, structured discursive spaces, and innovative interdependence. We discuss the practical and theoretical implications of our research and provide future research directions.

Keywords: Social Infomediaries, Digital Development, Recognition, Reposition, Representation, Reciprocation, Power Asymmetries, Community Dynamics, Participatory Development, Social Hierarchies, Gender, Caste

## 1 Introduction

Achieving social inclusion—the equitable participation of every member of society in social institutions and relationships that enhance well-being (Alam & Imran, 2015)—remains a significant grand challenge, prompting initiatives from institutions like the World Bank and UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

that emphasize its necessity in addressing poverty and inequality. This focus arises from a growing recognition that recent economic and social progress has not reached all levels of society, with rising income inequality, cultural divides, and entrenched social hierarchies underscoring the need for sustainable, inclusive development (Bhatt et al., 2022; Qureshi et al., 2023, 2024). Yet, traditional top-down development approaches often deepen community fragmentation by limiting participation in broader socioeconomic activities and reinforcing existing inequalities (Bhatt et al., 2022; Zainuddin et al., in press). Despite scholarly and policy interest in social inclusion, communitycentered processes to achieve it remain insufficiently explored. Here, we define communities as groups of individuals within a geographically defined area (a village, in our case)<sup>1</sup>, sharing “some elements of local culture, norms, identity, and laws” (Marquis & Battilana, 2009, p. 286).

The prominent discourse on social inclusion focuses on improving participation for marginalized groups (Bhatt et al., 2023; Meneses & Mominó, 2010). Historical and institutional factors perpetuate the exclusion of these groups through overt and covert sanctioning of cultural norms, social hierarchies, and power asymmetries (Bhatt et al., 2023; Qureshi et al., 2018, 2024; Zainuddin et al., in press). Addressing social divides requires proactive efforts to build equitable relationships and alter power dynamics within communities (Qureshi et al., 2022) and often requires moving beyond straightforward uncritical thinking to embrace adaptive approaches to development that consider social dynamics (Bhatt et al., 2022). Digital technologies, for instance, provide many avenues to design social development projects. However, research has demonstrated that it has proven to be particularly challenging to implement digital development projects for marginalized communities due to profoundly entrenched power asymmetries and persistent social hierarchies (Vaidya & Myers, 2021), which often present critical issues for social inclusion (Qureshi et al., 2018; Sutter et al., 2023).

In this paper, we define digital development as the application of digital technology in development initiatives specifically tailored to benefit marginalized populations. <sup>2</sup> Extant digital development literature underscores the potential of digital technology to address diverse social issues (Fabregas et al., 2019; Malecki, 2003). Indeed, given the increasing importance of digital technologies in fostering societal change, including enabling changes in social relations (Bhatt et al., 2022; Qureshi et al., 2021a), there is an interest in understanding their role in mitigating persistent hierarchies and fostering social inclusion within

## communities (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019).

In this context, the role of social intermediaries— intermediaries with social rather than commercial objectives (Parthiban et al., 2024; Zainuddin et al., in press)—has gained scholarly attention. Notably, social infomediaries are posited as potential alternatives for addressing power asymmetries and promoting social inclusion in communities (Qureshi et al., 2021a, 2022; Zainuddin et al., in press), as they leverage information provision to address socioeconomic challenges faced by marginalized groups. We build upon the social intermediation literature (Zainuddin et al., in press) and synthesize it with infomediary discourse to conceptualize social infomediaries. The role of social infomediaries in addressing socioeconomic challenges faced by marginalized groups adds a nuanced dimension to the ongoing debate on digital technologies’ effectiveness in fostering social inclusion, which emphasizes a pressing need to understand how power asymmetries influence the success of digital technologies among marginalized communities (Lin et al., 2015; Vaidya & Myers, 2021).

The literature presents mixed findings on the impact of digital technologies, with some studies highlighting their role in enhancing communication and information exchange for marginalized groups (Alam & Imran, 2015; Diaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016). This suggests that digital technologies can expand capabilities, enhancing agency and fostering inclusion. Conversely, critical perspectives argue that social inclusion efforts might lead to adverse digital incorporation of marginalized groups (Heeks, 2022; Yang, 2022), particularly within entrenched inequalities and social hierarchies (Qureshi et al., 2018; Sutter et al., 2023; Vaidya & Myers, 2021). Power imbalances may result in dominant groups exploiting marginalized communities (Bhatt et al., 2022; Qureshi et al., 2023), highlighting complex intergroup power dynamics within social inclusion efforts. Symbolic practices play a significant role in the construction, justification, and maintenance of power asymmetries, making social systems unyielding to change, thereby acting as barriers to progress (Vaidya & Myers, 2021). Given the mixed findings on social inclusion in marginalized contexts, there is a need for a clear definition of social inclusion, emphasizing its multidimensional nature and the importance of intergroup relations.

In contrast, digital development takes a broader view, encompassing the transformative impact of digital technologies (e.g., basic as well as advanced, such as blockchain, big data, and digital platforms) on societal, economic, and governance systems (Fabregas et al., 2019; Malecki, 2003). It emphasizes the integration of digital infrastructure, skills, policies, and ecosystems to drive systemic and sustainable development, while also addressing issues such as power dynamics, inequality, and governance.

Drawing on the literature, we define social inclusion as a multidimensional and relational community attribute expressed in parity of participation and transformative power relations among social groups. Parity of participation refers to equitable participation opportunities, while transformative power relations mean reducing dominant groups’ power over marginalized groups and empowering them to achieve their valued goals. Understanding power dynamics and overcoming exploitative social hierarchies is crucial for achieving and sustaining positive social outcomes (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019; Poveda & Roberts, 2018; Zheng & Stahl, 2011). Attention to power—who is included, who controls access, and who defines technology use—can help us understand the social processes that facilitate or hinder social inclusion. However, there is a limited understanding of how digital technologies can promote social inclusion in communities with entrenched inequalities. This paper explores how social infomediaries leverage digital development projects to build social inclusion within communities—specifically, examining a social infomediary’s use of a digitally enabled agriculture extension project to foster inclusion.

This paper presents the 4R social inclusion framework (recognition, reposition, representation, and reciprocation) derived from an eight-year case study of a digitally enabled agriculture extension program implemented by a social infomediary in rural India, a context marked by extreme gender- and caste-based exclusion. Through 12 months of fieldwork, we identified these iterative, nonlinear steps as critical to fostering social inclusion in digital development projects. Recognition involves acknowledging and addressing systemic barriers faced by marginalized groups, fostering empowerment through transformative narratives and dialogues. Repositioning shifts community perceptions and interactions with marginalized groups using empathic scaffolding, <sup>3</sup> embedding inclusion within broader objectives like information provision. Representation ensures marginalized groups’ participation in public spaces and decision-making through structured discursive spaces that uphold respect and dignity. Finally, reciprocation builds interdependence and mutual exchange through innovative interdependency, leveraging shared goals and resources to address systemic barriers collaboratively. Together, these steps offer a comprehensive framework to guide social inclusion in digital development initiatives.

Our study makes three theoretical contributions to the literature. First, we present a social inclusion framework for digital development projects through a 4R framework and TESI processes (transformative narratives and dialogues, empathic scaffolding, structured discursive spaces, and innovative interdependence) for digital development projects working on social inclusion under extreme marginalization. Second, we contribute to the emerging literature on social intermediation by exploring the role of digital technologies in achieving social intermediation. Finally, we add to the digital development literature by providing a framework that highlights systemic inequalities and power structures in communities and identifies the relevant processes for implementing digital development projects in communities characterized by social hierarchies and inequalities. Our findings suggest that digital technology can, at best, be an occasion to address some of these issues, but it cannot help build social inclusion by itself. The 4R framework proposed in this research also provides practical insights into how organizations can leverage digital technologies to build social inclusion in heterogeneous, hierarchical communities and bring about positive societal transformation.

## 2 Literature Review

## 2.1 Digital Inclusion and Social Inclusion in IS Research

Social inclusion has been variously understood in terms of improved avenues for equitable engagement in and with society. It is largely defined in terms of “participation” and has been conceptualized as the ability to fully participate in one’s social world (Silver, 2015) and control one’s destiny (Warschauer, 2003). It has emerged as a topic of significance in information systems (IS) research (Alam & Imran, 2015; Diaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016; Qureshi et al., 2022; Wagg & Simeonova, 2022), with studies focusing on how marginalized groups understand, use, and are impacted by information technologies (Qureshi et al., 2023; Sutter et al., 2023; Trauth, 2017). Technology can foster and hinder the social inclusion of marginalized groups (Diaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016; Pethig & Kroenung, 2019). The relationship between digital technology adoption and empowerment is complex, fluctuating between empowerment and subjugation (Bhatt et al., 2022; Pandey & Zheng, 2023), with research indicating how marginalized groups are often excluded from IS projects aimed at fostering inclusion (Pethig & Kroenung, 2019; Qureshi et al., 2023; Trauth, 2017; Zheng & Walsham, 2021). The dynamics between social inclusion and exclusion are complex and have the potential to be influenced by how IS projects are implemented in marginalized contexts (cf. Curto-Millet & Cañibano, 2023). It is crucial to recognize that the absence of social exclusion does not necessarily signify social inclusion (Silver, 2015). True social inclusion extends beyond mere tokenistic inclusion and entails actively engaging marginalized individuals in the decision-making processes affecting them, requiring capability expansion and overcoming social barriers (Bhatt et al., 2023, 2024). Traditional approaches to digital inclusion that emphasize the availability of or access to technology are not sufficient for effectively bridging the digital divide and would greatly benefit from the participation of marginalized communities in the decision-making processes around the implementation and use of the technology (Faik et al., 2024; Wass et al., 2023). Hence, understanding power dynamics within communities is crucial for fostering inclusive and equitable outcomes.

Research on digital technologies and capability expansion could provide insights into our understanding of facilitators and barriers to social inclusion. The extant literature in this domain has often overlooked power dynamics (Poveda & Roberts, 2018; Zheng & Stahl, 2011). Social hierarchies and norms in heterogeneous communities can hinder positive outcomes for marginalized groups (Garmendia & Karrera, 2019; Vaidya & Myers, 2021). Attention to power—who controls access, usage, and decision-making regarding technology—is essential, as digital initiatives are not inherently egalitarian or participatory (cf. Bhatt et al., 2022, 2023; Qureshi et al., 2018; Sutter et al., 2023). The following subsections further explore these arguments and highlight asymmetrical power relations within communities.

## 2.2 Social Context, Power Asymmetries, and Social Inclusion

Contexts of social exclusion are characterized by pronounced social inequalities embedded within social norms, informal institutions, rules, and routines, which often manifest as institutionalized power imbalances (Bapuji & Chrispal, 2020; Bhatt et al., 2022; Qureshi et al., 2023). For example, inherently unequal social or digital environments, such as those perpetuating ableism (Ameri et al., 2020) or normalizing stigmatization (Petter & Giddens, 2023), hinder digital inclusion by amplifying social biases. Understanding these complex contexts is essential for designing digital development projects that genuinely build capabilities and foster inclusion. Without careful consideration of these factors, projects risk reinforcing rather than bridging social divides.

The information systems literature on capability development emphasizes redefining approaches to address these challenges in marginalized communities, asserting that only digitally enabled projects that contribute to community well-being are sustainable (Vaughan, 2011). Vaughan’s research highlighted this sustainability in culturally homogenous Indigenous communities with shared well-being aspirations, which contrasts with the diverse sociocultural groups (Qureshi et al., 2018, 2024; Sutter et al., 2023) often found in heterogeneous contexts where capability developmen faces unique challenges (Bhatt et al., 2022, 2023; Qureshi et al., 2022). Digital development projects implemented through participatory engagement frequently encounter obstacles in settings marked by entrenched disparities and restrictive social norms (Bhatt et al., 2022; Qureshi et al., 2022, 2024). Research in participatory development further underscores that sociocultural heterogeneity, structural inequality, and socioeconomic hierarchies can hinder effective participatory approaches (Bhatt, 2022). In these contexts, social norms and opportunities often shape group aspirations in exploitative or discriminatory ways (Bapuji & Chrispal, 2020; Bhatt et al., 2022). Privileged groups may exploit these norms to further their interests, making participatory projects appear inclusive while potentially reinforcing inequalities (Platteau, 2004; Qureshi et al., 2018; Voors et al., 2018). This reality may limit the ability of marginalized groups to participate meaningfully in collective decision-making and, in some cases, even exacerbate existing inequalities (Sutter et al., 2023; cf. Bhatt et al., 2024). Consequently, research suggests that social inclusion must transcend individual capability development, as true inclusion requires group participation in social processes and balanced intergroup relationships (Silver, 2015). In highly unequal communities, individual capability development alone may not improve marginalized groups’ situations if they remain socially excluded and dependent on dominant groups (Bhatt et al., 2022).

However, implementing digital development projects that genuinely foster social inclusion poses significant challenges. For inclusion to be meaningful, implementing agencies must actively engage marginalized groups while remaining vigilant against project capture by dominant groups (Qureshi et al., 2023; Sutter et al., 2023). These organizations need to proactively create participation opportunities for marginalized groups, particularly in contexts marked by deeply entrenched and exploitative social norms (Bhatt et al., 2022; Faik et al., 2024). Consequently, the role of social intermediaries has become increasingly prominent in implementing projects with the potential for true social inclusion (Qureshi et al., 2021a; Zainuddin et al., in press). Many of these initiatives utilize digital technologies to support and achieve these objectives (Qureshi et al., 2021b).

## 2.3 Social Intermediation in Digital Development Projects for Social Inclusion

Social intermediaries are socially motivated organizations aiding marginalized communities by developing skills, capabilities, and opportunities for effective market participation (Bhatt et al., 2023; Qureshi et al., 2021a; Zainuddin et al., in press). They blend social practices emphasizing care and compassion with business principles of efficiency and scalability (Bhatt, 2022; Zainuddin et al., in press). This integration optimizes strategies to support disadvantaged populations (Bhatt et al., 2023), aiming to empower communities, promote social inclusion, and foster sustainable development for a more equitable society. Some intermediaries, termed social infomediaries, focus on information awareness and information-led capability development (Sutter et al., 2023). They act as conduits for information dissemination, which is crucial for addressing social exclusion in marginalized communities. Building on the literature on social intermediation and infomediaries, we define social infomediaries as social intermediaries that engage in digital development projects to cultivate information awareness and promote information-led capability development to address various social issues in marginalized communities.

Prior research has alluded to how social infomediaries design development projects to facilitate social inclusion by exercising embedded agency (Qureshi et al., 2021b), engaging in social value creation (Qureshi et al., 2022), and engendering social capital (Qureshi et al., 2021a). Examples include facilitating financial inclusion (Ravishankar, 2021), improving artisans’ livelihoods (Parthiban et al., 2024), promoting sustainable farming (Qureshi et al., 2021a), giving a voice to the voiceless (Bhatt et al., 2022), and challenging discriminatory practices (Qureshi et al., 2018). Recognizing the intricate interplay between digital inclusion projects and social contexts, some social infomediaries understand the significance of social dynamics and power structures on individuals’ interactions with digital technologies (Sutter et al., 2023). Individuals are seen as active agents within dynamic social contexts shaped by power relations (Zheng & Walsham, 2021). The intersection of social exclusion and digital exclusion often perpetuates a vicious cycle of marginalization, underscoring the significance of examining how digital technologies intersect with prevailing power structures to either exacerbate or mitigate social stratification (Qureshi et al., 2022, 2023a; Zheng & Walsham, 2021).

Social structures have been found to constrain the availability, accessibility, and usability of information by marginalized communities, even when social infomediaries are inclined to leverage digital technologies to disseminate information (Bhatt et al., 2022; Qureshi et al., 2018, 2022; Sutter et al., 2023). When considering the interaction between social exclusion and digital exclusion, it is pivotal to evaluate how various social groups are positioned within overlapping systems of power and how digital technology can lead to further exacerbation and reproduction of social stratifications (Zheng & Walsham, 2021).

To fully capitalize on the transformative potential of bottom-up initiatives, social infomediaries embedded within local contexts possess a distinct capacity to drive meaningful outcomes by tailoring their approaches to the unique characteristics, technological practices, and specific needs of each community (Bhatt et al., 2022; Leong et al., 2016; Qureshi et al., 2021b). Nevertheless, it is imperative to recognize that digital inequalities often mirror broader social disparities (Qureshi et al., 2023), especially in rural settings, where communities encounter unique contextual barriers that necessitate socially embedded projects and technofied implementation (Bhatt et al., 2023; Parthiban et al., 2024; Qureshi et al., 2021b, 2022). Contemporary literature increasingly highlights how social infomediaries, through their leadership in digital development projects, can effectively alleviate poverty, even within resource-scarce contexts where mistrust of technology and resistance to change are prevalent (Qureshi et al., 2018; Zainuddin et al., in press). This stream of literature suggests that social infomediaries have the potential to develop digital ecosystems that provide integrated and multilayered interventions to address grand challenges such as rural poverty (Bhatt et al., 2022; Jha et al., 2016).

While extant literature has provided valuable insights, a significant gap exists in understanding the specific processes social infomediaries employ to facilitate social inclusion within marginalized communities. As emphasized, social inclusion is a fundamentally relational concept, meaning that fostering true inclusion requires deep engagement with the intricate power dynamics within communities. This critical gap reveals a pressing need for focused research to “theorize processes that can explain inclusion/exclusion” within Information Systems (Trauth, 2017, p. 15). To address this need, novel perspectives are essential to uncover the complex, interwoven processes that shape social inclusion within digital development projects. Such perspectives must closely examine the “complex bricolage of access and inequity” (Windeler et al., 2023, p. 1199) that underlies social inclusion efforts. Therefore, this paper seeks to answer a core question: How do social infomediaries, involved in digital development projects, foster social inclusion within communities?

## 3 Methodology

To examine the role that social infomediaries play in promoting social inclusion through digital development, we employed an inductive single-case research design (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). Our study focused on Saath (a pseudonym), a social infomediary operating in rural India, which was selected through a purposeful sampling method (Yin, 2003). Saath is an exemplary case as it is well known for its work in creating sustainable, inclusive communities and has received many national and international awards. To understand the processes used by Saath to facilitate social inclusion in communities, we first discuss the social context in which Saath implements its digitally enabled solutions.

## 3.1 Research Setting

This study was conducted in Madhya Pradesh (MP), a central Indian province with a significant population (23.41 million) below the poverty line. MP was chosen due to its challenging setting for examining social structures and hierarchies, particularly regarding caste and gender dynamics. The region exemplifies strict social structures and pronounced hierarchies based on caste and gender, which are common across many Indian provinces. The caste system, an ancient hierarchical social system, has historically marginalized certain castes, perpetuating poverty and socioeconomic inequality. Women from “lower” castes and poor backgrounds in MP face multiple forms of discrimination, including limited access to education, healthcare, employment, and higher rates of gender-based violence. Despite efforts by NGOs, significant progress in addressing caste and gender discrimination remains elusive. Consequently, these issues persist as significant challenges in Madhya Pradesh and India as a whole.

## 3.1.1 Caste System

In India, the caste system officially classifies society into four groups: Schedule Caste (SC), Schedule Tribes (ST), Other Backward Classes (OBC), and the General category, reflecting a history of systemic discrimination against SC and ST groups due to their lower perceived social status. While the caste system’s influence has evolved, it continues to perpetuate deep-seated inequalities and marginalization (Bapuji & Chrispal, 2020). A 2005 survey of 41,554 households (Desai & Dubey, 2012) highlighted significant disparities between “upper” castes and SC-ST groups in landownership, education, household consumption, and social capital, with SC-ST groups consistently disadvantaged. Rural studies also reveal SC-ST overrepresentation among landless or marginal landowners and their limited access to agriculturerelated information (Dang & Lanjouw, 2018). Caste still dictates social life in rural India, enforcing occupational and social exclusion. For instance, SC groups are often barred from selling milk to village cooperatives (Thorat & Sadana, 2009), face residential segregation, and experience restricted access to shared resources such as wells. Violations of these norms are met with severe penalties, often including violence (Das, 2013).

## 3.1.2 Gender Issues

The societal context of this research is deeply influenced by gender-based hierarchy and patriarchal norms, despite gender equality being enshrined in the Indian constitution. India ranks 140 out of 156 countries in the Global Gender Gap Index, slipping 28 places between 2006 and 2021, reflecting worsening gender parity. In rural areas, where agriculture dominates, women’s labor is often undervalued, seen as secondary to caregiving roles or subsistence farming and perceived as low-skill compared to men’s work, involving advanced tools or cash crops (Desai & Joshi, 2019; Kingiri, 2013). Social norms further restrict women’s mobility and interactions, while the intersection of caste and gender exacerbates exploitation. Within this challenging environment of exclusion, Saath’s digital development project offers an ideal lens to explore how social inclusion can be fostered.

## 3.1.3 Saath: The Social Infomediary

Saath, based in New Delhi, operated in eight of India’s poorest provinces during the data collection period, focusing on women empowerment, grassroots governance, and livelihoods for marginalized groups. In partnership with an international social intermediary leveraging technology for knowledge transfer, Saath launched a digital project to strengthen rural livelihoods, particularly in MP’s poorest districts. The project used video recordings of effective agricultural practices by successful local farmers, screened in village group settings using portable, battery-operated projectors. Facilitators, or mediators, led these sessions, with a key challenge being the creation of inclusive screening environments amid sociocultural barriers. Later, Saath expanded the project to include digitally enabled market linkages to boost income opportunities for rural farmers. Data collection for this study covered the project’s inception, building on Saath’s two decades of grassroots experience.

## 3.2 Data Collection and Analytic Approach

We employed an inductive single-case research design (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007) combined with the Gioia method for data analysis (Gioia et al., 2013) to explore how social inclusion can be strengthened. This approach was well suited, as we did not have a predefined understanding of how this process would unfold. Data collection included observations, informal conversations, semi-structured individual and group interviews, and archival data review (Yin, 2003), enabling a nuanced understanding of local dynamics and triangulation to reduce bias. Seventeen field visits over eight years, totaling approximately 12 months,<sup>4</sup> provided rich data, including 82 interviews with Saath staff members, 202 individual and 85 group interviews with villagers, 34 interviews with other stakeholders, and observations of 37 video screenings. Additionally, we stayed in 51 villages to observe social dynamics; further details are summarized in Table 1.

Multiple visits enhanced the study by allowing us to observe unfolding dynamics, refine research questions and theory, and triangulate insights across villages over time, improving temporal validity (Tsang & Williams, 2012). Interviews, mostly conducted in Hindi and a few in English, followed a semi-structured format to explore emerging themes. <sup>5</sup> Transcriptions and translations ensured accuracy for analysis. Observations included documenting video production, screenings, agricultural practices, and Saath staff meetings, supported by extensive field notes, photographs, and videos, when permitted. Archival data, including Saath’s reports and public-domain project news, complemented the interviews and observations.

## 3.3 Data Analysis

The initial phase of the analysis comprised the compilation and organization of various forms of data, including field observations, interview transcripts, media reports, and other archived materials (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Yin, 2003). The analysis was then conducted in accordance with the methodology outlined by Gioia et al. (2013), which entailed an iteration of the data and emergent theoretical categories. The data analysis was, therefore, an iterative process comprising two stages, as described below.

## 3.3.1 First-Order Analysis

As part of the first-order analysis, we conducted a thorough scan of transcripts, field notes, and archival data to discern dominant themes for additional investigation. We then analyzed these themes to identify similarities and differences between them. Through this approach, we organized the themes into first-order codes and labeled them using terms that closely mirrored those employed by participants (Gioia et al., 2013). Given our particular focus on social inclusion, gender, and caste-related issues during the implementation of the digital project, we gave specific consideration during the coding process to examining the social dynamics surrounding group formation and the characteristics of participating and excluded social groups during the screening process.

For instance, our data analysis revealed numerous instances in which Saath executives and field staff reported discriminatory practices followed by “upper” castes against marginalized groups. Comments such as “dominant caste have more resources,” “marginalized groups are silenced in joint meetings,” and “dominant groups are used to making all the decisions” were prevalent.

These comments were documented as first-order codes. The process of identifying these codes was in accordance with the iterative methodology recommended by Gioia et al. (2013), entailing repeated evaluations of the data to refine our understanding and the reassignment of labels for the first-order codes in accordance with that improved knowledge.

## 4 Findings

Saath aimed to promote agricultural extension services by introducing video screenings of agricultural practices that were locally relevant to rural communities. The first critical step in this process was forming screening groups in a context marked by extreme social exclusion. Our fieldwork identified four key steps taken by Saath that not only established these groups but also fostered social inclusion at the village level. While the implementation process was iterative and overlapping, for analytical clarity, we present it as the 4R framework: recognition, reposition, representation, and reciprocation (see Figure 1). Below, we provide an overview of these steps before delving into detailed findings.

The 4R framework demonstrates how digital development projects can leverage technology to promote social inclusion. The first step, recognition, involves acknowledging individuals’ inherent worth beyond their social status, understanding sources of exclusion, and identifying power asymmetries and patterns of domination. This process sets the foundation for designing programs that facilitate positive interactions and avoid reinforcing inequalities. Transformative dialogues and narratives emerging from this step help spotlight exclusionary practices and establish a basis for inclusive digital development.

The second step, reposition, focuses on changing dominant narratives without directly challenging power holders. Digital development projects can address mutual interests to improve social dynamics and foster inclusion. By prioritizing marginalized groups’ needs while remaining inclusive to others, these projects can reposition schemas around mutuality and inclusion, leading to improved social interactions.

The third step, representation, emphasizes enabling marginalized groups to participate in public decisionmaking processes. This involves analyzing restrictions on their participation and creating fair, transparent mechanisms for deliberation and discussion. Structured discursive spaces are crucial here, where intergroup dynamics are managed through trained facilitators and civil dialogue. Technology can assist communicative actions, but without prior recognition and repositioning, dominant voices risk continuing to overshadow marginalized groups

The final step, reciprocation, builds interdependence through partnerships and mutual respect, fostering bottom-up collectivization. ## 4.1 Recognition Through Transformative Dialogue and Narratives

In the initial stages of implementing their project, Saath realized the importance of understanding community dynamics and identifying sources of marginalization and oppression that underpin community interactions. The following quotes from executives of Saath highlight their perspective about community:

[Dominant caste] also used to make all the decisions [in this village]. [SC, ST] usually do not have a say in such activities. To implement our program, we spend some time understanding these social dynamics between different caste (Field executive-3, Saath)

It is not only about providing technology or identifying information gaps. For us, it always starts with understanding community relations. We know that without supportive community relations, our program will not sustain. So, we start with that premise (Field Executive-1, Saath)

Our interviews with the field staff and observations in the field also corroborated with Saath’s community first approach. For example, villages in rural MP are still segregated based on caste. Our field observations showed that the rich and influential castes live near important resources such as a pond, temple, and main connecting road. In such a context, any attempt to provide resources and build capabilities rarely reaches marginalized groups (cf. Bhatt et al., 2022). To bring change, Saath prioritizes the interest of the most marginalized groups:

We are committed to provide opportunities to women. We are committed to helping socially and economically marginalized farmers. We ensure their participation in every activity [projects] we are involved in (Founder, Saath)

There has been various program in the past to provide the latest information and knowledge [on farming practices] in rural areas, but access [to these programs] was limited to only the rich and dominant farmers. We wanted to make sure that we provide information to those who did not have access to it before (CEO, Saath)

However, due to a history of marginalization and exploitation of SC and ST farmers, the initial inclusion attempts by Saath were met with many obstacles— particularly opposition from the dominant caste and mistrust of the marginalized groups. Similarly, Saath faced challenges in forming screening groups for women:

Members of [dominant caste] used to tell us that there is no use of conducting screening for [SC, ST] because they will not learn anything (Field staff-3, Saath)

Most [dominant caste] men were opposed to forming screening groups for women. They would say “it is against our culture.” Some didn’t feel any necessity of such [screening] groups for women. They would make all the excuses for not creating such groups (Field staff-5, Saath)

Our interviews with various social groups also captured the intergroups and gender-related tensions. These tensions were the result of historical exploitation and marginalization that resulted in resource control by dominant groups and mistrust among the marginalized groups. The dominant caste mostly believed and created narratives that the members of marginalized castes are ignorant and do not have the capacity to learn new practices. Therefore, they do not deserve access to screenings. The following quotes from the dominant caste during the earlier phase of our fieldwork capture these narratives:

We like this new company. They show videos about new farming practices, and it is useful. but it is only useful if you have knowledge and skills. It cannot benefit those [implying marginalized group] who are uneducated and do not know basic things (Male villager-3, dominant caste)

Things are changing now but people in these villages have to still follow their social norms. It is not possible in this village for people from different caste to mix together. We need to maintain distance from them [marginalized castes]. (Male villager-8, dominant caste)

Due to generations of exploitation and oppression, marginalized castes were resigned to their fate. They had accepted the discrimination they faced and bought into dominant castes’ narratives that if they wanted to live in these villages, they could not be seen as antagonizing the members of the dominant caste. They also lacked trust in outside organizations—as, in the past, these organizations often sided with the members of dominant castes. The following quotes from marginalized castes, during earlier phases of our fieldwork, capture these observations:

These people [dominant castes] constantly mock and taunt. They make stories that demean us [marginalized caste]... Most of these stories and traditions are designed to keep us poor and not make any progress. These made-up stories repeat our incompetence, clumsiness, ignorance, and impurity Unfortunately, many [marginalized caste members] themselves start believing in these stories. They lose their [self-] respect. They do not feel confident. (Social activist, marginalized caste, nearby town)

We did not interact with [Saath]initially. We did not want to provoke [dominant caste] by interacting with outsiders without their [dominant caste] blessings. (Male villager-52, marginalized caste)

Like caste-based exclusion, gender-based exploitation and marginalization were also common:

Women are mostly restricted to domestic work. They also work in their own fields … They are not supposed to talk to men outside their families. Sometimes they are not supposed to say anything in the presence of elderly male family members. (Field executive-11, Saath)

We struggled to organize video screenings for women. Male family members used to ask us: “What is the benefit of attending the screening for these women?” They were not convinced that women would learn anything by watching videos. (Mediator-6, Saath)

Women from various social groups also expressed the lack of support from family members:

We do want to participate in such programs. It can give us time to go out and interact with others. But we always need to take permission to go outside. They [family elders] would make fun and ask what the benefit is? Why would you go? They usually say it is not important (Female villager-27, dominant caste)

These caste and gender dynamics initially created difficulties for Saath in implementing their programs and getting acceptance from all community members. To address the challenge, Saath decided to hold meetings with the village elders and other prominent people to better understand their narratives around why screening groups for marginalized social groups should or should not be created. Saath also held similar separate meetings with each social group to understand their narratives about the formation of screening groups for the marginalized:

The transformative narratives that emerged through meetings conducted by a team of professionally trained field livelihood staff who were sensitive to community dynamics were helpful in changing conversations. They behaved respectfully with each social group in the villages, irrespective of their caste and gender identity, informing them about the project and its overall benefits. The staff also encouraged villagers to share their views, apprehensions, and expectations. These approaches by the staff proved very helpful in reaching out to and interacting with marginalized groups:

They [Saath staff] visited us many times at our home. They were very humble and respectful. They used to sit with us on the floor, ask our problems, and discuss with us how we would like to solve these farmingrelated problems. Sometimes they would also suggest what we can learn from [videos] if we join screening groups. No one [from an external organization] talked to us like that before (Male villager-67, marginalized caste)

These respectful and open interactions through transformative dialogue were useful in winning trust, encouraging narratives, and, over time, enhancing the self-respect and confidence of the marginalized groups. Saath repeated this process in several villages over 18 months. Although there was no direct dialogue between the dominant and marginalized castes at this stage, out of fear of repercussions towards the marginalized caste members, these mediated dialogues helped Saath understand the nuances of community dynamics. It also helped Saath realize that, given the prominence of exclusionary practices, it would be difficult to form inclusive joint screening groups comprising various castes or ensuring the social inclusion of marginalized castes through separate screening groups. Saath also realized that even if it succeeded in forming separate groups for the marginalized, the dominant caste would prevent the screenings. The transformative narratives and dialogues in the first phase helped identify challenges and issues of common interest for everyone.

## 4.2 Reposition Through Empathic Scaffolding

Drawing on the evolving scholarship on scaffolding (Bhatt et al., 2022; Mair et al., 2016; Qureshi et al., 2022; Sutter et al., 2023), we conceptualize empathic scaffolding—which emerged from our data—as a set of deliberate activities orchestrated by a social infomediary aimed at transforming the entrenched negative perceptions of marginalized communities and dismantling patterns of exclusion. In this framework, the empathic objective of social inclusion for the most marginalized is subtly integrated within a broader, ostensibly neutral goal, such as information provisioning. While the management literature frequently characterizes scaffolding as an inherently positive construct (Bhatt et al., 2022; Mair et al., 2016), it is imperative to acknowledge contexts where scaffolding mechanisms have been co-opted to serve hidden agendas: for instance, political manipulation, deceptive facades in criminal networks, or ideological smokescreens intended to perpetuate adverse outcomes for marginalized populations (Giroux, 2019; Jackson, 2015; Monterosso, 2018). By adopting the term empathic scaffolding, we underscore a distinctive dimension of our field observations, where the social infomediary’s actions were grounded in empathy-driven outcomes for marginalized communities—thus marking a salient deviation from the typical representations of scaffolding in the extant literature.

Through direct engagement with community members, Saath was able to discern the complex power dynamics within the community. In a context marked by exploitation and a rigid social hierarchy, fostering inclusion necessitates a transformation of these deeply embedded, unequal power structures. Extant research in community development underscores that dominant community members are often reluctant to relinquish power (Platteau, 2004; Qureshi et al., 2018; Voors et al., 2018), making it clear that a fundamental step toward social inclusion involves challenging and reshaping these inequitable power norms.

Motivated by empathy for marginalized groups, Saath undertook several initiatives to disrupt the prevailing power dynamics, though these efforts outwardly appeared as activities of shared community interest. Through its interactions with diverse social groups, Saath identified that issues such as reforestation and water recharging resonated across different segments of the community. Saath subsequently leveraged these common concerns to design community events that emphasized mutual interests, subtly fostering a sense of shared purpose and mutuality:

We wanted to find a common ground ... We focused on an issue that is important for [all the social groups]. Everyone need water. ... Building water recharge [structure] is a topic of common interest and it is easy to start discussion around it. (Field staff-16, Saath)

Finding a common issue that matters for all social groups is essential. It allows them to move beyond their narrow interest (Founder, Saath)

Reforestation initiatives, which involve planting saplings and tending young plants, focus on a common issue vital to all social groups. The resulting forest offers extensive benefits, preventing soil erosion, aiding rainwater retention, providing food for livestock, and supplying firewood. These shared projects provide an inclusive platform for each group to engage in and contribute to. Saath proposed assigning the responsibility of caring for newly planted trees to ST men and women, acknowledging their expertise in sustainable forest management. Given the relevance of these projects to every villager, objections from the dominant caste against the involvement of the marginalized caste were mitigated. The emphasis was on finding common ground and mutual benefits, fostering collaboration beyond narrow interests:

We understand how important forests are. We agreed with [Saath]that we need to plant more trees. We provided them with places where they can plant these trees - schools, panchayat, and also near our farms… We allowed them to use water from our wells for these plants (Male villager-42, dominant caste)

[Saath] has created new opportunities for us…We have been taking care of forests for a long time, but no one values that. But now, we can use our knowledge for the new nursery and we feel good when others ask us questions and want to learn new things from us (Female villager-71, marginalized caste)

These projects were implemented for several months—in some villages, for more than a year—and resulted in participation from the higher- and lower-status groups in most of the villages. Nevertheless, in most villages, this marked the initial collaboration of these social groups in a shared project, leading to challenges associated with a deficit in trust and respect. Saath facilitated conversations and deliberations addressing these issues. These projects also established a modest level of mutual understanding among the social groups, fostering reliance on each other for the successful implementation of the project.

During this process, Saath also shared the success stories of their previous environment-related projects. These stories highlighted the role of marginalized groups in successfully managing and running various common projects in the communities. Through these efforts, Saath was able to counter the narratives that marginalized groups do not have the capacity to contribute and learn anything. Saath used this occasion to reposition the narrative from the assumed lack of capability of the marginalized to the issues of their access to new knowledge and practices. This repositioning through empathic scaffolding helped Saath initiate some screening groups for marginalized castes. Saath used the pretext of reforestation and a similar common project to show videos on forest management to marginalized caste screening groups.

## 4.3 Representation Through Structured Discursive Spaces

Encouraged by some degree of repositioning of the narratives, successful implementation of participatory reforestation and water recharge projects, and identification of the mutuality of these projects—as well as for screening groups of marginalized castes on forest management—Saath introduced the idea of regular screening groups. Due to their long-term engagement and influence in the villages and building on the success of reforestation and water recharge projects, Saath was able to form mixed caste and, with some difficulties, mixed-gender groups.

In addition to forming screening groups, creating spaces for interactions between various social groups and ensuring that such interactions are not shaped by existing power relations is crucial for building social inclusion. Saath faced this tension when deciding on the location of such screenings and screening-related meetings:

Finding an ideal location for meeting is challenging. If it happens at the [village head’s house], or at the home of a [dominant farmer], then many people can come and watch video. Space is not a problem. However, then the marginalized people might not be able to enter or might not be treated equally. (Field staff-12, Saath)

We always choose a neutral place [for screening]. It can be a schoolroom, space in the panchayat office, and other such places. [Marginalized caste] feel more comfortable in such locations. (Regional manager, Saath)

Saath identified Choupals and, in some cases, school premises and other such common locations as neutral places for conducting the screenings. Furthermore, instead of relying on external experts, Saath hired a local resource person to facilitate the meetings. These local resource persons (also known as mediators) were trained by the staff in facilitation skills. Mediators engaged with diverse social groups, addressing their inquiries, clarifying doubts, and aiding farmers in embracing new practices.

These meetings were further used to create inclusionary norms. For example, it was required that all the participants sit on the floor and each person be treated respectfully. In several meetings, we observed mediators from Saath skillfully encouraging marginalized groups to participate in the discussion, ask questions, and share their experiences.

We train our mediators to encourage healthy participation from everyone, including marginalized groups. They are sensitized to be inclusive while conducting meetings. (Senior executive-2, Saath)

We have been taught how to facilitate these meetings. One of the most important aspects is to encourage questions and discussion among the participants. Such interactions can help in learning from each other. (Mediator-7, Saath)

The formation of mixed-gender screening groups was a sensitive issue. In a social context where women were not used to speaking in the presence of men in their own families and were not allowed to step out of their homes, it was difficult to initiate mixed-gender groups. Saath, therefore, first formed women-only groups:

In most villages, farming is an effort where both men and women help out in the fields…. They do not work at the same time and they do engage in different activities. However, due to the typical setup in villages, it may not be possible to have them all together in one screening group. We want to show our videos to both. It may not be possible to show [the videos to] them together. (CEO, Saath)

We wanted to follow community norms, at least initially. Women were then encouraged to invite their male family members in their group. Now, some women’s groups have men regularly joining [the screenings]. (Field staff-9, Saath)

The formation of mixed-gender groups was structured through a gradual and deliberate process. Once womenonly groups were formed, Saath let these screening groups evolve their own norms for discussion and respectful listening to others’ views. The structured discursive spaces involved encouraging women to ask questions, discuss, and share their own, at times rich, experiences. Once these norms were set in, Saath suggested that each woman could bring one male family member to join these screenings if they felt comfortable. In these structured discursive spaces, it was clearly communicated to the men joining these groups that these were primarily women-only screening groups. They could attend the screening but were expected not to interrupt women and wait for their turn to ask questions. Thus, mixed-gender groups emerged from women-only groups. In the mixed-gender groups, we observed that mediators structured the interaction and discussion from the start by requesting that women be allowed to ask questions first before men started asking questions. In this way, women were able to gain confidence in asking questions in the presence of men. They also became comfortable with sharing their expertise. Our interviews and observations of these screenings provided details on the benefits of these screening sessions:

Earlier I would only attend but not speak anything. But now, I ask questions if something is not clear. (Female villager-64, marginalized caste)

I would never say a word [in presence of men]. Then I joined this group… Men started joining our group after six months … I was hesitant. I saw other women ask questions.

They share [their experiences]. Slowly I also started asking questions in presence of men. Now I am not afraid of their presence. (Female villager-14, dominant caste)

Screenings, in general, had a positive impact on those who had access to them, highlighting the need for socially inclusive screening sessions:

We used to think the more pesticides we use, the better it is for our farm, the more seeds we throw in the farm, the better crop we will have … but we learned this is not always the case, we used to waste so many seeds. (Male villager-37, dominant caste)

I learn a lot from videos on pomegranates … I asked bhaiya [mediator] to bring more videos on this topic. Now I grow them in my farm. Few weeks ago, I was asked to give [a demo] to others who were interested in growing pomegranates in their own farms. (Male villager-72, Marginalized farmer)

These screenings proved beneficial in fostering interactions among diverse groups. The mediator played a crucial role in facilitating fair participation processes. They provided a platform for various community members to express their opinions and share experiences, fostering an environment where marginalized groups felt at ease speaking, experimenting, and exchanging their knowledge with others. Consequently, Saath successfully established structured discursive spaces, utilizing screenings as opportunities to bring various social groups together.

## 4.4 Reciprocation Through Innovative Interdependency

The experience gained through the reposition and representation subprocesses helped Saath understand the importance of scaffolding in highlighting mutuality, achieving joint participation, and enacting discursive spaces. However, scaffolding is meant to be temporary and transient. It cannot stay forever. Thus, it became important to establish reciprocation through creative interdependency among the members of various castes. We define reciprocation as the dynamic process of identifying, structuring, fostering, and reinforcing mutually beneficial interdependencies among members of various social groups within a community. This involves creating a framework where different social groups rely on each other, fostering collaboration and shared benefits. The process of reciprocation is highlighted through specific activities undertaken by Saath, including structuring video screenings to highlight interdependencies, the inclusive process of forming screening groups, and the establishment of local institutions like village-level committees (VLC) to institutionalize these interdependencies.

Continuing with its activities of reforestation and water recharge projects, Saath suggested that the ST would be able to take better care of these trees and ponds if they were exposed to additional information through video screenings, which in turn would help everyone in the village. Thus, ST members would benefit from being included, and others would benefit from their greater expertise in managing trees and ponds. Saath used a similar approach to create screening groups for the SC under the pretext of providing information on better management of water bodies for irrigation, the outcome of which would be beneficial for everyone in the village. Once screening groups for the marginalized were created, Saath identified other interdependencies. For example, certain crops and vegetables were only cultivated by the lower caste members but consumed by others. Similarly, some members of the marginalized castes had previously worked on the farms of dominant castes. Saath structured its screening sessions in such a way that the information shared would directly benefit the marginalized castes and indirectly benefit the dominant castes, thus reinforcing interdependencies. This avoided challenges from dominant castes and still benefited both the marginalized and dominant castes. As there was an indirect benefit to be gained, the dominant caste did participate in the discussions and mutual learning, thus reinforcing interdependency.

The villagers, including those from marginalized castes, mentioned the impact of Saath’s initiatives in creating mutually beneficial interdependencies among various social groups within the community. Through structured video screenings, Saath has facilitated a dynamic learning process, allowing marginalized caste members to gain valuable insights into farming practices. In the past, marginalized caste farmers lacked information about various framing practices. Seeking information from dominant caste farmers was often met with reluctance, hindering knowledge sharing. However, the introduction of video screenings allowed the marginalized farmers to acquire essential knowledge that is valued by the dominant caste farmers. This had an empowering effect on marginalized farmers through enhanced knowledge. These videos not only improved marginalized farmers’ agricultural practices but also elevated their status within the community. By acquiring knowledge, marginalized farmers became valuable contributors, recognized and relied upon by dominant caste farmers for cultivating their fields. This shift illustrates the establishment of a mutually beneficial reciprocal relationship—the marginalized farmers gain recognition and the dominant caste farmers benefit from the specialized knowledge possessed by the marginalized group:

We learn many things from this [videos] … Earlier we used to go to [dominant caste farmers] and ask [about farming]. Most of the time they won’t tell us anything … Now we know many things. Some time they ask [about practices]. (Male villager-68, marginalized caste)

These [videos] have helped me work better in the fields of [dominant caste]. Now they rely on me and other [marginalized farmers] to help cultivate their fields… They have money and big land but we have “gyan” [knowledge]. (Male villager-59, marginalized farmer)

In essence, Saath’s strategy involved using video screenings to share knowledge, fostering mutual learning and collaboration among diverse social groups. This empowerment of marginalized groups created a more inclusive environment, enhancing community wellbeing. Additionally, the establishment of VLCs mirrored gram panchayats, providing formal spaces for interaction, economic collaboration, and decision-making. These committees promoted mutual interdependencies within the community. Utilizing digital technologies like mobile apps enhanced economic interactions, facilitated negotiations and efficiency in agricultural produce sales. VLCs were gender and caste-representative, to create inclusive environments:

Our goal is to organize and connect community member through building local institutions such as producer groups, villagelevel committees. It is a long-term and slow process. But once created, these institutions can guarantee sustainable and inclusive community development. (CEO, Saath)

These committees provide formal spaces for interactions and helps the members connect with the external agencies. We have been using them as platform to introduce various activities. (Male villager-92, marginalized caste, village committee member)

The VLC further created mutual interdependencies and enhanced economic interactions among community members. During our visit, many farmers confirmed that they were able to negotiate better terms for buying inputs, selling village produce, and securing group-based loans due to an aggregation effect. VLC used a mobile application created by Saath to get advice on agriculture and market prices. The VLC relied on digital technologies such as mobile apps for market linkages. For example, a mobile app was used to get information on the market prices and facilitate the fast collection and sale of agricultural produce from the village to the market. The VLC also arranged shared transportation to sell the produce to the market. This further deepened and formalized interdependencies:

Earlier I had to go to market to sell my vegetables … on bicycle. It was very tiring. Roads are not good. Now, I do not have to go anywhere. Now I talk to the committee on phone and check the time for collection. (Male, marginalized caste)

It [the aggregation service] has saved us a lot of time and lot of hustle. We can use that time to relax and spend more time with each other and learn new things. (Male, dominant caste)

Thus, the market linkages saved time and effort in selling the produce to the market. Additionally, the gradual development of mutual understanding and interdependence, fostered by shared economic interests, facilitated collaboration among group members. The establishment of the VLC also created a space for social engagement, fostering the formation of social ties among individuals from diverse backgrounds. During our field visits, many farmers reported that the committee meetings provided opportunities for learning and interpersonal engagement. Furthermore, the staff and some members of the community observed that the frequency of interactions among community members had increased, and there was a heightened sense of collaboration and information-sharing, even between members of different caste groups.

## 5 Discussion

In the findings section, we observed and reported the processes, opportunities, and challenges of strengthening social inclusion through a digitally enabled agriculture extension project. This project was implemented in rural India, which is marked by extreme power asymmetries and the social exclusion of marginalized people. Specifically, we investigated how a social infomediary was able to overcome historic social hierarchies and ensure the inclusion of marginalized members in the face of challenges posed by dominant groups through the creation of either common or separate screening groups. We combined our insights to present a theory of technology-enabled social inclusion.

Through our social inclusion framework for digital development projects, we explain (1) how social organizations can use digital development projects to address social challenges and overcome power asymmetries and (2) under what conditions these attempts are more likely to build social inclusion in communities. We identify four steps leading to social inclusion (4R framework): recognition, reposition, representation, and reciprocation. Although we separate them for analytical purposes, all four steps reinforce one another, and are interdependent and crucial for social inclusion. They are further connected by the underlying intent of transforming power relations. In social inclusion projects, a multitude of norms and social hierarchies drive exclusion (Bhatt et al., 2022, 2024), as dominant groups often control both implicit and explicit exclusionary practices (Yang, 2022). It is important to understand the complexities of social inclusion in designing development projects (Wass et al., 2023), as power dynamics among various social groups within the communities is an often-overlooked dimension of digital development research (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019). Our 4R framework and the processes associated with each step: (1) recognition through transformative dialogues and narratives; (2) reposition through empathic scaffolding; (3) representation through structured discursive spaces; and (4) reciprocation through innovative interdependency help navigate these power asymmetries. We label these processes collectively as TESI processes, as shown in Figure 2.

Recognition is the first step towards achieving social inclusion in the context of digital development projects. Recognition involves identifying the processes based on historical and structural exploitation (cf. Fraser, 2001). Recognition goes beyond providing access to resources and brings attention to sociostructural dynamics. It involves identifying patterns of power, domination and marginalization, enhancing dignity, and encouraging diverse narratives. A social infomediary rooted in the local reality understands that simply providing access to resources without changing existing exploitative social structures will result in resources being captured by dominant members of the community (Platteau, 2004; Bhatt et al., 2022; Vaidya & Myers, 2021; Voors et al., 2018). Thus, it is important to create conditions where narratives from various social groups, including the marginalized, are encouraged in a way that results in cross-social group dialogue. Social inclusion concerns with the participation of members as peers. At the core of social inclusion, therefore, is a concern with the existing power asymmetries that have the potential to be exacerbated if digital development projects are not implemented inclusively (Wagg & Simeonova, 2022). Recognition draws attention to the implications of these asymmetries for inclusion. It underscores the power relationship that is the root cause of exclusion (Lin et al., 2015).

The process of recognition starts with a deep understanding of the social dynamics and the sources of exclusion and marginalization. Only after understanding these structural sources of exclusion can social intermediaries design and deliver their programs in a way that might lead to positive interactions between social groups. Recognition through transformative dialogues and narratives (Figure 2) forms a crucial step in inclusive digital development projects. Extant research on digital development provides examples of economic inclusion through improved access to essential and timely information such as commodity prices, weather information, and access to financial services (Jensen, 2007; Ravishankar, 2021). We extend this research by foregrounding attention to the underlying process of exclusion and marginalization. We suggest that the current dominant perspective on digital development, which views technology as a resource (cf. Sutter et al., 2023), running the risk of not identifying and acknowledging the social processes that are based on historical and structural exploitation (Sutter et al., 2023). Without a critical understanding of these exploitative processes, digital development projects could suffer from the risk of exacerbating existing inequalities.

Reposition is the second step towards achieving social inclusion in the context of digital development projects. Reposition represents a shift in how individuals and social groups understand and interact with marginalized groups. This requires changing the dominant narratives without directly confronting those in power. Reposition involves acknowledging and reducing power imbalances and addressing the ways in which these imbalances contribute to marginalization. It also involves developing new relationships and partnerships grounded in mutual respect, understanding, and collaboration. To do this, the social infomediary iterates between Steps 1 and 2 to identify historical issues and reposition dominant narratives around these issues. In the organizational context, scholars have highlighted how power relations play an important role in designing and implementing information systems projects (Jasperson et al., 2002). However, issues related to power in the context of marginalized communities have received less attention than they deserve in IS literature (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019). These issues are especially prominent in the context of diverse communities marked by social hierarchies, as dominant groups do not easily give up their power, which is often rooted in enduring social structures (Bhatt et al., 2024; Platteau, 2004; Qureshi et al., 2018; Voors et al., 2018).

Consistent with existing literature (Bhatt et al., 2022; Sutter et al., 2023), we observed that the social infomediary initially faced challenges in changing the terms of participation to make them equitable towards the marginalized groups. Furthermore, the social infomediary also found that directly confronting those in power had its own difficulties in a hierarchical and unequal community setting (cf. Qureshi et al., 2018; Wagg & Simeonova, 2022). Therefore, the social infomediary resorted to repositioning through empathic scaffolding (Figure 2), which suggests that the social infomediary first identified one or more common issues that concern various social groups—dominant as well as marginalized. The social infomediary then used these issues as empathic scaffolding to conceal their real intention of changing social dynamics and improving social inclusion for marginalized communities. By prioritizing the interests of the most marginalized but not restricting access for other groups, Saath was able to engage in repositioning during the formation of screening groups and screening sessions. Thus, repositioning goes beyond the simple appropriation of technology (Avgerou, 2010; Heeks, 2022) and shows how organizations must address the challenge of lack of acceptability from dominant groups without overtly challenging their power.

Representation is the third step towards achieving social inclusion in the context of digital development projects. Representation means creating processes that allow the marginalized group to participate in public spaces and public forums and give voice to the voiceless in decision-making. The inadequate representation of marginalized communities in public spaces and decision-making can perpetuate asymmetric and unequal relations and reinforce existing power imbalances. To counter this, the social infomediary iterated between Steps 2 and 3 to reposition dominant narratives and used this repositioning to create representation for marginalized voices in decisionmaking (Figure 2). It requires establishing fair, transparent, and interactive processes for decisionmaking. At the center of representation is deliberation and discussion among different social groups on programs related to issues from multiple points of view. As such, representation highlights the possibility and desirability of consensus and cooperation. Social infomediaries should play a supportive role by providing infrastructure, such as creating structured discursive spaces for discussion and appointing staff members trained in managing intergroup dynamics. These are prerequisites for managing participatory processes. Such structured interactions between different groups, if managed adroitly, have the potential to broaden perspectives, opinions, and the understanding needed to build social inclusion. Representation can be achieved through an informal process such as interactions during group screening in the village Choupal (common public space) or through formalized interactions, such as village-level committees. Both types of efforts to enroll social groups in participatory initiatives also foster the development of various skills, such as bargaining, negotiation, and consensus building. While prior IS research has highlighted the role of technology in facilitating communicative actions (De Bakker et al., 2011), which are at the center of representation, without the process of recognition and reposition, such communicative actions might amplify dominant voices in decision-making. For example, the socially and economically well-off might dominate the conversation (Bhatt et al., 2022) and marginalized groups might simply concede to a compromise (as noted initially by Saath). Therefore, in hierarchical communities, building social inclusion requires the representation of each group by identifying neutral places or creating structured discursive spaces that are void of power structures. Representation is a crucial step. Unless sufficient representations of marginalized groups are ensured, a move toward reciprocation is not possible (Figure 2).

Finally, reciprocation, i.e., creating innovative interdependency through a bottom-up collectivizing process, is central to achieving social inclusion in the context of digital development projects because continuous interaction among participants can generate the norms and reciprocity needed to sustain social inclusion. Through this step, the social intermediary worked to identify the possibility of interdependencies, structure activities around these interdependencies, encourage discussion and mutual learning while working through these interdependent activities, and in the process, reinforced these interdependencies. As noted in Saath’s social inclusion efforts, communitylevel institutions, once built, can be used to facilitate mutual interactions for multiple purposes. They can be used to disseminate innovative farming practices and, depending on the context, can also be used to address the economic needs of marginalized groups by establishing market linkages (as done by Saath). Most importantly, the time and effort saved through these community-level institutions can also be used in debates and discussions on context-related issues. Such meetings and interactions can also be used to understand the sources of exclusion and marginalization. As such, the process of building inclusion continues. The 4R framework, thus, shows that the process of building social inclusion is neither linear nor something that takes place in a social vacuum (Avgerou, 2010). Instead, it takes place in the presence of several supporting social processes (illustrated in Figure 2).

## 5.1 Theoretical Contributions

## 5.1.1 Social Inclusion in IS Research

Social inclusion has been widely understood as the improvement of equitable participation in society. However, within IS research, consensus is lacking regarding the implications of digital development projects for social inclusion. Existing studies often diverge in their approaches, with some emphasizing digital inclusion as a pathway to social equity (Kvasny & Keil, 2006; Leong et al., 2016) and others adopting a capability approach to assess inclusion (Vaughan, 2011; Zheng & Stahl, 2011). While the capability approach offers significant insights into enhancing individual and community potential, its critique lies in omitting underlying power dynamics that perpetuate inequities (Bhatt et al., 2022; Poveda & Roberts, 2018; Sutter et al., 2023; Zheng & Stahl, 2011). Our study directly addresses this gap by proposing a holistic framework that integrates a power-sensitive perspective to social inclusion in digital development projects.

We advance the discourse on social inclusion by introducing a robust framework that incorporates the concept of power asymmetries, an element underexplored in prior studies. This framework highlights the dynamic and multidimensional nature of social inclusion by identifying four critical steps—recognition, reposition, representation, and reciprocation—and the processes underpinning each. Specifically, we outline actionable processes such as transformative narratives, empathic scaffolding, structured discursive spaces, and innovative interdependency, collectively termed TESI processes, as mechanisms to dismantle entrenched systemic disparities. We not only identify four broad steps that social intermediaries need to take but also the processes associated with each of those steps to address liability of difference—the entrenched systemic disparities and power imbalances that hinder social inclusion, especially for marginalized communities navigating dominant socioeconomic systems.

While extant IS literature recognizes the importance of digital skills in shaping experiences of inclusion and exclusion in the digital economy (Meneses & Mominó, 2010), the focus on digital literacy as a panacea for marginalization oversimplifies the complex interplay of skills, social inequalities, and discrimination (Wagg & Simeonova, 2022). Our findings underscore that digital literacy, while necessary, is insufficient on its own to drive inclusion. Systemic discrimination and entrenched hierarchies often prevent marginalized groups from acquiring or effectively utilizing digital skills, thereby limiting the positive outcomes associated with digital literacy (Garmendia & Karrera, 2019). By grounding our framework in the realities of power dynamics and social stratification, we illuminate the structural challenges that must be addressed to achieve meaningful inclusion through digital interventions.

Furthermore, our research contributes to IS theory by reconceptualizing social inclusion as not merely the absence of exclusion but as an active process of transforming power relations. Through our longitudinal case study of a social intermediary operating in rural India, we reveal how digital technology projects, when integrated with community-sensitive approaches, can foster transformative social outcomes. Our framework enriches the theoretical conversation by bridging the gap between capability expansion and the recognition of intergroup power asymmetries, offering a scalable model for researchers and practitioners in IS and development studies.

## 5.1.2 Social Intermediation Theory

Social intermediation theory, as contextualized within management and development research, emphasizes the role of socially motivated organizations in empowering marginalized communities rather than pursuing economic rents (Qureshi et al., 2018, 2021a; Zainuddin et al., in press). While prior studies have highlighted the potential of social intermediaries to leverage digital technologies for reducing transaction costs and improving opportunities for marginalized groups (Bhatt et al., 2023; Qureshi et al., 2022; Zainuddin et al., in press), our research provides critical nuance by challenging the assumption that such benefits are given. In contexts marked by entrenched social hierarchies and power asymmetries, we find that the use of digital technologies may not necessarily reduce transaction costs in the short term, as significant efforts and resources are required to overcome systemic inequities. However, our findings suggest that once social inclusion is achieved, transaction costs may decline over time, presenting an important empirical question for future research.

This study also extends social intermediation theory by introducing the refined concept of empathic scaffolding, which emphasizes scaffolding efforts explicitly driven by empathy toward the most marginalized groups. Scaffolding has traditionally been conceptualized in the management literature as a positive mechanism to address inequalities (Mair et al., 2016; Qureshi et al., 2022; Sutter et al., 2023). However, insights from other domains reveal instances where scaffolding is co-opted for manipulative or exploitative ends, such as political agendas, gang operations, or ideological façades (Giroux, 2019; Jackson, 2015; Monterosso, 2018). Drawing on field observations and inspired by Gandhian principles of Antyodaya (upliftment of the last person) and constructive work (Bhatt et al., 2023), we refine the concept of scaffolding to foreground empathic scaffolding. This construct emphasizes actions grounded in empathy that aim to transform entrenched inequalities while fostering inclusion. By positioning empathy at the core of scaffolding, our framework offers a principled approach to understanding how social intermediaries can actively facilitate the empowerment of marginalized groups, particularly in contexts characterized by extreme social exclusion.

Our findings demonstrate that empathic scaffolding enables social intermediaries to navigate resistance and alter dominant narratives that often depict marginalized groups as incapable or undeserving of resources. By embedding inclusion efforts within broader, ostensibly neutral objectives such as information provision or capacity building, social intermediaries can subtly challenge negative perceptions about marginalized groups and promote their equitable participation. This repositioning highlights the importance of empathy in transitioning marginalized groups from excluded recipients to active contributors in digital development projects. Furthermore, our study underscores the longterm interplay between digital inclusion and structural transformation. Digital technologies are revealed not as isolated tools but as catalysts embedded within broader social processes aimed at equity and justice. This nuanced understanding aligns digital development goals with strategies for altering power dynamics, thereby positioning social intermediaries as critical agents for sustainable, inclusive development.

The theoretical contributions of this research for social intermediation are threefold. First, it challenges the assumption that digital technologies inherently reduce transaction costs, emphasizing the critical role of power dynamics in hierarchical and inequitable contexts. Second, it introduces empathic scaffolding as a novel theoretical construct that emphasizes the role of empathydriven inclusion mechanisms rooted in transformative principles. Finally, it positions social intermediaries as pivotal agents capable of aligning digital development with structural equity, thereby advancing the theoretical discourse on the implementation of digital projects in marginalized contexts. Together, these contributions not only enrich social intermediation theory but also provide a robust framework for integrating empathy, agency, and technological facilitation into strategies for achieving social inclusion.

## 5.1.3 Digital Development

This study makes significant contributions to the digital development literature by offering an in-depth conceptual understanding of how digital development projects can enhance social inclusion. Earlier research in digital development adopted a digital divide perspective that primarily emphasized resource provisioning as a means to bridge digital gaps (Sutter et al., 2023). The assumption that access to resources, such as telecentres, would inherently overcome the digital divide and facilitate successful digital development projects has been challenged by more nuanced approaches (Sutter et al., 2023). Contemporary research has shifted its focus to understanding the multifaceted roles of digital technologies in addressing complex social issues. Within this evolution, debates persist about whether digital inclusion leads to empowerment (Jha et al., 2016; Pandey & Zheng, 2023; Zainuddin et al., in press) and the extent to which digital technologies can empower marginalized communities versus reinforcing existing power hierarchies (Bhatt et al., 2022; Qureshi et al., 2018, 2023). Proponents of digital technologies highlight their potential to enhance access to information, enable collaboration, and create economic opportunities for disadvantaged groups. Conversely, critics argue that digital technologies can perpetuate inequalities and exacerbate marginalization by reinforcing existing power structures. Building on this discourse, Heeks (2022) advances the debate by presenting the concept of adverse digital incorporation, which highlights the risks associated with integrating marginalized groups into digital systems that perpetuate structural inequalities. According to this model, technology adoption often mirrors unequal social relationships, enabling dominant groups to capture most of the value generated while further marginalizing disadvantaged communities. Our research adds critical insights to this conversation by shedding light on the institutional environments in which digital development projects operate and the specific developmental efforts required to mitigate adverse incorporation.

Our findings underscore that adverse digital incorporation can be avoided if social intermediaries adopt a comprehensive engagement approach with marginalized communities, emphasizing the principles of recognition, reposition, representation, and reciprocation. Through our analysis, we reveal that social inclusion is not an automatic outcome of resource provisioning or digital access; instead, it requires deliberate, context-sensitive processes to dismantle power asymmetries. By integrating social dynamics into the implementation of digital development projects, our framework ensures that the inclusion of marginalized groups goes beyond token participation and addresses the root causes of exclusion. These findings are critical for reframing digital development as a relational and transformative process rather than a purely technological intervention.

In conceptualizing social inclusion within digital development, our research provides three theoretical advancements. First, we highlight that digital technologies, while necessary, are insufficient to achieve empowerment in the absence of systemic efforts to address underlying social hierarchies. Second, by aligning our findings with Heeks’s (2022) model of adverse digital incorporation, we extend the understanding of how unequal power dynamics manifest in the implementation of digital systems and propose actionable strategies to mitigate their effects. Third, our framework explicitly links the success of digital development projects to their ability to facilitate the four interrelated processes of recognition, reposition, representation, and reciprocation. These processes ensure that digital technologies serve as catalysts for social transformation rather than instruments of further exclusion.

In summary, this study advances the discourse on social inclusion, social intermediation theory, and digital development by offering novel theoretical insights and actionable frameworks to address entrenched inequalities in marginalized contexts. By integrating power-sensitive perspectives into the study of social inclusion, this study reconceptualizes the phenomenon as a transformative process that addresses systemic inequities through the 4R framework and the associated TESI processes. The introduction of empathic scaffolding as a refined construct extends social intermediation theory by emphasizing empathy-driven mechanisms to foster inclusion. Furthermore, the study critiques the simplistic assumptions of resource-based digital development approaches, highlighting the importance of addressing power asymmetries to mitigate adverse digital incorporation. Overall, this research not only bridges critical theoretical gaps but also provides a robust model for practitioners and scholars aiming to leverage digital technologies to achieve sustainable and equitable social transformation.

## 5.2 Practical Implications

Our research seeks to contribute to practice in several ways. First, the 4R framework implies a need for longterm commitment and observing gradual change, as opposed to aggressive action and short-term horizons. A gradual pace can help organizations gain greater acceptance from marginalized communities for their technological projects and successfully change dominant narratives. We would encourage practitioners to be aware of the multiple ways in which the social context can influence social infomediaries’ implementation of digital development projects for social inclusion. Our study implies that practitioners may want to pay special attention to context-driven accessibility issues so that all community members can benefit from digital projects. In our study, the social infomediary was sensitive in explicitly focusing on increasing access to screening for marginalized groups and using various processes to ensure social inclusion. We also offer a word of caution. While technology is offered as a solution to several social problems, even in a technology-enabled project, the social aspects are salient, and without addressing these social problems, the implementation of technology projects may not reach those who most need them. While we believe that digital technologies such as mobile technology are incredibly promising in addressing grand societal challenges, we would encourage practitioners to carefully evaluate the social embeddedness of digital development projects.

## 6 Conclusion

In this study, we propose a comprehensive social inclusion framework for digital development projects, emphasizing the pivotal role of social intermediaries in fostering social inclusion within communities marked by entrenched hierarchies and extreme marginalization. Our framework, rooted in recognition, reposition, representation, and reciprocation (the 4R framework), elucidates the critical processes—transformative dialogues and narratives, empathic scaffolding, structured discursive spaces, and innovative interdependency (TESI processes)—essential for promoting social inclusion. Our research underscores the significance of recognizing the inherent worth and dignity of individuals and groups external to their social status. Through transformative dialogues and narratives, social intermediaries can initiate a radical act of acknowledging and respecting the identities, experiences, and contributions of marginalized groups. This recognition is foundational to fostering a sense of belonging and empowerment among historically marginalized communities. Repositioning involves a paradigm shift in understanding and interacting with marginalized groups, moving away from dominant narratives and stereotypes towards inclusive, respectful engagement. By employing empathic scaffolding, social intermediaries can facilitate this shift, fostering mutual interdependence and highlighting the capabilities of marginalized groups. Representation, the third step, emphasizes the importance of equitable participation and honest communication in public spaces and decision-making processes. Through structured discursive spaces, social intermediaries can create environments conducive to productive dialogue, challenging stereotypes and empowering marginalized communities to articulate their needs and perspectives. Finally, reciprocation entails fostering mutually beneficial relationships and collaborations grounded in mutual respect and understanding. Through innovative interdependency, social intermediaries can leverage diverse perspectives and resources within communities to drive positive change and address shared challenges collectively.

Our framework offers valuable insights for both researchers and practitioners. Social intermediaries should recognize the iterative and interdependent nature of social inclusion processes, fostering long-term commitment and gradual change rather than pursuing short-term solutions. Moreover, social intermediaries should prioritize context-driven accessibility to ensure equitable participation in digital development projects. While technology holds promise in addressing societal challenges, practitioners must acknowledge the social embeddedness of digital development projects and address underlying social issues to ensure inclusivity. By engaging with communities and prioritizing recognition, repositioning, representation, and reciprocation, social intermediaries can mitigate the risk of adverse digital incorporation and foster sustainable, inclusive community development (cf. Heeks, 2022).

Thus, our framework demonstrates that building social inclusion is not a linear process or something that occurs in a social vacuum (Avgerou, 2010) but takes place among several supporting social processes. Building inclusive communities through digital development requires a holistic approach that transcends technological solutions, prioritizing social processes that promote equity, justice, and empowerment. Our framework provides a roadmap for navigating the complexities of social inclusion in diverse community contexts, paving the way for transformative change and sustainable development.

## About the Authors

Israr Qureshi is a professor of social entrepreneurship and digital development at the Queen’s University Belfast. His research focuses on the mechanisms of social value creation, emphasizing social entrepreneurship, indigenous entrepreneurship, and digital social innovation. Israr’s interdisciplinary scholarship bridges entrepreneurship, information systems, and social impact studies, contributing to Academy of Management Annals, the Academy of Management Learning and Education, Journal of Business Venturing, Journal of Management, Journal of Management Studies, MIS Quarterly, Organization Science, and Organization Studies, among others.

Babita Bhatt is a reader at Queen’s University Belfast, specializing in the intersection of community dynamics, civil society, and organizational studies. Her research explores alternative organizing frameworks to investigate how organizations generate social impact within economically disadvantaged communities. Babita’s work is recognized for advancing the understanding of organizational theory and social innovation, with publications in Academy of Management Annals, Journal of Business Ethics, Journal of Business Venturing, Journal of Management Studies, Organization Science, and Organization Studies, among others.

Shena Shaikh is a PhD candidate in the Department of Liberal Arts, Indian Institute of Technology, Hyderabad. Her interdisciplinary research examines the systemic determinants of grand societal challenges and the sociocognitive and institutional barriers that shape and sustain social inequalities. Drawing on social entrepreneurship and innovation theories, feminist ethics, critical management studies, and critical social psychology, her work investigates the complex relationships between power structures and social justice outcomes, focusing on identifying catalysts for transformative social change and avenues for creating sustainable and equitable futures.
