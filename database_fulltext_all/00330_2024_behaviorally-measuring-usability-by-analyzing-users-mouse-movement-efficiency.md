# BEHAVIORALLY MEASURING USABILITY BY ANALYZING USERS’ MOUSE MOVEMENT EFFICIENCY<sup>1</sup>

David W. Wilson Marriott School of Business, Brigham Young University Provo, UT, U.S.A {davidwilson@byu.edu}

Joseph S. Valacich Eller College of Management, University of Arizona Tucson, AZ, U.S.A {valacich@arizona.edu}

Jeffrey L. Jenkins Marriott School of Business, Brigham Young University Provo, UT, U.S.A {jeffrey\_jenkins@byu.edu}

Usability—the extent to which a system can be operated to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction—is a hallmark of many successful systems. Identifying usability concerns with existing measures, however, can be problematic. We use biased competition theory (BCT) to explain how interfaces with low usability create attentional interference, which can be measured through mouse movement efficiency (MME). We tested our hypotheses in four studies. Study 1 manipulates attentional interference and examines its influence on MME using eye tracking to validate BCT as an appropriate theoretical lens for our research. Study 2 manipulates usability and shows similar markers of attentional interference in MME on a website with two different populations. Study 3 is a field test that compares the reliability of MME in ranking the usability of different components of a commercial web application to rankings produced by a common perceptual measure of usability: perceived ease of use. Collaborating with a Fortune 100 software vendor, Study 4 further demonstrates the efficacy of MME in ranking the usability of various system components compared to a standard industry measure of usability across three different products. The results consistently show that MME can help identify usability differences among parts of systems. The results have implications for facilitating more efficient and temporally precise usability research and mass-deployable usability testing.

Keywords: Ease of use, usability, biased competition theory, response activation model, attentional interference, multimethod research, mouse movements, technology acceptance, human-computer interaction, eye tracking, product-led growth

## Introduction

Modern products and services are increasingly judged on the quality of the user experience they provide, and companies that prioritize user experience enjoy a significant competitive advantage (Sheppard et al., 2018). End users drive significant product growth for software providers who can both measure and improve user experiences along many stages of a user journey (OpenView Partners, 2022). Usability—the extent to which a system can be operated to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction (ISO, 2019)—is a critical component of the user experience (Hassenzahl & Tractinsky, 2006). Improving system usability has been a focus within academic disciplines for decades (Hess et al., 2014; Hornbæk, 2006), and businesses continue to allocate significant resources to usability engineering and testing with compelling returns on those investments (Hogan & Laufer, 2016).

A variety of methods for measuring system usability have been developed, including questionnaires, user interviews, and objective measures of users’ performance (e.g., time to complete a task, number of errors) (Maia & Furtado, 2016). The most commonly used approach is survey questionnaires conducted after an interaction is complete (Maia & Furtado, 2016). For example, the perceived ease of use (PEOU) instrument (Davis, 1989)—frequently used to measure the degree to which a person believes that using technology will be free of effort—has provided a convenient, reliable measure of system usability in hundreds of academic studies (Hess et al., 2014). Survey measurement is routinely used and recommended in industry settings as well (Laubheimer, 2018). However, using survey measures to evaluate usability can present several challenges, particularly for live software. Response rates to usability surveys are typically low and are biased toward highly positive or negative experiences; thus, the typical real-world usability dataset excludes nearly all of the relevant population (Travis, 2017). Frequent requests for usability feedback can also irritate users (Pecoraro, 2012), so responses usually happen just once for each user and are directed at the system as a whole (Maia & Furtado, 2016). These shortcomings underscore the need for comprehensive measurement strategies that evaluate usability across broad user populations and for various system components.

We propose that mouse movement efficiency (MME)— performing an intended mousing action with minimal deviation—is a theoretically sound indicator of usability that can be used to assess usability across broad user populations and different system components. We use biased competition theory (BCT) (Desimone & Duncan, 1995) to explain how poor usability can cause resource competition—or attentional interference—impacting motor movements and degrading MME. We develop a theory to better understand the relationships among usability, attentional interference, and MME, guided by the following research question: Can mouse movement efficiency be used to measure system usability?

The research question and our guiding theory inform hypotheses predicting how attentional interference influences MME. We developed and refined a set of measures of MME that are independent of task length, which we used to test our research question over four studies. The results affirm the usefulness of BCT and attentional interference as a lens for understanding usability issues. Namely, attentional interference is correlated with the efficiency of users’ mouse movements, and MME is reliably correlated with various measures of usability. Moreover, MME can identify usability differences among different parts of systems with no effective loss in predictive validity compared to standard measurement approaches. Our theoretical and methodological contributions have important implications for future usability research and for facilitating more efficient usability measurement and mass-deployable usability testing.

## Literature Review

Usability has been studied since the earliest personal computers (e.g., Bennett, 1979). Although the definition and scope of usability have not been without debate (Lewis, 2014), we align our treatment of usability with the current consensus among industry stakeholders, namely that (1) usability is a critical subcomponent of the broader concept of user experience (Hassenzahl & Tractinsky, 2006), and (2) usability is defined as the extent to which a system can be operated to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction (ISO, 2019). Thus, we focus on the extent to which a system interface allows the user to be quick, efficient, accurate, and otherwise accomplish a desired task with minimal effort.

Within the information systems (IS) literature, ease of use (EOU) has persisted as the most common measure of effort when studying technology use (Davis, 1989) and has been shown to influence a wide variety of important user outcomes (King & He, 2006; Venkatesh, 2000). However, although EOU has received the most theoretical and empirical attention in the IS literature, it is just one of several related usability terms relating to perceptions or expectations about the degree of difficulty associated with using a system. Past literature has also referred to this concept as complexity (Thompson et al., 1991), effort expectancy (Venkatesh et al., 2003), or usability<sup>2</sup> (Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002; Benbunan-Fich, 2001; Delone & McLean, 2003; Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015; Lee & Kozar, 2012). Historically, usability has been the preferred term in the human-computer interaction literature (Hornbæk, 2006), has seen greater adoption among industry standards groups (ISO, 2019), and is the term adopted throughout this paper.

In some situations, using perceptual survey measures for EOU provides an adequate measure of usability. However, self-report measures also present some challenges, particularly when evaluating the usability of systems in real-world, noncontrolled settings. System feedback surveys suffer from low response rates (Travis, 2017), irritate users (Keusch, 2015), and can even be influenced by the mere framing of the questions (Hassenzahl & Ullrich, 2007). They also typically happen just once—at the end of a system-wide interaction (Lewis et al., 2015). As a result, system designers have difficulty identifying individual components of a system that need improvement once that system is in the hands of end users. That usability judgments are unstable over time only exacerbates the issue (Yuhui et al., 2019). These issues indicate the need for complementary usability evaluation techniques.

## Mouse Movements as Behavioral Data

Research has stressed the need to corroborate self-report measures with behavior-based measures (Lonati et al., 2018). We propose that users’ mouse movements can be used to behaviorally measure usability, both unobtrusively and in users’ natural settings. Prior research in neuroscience and psychology has unequivocally demonstrated that linkages exist between cognitive processing and hand movements, suggesting that “attention and action are intimately linked” (Welsh & Elliott, 2004, p. 1054). Hand movements attached to computer mice can be recorded with precision, leading researchers in other domains to employ mousing behavior as an innovative method for advancing theory (Freeman et al., 2008; Spivey et al., 2005).

Although the potential of mousing data for understanding usability was identified in the formative years of the internet (Chen et al., 2001), there has been a notable lack of theorybased research since. Much of the literature has focused on tools for collecting or using mouse movement data (e.g., Gardey et al., 2022; Theis & Darabi, 2019) or been exploratory or proofof-concept in nature without formal theory development (e.g., Hurtienne et al., 2014; Tzafilkou & Protogeros, 2017). Thus, we see a clear opportunity to fill this gap in prior mousing research by building strong theory to inform the study of mouse movements in the context of usability. Although theory building has been sparse in the study of mouse movements and usability, we find encouraging applications of theory in related IS contexts. Cognitive load theories have informed the study of mouse movements as a measure of cognitive load (Ericson et al., 2021; Grimes & Valacich, 2015; Thorpe et al., 2021). When users conceal information, cheat, or lie, cognitive demand has been used to explain why their mousing behavior can help identify the culprits (Jenkins et al., 2021; Jenkins et al., 2019; Weinmann et al., 2022).

Most relevant to the present research, Hibbeln et al. (2017) explain how negative emotions tax cognitive resources and slow mouse movements, including manipulations of frustration in an online shopping context. They employed a domain-specific theory—attentional control theory (ACT) (Eysenck et al., 2007)—to investigate the relationship between negative emotions and mouse movements, demonstrating that negative emotions affect mouse cursor distance and speed. Their findings demonstrate a link between visual attention processing and mouse movements; however, applying their theory to the usability context may be problematic for at least two reasons. First, ACT “is not a general theory of attentional control but rather is concerned with attentional control in the context of anxiety and cognitive performance” (Eysenck et al., 2007, p. 337). This limits the generalizability of their findings beyond emotion detection; indeed, both hypotheses advanced are explicitly scoped to the effect of negative emotion on (1) movement distance and (2) movement speed (Hibbeln et al., 2017, p. 6). Second, although Hibblen et al. (2017) made reference to “distracting stimuli” in relation to attentional control and subsequent effects on mouse movements, all such references are made in the pursuit of linking their primary phenomenon of interest—negative emotion—to mouse movements.

We build on Hibbeln et al. (2017) in three important ways. First, we incorporate BCT—a broad theory of attention processing that has been used in many different attention processing settings (Beck & Kastner, 2009)—to explain the mechanism (i.e., attentional interference) through which usability affects attention allocation. Using this theory to link usability with attention processing affords us stronger, more generalizable contributions that extend beyond negative emotions. In addition, we use eye tracking to verify that BCT and attentional interference provide an appropriate theoretical lens for understanding usability. Second, we explicitly integrate BCT with the response activation model (RAM) Welsh & Elliott, 2004) to derive mechanisms linking attention processes to mouse movements—explicitly regarding the efficiency of mouse movements as implied by these theories. Third, we contribute to research methodology by developing a set of normalized MME measures that support our goal of measuring attentional interference in a generalizable way regardless of task length, providing strong ecological validity and enabling usability evaluation across different parts of a system.

In summary, perceptual usability assessment is pervasive but not without drawbacks. Despite longstanding interest in the use of mouse data for usability research, a gap remains in the usability literature, as no theoretical mechanisms explain how usability problems affect mouse movements. Strong theory has been used to explain mousing behaviors in other contexts, with the central theme that taxing a user’s cognitive resources—whether by anxiety, emotions, lying, or explicit cognitive load manipulations—affects motor function, translating to observable changes in mouse movements. In the sections that follow, we apply this principle to the usability context, integrating BCT and RAM to predict how poor usability affects MME.

## Theory and Hypotheses

We draw on an established theory (BCT) to explain how poor usability causes competition among finite attention processing resources, consuming working memory, and generally taxing cognitive resources (Desimone & Duncan, 1995). We then incorporate RAM (Welsh & Elliott, 2004) to link this cognitive resource drain to MME.

## Biased Competition Theory

The human brain is perpetually processing the large volume of sensory input in the surrounding environment to identify stimuli that are relevant for accomplishing a given task (Norman & Shallice, 1986; Posner & DiGirolamo, 2000). The brain conserves limited resources by relegating most input to an efficient, subconscious processing system that heuristically evaluates and disregards irrelevant stimuli, while a second system selectively allocates attention to task-relevant stimuli (Corbetta & Shulman, 2002). BCT is a theory of attention processing that explains that “attention is an emergent property of many neural mechanisms working to resolve competition for visual processing and control of behavior” (Desimone & Duncan, 1995, p. 194). This competition for attention is biased (i.e., prioritized) in favor of certain stimuli, either involuntarily toward stimuli that capture attention due to salient characteristics (e.g., size, color, motion) (Wolfe & Horowitz, 2017) or voluntarily by directing attention toward stimuli with (perceived) relevance to the task at hand (Beck & Kastner, 2009; Duncan, 2006; Marchi, 2020).

Within this biased competition lens, goal-directed behavior results from selectively applying attention resources to goalrelevant stimuli through top-down control while actively ignoring irrelevant distractors that compete for attention (Luck et al., 2021; Pashler et al., 2001), an effortful process that requires working memory (Lorenc et al., 2021). Distracting or confusing stimuli compete for attention resources as they are evaluated for their relevance to a task, placing additional demands on working memory (Lavie et al., 2004). We adopt the term attentional interference<sup>3</sup> from the BCT literature (e.g., Cho & Cho, 2021) to describe this competition for attention resources. Attentional interference occurs when competing distractors are present and is more pronounced when the competing stimuli are visually jarring (Corbetta & Shulman, 2002), positioned near task-relevant objects (Moacdieh & Sarter, 2014), or incorrectly judged as task relevant (Moore et al., 2009). Attentional interference is characterized by slower reaction times, longer task completion times, and more mistakes (Fan et al., 2002; Lorenc et al., 2021). Notably, attentional interference is especially likely to occur in small, visually cluttered environments (Moacdieh & Sarter, 2014)—a description befitting a computer interface with poor usability.

In addition to extensive applications of BCT in vision and attention research (Carlisle, 2019), the theory has been used in a variety of other domains. For example, BCT has been used to inform the role of attention and top-down control in habit formation (Bickel, 2023) and to explain how elite athletes relegate complex physical movements to automatic processes to free up attention resources for more strategic processing that enhances their performance (Furley & Wood, 2016). BCT has also been used to understand the impact of visual complexity on the effectiveness of advertising images (Wu et al., 2016) or to study driver distractions while using semiautonomous vehicles (Naujoks et al., 2016). In general, BCT is a versatile theoretical lens for understanding the cognitive processes involved in regulating attention and maintaining focus during goal-directed behavior (Carlisle, 2019; Marchi, 2020).

## Biased Competition and Usability

Using BCT as our theoretical lens, we propose that a system exhibiting poor usability creates attentional interference. Past work has established that usability is best supported by interfaces that are easy to navigate (Cyr, 2008), provide interaction elements that are obvious and easy to learn (Tung et al., 2009), and generally do not direct the user’s attention away from the task at hand (Lin, 2013). The impact of poor interface design on users’ cognitive resources and working memory has been a hallmark of HCI research for decades (Hu et al., 2017; Rogers, 2004), and usability guides advocate reducing cognitive load (e.g., Shneiderman et al., 2016).

Framed within BCT, a usable system interface that provides efficient, logical navigation, clear interaction mechanisms, and few distractions will allow a user’s attention to remain focused on goal-relevant interface elements without allocating working memory to competition from irrelevant stimuli. An interface with explicit distractions—such as advertisements or an animated email subscription prompt—will cause interference as those stimuli compete for attention in the user’s visual field (Corbetta & Shulman, 2002). Moreover, we would expect attentional interference from a system interface in which task-relevant elements are hard to distinguish from other elements (Moore et al., 2009; Wolfe & Horowitz, 2017), such as being cluttered close together (Moacdieh & Sarter, 2014) or grouped in an illogical or unhelpful manner (Wolfe & Horowitz, 2017). Such interfaces will obscure the path to accomplishing the task at hand, causing the user to distribute attention resources more broadly, thereby consuming working memory (Lavie et al., 2004) and lowering task performance (Lorenc et al., 2021).

## Attentional Interference and Movement Efficiency

We incorporate RAM (Welsh & Elliott, 2004) to connect the attentional interference users experience when interacting with a system low in usability with a user’s motor function, resulting in decreased MME. Traditional BCT research examines attention using intentionally simplistic constraints to isolate the effects of certain phenomena (e.g., visual contrast between targets and distractors) (Burnham, 2007). As real-world visual environments are far more complex than the typical lab test interface, attention researchers tend to integrate general concepts from complementary theories to more accurately model human behavior (Schneider et al., 2013). One such theory that has been applied successfully to mousing studies (e.g., Jenkins et al., 2019) is RAM (Welsh & Elliott, 2004), which formalizes the relationships between attention and fine motor movements. According to RAM, all stimuli with actionable potential (e.g., links, images, ads) will capture a user’s attention. As these stimuli are evaluated for their relevance to a goal-oriented task, the process of evaluation will prime movement responses—or initiate movement through nerve impulses to the hand and arm muscles—toward or away from the stimulus (Welsh & Elliott, 2004). As top-down processing ultimately selects the target stimulus and overcomes the subconscious attraction toward nontarget stimuli—referred to as selective inhibition—the intervening evaluation process results in “longer reaction and movement times in situations when distractor stimuli are present” (Welsh & Elliott, 2004, p. 1032). In real-world mouse movements, this is observed as deviating (i.e., inefficient) mouse movements with longer movement distances and greater deviation from optimal paths (e.g., Freeman et al., 2010).

The tension between multiple actionable stimuli described in RAM (Welsh & Elliott, 2004) aligns with the attention resource competition found in BCT. From a BCT perspective, these impulsive motor movements toward actionable stimuli and the need to subsequently inhibit movement toward nontarget distractors correspond to the biased competition occurring as distractors are evaluated for their relevance to the task at hand. Prior research demonstrates that competing stimuli capture a user’s attention and prime movement responses (Jenkins et al., 2019). Empirical data show that attentional interference (in the form of various distractions) directly affects motor movement in various arm- and hand-based movements (e.g., Kumawat et al., 2022; Song & Nakayama, 2008; Welsh, 2011). Thus, whereas the BCT literature has seldom investigated how attentional interference impacts real-world motor movements, RAM provides sufficient supplementary support for our theory that usability issues and the attentional interference they cause should be observable in mouse movements.

Furthermore, mounting evidence suggests that mouse movements are ideally suited for studying cognitive processes, especially when contextual factors are expected to create additional cognitive load. Mouse tracking provides high-fidelity information about the processes of perception and cognition (Spivey et al., 2005), with the added benefit that the method of capture is both familiar and noninvasive. Researchers have used mouse movement data to examine cognitive processes in a wide variety of contexts in social and cognitive psychology (Freeman et al., 2010; Incera & McLennan, 2016; Thorpe et al., 2021), emotion (Hibbeln et al., 2017; Yamauchi & Xiao, 2018), and attentional interference (Linnman et al., 2006; Yamamoto et al., 2016). Given our goal of examining attentional interference as it relates to the usability of systems—for which a computer mouse is a common interaction method—mouse movement data offer an excellent tool for investigating our research question.

In summary, BCT suggests that goal-directed behavior entails dynamic competition for attention as visual stimuli are evaluated for their relevance to the task. Environments that are distracting or visually confusing place strain on limited cognitive resources, adding interference and degrading task performance. Applying this theory to the usability context, we expect confusing or distracting interfaces to create attentional interference as stimuli compete for a user’s cognitive resources. RAM suggests that as attentional interference taxes the user’s cognitive resources, the user’s motor movements will be influenced. Specifically, as a user moves the mouse to navigate an interface with poor usability, distracting or confusing interface stimuli draw the user’s attention and prime movement toward these other stimuli. This priming and subsequent inhibition will cause the trajectory of movement to deviate from the ideal trajectory to the intended destination, resulting in observable differences in MME. We thus propose MME as a viable measurement proxy for usability, suggesting the following hypothesis:

H1: Users of interfaces with lower usability will have a lower mouse movement efficiency than users of interfaces with higher usability.

Modern systems provide functionality for a variety of tasks, often with different interfaces depending on the task. Usability can vary for different components of the same system, a fact that may be lost when a single usability evaluation is administered at the end of an interaction (Brinkman et al., 2008). BCT suggests that the visual environment is constantly evaluated during goal-directed behavior (Desimone & Duncan, 1995), and studies have shown that attentional interference varies with corresponding changes in the number, size, or complexity of visual cues presented to the viewer (Wolfe & Horowitz, 2017). Within the usability context, this suggests that a system comprised of multiple components will create different levels of attentional interference, as determined by the relative distraction, confusion, or visual clarity encountered during each component interaction. Following the same logic presented in support of H1 above, we expect these usability differences to create corresponding differences in MME. Mouse movements are widely accepted as reliable measures of dynamic cognitive processes as they evolve over time (Freeman, 2018; Spivey et al., 2005), suggesting that measuring and comparing MME across multiple components during a system interaction will reveal relative differences in the usability of those components. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: Mouse movement efficiency will differentiate usability among different system components.

## Methodology

This section describes extensive work to (1) derive an initial set of MME measures to be used as a proxy for attentional interference and usability in a wide variety of systems, platforms, and tasks, and during free-browsing activities of different lengths typical of real-world system use, and (2) validate and expand this set of MME measures over the course of four studies.

## Measuring Mouse Movement Efficiency

Measuring MME during real-world use requires several adaptations to the movement statistics used in the past literature, which typically employs highly controlled experiments to examine psychological phenomena.

For example, Freeman and Ambady (2011) asked participants to classify faces as male or female by (1) clicking an anchoring button to display the face and (2) moving the cursor to the upper left or right corners of a screen. This design controls the anchor and target positions, allowing an efficiency measurement by drawing a straight line from the starting position to that of the correct answer—the idealized response trajectory (IRT), or the most efficient movement path—and comparing the IRT to the actual trajectory to measure deviation. Starting with three common measures of deviation from the mouse tracking literature—area under the curve (AUC), additional distance (AD), and maximum deviation (MD)—we derived normalization techniques to measure MME in both controlled (Studies 1-2) and free-browsing settings (Studies 3-4). Study 4 included additional measures and a combination strategy to further explore the use of MME for real-world usability evaluations. As shown in Figure 1, AUC is the geometric area between the actual mouse trajectory and the IRT; it is a measure of the total deviation from the IRT. AD is the distance that a user’s mouse cursor travels minus the distance along the IRT. MD is the largest perpendicular deviation between the actual trajectory and its IRT (Freeman & Ambady, 2010).

Measuring MME during a free-roaming interaction is considerably more complex than doing so in highly controlled experiments as users navigate through interfaces with varying elements and navigation paths to accomplish tasks of varying length and complexity. Importantly, traditional deviation measures are also biased by task length, such that longer tasks require more movement, in general producing higher AUC and AD values. To account for this complexity, we propose two modifications to the measures used in prior studies.

First, we devised an approach to generate a personalized IRT for each person, accounting for diverse movements characteristic of real-world mousing behavior. While browsing on a given interface, a person may move the mouse to enter information in an input box, click on a button, pause to consider content, or interact with a variety of other components. To measure the efficiency of one’s movements in this free-roaming scenario, the IRT should include (1) all endpoints a user intends to visit on a screen, and (2) all paths between those endpoints (see annotations in Figure 2); this IRT will be expected to vary by user and by interface screen.

We used two heuristics to determine the set of endpoints on a screen for each user. First, if a person clicks on a target, we assume that the location of the click is likely the location the user intended to reach and treat this as an endpoint. Second, if a person stops moving the mouse, we assume that this point likely denotes the end of a continuous movement.<sup>4</sup> The paths between each endpoint were then accumulated to create a set of segments constituting the user’s IRT for that screen. We note here that although computing this complex IRT with complete accuracy is likely impossible—a heuristic is, after all, an approximation—we contend that this approach will generate a more representative estimation of the IRT than simply using the starting and ending points on a given screen, as is common in more controlled settings.

Next, we normalized the AUC and AD by the distance of the IRT for each person. Even under optimal circumstances, hand movements will naturally deviate from a person’s IRT due to neuromotor noise—natural variability in the neuromotor channel that prevents perfectly precise movements (Van Gemmert & Van Galen, 1997). As AUC and AD are additive, they tend to overestimate deviations for movements that have longer IRTs. (By contrast, we assumed that MD will be robust to longer trajectories.) As an illustration, if the average user adds 10 pixels for every 100 pixels due to normal neuromotor noise, a 1000-pixel movement would include 100 pixels of noise, whereas a 500- pixel movement would include 50 pixels of noise; we should consider both to be similar in terms of precision. Thus, we divided a person’s AUC and AD by the linear distance of the segments on their personalized IRT. This normalization resulted in two ratios of AUC and AD over distance traveled,<sup>5</sup> distinguished using the terms normalized area under the curve (NAUC) and normalized additional distance (NAD), respectively. These two normalized measures of deviation, together with MD, can be calculated for each segment of a user’s IRT. They comprise an initial set of measures designed to quantify the extent to which a user efficiently moves the mouse while interacting with a system interface. Smaller values of NAUC, NAD, and MD indicate that the user’s actual mouse movements are close to efficient. Note that these three need not constitute the only possible measures of MME; indeed, we proposed and tested additional candidate measures as part of Study 4.

According to our hypotheses, we expect interfaces with poor usability to produce higher NAUC, NAD, and MD values indicative of inefficient mouse movements. To illustrate, consider the mousing paths of two hypothetical users who are searching for manuscript formatting requirements on the MIS Quarterly website (see Figure 2). In the left pane, User A would deploy attention resources to locate and then click on the desired link, aided by a logical visual hierarchy, and hampered by few irrelevant distractions. This goal-directed movement would be accomplished with little interference from irrelevant or confusing stimuli in the visual environment. According to our theory, mouse movements would be relatively efficient. By contrast, the right pane illustrates a page with the same core information but also includes distractions (e.g., a banner ad and an irrelevant sidebar) and text-only links that are ordered alphabetically. User B’s attention would be more likely drawn to the taskirrelevant distractors and the user would assume extraneous cognitive load while searching for the desired information among the poorly organized list of links. These factors would create attentional interference, which would be observed as relatively inefficient mouse movements.

## Study 1

Our first study was designed to accomplish three important goals, each related to H1: (1) use eye tracking to validate BCT and attentional interference as appropriate for the study of usability, (2) establish MME as a valid operationalization of attentional interference, and (3) demonstrate that our MME measures discriminate between usability and task difficulty (i.e., the difficulty of the task the system is used to accomplish). To achieve these objectives, we used a controlled experiment with a 2 × 2 (high/low attentional interference by high/low task difficulty) design while capturing MME and eye tracking data. This section will first explain the need to differentiate usability from task difficulty in our research and then proceed to describe the study design, data collection procedures, and subsequent analysis and results.

## Usability vs. Task Difficulty

In goal-oriented system use, past research has often recognized task difficulty (or complexity) as an important correlate with usability. In reality, however, task characteristics and performance are closely intertwined with system perceptions and usage (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). Traditionally, task characteristics have been studied as constructs separate from usability, although there has been variation in how these constructs influence—or are influenced by—system usability and use (Petter et al., 2013). Task complexity is often positioned as a predictor of system usability or satisfaction (e.g., Petter et al., 2013), but it can also moderate or interact with users’ perceptions of the system (e.g., Lauterbach et al., 2020). However, system design characteristics can also influence perceptions of task or task performance (e.g., Li et al., 2021).

We agree that task difficulty should be modeled and measured separately from usability; however, the considerable overlap between the cognitive processes that characterize both task difficulty and system usability can be problematic. Consider, for instance, Campbell’s (1988) seminal definition of task complexity as the extent to which a task places high cognitive demands on the task doer (p. 43). As we have noted, cognitive demands can also be placed on users because of poor interface design, with cognitive load and working memory forming a central theme of usability research for decades (Hu et al., 2017; Rogers, 2004). Indeed, we have incorporated attentional interference—a concept closely related to cognitive load— as the primary mechanism through which we expect to observe changes in MME due to usability issues. Working memory is a shared resource across many cognitive subsystems (Baddeley, 2001), indicating that the line of distinction between task difficulty and usability may not be especially sharp, particularly in terms of observable MME behaviors stemming from that cognitive load. In other words, using a system with poor usability to perform a task will deplete the user’s cognitive resources, but knowing whether the blame for that depletion rests on the system or the task may prove difficult (Ilany-Tzur & Fink, 2022).

Thus, Study 1 was designed to ensure that our proposed MME measures can measure attentional interference as hypothesized, with sufficient discrimination from the expected overlapping effects of task difficulty, and that our normalized measures (NAUC and NAD) are robust across tasks of varying difficulty and duration. Accordingly, we manipulated both constructs in a 2 × 2, within-subjects design consisting of four trials per participant.

## Experimental Task

The task was adapted from a common experimental task used in the BCT literature in which participants perform a visual search to identify a single target among several distractors that vary on some theoretically relevant trait (e.g., spatial orientation or color similarity) (Nothdurft, 2006). We adapted this approach to our usability context as follows. During each trial, the participant clicked on a series of numbered targets in sequential order—twice with four targets (low task difficulty) and twice with eight targets (high task difficulty). Within each task difficulty condition, the interface either displayed the numbered targets alone (low attentional interference) or also displayed four (in the low task difficulty treatment) or eight (in the high task difficulty treatment) distractor targets (high attentional interference) that were visually identical to the numbered targets, except that they contained letters. All targets were relatively small, such that it was hard to differentiate between numbers and letters, ensuring sufficient resource competition (Desimone & Duncan, 1995). After providing consent and reading the instructions, the participants practiced the sequential clicking task on a few numbered targets. We further avoided learning or ordering effects by presenting the four trials in random order. The experiment concluded with a survey containing demographic and manipulation check questions.

Although our experimental task largely replicated an established approach from the BCT literature, our sequential clicking procedure—designed to approximate real-world mousing behavior—was a significant adaptation. Thus, to maintain confidence in our attentional interference manipulation, we also captured eye gaze data for all participants. Eye tracking is common in visual processing studies (e.g., Kim & Anderson, 2020) and is used in IS research as a proxy for attention (Bera et al., 2019). Thus, we collected this data to provide evidence for the validity of our attentional interference manipulations and to provide additional empirical support for our theoretical framing. All data collection took place in one-on-one sessions using a laptop computer with a mouse and an eye tracker attached. Collecting data using the same equipment for all trials also eliminated variation due to screen size, browser version, operating system, etc.

## Calculating Mouse Movements Statistics

The experiment was implemented on a simple website created by the researchers. A JavaScript library captured mouse movements and sent them to a web service that calculated the MME measures. Consistent with past research (e.g., Hehman et al., 2015), mouse movement data (i.e., x- and y-coordinates) were normalized to an 8 × 6 grid to account for different screen sizes and ensure that our results can be easily compared to those of past research (Freeman et al., 2010).

The IRT was calculated for each participant using the method described above. As no prior literature<sup>6</sup> has established a method for breaking up a free-browsing pattern into the set of paths required for our deviation calculations, we empirically derived a 200-ms threshold to denote an intentional stop. This cutoff was derived using data from over 6,800 users<sup>7</sup> performing directed tasks between two points while mouse movements and pauses were recorded. Our goal was to select a cutoff that reliably differentiated unintentional pauses from intentional pauses. Unintentional pauses may occur for a variety of legitimate reasons in real-world movements, such as people repositioning their hands to continue moving the mouse or differences in sampling rates on computers, among others. On average, movements between the two destinations in our data had unintentional pauses of 19.8 ms (SD 61.8 ms). Thus, a pause of 200 ms or more suggests (with a probability of $p < 0 . 0 0 2 )$ that the break in movement is not a part of a normal movement between endpoints. Although no heuristic is perfect, this approach was deemed reasonable for measuring MME in free-browsing contexts.

NAUC, NAD, and MD were calculated based on the actual mouse trajectories’ deviation from the IRT. NAUC is calculated in several steps. First, the area is calculated between the actual trajectory and the IRT (see Figure 3). The movement trajectory and IRT are rotated and transformed so that the IRT is along the x-axis. A Riemann sum method is then used to calculate the area (Mathur & Reichling, 2019). Namely, for each point on the actual movement trajectory, the area of a rectangle is calculated (approximating the area between the current point and the IRT) by multiplying the difference between the current and previous y-values (i.e., the rectangle height) by the difference between the current and previous xvalues (i.e., the rectangle width). We took the absolute value of this product (i.e., the rectangle area), repeated this calculation for each point on the actual trajectory, and then added the areas together. Lastly, we divided this total area by the distance of the complete IRT to normalize the distance.

NAD was calculated by subtracting the total distance required to travel each segment of the IRT from the total distance traveled on the actual trajectory. The Euclidean distance formula was used to calculate the distance between the start and endpoints for each segment of the idealized trajectory and between each point in the actual trajectory. The total additional distance was then divided by the total distance of the IRT to normalize the distance.

For MD, the system first computed the line equation between each pair of associated endpoints on the IRT. Then, for each point (x-, y-coordinate) on the actual trajectory, the system calculated a perpendicular line from the x-, y-coordinate that intercepted the line equation of its associated IRT segment. Through substitution in the two line equations, the point of interception was derived, and the distance was calculated between the x- and y-coordinate positions on the actual trajectory and the calculated intercept point on the IRT using the Euclidean distance formula. MD is the maximum value from the set of these distances.

## Eye Tracking Analysis

Next, we analyzed eye tracking data to validate BCT as an appropriate theoretical lens for our research and, specifically, to verify that attentional interference was indeed occurring as predicted by BCT and as manipulated. Eye gaze data were captured at 60 Hz using a Tobii Pro X2-60 sensor. We followed established eye tracking data extraction, cleaning, and interpolation techniques (Bera et al., 2019; Jacob & Karn, 2003) to extract x-y gaze point coordinates, with missing coordinates interpolated where possible. We then used a standard fixation identification algorithm (I-VT) to derive fixations and saccades.

One measure that has been used as a relevant indication of visual search behavior in prior eye tracking studies (Orquin & Loose, 2013) is an overall count of fixations as the person scans the interface for the targets that will allow them to accomplish the desired task. The logic underlying this measure is simple: more searching (driven in our case by experimentally manipulated distractors) will create more fixations. However, fixation count will also be confounded with task duration when completion time is inconsistent. Recall that our task difficulty manipulation varied the count of numbered targets to be clicked. In this situation, as well as in free-form system interactions, prior literature (Jacob & Karn, 2003) recommends standardizing the count of fixations as a rate of fixations per second. We thus calculated the fixation rate for each individual trial by dividing the overall fixation count by the task duration.

## MME Analysis

We performed our main analysis of MME using mixed-effects linear regression, which uses random effects to account for individual differences in repeated observations (Pinheiro & Bates, 2000)—for example, differences in natural computing ability or demographics—and fixed effects to model the treatment effects—in our case, task difficulty, attentional interference (distractions), and the interaction between the two. (See Table 3 for descriptive statistics.) We specified separate models for each of the MME measures, summarized in Table 4, 5, and 6. In each case, attentional interference increased NAUC, NAD, and MD whereas task difficulty did not. The interaction term—indicating a longer task and a greater number of distraction stimuli (eight distractors instead of four)—was significant in each model, indicating that attentional interference is particularly evident when there are more potential relevant and distraction targets.

## Study 2

To further investigate H1, Study 2 employed a betweensubjects, controlled experiment using an online shopping task with two versions of a website (designed to be relatively high or low in attentional interference and usability) while capturing their mouse movements. To aid in generalizability, the results were cross-validated with two different populations.

## Task and Manipulations

Participants were given an online shopping scenario in which their task was to locate and purchase a replacement laptop part on a website equipped with the mouse tracker from Study 1. The website was built by the research team (to ensure that no one had previous experience with the site) and contained a four-page flow that supported four sequential activities: home page, category page, search/filter page, and product page. The task ended when the user clicked a purchase button on the product page, after which they were redirected to a postsurvey. Participants were randomly assigned to complete the task using a high- or low-usability version of the site (with correspondingly low vs. high attentional interference, respectively). The versions were designed to hold the layout and structure of each page constant—minimizing differences in mousing distances and overall browsing experience—while also providing sufficient differences in manipulation effects between the two experiences. Although usability can be characterized by several different concepts, one aspect of poor usability is interface clutter, which aligns particularly well with our theory on attentional interference. Cluttered interfaces “impose costs by increasing retrieval demands on memory” (Altmann, 2001, p. 189), require users to search more carefully for desired information (Rosenholtz et al., 2007), and are widely recognized as deterrents to usability (e.g., MacDonald & Atwood, 2014; Rogers et al., 2013). Attention research also demonstrates that clutter increases attentional interference (Moacdieh & Sarter, 2014; Mounts & Gavett, 2004). Examples of each condition are shown in Figure 4. Crucially, this study design was an intentional balance between experimental control and ecological validity. The post-survey captured measures of a PEOU for the websites as a manipulation check. (See Table A2 in the Appendix.)

## Participants

The experiment was conducted with two samples to crossvalidate and extend the generalizability of the results. Population 1 included 43 students from a large private university in the U.S. who were recruited from management classes in exchange for 0.25% extra credit in a course of their choice. The students were 69% male, 83% from the United States, and age 21 on average. Population 2 included 126 participants recruited from Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk). Demographically, 48% were from the United States, 37% were from India, and 15% were from other nations, while 53% were female; the average age was approximately 35. All MTurk participants were paid U.S. \$0.35 for a 2- to 3-minute task—equaling a U.S. \$7-\$10.50 hourly wage.

## Study 3

Study 3 compared our MME measures to users’ PEOU ratings in a field test of a commercial internet application and compared rankings of different system components using MME measures vs. PEOU (H2). We engaged with a small, privately held corporation (hereafter, “X Inc.”) that had released a new prototype of an online administrative portal for a suite of analytics tools. Since it lacked extensive usability testing resources to thoroughly assess the portal’s usability, X Inc. was an ideal candidate for utilizing mouse cursor movements to assess the portal remotely, unobtrusively, and without cumbersome procedures or costly equipment. The mouse tracking script from Studies 1 and 2 was installed on X Inc.’s web application for this study.

## Field Test Procedure

Participants were sent a link via email to a questionnaire that guided them through the usability test. The questionnaire began with a background video describing the company and its software, after which participants were provided with credentials for the portal. Users were given six system use tasks to accomplish in sequential, random order (see Table 8); each task required multipage interactions and was immediately followed by the PEOU scale from Study 2 and a free-response prompt for the user to evaluate the system’s usability during the task. After completing all six tasks, a postsurvey asked participants to rank the six system use tasks from 1 (lowest usability) to 6 (highest usability) and gathered demographic information.

A total of 40 students were recruited from a private university in the U.S.; each completed six system use tasks for a total of 240 observations. We first calculated Pearson correlations between PEOU and the participants’ MME measures. Next, using the baseline rank for comparison, we compared the usability rankings provided by PEOU with those provided by the MME measures. We averaged PEOU, NAUC, NAD, and MD for the population to rank each system use task from lowest to highest usability. ## Study 4

Study 4 had two additional objectives beyond those achieved in Studies 1-3: (1) address a real-world need for a company to create a singular measure of usability based on MME that adequately differentiates usability among system components (H2), and (2) explore a broader array of MME measures as inputs to this singular measure. This study was conducted in collaboration with a Fortune 100 software company (> 150K employees) to evaluate the usability of new features developed for three desktop software products (Products A, B, and C). All three products are used ubiquitously across industries and throughout the world.

## Participants and Testing Procedures

The company’s user research division oversaw the usability tests for each product. As required by the company’s Institutional Review Board, some details about the participants in each sample—such as demographic information—were withheld. Instead, our team was given a general description of the recruitment procedures and the tasks completed for each test, as well as the data described below, all of which were carefully anonymized to protect the participants’ privacy and to ensure that our analyses were unbiased. The “blind” nature of the study thus constitutes a robust test of the methodology that mitigates researcher biases.

Recruiting from a pool of users who had voluntarily enrolled in the company’s usability testing program, the company selected a sample of users for each product test, designing each sample to represent the population of end users for whom the product was intended. Participants completed their testing sessions on desktop computers in a dedicated lab facility at the company’s headquarters. Sessions averaged 1-2 hours in duration and participants were compensated by the company for their time. For each test, the participants’ mouse movements were captured during a set of system use scenarios. The company also collected a standard industry perceptual usability measure (hereafter, perceptual usability), asked after each of the scenarios was presented.<sup>8</sup> The industry research team routinely used perceptual usability with repeated administration during testing; however, they acknowledged this as a pain point for the participants in their studies. Thus, comparing perceptual usability to a combined, unobtrusive MME measure of usability for many system use scenarios was of interest to the corporation. The data were collected from 23 system use tasks completed by 28 participants (644 observations) for Product A, eight tasks completed by 25 participants (200 observations) for Product B, and 11 tasks completed by 31 participants (341 observations) for Product C.

In calculating MME for Study 4, we made two changes to our approach. First, the corporation requested that we combine individual measures into a single measure of usability (hereafter, behavioral usability) and compare it with perceptual usability. Second, we added three measures of MME to determine whether they contributed to the predictive utility of behavioral usability (see Table 13). We added these to illustrate that other statistics of MME could be added in the future. After creating our combined measure of behavioral usability, we then compared it to perceptual usability in ranking the usability of different system use scenarios.

## Creating a Single Behavioral Measure of Usability

At the request of our partner company, we combined the various MME measures into a single measure for easier comparison with perceptual usability. The combination strategy was a linear regression model used to predict the perceptual usability measure. Using standard tests for dimensionality, we verified that the four items for perceptual usability could be averaged into a single construct.<sup>9</sup> We used this construct as the dependent variable in the linear regression model, allowing the model to optimize the relative weights of the MME measures for predicting perceptual usability. Starting with both raw MME values and their centered-andscaled counterparts, we used backward selection with the Akaike information criterion to select the most useful independent variables for the model and to remove variables that explain the same variance. We then used the resulting model to generate a behavioral usability score for each system use task that each person completed; higher behavioral usability scores corresponded to higher perceptual usability. The behavioral usability scores explained 32%, 62%, and 36% of the variance in the perceptual usability scores for Products A, B, and C, respectively, with correlations of 0.57, 0.79, and 0.60, respectively.

## Usability Ranking Results

We then compared how well behavioral versus perceptual usability differentiated usability for the different system use scenarios. We averaged the behavioral and perceptual scores for each scenario and then computed two ranks using these averages: (1) an absolute ranking from (highest to lowest usability), based separately on the behavioral and perceptual usability scores, and (2) a quartile rank ranging from 1 to 4 (highest quartile of usability to lowest quartile of usability). The quartile ranking helps mitigate differences in rankings that may not be material. The results (see Tables 14, 15, and

16) show relatively similar rankings for most system use scenarios between the behavioral and perceptual usability scores for all three products—particularly in the quartile rank. In terms of reliability, the two usability scores show moderate to high interrater reliability $( \kappa _ { \mathrm { ( A ) } } = 0 . 6 4 7 , \kappa _ { \mathrm { ( B ) } } =$ $0 . 9 0 4 , ~ \kappa _ { \mathrm { ( C ) } } = 0 . 8 0 0 )$ , while the reliability of the quartile rankings reveal substantial to perfect interrater reliability $( \kappa _ { \mathrm { ( A q ) } } = 0 . 8 0 5 , \kappa _ { \mathrm { ( B q ) } } = 1 . 0 0 0 , \kappa _ { \mathrm { ( C q ) } } = 1 . 0 0 0 )$ (McHugh, 2012). Based on these results, we suggest that the behavioral usability score is a strong surrogate for the perceptual usability score, even when administered in a “blind” data collection handled outside of our research team.

## Discussion

Our research developed a behavioral measure of usability that could be used when soliciting perceived usability measures is not optimal or when finer-grained precision is needed to determine which components of a system have low usability. This study explored the following research question: Can mouse movement efficiency be used to measure system usability? We used BCT to explain how stimuli are processed during goal-directed behavior and how cluttered, confusing, or otherwise distracting interfaces create attentional interference as limited cognitive resources are consumed by the need to evaluate competing stimuli. We then integrated RAM to link attentional interference from poor usability to decreases in users’ MME. We tested measures of MME in four studies, validating their use and comparing their utility to that of other usability measures commonly used in both academic and industry settings.

The first two studies used controlled experiments to establish the relationship between attentional interference and MME (Study 1) and between both usability and attentional interference and MME (Study 2). Study 1 adapted a common attentional interference study design to establish MME as a valid proxy measure of attentional interference. The results demonstrate significant differences across the three MME measures for low versus high attentional interference. The eye tracker data confirmed that participants experienced attentional interference, as demonstrated in prior attention research, indicating strong support for BCT as a theoretical lens for studying usability. Study 2 manipulated usability and attentional interference in an online shopping scenario, capturing the same three MME measures in the student and MTurk samples. The manipulation created significant increases for NAUC and NAD in both populations, whereas the effect on MD was observed for the student sample but not the MTurk sample. Studies 1 and 2 provide strong evidence that MME measures are effective markers of attentional interference and that usability issues create observable differences in MME.

Studies 3 and 4 applied MME measurement techniques to systems in the field, increasing the generalizability of our theory and demonstrating the utility of using MME to rank components of the same system according to their relative usability. Study 3 used MME measures to evaluate the usability of different components of a newly developed system for a small corporation compared to the most common perceptual scale used in academic IS research—the PEOU scale (Davis, 1989). We found that PEOU was significantly and negatively correlated with NAUC, NAD, and MD. We also compared the relative usability rankings of the PEOU and the MME measures to the participants’ subjective rankings of each system use task. The results suggest that NAUC and NAD can rank different components of a system in terms of usability, with as much reliability as the PEOU scale method (while MD was less reliable).

Study 4 entailed a collaboration with a Fortune 100 corporation to explore the efficacy of combining various measures of MME into a single metric to evaluate and rank the usability of system components for three software products. The tests were administered by an independent industry usability testing group, adding to the strength of the results as a blind replication. MME measures were combined into a single behavioral usability measure and compared to an industry measure of perceptual usability. The corporation collected mouse movement data as users interacted with three software products and measured perceptual usability after each component interaction scenario. The results indicate that behavioral usability had higher interrater reliability than perceptual usability in ranking the system use scenarios in terms of usability. These findings demonstrate ecological validity and suggest that behavioral usability can be used in industry settings to reliably measure usability when collecting survey data is not optimal.

The cumulative empirical evidence across four studies demonstrates that (1) attentional interference can be effectively measured using MME measures, (2) similar differences in MME measures can reliably indicate differences in system usability, and (3) MME measures can be used individually or in combination to evaluate and compare the relative usability of different system components with reliability that meets or exceeds that of perceptual measurement techniques commonly used in both academic and industry settings. These findings have important theoretical and practical implications, as detailed in the sections that follow.

## Implications for Research

This paper has important implications for theory and research methods. We are the first to apply BCT (Desimone & Duncan, 1995) to explain how usability issues can create attentional interference. Namely, when usability decreases, people’s attention becomes more stimulus-driven, drawing on attention resources as additional or confusing stimuli are evaluated for their relevance to the task at hand. This theory builds on recent IS research exploring the link between attentional control and negative emotions that can occur during online shopping experiences, as determined by mouse movements (Hibbeln et al., 2017). The theory advanced in this paper incorporates BCT as a generalizable theory of attention that could be valuable in the study of usability. User experience researchers have lamented the lack of theoretical explanations for the mental processes that shape users’ interactions with system interfaces (Jokinen et al., 2018; Saariluoma & Oulasvirta, 2010). Prior IS research investigating system use has been dominated by theories that incorporate EOU as the primary representation of usability (Venkatesh et al., 2003; Venkatesh et al., 2016). This has led to a heavy emphasis on understanding usability through the cognitive behavior lens, especially the theory of reasoned action (Ajzen, 1980) from which EOU was originally derived (Venkatesh, 2000). Without discounting the importance and utility of prior research informed by EOU, we argue that alternative theoretical perspectives will inform advancements in this domain. For example, Hu et al. (2017) used cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988) to identify cognitive mechanisms that more accurately explain the (mediated) influence of website navigability on user satisfaction. Likewise, BCT provides a unique lens for understanding usability, suggesting that an effective interface design is one that preserves users’ attention resources through logical visual structures and minimal distractions.

Thus, the theory advanced in this paper provides a fresh, complementary perspective that future researchers can use to study various usability- or attention-related issues. For example, BCT research has accumulated a diverse set of empirical evidence about relevant attributes that capture attention; examples include visual contrast (Nothdurft, 2006), movement (Bayerl & Neumann, 2007), perceived threat (Bishop, 2008), spatial proximity, and visual grouping (Wolfe & Horowitz, 2017). Many of these attributes have surfaced in some form in user experience research and practice (e.g., Djamasbi, 2014; Hong et al., 2004; Jung et al., 2021), and BCT can provide relevant theoretical framing for understanding and improving the effective guidance of attention through a system interaction experience. Moreover, the relationships between usability, interference, and motor movements can inform the study of usability on mobile or touch-based devices, with data from motion sensors providing innovative proxies for attentional interference. Alternatively, BCT could be used to apply MME measures to other contexts where measuring attention in web-based environments could prove valuable. Recent IS research on online learning, for example, has demonstrated improvements in learning outcomes when learners can appropriately manage attention resources (Sullivan et al., 2022). Online learning can be further improved when technology provides feedback mechanisms that promote learners’ self-awareness (Janson et al., 2020) or encourage them to focus their attention (Santhanam et al., 2008). Accordingly, monitoring MME as suggested by our theory in online learning environments could be a fruitful way to identify content or activities during which learners lose focus or become distracted and potentially provide a mechanism to trigger feedback to encourage attentive focus.

We also contribute methodologically to research on mouse cursor movement by defining and empirically evaluating normalization techniques used to measure free-form system navigation across a variety of screen sizes and system interfaces. More primitive versions of mouse movement measures have been used in past studies in which researchers enjoyed tighter experimental control (e.g., Freeman et al., 2011). We adapted these measures to capture MME during the free navigation of a system with different and dynamic ending and starting positions, demonstrating improved empirical performance compared to their unstandardized counterparts (Study 1). These adjustments extend the validity of MME to less controlled, free-navigation environments, where pages and system use tasks may inherently require different amounts of movement. These techniques can be adapted by researchers to study phenomena across diverse, distributed populations and to evaluate phenomena in real-world settings.

We found that usability generally influenced MME, although some measures were more robust than others. MD was significantly different in only one of the two populations (Study 2), was weakly correlated with PEOU, and did not differentiate well between system use tasks with different levels of usability (Study 3). One plausible explanation for this is that MD was not normalized for distance as were NAUC and NAD. MD represents a discrete event (the maximum deviation from the IRT at any given time) and may be biased by movement length and more susceptible to random noise (e.g., a large deviation from bumping the mouse).

Finally, we reveal the utility of combining various measures of MME into a single behavioral measure of usability that is robust in real-world applications. We demonstrate a methodology using linear regression to combine MME measures into a single measure of behavioral usability. In Study 4, we compared the reliabilities of behavioral usability and perceptual usability across three different products, showing that behavioral usability is a robust measure with high interrater reliability with perceptual usability. With the added MME measures, we show that MME can be measured in a variety of ways, suggesting that researchers can add additional measures as needed to fit a targeted research context.

## Implications for Practice

Usability is a hallmark of successful systems, and billions of dollars are spent annually to understand and improve the usability of systems (Rodriguez 2019). In academic or industry usability research settings, researchers often measure usability through perceptual survey instruments (Bargas-Avila & Hornbæk, 2011; Laubheimer, 2018). However, measuring usability in live settings is much more difficult because response rates to surveys are typically very low. Further, organizations frequently need precision to understand which specific system components have low usability, which is not always assessable by a single instrument at the end of an extended interaction. This paper addresses these needs with an approach to behavioral measurement of usability that (1) can be collected unobtrusively from most users of a system and (2) can robustly differentiate relative usability among different system components. We demonstrate the validity of these techniques in both controlled and real-world settings.

Our MME measures of usability can be collected noninvasively from every user of a system. Capturing data from all system users decreases the likelihood that usability assessments will be unduly biased by extreme responses. Further, data can be captured without requiring the attention or effort of the user, which decreases the likelihood of the measurement influencing the user experience. Finally, as data can potentially be collected continuously from all users, it can be used for continuous usability testing. This can be useful for detecting unexpected changes to the website or changes in the user base that may have different expectations of usability.

We demonstrate across four different real-world systems that MME measures can be used to rank different system use tasks effectively and reliably in terms of their relative usability, which is generally not feasible with survey measures. MME measures can be captured throughout the interaction and therefore allow system designers to differentiate more granularly which components have usability problems and which do not, rather than relying singularly on a post-interaction evaluation. This approach can enable organizations to apply scarce design and engineering resources to the components of their systems most in need of improvement.

Continuous measurement and broad user coverage are at the heart of an emerging trend among modern digital service providers, known as product-led growth (PLG) (OpenView Partners, 2022). PLG is a user-focused growth model that relies on positive user experiences to drive customer acquisition in an economy in which users expect to try software in an easy, frictionless way (Bonfiglio et al., 2017). Beyond the assumption that a more usable product will be easier to learn and more widely adopted by end users, the usability measurement approach presented in this paper could make this attractive trend more accessible to resourceconstrained software providers. Specifically, PLG advocates driving product improvements with rich user data (OpenView Partners, 2022). Whereas leading SaaS providers have the resources to build and exploit such user-tracking capabilities, smaller companies may struggle to balance the need for user insights with the other core functionality that demands engineering resources (Poyar, 2021). Although the scope of this paper is narrower than that implied by the rich PLG datasets we have referenced here, our approach provides a practical example of using rich interaction data to understand the behavior of an entire user base for product and user experience improvement.

## Limitations and Future Research

The results of our research must be interpreted within the limitations of our theoretical development and methodology. First, the dual-process theory developed and validated through a series of studies is based on BCT and is thus most applicable to usability in goal-directed tasks in which a distracting or confusing interface will compete for the user’s attention resources, as we have prescribed. Although it is possible that usability issues have a similar effect during experiential tasks, the studies reported here did not verify this. Future research should examine mousing features, such as those studied here, in experiential usage contexts.

Second, we chose to treat usability as a broad, high-level construct. While prior research has treated usability similarly (e.g., Wagner et al., 2014)—especially when ease of use is used to represent the concept within a theoretical framework (for reviews, see Hess et al., 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2016)— other research treats usability as a complex construct with subdimensions such as simplicity, logical structure, and navigation efficiency, among others (e.g., Lee & Kozar, 2012). Furthermore, the manipulations used in Studies 1 and 2 were primarily visual in nature, potentially reducing the generalizability of our approach for multifaceted usability assessment. As there may be subdimensions of usability that are not well suited for measurement using mouse movements, future research could examine how mousing behavior relates to specific subdimensions of usability and their relative importance.

Finally, the studies reported here are limited to mouse-based systems, with web applications as the primary use case. Although there has been a trend in recent years toward mobile devices, desktop devices account for nearly half of internet traffic and remain the primary device used for B2B transactions (ContentSquare, 2022). Mobile devices have higher search costs (Ghose et al., 2013), and desktop experiences are preferred for evaluating and purchasing more complex products (Fink & Papismedov, 2023). Furthermore, desktop devices constitute an important and lucrative platform for many online businesses: on average, desktop browsing sessions last over twice as long, convert to purchases at twice the rate, and have a 91% higher average order value than mobile sessions (ContentSquare, 2022). Still, we acknowledge the increasing importance of mobile experiences, particularly for low-involvement products (Fink & Papismedov, 2022) and for increasing customer engagement and loyalty (Khan et al., 2023). That usability is typically lower for mobile and touch devices (Ghose et al., 2013) only underscores the need for better assessment tools, but that endeavor falls outside the scope of this paper. As we have noted, our theory—with underlying mechanisms tying attentional interference and usability issues to fine motor movements—could be applied to the mobile context. Data from sensors commonly found in modern mobile devices, especially in terms of touchscreen usage patterns, could be studied for their relevance to usability; these data present a promising opportunity for future research.

## Conclusion

This paper examines how usability influences users’ attention processing and how interference from poor usability can be measured by indicators of MME as an alternative to traditional approaches. We show how MME can be used to evaluate usability and to rank different components of a system in order of relative usability. Two controlled experiments established the relationships among attentional interference, usability, and MME; two subsequent field studies applied the measurement techniques to four real systems, demonstrating that MME measures can be used individually or in combination to evaluate and compare the usability of many system components. The results reported have important theoretical and practical implications for facilitating more efficient and temporally precise usability research.

## Author Biographies

David (Dave) W. Wilson is an assistant professor of information systems at Brigham Young University (BYU). Prior to joining the faculty at BYU, he was the director of data science at Neuro-ID, a behavioral analytics company providing fraud and customer experience solutions for the fintech industry. His research focuses on digital behavior and its application to cybersecurity and humancomputer interaction, among other domains. His research has been published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, the European Journal of Information Systems, the Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and other outlets.

Joseph (Joe) S. Valacich is the Muzzy Endowed Chair in the MIS Department at the University of Arizona. His primary research focus is on human-computer interaction (HCI), cybersecurity, and e-business. He co-founded Neuro-ID—a university tech startup based on his research. His work has been published in numerous prestigious journals, including Academy of Management Journal, Accounting Review, Information Systems Research, Journal of Applied Psychology, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of Management Information Systems, Management Science, MIS Quarterly, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, and many others.

Jeffrey L. Jenkins is an associate professor of information systems at Brigham Young University. He has developed, validated, and patented algorithms to detect cognitive and emotional reactions using human-computer interaction devices. He co-founded Neuro-ID—a university tech startup based on his research. His work has been published in the top information systems journals, including MIS Quarterly, the Journal of Management Information Systems, Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association of Information Systems, and others.
