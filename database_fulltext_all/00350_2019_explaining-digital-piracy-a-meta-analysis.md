# Explaining Digital Piracy: A Meta-Analysis

Martin Eisend<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> European University Viadrina, 15230 Frankfurt (Oder), Germany

Contact: eisend@europa-uni.de, http://orcid.org/0000-0003-2564-6417 (ME)

Received: August 25, 2017<sub>Revised:</sub> Accepted: Published Online in Articles in Advance: June 5, 2019

https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2018.082

Copyright:

Abstract. Many studies have investigated why users engage in digital piracy. The theoretical explanations are particularistic, and the empirical findings are fragmented, sometimes divergent, and reveal unexplained variations of effects. Managers and academics have thus had little guidance on how to explain and combat digital piracy. To help fill this gap, the present meta-analysis synthesizes past research on digital piracy and accumulates 1,373 effect sizes from 174 studies collected in 36 countries. The meta-analysis identifies the key drivers of users’ engagement in digital piracy and tests a new, comprehensive model that integrates all prior theoretical perspectives—social influence and control, outcome evaluation, dilemma solving, and reinforcement. The model explains 42%–53% of the variance in digital piracy attitudes, intentions, and behaviors and identifies the reinforcement perspective as the strongest theory. A moderator analysis shows that the influence of key drivers varies with cultural dimensions linked to the theoretical perspectives—individualism moderates social influence and control variables, masculinity moderates dilemma-solving variables, and uncertainty avoidance moderates reinforcement variables. These findings provide guidance for future digital piracy research and also have managerial implications in terms of possible revisions to antipiracy measures.

## Introduction

Digital piracy refers to copying, downloading, or purchasing copyrighted files without explicit permission from the copyright holder (Gopal and Sanders 1998, Tunca and Wu 2013). Digital piracy has become a global economic issue. According to the most recent study by the Business Software Alliance (2018), globally, PC software was installed without proper licensing at a rate of 37% in 2017. The commercial value of unlicensed PC software installations totaled US\$46.3 billion worldwide in 2017. Although the presence of free products does not always harm paid sales (Smith and Telang 2009), and piracy can users serve for sampling purposes and even increase the diffusion of subscription software and thus benefit a company (Chellappa and Shivendu 2005, Givon et al. 1995), the adverse effects of digital piracy on businesses and economies have a far greater impact. For instance, music piracy in the United States results in \$12.5 billion in annual economic losses, costs 71,000 jobs in the United States each year, and annually reduces workers’ earnings by \$2.7 billion (Recording Industry Association of America 2015). Even with legislation in place, companies and governments have continued to struggle to tackle this widespread problem effectively; however, despite their efforts, piracy rates remain high. This calls for an exploration of alternative ways to prevent the theft of digital goods and to curb the seemingly insatiable global appetite for pirated digital items.

Many studies have investigated the demand side of digital piracy and analyzed the variables that influence users’ attitudes, intentions, and behaviors regarding digital piracy, to more fully understand why users engage in digital piracy. Despite the vast amount of research, several gaps remain. First, the findings on the explanatory variables of digital piracy are plenty but are fragmented and sometimes divergent, as several qualitative reviews have noted (Liang and Yan 2005, Peitz and Waelbroeck 2006, Lee and Yoo 2009). While some explanatory variables, such as the price of digital goods, provide rather consistent effects on user responses toward digital piracy, several other variables have led to ambiguous findings such as the users’ expertise, gender, or materialism. Moreover, the relative extent of the effect of the numerous explanatory variables is still unknown—an insight that would help practitioners and academics focusing on the major influencing factors of digital piracy.

Second, four main theoretical perspectives have been presented to explain the findings that cover several theories such as the theory of planned behavior, ethical decision-making models, or the expected utility theory. While several studies have analyzed modified and extended versions of single theories, in which they add, omit, or substitute specific elements (e.g., Cronan and Al-Rafee 2008), the theories remain particularistic, and no attempt has been made to integrate all major theoretical approaches, although they partly suggest alternative and competing explanations for key drivers of digital piracy. As a result, this stream of research is still lacking a comprehensive theoretical framework that connects the most relevant predictors and identifies the major theoretical mechanisms underlying users’ engagement in digital piracy to guide future research.

Third, the effects of key predictors vary considerably. While prior research has suggested that digital piracy behavior is embedded in different value contexts and that culture is a crucial moderator (Shore et al. 2001, Chiou et al. 2011), no comprehensive theoretical and empirical attempt has been made to investigate the moderating effect of differences in cultural values. The few cross-cultural studies that exist compare a few countries at a time, which limits multicountry generalization and the investigation of a multidimensional concept of culture. Such investigation is crucial considering that piracy is a global phenomenon and managers and academics have little actionable guidance on how strategies to combat the global phenomenon of digital piracy should be adapted in different countries. Furthermore, all of the main antecedents have been identified and developed using theories from Western countries and—as with many other information systems (IS) theories—it is questionable whether the findings can be generalized to non-Western countries (Burgess and Steenkamp 2006).

Two recent meta-analyses have tried to address some of the gaps in digital piracy research. A metaanalysis by Eisend (2019) investigates morality effects on the acquisition of both counterfeit and pirated products, but does not provide information about the relative effects of different drivers of digital piracy. The meta-analysis by Lowry et al. (2017) investigates major antecedents of digital piracy and evaluates the contribution of various theoretical explanations by comparing the corresponding drivers’ effect size. While the meta-analysis is quite comprehensive, it does not cover all relevant drivers (e.g., user psychographics), nor integrate and test the theoretical perspectives or address the question of cross-cultural variability of digital piracy drivers.

A thorough quantitative and meta-analytic review of the research stream on digital piracy that addresses all three major research gaps in a comprehensive way should therefore benefit both practice and research for the following reasons.

First, the current state of research on digital piracy provides numerous but fragmented and sometimes divergent findings on its explanatory variables. These findings can be evaluated through a meta-analysis by estimating the mean values and range of effects of their relationships with various antecedents. The findings could help researchers in deciding which explanatory variables should be considered in future studies on digital piracy. For instance, the metaanalytic findings indicate that demographic variables play a negligible role as piracy predictors, but that variables such as morality, perceived behavioral control, piracy experience, justification, product quality, and subjective norms have strong effects.

Second, meta-analytic evidence obtained by aggregating empirical findings across studies can be used as an input for structural model analysis to test a comprehensive theoretical model. The proposed and tested theoretical model identifies the key drivers of users’ responses toward digital piracy, integrates all theoretical perspectives that prior research has largely applied separately, clarifies alternative and competing explanations, identifies the major theoretical mechanisms, and thus guides future research. The integrated and extended model explains 45% of the variance in attitudes, 53% in intentions, and 42% in behaviors toward digital piracy. Notably, the theoretical perspective of reinforcement that has been applied the least in prior research explains the most variance in digital piracy behavior.

Third, the meta-analysis accumulates findings from numerous countries and provides a unique opportunity to examine the cross-cultural differences of the influence of digital piracy’s main antecedents and can thus provide insights on the cross-cultural generalizability of key drivers. By conceptually linking Hofstede’s cultural dimensions to the different theoretical perspectives of the integrated model, the meta-analysis shows how the influence of key drivers varies across cultures. For instance, the cultural di mension of individualism–collectivism is linked to the theoretical perspective of social influence and control, and in accordance with this link, the findings show that the influence of subjective norms decreases with individualism. The findings add to the discussion on the transferability of Western theories to non-Western countries (Burgess and Steenkamp 2006). Research that does not take into account the cultura context may provide misleading guidance by failing to acknowledge the cross-cultural heterogeneity of effects suggested by theory.

Fourth, the findings put forth practical implications for managers as they devise strategies for preventing digital piracy and show which strategy is most efficient either globally or in particular cultural contexts. The paper further discusses how our prior knowledge of antipiracy strategies is supported or has to be modified and when an adaptation or standardization strategy for combating digital piracy is appropriate. Technical control strategies such as volume-bound services help to break piracy habits and are supported by the results of this meta-analysis, while the oftendiscussed strategy of making more legal copies freely available to users is not supported by the meta-analytic findings.

To accomplish the objectives mentioned above, we conduct a meta-analysis of digital piracy literature that comprises 177 studies published in 164 manuscripts employing data from 36 countries. We proceed as follows: Based on the extant digital piracy literature, we categorize the major antecedents of attitudes, intentions, and behaviors related to digital piracy. We discuss the theoretical perspectives applied to explain digital piracy and develop a comprehensive model. The model integrates all particularistic theoretical perspectives and depicts the relationship between the main antecedents and the consequences of digital piracy. In addition, we suggest and hypothesize further relationships to consider additional, alternative, and competing explanations. We provide a rationale for the moderating effects of culture and develop specific hypotheses on culture’s moderating influence on the main relationships in the comprehensive theoretical model. Then, we describe the development of the database for the meta-analysis. We use the meta-analysis to provide a quantitative summary of the influence of the key antecedents; to run a structural model analysis to test the proposed theoretical model; and to run metaregressions on the influence of cultural moderators. We conclude with a discussion of our findings and their implications for researchers and managers.

## Theoretical Background

## Antecedents of User Attitudes, Intentions, and Behaviors Related to Digital Piracy

Prior reviews (Liang and Yan 2005, Peitz and Waelbroeck 2006, Lee and Yoo 2009) have suggested numerous variables that influence users’ responses toward digital piracy, as measured by users’ attitudes, intentions, and behavior. Attitudes refer to how users evaluate digital piracy and pirated products, intentions refer to users’ willingness and likelihood to pirate digital products, and behaviors refer to acts of illegally downloading or otherwise acquiring digital products. While managers are typically mostly concerned with intentions and behaviors related to piracy, many antipiracy strategies focus on changing attitudes (e.g., through persuasive and informative campaigns), which in turn influence intentions and behaviors.

The antecedent variables can be broadly categorized as characteristics that relate to the user, the acquisition and consumption process and its outcomes, the product, and the context. The user characteristics are the largest groups of antecedents investigated in prior research; they include user demographics, psychographics, morality and ideology, experience, and capabilities. The acquisition and consumption process refers to variables such as feelings, perceptions, and evaluations that are related to how pirated digital material is acquired and consumed and to its possible outcomes. Product characteristics include all intrinsic and extrinsic properties of both pirated and genuine products, while context refers to influence exerted by close social groups, institutions, companies, and situations.

The expected relationship is sometimes straightforward and obvious, such as when price advantage increases user responses. When it is less obvious, we refer to the common assumptions found in prior literature reviews. In some instances in which no common assumption emerges and the findings are too ambiguous, we do not attempt to determine a relationship. Table 1 shows that there are plenty of antecedent variables, raising the question of which antecedents are most important in explaining digital piracy. As further described in Table 1, several of the antecedent variables are related to the theoretical perspectives that are discussed next.

## Theoretical Approaches Explaining Digital Piracy: Developing a Comprehensive Model

Individual studies typically examine subsets of the antecedents of digital piracy depicted in Table 1, such as the influence of product characteristics or the influence of ethics. Therefore, different theoretical approaches that each focus on a set of particular variables have been employed to explain digital piracy. A review of conceptual papers that discuss theoretical explanations of digital piracy (e.g., Liang and Yan 2005, Peitz and Waelbroeck 2006, Hill 2007, Eisend 2019) in combination with a review of the theoretical perspectives that have been used in the studies included in this meta-analysis reveals a variety of theories. The theories were assigned to four major theoretical perspectives after careful consideration of their commonalities. For instance, the theory of reasoned action and the theory of planned behavior both emphasize social influence as explanation for digital piracy and were therefore grouped together; perceived risk theory and expected utility theory both emphasize the expectation of outcomes of digital pi racy and were therefore grouped together. Four major theoretical perspectives emerge: social influence and control, dilemma solving, outcome evaluation, and reinforcement. Table 2 provides an overview of these perspectives, the related theories and key literature, the key assumptions of each perspective, and suggested variable relationships. The suggested relationships refer to key variables provided in Table 1 and that have been reported in a sufficient number of studies to be included in our empirical analysis (i.e., a meta-analytic structural model).

The first perspective is the social influence and control perspective that refers to the idea that attitudes, intentions, and behaviors are driven by the social environment and by an individual’s perceived control ability. Thus, there are two major influence factors of digital piracy: social influences (that is, whether people important to the individual think that digital piracy behavior is acceptable) and control perceptions (that is, beliefs about how easy or difficult it is to perform digital piracy behavior; e.g., downloading pirated material). This perspective, in particular the theory of planned behavior, dominates research in this field.

The dilemma solving perspective refers to the fact that users experience a dilemma when engaging in digital piracy, which is usually a moral dilemma. Users’ morality reduces digital piracy; morality is also linked to justifications that users seek and apply to reduce their dilemma. While justifications encourage digital piracy, the relationship between morality and justification is somewhat ambiguous as discussed in more detail below.

Outcome evaluation is a theoretical perspective that refers to the users’ expectancies of benefits, risks, costs, or punishments associated with digital piracy behavior. Users are deterred by expected negative outcomes and encouraged by positive outcomes, and they often compare expected costs (e.g., risks of being caught) and benefits (e.g., price and quality advantages) of digital piracy. These expectations explain why users engage in digital piracy behaviors.

The reinforcement perspective refers to the explana tion that current digital piracy behavior can be predicted from past piracy behavior, because users develop routines and habits and they learn from their past behaviors. Thus, experience and past behavior is suggested as a major explanation of digital piracy. The influence of past behavior and experience as reinforcement variables have been considered only in a few prior studies, but without a consistent theoretical lens.

Several studies have analyzed modified and extended versions of single theories, in which they add, omit, or substitute specific elements. For instance, Cronan and Al-Rafee (2008) add morality and past experience to the theory of planned behavior; Peace et al. (2003) combine the theory of planned behavior with expected utility theory and deterrence theory.

These efforts have been undertaken without any certainty about whether the most relevant and predictive variables are being considered. While several studies combine typically two or even three single theories, the models and model extensions are still particularistic. No attempt has been made to integrate all four major theoretical approaches, although they partly suggest alternative and competing explanations for key drivers of digital piracy (e.g., the influence of justification or the relationship between morality and justification, as described below). To develop a nomological network that connects the most relevant predictors of digital piracy, these approaches were integrated into a comprehensive theoretical model.

As described and hypothesized below, the integrative model considers additional relationships that address problems with the basic assumptions of these models (i.e., the rational decision-making assumption of the social influence and control perspective) and alternative interpretations or explanations of the different theoretical approaches (i.e., the role of subjective norms in dilemma solving, the alternative interpretation of morality and justification from either a deontological view or a cognitive dissonance view, and the role of justification as a reinforcement heuristic). ## Culture’s Moderating Effects on the Model’s Key Relationships

Culture has been defined as the collective programming of the mind, which distinguishes the members of one group of people from others (Hofstede et al. 2010). Users who grow up in a particular culture become accustomed to that culture’s values, beliefs, attitudes, and even perception processes (Markus and Kitayama 1991). Why and how they consume digital products may reflect deep influences from their cultures. Culture and its different dimensions have a varying influence in terms of the theoretical perspectives described above. For instance, social influences play a stronger role in countries with a collectivistic orientation, and decisions that solve moral dilemmas are made differently depending on the moral orientation of a culture (Swinyard et al. 1990). National culture influences whether innovative behavior is appreciated or whether people are resistant to change and stick to routinized behavior (Steenkamp et al. 1999). Notably, all of the theoretical perspectives mentioned above have been developed in Western contexts. The increasing globalization of scholarship has encouraged researchers to question whether these Western theories are valid and generalizable across the globe or are contingent on cultural contexts (Burgess and Steenkamp 2006).

Thus, a country’s culture is a contextual component and contingency factor that can shape the influence of the key antecedents of digital piracy in the proposed theoretical model. To evaluate how cultural differences influence the effect of digital piracy’s key antecedents, the theoretical perspectives in the model are conceptually linked to Hofstede’s four primary cultural dimensions—individualism–collectivism, masculinity– femininity, uncertainty avoidance, and power distance. While we recognize that culture is fluid, dynamic, and often difficult to define, we chose to take Hofstede’s approach as a proxy for culture as it is commonly done in business research for pragmatic reasons. National cultural priorities influence how individual values are evaluated and what kinds of expressions are encouraged or discouraged (Triandis 1989). Ideally, a value analysis would therefore include values at both the national culture level and the individual level. Because of the data aggregation level in a meta-analysis, however, we do not have access to individual-level data and are restricted to national culture variables. Therefore, we follow the approach that is commonly applied in both meta-analyses and primary studies in IS and management research that use national culture variables to investigate individual and organizational behavior (e.g., Leidner and Kayworth 2006, Samaha et al. 2014)

## Method

## Data Collection and Coding

We performed an exhaustive search of published and unpublished studies providing estimates of antecedents of digital piracy attitudes, intentions, and behaviors. To identify relevant studies, we first searched several review articles (Liang and Yan 2005, Peitz and Waelbroeck 2006, Lee and Yoo 2009), examining their references and applying an ancestry tree search by searching for all articles referring to the review papers in the Web of Science database and on Google Scholar. Second, we performed a keyword search of electronic databases (such as Business Source Complete, JSTOR,

PsyINFO, ProQuest Dissertations & Theses, and Google Scholar), using keywords such as “file sharing,” “piracy,” or “pirat\*,” combined with “digital,” “software,” “music,” and “movie.” Third, once a study was identified, its references were examined to search for further studies. This study retrieval approach is consistent with recommendations in the literature (e.g., Hunter and Schmidt 2004). The search period covered all studies available by October 2016, with the earliest manuscript dating from 1990. The sample of this meta-analysis includes journal articles, book chapters, working papers, unpublished theses, and conference proceedings, thus reducing the risk of a biased representation of the state of research because of the source of publication.

After identifying manuscripts for potential inclusion in the data set, we applied inclusion and exclusion criteria to determine which manuscripts would be retained. We included all manuscripts that reported on empirical studies based on samples of (prospective) users of pirated digital material and quantitatively measured and provided estimates regarding any of the antecedents of users’ attitudes toward digital piracy, intentions to pirate, and actual pirating behaviors. We excluded any manuscripts outside this scope. In particular, we did not consider studies that investigated aggregate data such as piracy rates per country; instead, we focused on studies that investigated individual data. We also excluded studies that did not provide sufficient data for the purpose of our meta-analysis, such as those that lacked enough statistical information to calculate a correlation coefficient and for which the necessary information could not be retrieved from the authors Apart from these exclusions, we considered any manuscripts that provided the appropriate empirical data.

Our search resulted in an initial set of 174 usable manuscripts that included 185 studies with independent samples and that reported on one or more estimates for any antecedent of digital piracy attitudes, intentions, or behaviors. Two coders, the author and one student assistant who was selected, hired, and trained for the coding task, independently coded the data and assigned the antecedents to a predefined set of variables of interest that were extracted from prior review studies (Liang and Yan 2005, Peitz and Waelbroeck 2006, Lee and Yoo 2009). The initial coding yielded 93% agreement on antecedents that fit the coding scheme, and the majority of variables could be assigned to the categories, because most of the variables are unambiguously labeled in the primary studies (e.g., age, gender, subjective norms). Inconsistencies were resolved by discussion between both coders. To assign the few remaining variables that did not fit the coding scheme (i.e., that were not assigned by the coders to the set of predefined antecedents), the author went through these variables and created new categories for those variables that appeared in at least three studies. To code these variables and to assign them to broader categories, the procedure applied in other meta-analyses was followed (e.g., Pick and Eisend 2016): highly related variables were combined into composite constructs referring to one type of antecedent with a consistent influence on user response toward digital piracy. To that end, the definitions and measurement models of the variables were carefully examined to ensure that only variables from the same nomological network were combined. For instance, the newly created category smart shopping covered the variables price consciousness and value consciousness. Any antecedent that appeared either only in one or two independent studies or in only one manuscript was eliminated from further analysis. This was done to ensure a minimum degree of generalizability, because a meta-analysis should provide a high degree of generalization and thus more information than a single manuscript or a single-study manuscript that is followed by a replication study. The second coder independently assigned the variables to the new categories, with an agreement rate of 95%. Inconsistencies were resolved as described above. Table 1 provides the final list of antecedent variables.

The selection of estimates left us with 164 manuscripts to be considered in the meta-analysis (with 15% of the manuscripts being unpublished). Some manuscripts reported on more than one relevant study (e.g., different country samples), while multiple manuscripts reported estimates based on data from a singlestudy sample. Ultimately, the 164 manuscripts included estimates from 174 studies with independent samples collected in 36 countries,<sup>1</sup> which provided 1,373 effect size estimates describing the relationships between any antecedent and digital piracy attitudes, intentions, or behaviors. The combined sample size includes 63,662 users. The online appendix lists the manuscripts.

## Effect Size Computation

The effect size metric selected for the meta-analysis is the correlation coefficient; higher absolute values indicate a stronger influence of an antecedent variable on attitudes, intentions, or behaviors. A negative sign in the correlation coefficient indicates that the antecedent variable reduces attitudes, intentions, or behaviors, while a positive sign indicates that the antecedent variable increases these responses. All correlations were adjusted for measurement error, following the procedure proposed by Hunter and Schmidt (2004). When a study did not report on reliability or when it used a single-item measure, we used the mean reliability for that construct across all studies.

We deal with integrating dependencies between effect sizes using the following approach. When a study presented findings for different antecedent variables, the findings were treated as independent, because we integrated and analyzed the estimates for each antecedent variable separately. Some studies reported multiple relevant tests for the same antecedent variable. Multiple effect sizes in the same study are not statistically independent. We account for the dependencies of effect sizes and the nested structure of meta-analytic data by using a mixed-effects multilevel model to perform the meta-analytic procedures in hierarchical linear modeling (Raudenbush and Bryk 2002). By specifying effect sizes as being clustered under the higher-level unit, multilevel modeling is designed to address the dependence problem. We estimate the following model:

$$
r _ {j k} = \rho + \mu_ {k} + \mu_ {j k} + e _ {j k},
$$

where $j = 1 , 2 , 3 , \dots J$ effect sizes, and $k = 1 , 2 , 3 , \dots K$ studies. This formula is an “intercept-only” model in that no moderator is included as a predictor. It estimates the average effect size $\rho ,$ the deviation of the average effect size of a study from the grand mean $\left( \mu _ { k } \right)$ , and the deviation of each effect size in the kth study from the grand mean $\left( \mu _ { j k } \right)$ . The latter two terms have a variance of $\sigma _ { k } ^ { 2 }$ and ${ \sigma } _ { j k } ^ { 2 } ,$ respectively. The error term $e _ { j k }$ is the known sampling error at the individual level and is supplied as a data input.

We compute fail-safe Ns to address publication bias. For any relationship of interest, fail-safe N represents the number of additional studies with null results needed to render the results for that relationship nonsignificant at $p = 0 . 0 5$ . We calculated the fail-safe Ns for all integrated effect sizes that turn out to be significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ by using the effect size estimates that were adjusted for measurement error. Furthermore, we provide a homogeneity test as an aid in deciding whether observed effect sizes are more variable than would be expected from sampling error alone. If they are, then there is a strong basis for including moderators. The homogeneity test involves the Q statistic, in which the distribution is similar to chi-square with K – 1 degrees of freedom (Hedges and Olkin 1985).

## Structural Model Estimation

To apply a structural equation model, we developed a correlation matrix including meta-analytic correlations of the antecedents of digital piracy and added metaanalytic correlations for the interrelationships between them (e.g., between subjective norms and quality), following recommendations in the literature (Bergh et al. 2016). For a construct to be included in such analysis, multiple study effects must relate it to every other construct in the model. We required a minimum of three correlations for each relationship, which equals or exceeds the requirements of other metaanalytic correlation matrices found in the IS literature (e.g., Joseph et al. 2007). We searched the studies in the meta-analysis and added 532 correlations for the interrelationships between variables. They were integrated and adjusted in the same way as the correlations of the bivariate relationships. The correlation matrix is provided in the online appendix.

A single indicator measured all of the constructs in the structural model. Error variances for the indicators were set to zero because we had already considered measurement errors when integrating the effect sizes. The harmonic mean of the cumulative sample size underlying each meta-analytic correlation was used as the sample size for the analysis, as is commonly practiced in meta-analytic structural equation modeling studies (Bergh et al. 2016). For the final analysis, chi-square test statistics and fit indices were provided: goodness of fit (GFI), adjusted goodness of fit (AGFI), comparative fit index (CFI), root mean square residual (RMR), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). Furthermore, we report the explained variance in attitudes, intentions, and behaviors.

## Meta-Regression Models

To test the moderating effects of cultural dimensions, we ran multilevel meta-regression models on the effect size estimates that represent the relationships suggested in Table 3, but only if relationships were based on a sufficient number of effect sizes and studies, and showed a reasonable ratio of effect sizes per study<sup>2</sup>: subjective norm–intention, perceived behavioral control– intention, morality–attitude, justification–behavior, and experience–intention. These are the main relationships in the proposed theoretical model that were described in Table 3. The data sets of the five relationships encompass between 40 and 62 effect sizes, between 14 and 47 studies, and between 8 and 15 different countries.

To assess the effects of Hofstede’s four main cultural dimensions—individualism–collectivism (IND), power distance (PD), masculinity–femininity (MASC), and uncertainty avoidance (UA)—on the effect size estimates representing the selected relationships, we used the country-of-study information from each study to assign scores for each cultural dimension (that are provided by Hofstede online, www.hofstede-insight .com). We modeled the coefficients as a linear function of these variables, proceeding with the mixedeffects multilevel model in HLM (hierarchical linear modeling) to account for multiple estimates in a study and their dependencies. A model including moderators is termed a conditional model, which is a mixedeffects model, since fixed effects for the moderators are considered in addition to random components.

Countries differ not only in terms of culture but also in terms of their socio-economic, legal, and infrastructural characteristics (Burgess and Steenkamp 2006). Furthermore, Hofstede’s cultural dimensions are related to several of these country characteristics (Tang and Koveos 2008). Therefore, we include three country-leve control variables that describe the economic, technological, and legal differences between countries and developments over time, which are also relevant for the digital piracy phenomenon and are related to Hofstede’s cultural dimension. Although many other control variables would theoretically be feasible (e.g., antipiracy regulations, educational measures), the sample size (i.e., number of effect sizes) for each model restricts the number of predictors. The three selected control variables overlap with other possible control variables; for instance, legal differences and developments also refer to antipiracy regulations and measures, while economic differences and developments are strongly related to educational efforts (Goel and Nelson 2009).

We measure the economic differences between countries by means of the GDP per capita (GDP) and the technological differences by using the number of Internet users per 100 inhabitants (INT). To assess legal differences, the rule of law (RULE) measures the establishment of formal rules in a country and its society members’ conformity to these rules (Kaufmann et al. 2010). The data were extracted from the World Bank database (databank.worldbank.org). Because the values of the control variables vary not only over countries, but also over years, we assigned these variables by country and by year of data collection (e.g., if the data were collected in the United States in 2012, we assigned GDP, INT, and RULE values from the United States in 2012). If the information on year of data collection was not provided in a manuscript, we computed the publication year minus three, as three years is the average time a study took from data collection to publication.

A major threat to robustness in meta-analytic regression is collinearity. Since no direct multicollinearity diagnostic exists in HLM, we first examined the correlations among the cultural and country control variables. As an additional check, we ran the models using OLS regression; if these findings revealed high values of the variance inflation factor (VIF), we reran the HLM models without the predictor causing collinearity and compared the findings with the full model. If the findings remained stable, we could report the findings of the full HLM models. If they were unstable, we decided to drop the moderator that caused high collinearity. As in prior research, the cultural dimensions of individualism and power distance show high correlations and often lead to multicollinearity (Steensma et al. 2000). If that was the case (i.e., for the models explaining the relationships subjective norm–intention, justification–behavior, and experience–intention), we elected to provide results for two models—one that excludes power distance and one that excludes individualism—in line with the procedure applied by Samaha et al. (2014). For the perceived behavioral control–intention relationship, the cultural dimensions of uncertainty avoidance and masculinity led to multicollinearity, and we proceeded in the same way.

To account for possible influences of methodological variables, as is common procedure in most metaanalyses, we proceeded as follows. Because adding further control variables to the described models challenged their robustness, we ran additional and separate models that considered the following methodological variables: participants (student vs. nonstudent sample), sampling (convenience vs. random sampling), and study type (survey vs. experimental). In addition, we considered the publication status of the manuscript (published vs. unpublished) and included the year of publication to account for variations over time. We added three more dummy variables that accounted for the piracy product category—software, music, or movie. The base alternatives here are studies that deal with a mix of product categories. If one of the control variables turned out to be significant, we reran the main model with cultural and country-level control variables and added the significant methodological variable.

## Results

## Antecedents of Digital Piracy: Summary of Bivariate Relationships

Table 4 presents an overview of the bivariate correlations for all antecedents of digital piracy attitudes, intentions, and behaviors. The direction of effects is consistent with the assumptions described in Table 1 (and depicted in Table 4 in parentheses next to each antecedent variable), but not all effects turned out to be significant. With regard to the relationships for which we had no assumptions (indicated by “(?)” in Table 4), some turned out to be not significant (employment, ethnicity, materialism), while gender, innovativeness, and expertise show significant positive effects, and susceptibility and attribute importance show significant negative effects.

The effects for each category are summarized as follows. Among the demographic variables, age is negatively related to attitudes, intentions, and behavior. Men show more positive attitudes, intentions, and behaviors than women. Other demographics (education, employment, ethnicity, and income) show no significant effects. Among the psychographic variables, collectivism, innovativeness, Machiavellianism, optimism bias, and smart shopping all increase attitudes, intentions, and/or behaviors, while integrity, risk aversion, and susceptibility decrease one or more of them. As for morality and ideology, idealism and relativism have no effect, but morality decreases attitudes, intentions, and behaviors. As for experience and capability, piracy experience and perceived behavioral control increase attitudes, intentions, and behaviors. Low self-control increases intentions. Genuine product experience decreases intentions but does not affect behaviors. Expertise does not affect attitudes but does increase intentions and behaviors.

The process and outcome variables reveal that positive feelings, perceived positive individual outcomes, and justification increase one or more of attitudes, intentions, or behaviors. Negative feelings, perceived individual negative outcomes, perceived negative social outcomes, and risk reduce attitudes, intentions, and/or behaviors. As for product variables, the importance of the original product’s attributes decreases attitudes, intentions, and behaviors, while the price advantage and the quality of the pirated product increase these responses. As for context variables, availability and accessibility and subjective norms increase attitudes, intentions, and behaviors, while regulations and measures decrease these responses. Social sanctions decrease intentions, and social morality does not show any influence.

The insignificant findings indicate that demographics play a negligible role as digital piracy predictors. Most variables that are considered in the theoretical models show consistently moderate to large effects according to Cohen’s (1988) classification: morality, perceived behavioral control, piracy experience, justification, quality, and subjective norms.

The homogeneity test indicates heterogeneity for all except two relationships (susceptibility–intentions and price–behavior). ## Theory Testing: Structural Model Results

Table 5 provides the results of the structural model analyses (the corresponding correlation matrix is provided in the online appendix). The models (1)–(4) separately describing the four theoretical perspectives show that social influence and control and outcome evaluation show a very good model fit (as indicated by the model statistics and fit indices with GFI, AGFI, and $\mathrm { C F I } < 0 . 9 ,$ , and RMR and RMSEA < 0.1), while the fit of the dilemma solving and reinforcement models are lower. The explained variance shows that the reinforcement model explains most variance in behavior (33%), while the social influence and control model explains most variance in intentions (41%). The chisquare difference tests $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f }$ reported under model statistics) show that restricting the final model (6) to either of the models (1) to (5) significantly reduces the model fit. Hence, the integrated and extended model that is depicted in Figure 2 is the superior model, which also shows a very good model fit.

The path coefficients of the integrated and extended model (model (6)) are depicted in Table 5 and Figure 2. Except for the path from justification to attitude, all paths are significant $( p < 0 . 0 \dot { 1 } )$ ). Moreover, all four hypotheses that explain the extension of the model are supported: subjective norms increase attitudes (Hypothesis 1), attitude increases behavior and the paths support partial mediation (as tested following the procedure suggested by to Iacobucci et al. 2007) via intentions (Hypothesis 2),<sup>3</sup> justification is positively related to experience (Hypothesis 3), and justification increases intention and behavior (Hypothesis 4). As for our research question, morality is negatively related to justification, therefore supporting the ethical decision-making argument over the justification argument.

## Meta-Regression Results

Table 6 presents the results of the meta-regression models. If a model suffers from multicollinearity as described in the notes to Table 6, two models were presented. For instance, the subjective norm–intention model shows high collinearity for individualism and power distance. Therefore, two separate models were computed and presented, the first model including individualism as predictor and dropping power distance, and the second model including power distance as predictor and dropping individualism.

The intercepts in each model are significant and represent the meta-analytic effect size after controlling for the influence of the moderator variables. They are in line with the bivariate findings in Table 4, thus supporting the robustness of our results.

The findings in the various regression models are used to test Hypotheses 5–10 as indicated in the lower part of Table 6. Hypotheses 5 and 6 refer to the social influence and control perspective. As expected, we find a negative regression coefficient related to individualism when testing the influence on the effect size estimate representing the relationship subjective norm– intention. This finding shows that individualism reduces the influence of subjective norms on intentions (Hypothesis 5). We find a marginal significant and positive effect of individualism when testing the influence on the effect size representing the relationship perceived behavioral control–intention. Hence, individualism tends to increase the influence of perceived behavioral control on intention (Hypothesis 6).

The dilemma-solving perspective is tested with Hypotheses 7 and 8: with increasing masculinity, the influence of justification on behavior decreases (Hypothesis 8). However, morality effects are independent of any cultural influences (Hypothesis 7).

Hypotheses 9 and 10 refer to the reinforcement perspective. Uncertainty avoidance plays a role in the reinforcement perspective, and the influence of experience on intention increases with uncertainty avoidance (Hypothesis 10), but it does not moderate the influence of justification on behavior (Hypothesis 9). The findings provide support for the cross-cultural variations in the influences of the key antecedents of digital piracy, as suggested by three out of four theoretical perspectives that were integrated in our model.

## Discussion

This study presents a meta-analysis of literature on digital piracy. The meta-analysis identifies the major drivers of users’ engagement in digital piracy and integrates particularistic theories that have been separately presented in prior research into an integrated model that explains 42%–53% of the variance in digital piracy attitudes, intentions, and behaviors. It also identifies the major theoretical mechanisms explaining user responses toward digital piracy. The cross-cultural analysis of the key drivers in this model shows that the influence of these drivers vary across cultures in line with the cultural contingency of the underlying theoretical perspective. These findings offer guidance for future research on digital piracy and also have managerial implications.

## Implications for Theory and Research

The study makes several contributions to the literature and also has various implications for theory and research. First, several qualitative reviews on digital piracy have noted that the current state of research provides fragmented and sometimes divergent findings regarding digital piracy drivers (Liang and Yan 2005, Peitz and Waelbroeck 2006, Lee and Yoo 2009). This meta-analysis identifies the direction of the effects of variables with contradictory findings in prior studies (e.g., gender, expertise, innovativeness) and measures the relative importance of digital piracy’s drivers by means of their explanatory power as assessed by effect sizes. Except for age and gender, demographics are unimportant predictors. Most other variables show small to moderate effects and a set of few variables stands out with moderate to large effects—morality, perceived behavioral control, piracy experience, justification, quality, and subjective norms. These variables can be considered as key drivers of digital piracy and should be included in further attempts to develop and test comprehensive explanatory models. While bivariate relationships are less informative than findings from multivariate models that take into account the interdependencies between antecedent variables, bivariate relationships do provide guidance for future research on digital piracy, because they inform researchers about which variables to consider in future models that strive to explain digital piracy. Any new variables that have not yet been tested and thus do not appear in this meta-analysis should be evaluated in light of the findings of this meta-analysis. If a new variable is correlated with a variable included in this meta-analysis that shows a moderate or even large effect size, the variable from the meta-analysis should be included in the new model as a control variable. For instance, a new study of the five factor model of personality traits as possible antecedents of digital piracy should include related and correlated drivers from this meta-analysis; the personality trait “agreeableness” is likely to be related to susceptibility to influences, the trait “conscientiousness” is related to smart shopping, and the trait “openness” to innovativeness (Steenkamp and Maydeu-Olivares 2015).

Second, the structural model shows that integrating the four major theoretical perspectives from prior research and connecting the variables by additional and theoretically founded paths leads to a well-fitting model that has high explanatory power and explains 53% of the variance in intentions and 42% of the variance in behavior. Interestingly, most variance in behavior is explained by the reinforcement perspective that has largely been neglected as a theoretical lens for explaining digital piracy. This perspective works better than the approach most prominent in the digital piracy literature (the social influence and control perspective, in particular the theory of plan ned behavior; Ajzen 1985), which generally works well for explaining many user behavior phenomena. Ethical decision-making approaches that are subsumed under the dilemma-solving perspective are found quite often in the literature on digital piracy, addressing the fact that digital piracy is considered unethical behavior from a societal and economic perspective. However, the dilemma-solving perspective shows comparatively weak explanatory power for intentions and behavior, and the effects of morality are significant but moderate. The ethical dimension of digital piracy seems less important to users than the public and academic discussion suggests. The model also clarifies the role of justification that has been discussed in the literature as a dilemma-solving approach and been tested in a recent meta-analysis on counterfeiting and piracy (Eisend 2019): justifications are not only related to morality, they also function as a heuristic that supports habitual piracy behavior. This extends the findings of the prior meta-analysis by Eisend (2019) that has investigated justification in the context of counterfeiting and piracy only within the dilemma solving perspective. These findings provide advice for further research on adopting the most promising theoretical lens to explain digital piracy. The integrated model provides a good starting point for further studies. The model also identifies the important drivers after accounting for interrelationships with other drivers. With regard to behavior as the managerially most important variable, experience, perceived behavioral control, justification, and attitudes are the key drivers.

Third, we find cross-cultural variations in the influence of key drivers in the integrated model that reflect their dependency on major cultural values. The differences are considerable and predict a change in the effect size of 0.2–0.3 between the countries that score lowest and highest on the respective cultural dimension. For instance, the predicted difference in effect size describing the influence of subjective norms between the United States as the most individualistic country in the analysis and China as the most collectivistic one is 0.25. The cross-cultural findings contribute to the debate about the applicability of scientific knowledge that has been developed and tested in a Western context (Burgess and Steenkamp 2006). Since globalization is one of the major challenges for today’s companies, theoretical knowledge can only contribute to improve companies’ strategies if this knowledge identifies and integrates cultural contingencies (i.e., country characteristics that moderate structural relations between constructs in a theoretical model). The test of the cross-cultural applicability of the influence of digital piracy’s key drivers within the integrated and extended model is an important step for a contingency theory of digital piracy.

The cross-cultural findings provide researchers with several actionable insights for future research on digital piracy. First, if only some of the suggested variables can be included in a new study that strives to explain digital piracy, the cultural context of that study will indicate which ones are more or less important to consider; for instance, a study that investigates digital piracy in collectivistic countries should consider including subjective norms, and a study that investigates digital piracy in individualistic countries should consider perceived behavioral control. Likewise, justifications are important to consider in societies that score high on femininity, while experience in digital piracy is a variable to be considered in high uncertainty avoidance countries. Second, any study with participants from more than one country should consider a cross-cultural moderator effect for the drivers of piracy that vary across cultures. Third, since we could not test cultural effects of other less important drivers of digital piracy because of data constraints, future research should keep possible cross-cultural variations in mind, especially if the drivers are related to the theoretical perspectives and corresponding variables in our model and cultural effects can be expected. For instance, individualism–collectivism moderates the relationships in the social influence and control perspective and might therefore relate to other social drivers—whether already investigated or yet to be investigated—such as social morality, social sanctions, peer group influences, family influences, etc.

## Implications for Management

Knowing the extent of the influence of digital piracy drivers and their cross-cultural variations is of practical importance, given the struggle to apply appropriate measures to confront the global threat of digital piracy. Several antipiracy measures have been implemented by companies in the past that can be categorized as marketing of the genuine product, antipiracy campaigns, technical control strategies, and legal enforcements (e.g., Conner and Rumelt 1991, Sundararajan 2004, Castro et al. 2009, Appleyard 2015). Table 7 provides an overview of these commonly applied strategies and revises them in the light of the findings of this metaanalysis. Some of the strategies are encouraged, some are discouraged, and some need to be adapted to cultural contexts.

The four major drivers identified in the meta-analysis are experience, followed by perceived behavioral control, justification, and attitude. They imply that effective antipiracy measures focus on breaking habits, reducing users’ control, suppressing justifications, and changing attitudes.

Breaking habits requires particular technical control strategies such as impeding access to sites that provide pirated material (e.g., by technical restrictions implemented in browsers). They can be accompanied by legislative demands that force local Internet service providers to block sites encouraging piracy and legislation for search engines to suppress these sites. Such blocks have been introduced by many countries, but with varying success (e.g., Bhattacharjee et al. 2006, Adermon and Liang 2014, Poort et al. 2014, Peukert et al. 2017). The strategy should be particularly effective in high uncertainty-avoidance countries (e.g., Turkey or many Latin American countries), but not for users in low uncertainty avoidance countries. Furthermore, breaking habits does not necessarily benefit the traditional sale of digital products but can direct users to streaming sites such as Netflix for digital movies and shows (Danaher et al. 2015). Hence, companies that try to break piracy habits should also provide users with new and easy-to-control digital consumption habits, which might require changes in traditional distribution channels of music and movie providers that strongly focus on CD/ DVD/Blu-Ray purchases and paid digital downloads toward digital streaming services.

Users of piracy sites run higher risks of unwittingly downloading malicious software or being exposed to scams, which lowers their perceived control.

Weakening illegal users’ perceived control therefore cannot only be achieved by implementing technical restrictions in browsers to hinder access to piracy sites but also by impairing the attractiveness of pirate websites by providing wrong, low-quality, or faulty music or movie files on pirate websites while providing high-quality content on legitimate channels. For instance, the cable TV channel HBO faced high piracy rates and responded to it by producing new high-quality and innovative content (Lahiri and Dey 2013). At the same time, strengthening legal users perceived control can be achieved through the enhanced features and functionality of legal software products and websites that are easily accessible (Sinha and Mandel 2008). For instance, Spotify and iTunes are not interrupted by commercials and offer extensive music catalogs and extras, which provides users with better control. The differential control perception between legal and illegal users can further reduce digital piracy. Perceived control can also be changed by antipiracy campaigns. While many existing antipiracy campaigns foster positive societal appeals such as supporting creativity and the industry through legal downloads, describing the risks and loss of control seem more promising. In fact, the creative industry in Australia has introduced a successful “Price of Piracy” campaign in 2017 (https://thepriceofpiracy.org.au) that focusses on digital piracy’s risks related to viruses, spyware, stolen credit card details, and identity theft. This is in line with our findings, as all measures that focus on changing perceived control are important in individualistic countries (e.g., Australia, United States).

Communication campaigns aiming to change piracy attitudes in collectivistic countries should focus on subjective norms and try to increase the private self over the collective self by making users aware of their differences compared with others and by demonstrating that the close social group might be wrong (Ybarra and Trafimow 1998). Communication, information, and education about, for instance, virus threats, scams, and the risks of being caught (e.g., as evidenced by a number of publicly documented copyright infringement lawsuits) can further reduce users’ justification strategies. Changing justification strategies is particularly important in feminine countries—i.e., Scandinavian countries. In line with this advice, antipiracy organizations in Scandinavian countries have been appealing to stricter legislation and better information of the public about their responsibility and all possible consequences of digital piracy (e.g., blackmarketwatch.org in Sweden). In 2016, Norway was all over the media for handing down a very harsh sentence for online piracy (Kamen 2016). The high public attention to such a sentence might be one of the reasons why the country’s antipiracy efforts have been quite successful.

The meta-analysis provides further information on targeting strategies—information that can help identify the specific media channels through which pirating users should be addressed by communication campaigns. Pirating users tend to be younger and male, but these demographic differences are rather small. Psychographics provide a better portrait of pirating users than demographics—they appreciate sharing and are innovative, less risk averse, less susceptible to influence, and of lower integrity.

An important antipiracy strategy that has been discussed intensively is making more legal copies freely available to users (Dootson and Suzor 2015). The strategy is not only challenging to companies, it is also not supported by the findings of this metaanalysis: neither genuine product experience nor attribute importance show a significant reduction effect on piracy behavior. For the same reasons, digital rights management and content identification are less successful antipiracy strategies in influencing pirating users (Vernik et al. 2011).

## Limitations

The study has some limitations common to metaanalytic techniques that both benefit and suffer from a high degree of generalization. As already outlined above, this study examines cross-cultural differences and considers the average measures of cultural dimensions in line with prior intercultural research and meta-analyses in the IS and management literature (e.g., Leidner and Kayworth 2006, Samaha et al. 2014). Although this approach is methodologically rigorous, it neglects the individual variation in cultureimprinted values of users within a country. Ideally, a value analysis would therefore include values at both the national culture level and the individual level. Because of the data aggregation level in a meta-analysis however, we do not have access to individual-level data and are restricted to national culture variables. Future primary research could therefore assess the influence of cultural dimensions on both an individual and country level, taking into account appropriate cultural values (e.g., Schwartz 1992). We used Hofstede’s four original dimensions that are commonly applied in the literature, but we did not consider further Hofstede dimensions (e.g., indulgence) or cultural dimensions suggested by other authors. These four dimensions provide scores for a large set of countries, while other cultural dimensions are usually available for smaller set of countries and would have restricted the metaregression analysis by reducing the sample size due to missing country scores.

A second limitation arises from restricting the sample to studies published in English. While such a language bias is broadly accepted in meta-analysis for practical and substantial reasons, the use of country-related moderator variables brings about the question whether the meta-analytic sample is indeed representative of all countries in the world or whether it overrepresents countries from the Western hemisphere, in particular, English-speaking countries. Although such a bias does not necessarily negate the results of this meta-analysis, it might affect the power of the test results.

Third, a related problem refers to the assumed measurement invariance across countries, which is required to explain the observed differences in effect sizes as culture dependent instead of depending on the way measures (e.g., subjective norm, attitude) perform across countries (Steenkamp and Baumgartner 1998). As a consequence of the level of data aggregation in meta-analysis, meta-analytic effect sizes do not allow testing for measurement invariance. This problem has so far been ignored in meta-analyses that test for differences across countries. Future metaanalytic studies should take this problem into account when applying country moderator variables to explain the variance in effect sizes.

Fourth, although certain key factors that explain software piracy also relate to music and movie piracy, the literature also indicates some differences between digital products (e.g., Bhattacharjee et al. 2003). As the main objective of the meta-analysis was to look at digital products in general, the integrative results did not differentiate between the three types of digital products, which might have partly contributed to the heterogeneity of results in Table 4. The metaregression models, however, did try to solve the differences in effect size and the additional analysis considered the type of digital product as explanatory factor (presented in the online appendix). The metaregression revealed only one significant difference across product types for the positive relationship between behavioral control on intention: the effect is stronger for music piracy and weaker for movie piracy, because it is presumably easier to download illegally or to stream music files compared with movie files (because of file size). While the current metaanalysis took the difference between digital products only for the major relationships into account, future meta-analysis should consider and test differences between product types once more effect sizes become available.

## Conclusion

This study sought to advance research on the variables that explain users’ engagement in digital piracy through a quantitative review of prior research. It identifies digital piracy’s key drivers, develops a new comprehensive model that integrates prior particularistic theories and increases their explanatory power, and explains cross-cultural differences in the structural relations of the model. In achieving these outcomes, we enhance the IS community’s understanding of the digital piracy phenomenon. We encourage researchers to use this new model to explain digital piracy while con sidering the cultural contingencies discussed in this study for its applicability. We encourage managers to rethink currently applied antipiracy measures in light of the findings.
