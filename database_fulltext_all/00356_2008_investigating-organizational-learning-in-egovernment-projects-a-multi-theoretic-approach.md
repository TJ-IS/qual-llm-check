# Investigating organizational learning in eGovernment projects: A multi-theoretic approach

Chee Wei Phang <sup>\*</sup>, Atreyi Kankanhalli, Caizuan Ang

Department of Information Systems, School of Computing, National University of Singapore, Computing 1, #03-68, Law Link 117590, Singapore

Available online 30 January 2008

## Abstract

Organizational learning is essential during eGovernment implementation to leverage the benefits of such eforts. However, there is a lack of holistic understanding of organizational learning in IT-related change in public organizations. Motivated by such concerns, we construct a structurational framework that inter-relates elements pertinent to IT-related change from four theoretical perspectives: organizational politics, organizational culture, institutional theory, and organizational learning itself. Inter-relationships between elements of these perspectives are explicated through the notions of structures, modalities, and actions from structuration theory. Guided by the framework, we conducted a case study to examine the organizational learning occurring during a successful eGovernment project. The analysis indicates the inextricable interactions between ele ments from the diferent perspectives during the project implementation. The proposed framework ofers both theoretical and practical benefits for understanding and facilitating organizational learning in eGovernment projects. © 2007 Elsevier B V All rights reserved

Keywords: eGovernment; IT implementation; Organizational learning; Structuration theory; Organizational politics; Organizational culture; Institutional theory; Structurational framework

## 1. Introduction

The implementation of a new information technology (IT) has been recognized as an intervention that can induce organizational change (Orlikowski and Robey, 1991). As change occurs, organizations need to learn to adapt to new uncertainties in order to achieve anticipated change benefits (Argyris, 1977; Gregor et al., 2006; Robey and Sahay, 1996). For instance, the implementation of computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) systems requires engineers to collectively learn new modes of communication, so as to reap the systems’ benefits of shortening product development cycle time (Black et al., 2004). Public organizations have recognized the importance of organizational learning necessitated by changes (Mahler, 1997) such as the introduction of new technologies through eGovernment eforts.

eGovernment broadly refers to the strategic application of IT to transform the public sector. This involves the use of IT to provide citizens and organizations with better information and services, and for governments to interact with business partners and transact internally (Gronlund, 2002; Tung and Rieck, 2005). Implementation of eGovernment initiatives requires substantial learning for public organizations, which have typically been considered as bureaucratic entities with conservative cultures that are resistant to change (Robertson and Seneviratne, 1995). Moreover, public organizations are confronted with multiple and often conflicting interests of diferent constituents and an institutional context with complicated regulations (Thong et al., 2000; Tung and Rieck, 2005), all of which contribute to the complexity of change and required learning associated with eGovernment eforts. These challenges motivate us to investigate organizational learning in the context of eGovernment implementation.

While organizations recognize the importance of learning, there is a lack of holistic approaches towards understanding organizational learning in IT-related change management. Robey and Boudreau (1999) consider four theoretical perspectives relevant to investigating IT-related organizational change: organizational politics, organizational culture, institutional theory, and organizational learning. The authors show that the four perspectives focus on diferent, complementary elements of organizations and can better account for complex IT-related change phenomena. However, their study deals with the four perspectives separately and does not explore the possible interplay between them. Yet, inter-relationships among the diferent theoretical perspectives are evident. For instance, previous studies have emphasized the need to consider the influence of organizational politics (Robey et al., 2002), organizational culture (Mahler, 1997), and institutional conditions (Newman, 2000) on organizational learning in the event of change. This calls for a holistic approach that incorporates the diferent theoretical perspectives and their inter-relationships. Thus, our research question is: How do relevant elements from organizational politics, culture, and institutional perspectives inter-relate to influence successful organizational learning in response to IT implementation related change?

A problem facing studies aiming to develop a holistic view of complex phenomena is the dificulty to manage the multitude of elements involved. To overcome this problem, we propose a framework that employs structuration theory as a lens to organize and understand the logics of inter-relationships among the elements of organizational politics, culture, institutions, and learning. The structurational framework centers on the structures related to the diferent organizational elements and the agencies involved in the enactment of such structures favorable to organizational learning. Guided by the framework, we conducted an in-depth case study of a successful eGovernment efort that aimed at improving the internal eficiency of a public organization in Singapore with the ultimate goal to provide better services to its customers (i.e., citizens and businesses). Of particular interest is the observation that the organization started of with low IT proficiency of employees but went on to exploit IT so successfully that it became a leading organization in IT deployment in its sector. We examine the learning that the public organization went through during the implementation of such a strategic information system to fulfill its eGovernment goals. Our analysis informed by the framework reveals that elements of politics, culture, institutions, and learning inextricably interacted during diferent phases of the eGovernment project implementation that eventually led to its success. The case intends to unveil insights about how successful organizational learning during eGovernment initiatives can be facilitated, particularly amidst the high failure rates of eGovernment projects globally<sup>1</sup>.

## 2. Theoretical background

In this section we will first describe structuration theory and the reasons for using it as a lens to organize elements from the four perspectives of organizational learning, culture, politics, and institutional theory. Subsequently we discuss each of the perspectives individually and then describe the structurational framework that inter-relates these perspectives.

## 2.1. Structuration theory

Structuration theory was proposed by Giddens (1979) to reconcile the division between structure and agency in social science (Jones, 1999), by re-conceptualizing the two as mutually interacting duality. Structure refers to rules and resources that human agents draw on in their interactions, which both enable and constrain their action. Action is defined as ‘‘stream of actual or contemplated causal interventions of corporeal beings in the ongoing process of events-in-the-world” (Giddens, 1979, p. 55). The emphasis of action is on the transformative capacity of human agents in bringing about interventions to events surrounding them. Structure is thus both the medium drawn on by human agents in their action and also the outcome of such action, a nature termed by Giddens as ‘‘duality of structure”. The duality of structure encompasses three fundamental dimensions i.e., signification, domination, and legitimation. The three dimensions of structure respectively interact with agency dimensions of communication, power, and sanctions through modalities of interpretive schemes, resources, and norms. Modalities are ‘‘lines of mediation” through which structure is realized in human action (Baert, 1998). To illustrate, individuals communicate through interpretive schemes that inform and define communication on the basis of shared meanings, and produce and reproduce structure of signification.

Structuration theory has been widely employed in IS research (e.g., DeSanctis and Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 1992, 2000; Orlikowski and Robey, 1991) to study IT implementation in organizations. Particularly, Orlikowski and Robey (1991) developed a structuration model of technology, in which IT is conceptualized as part of the structuration process. DeSanctis and Poole (1994) proposed an adaptive structuration theory to investigate the appropriation process of IT by organizational actors. Both these models see IT as embodying structure, such as rules of certain work procedures (Walsham, 1993). However, structure is instantiated in human action and is not something that exists independent of human action. This idea is addressed by Orlikowski’s (2000) practicelens of technology, in which human action is placed at the central position and structure only emerges when technologies are ‘‘enacted” through human’s situated interaction with technologies (Orlikowski, 2000). This re-conceptualization reinstates the dynamic and emergent nature of structure.

Several factors make structuration theory a relevant device to organize the elements from the four perspectives of interest (organizational learning, culture, politics, and institutional theory). First, being a meta-theory (Gregson, 1989), structuration theory is particularly apt for organizing and making sense of elements from multiple perspectives (Jones, 1999). Second, structuration theory has been related to each of the four perspectives. Organizational culture (Walsham, 1993), power relations (Jasperson et al., 2002), and institutions themselves (Giddens, 1979) are viewed as forms of structure while organizational learning is considered as a structuration process (Huysman, 1999). Third, structuration theory can address multiple levels of analysis (Jones, 1999), which makes it particularly useful to handle the diferent levels of analysis of the four perspectives (as we will discuss in subsequent sections).

In order to develop the structurational framework, we first conducted a review of key literature related to the four perspectives (i.e., organizational learning, culture, politics, and institutional theory). We analyzed the concepts consistently highlighted in these perspectives and derived a set of elements relevant to IT-related organizational change and associated learning. These elements were then categorized into structures, modalities, and actions according to structuration theory (see Table 1). The identification and categorization of the elements are described in the subsequent sections.

## 2.2. Organizational learning

Implementation of IT such as in eGovernment projects requires organizational learning to achieve anticipated change benefits (Mahler, 1997; Robey and Sahay, 1996). The organizational learning perspective considers organizations as cognitive entities capable of observing their own actions, experimenting to discover the efects of alternative actions, and modifying their actions to improve performance (Fiol and Lyles, 1985). Organizations are seen as learning through processes that create new knowledge or modify existing knowledge. Particularly, there is a constant tension between exploration (that captures risk taking, experimentation, discovery, and innovation) and exploitation (that captures refinement, eficiency, selection, and execution) (March, 1991). Correspondingly, previous research has highlighted two types of organizational learning, i.e., first-order and second-order. First-order learning refers to incremental change in routines within the existing schema and is related to exploitation. Second-order learning involves the search for new routines and schemas that is related to exploration (Argyris and Schon, 1978). Routines serve as repositories of an organization’s rules, procedures, ideologies, culture (that includes assumptions, values, artifacts, and symbols), strategies, paradigms, systems, technologies, and conventions that define the way in which the organization undertakes activities (Levitt and March, 1988). Schemas are mental maps that help the organization assimilate, process, and interpret information (Newman, 2000).

Organizational learning is a multilevel phenomenon (Levitt and March, 1988) occurring through individuals who are active learning agents. However, organizations (comprised of individuals) develop and maintain learning systems that not only influence their members but are also transmitted to others through organizational histories (Lawrence and Dyer, 1983). This highlights the notion of organizational memory, which refers to ‘‘stored information from an organization’s history that can be brought to bear on present decisions” (Walsh and Ungson, 1991, p. 61). Organizational memory is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it provides the stock of knowledge that guides organization members’ actions. On the other hand, it may become a competency trap that prevents organizations from adapting to environmental changes (Miller, 1993). The ability of an organization to improve is thus linked to its capacity to revise organizational memory. From the perspective of structuration theory, organizational learning is a structuration process in which organization members collectively draw on the modalities of schema and routines to enact refined (first-order learning) or new (second-order learning) rules and resources that guide the organization’s decisions (i.e., structure of organiza tional memory) (see Table 1).

## 2.3. Organizational culture

Organizational culture includes the basic values and assumptions that unconsciously guide members behavior (Schein, 1985), and may thus afect how organizations learn when faced with uncertainties (Mahler, 1997). Culture manifests itself through the fundamental elements of artifacts, values, assumptions, and symbols (Hatch, 1993; Schein, 1990). Artifacts and symbols are also referred to as signifiers of culture (Trice and Beyer, 1993) and include such observable objects as icons, rituals, stories, myths, ofice layout, and decorative displays (Mahler, 1997). Values and assumptions refer to the beliefs, philosophies, norms, and justifications that organization actors collectively hold about the meaning of the organization and their work (Mahler, 1997). From a structurational perspective, as organization members socialize shared meanings with each other and collectively make sense of events in the organization over time (Mahler, 1997), organizational culture is produced and reproduced through modalities of artifacts, values, assumptions, and symbols (see Table 1).

## 2.4. Organizational politics

Previous research has highlighted the need to consider organizational politics in investigating learning associated with IT implementation (e.g., Robey et al., 2002). The perspective of organizational politics revolves around the notion of power (Sillince and Mouakket, 1997) and power relations, in which power entails in relationships between two or more actors (Hall, 1999). Power has been known to carry a diverse array of meanings (Jasperson et al., 2002). It has been seen as deriving from authority and resources (e.g., information and expertise); as involving conscious negotiation based on control of resources; as socially constructed; and as an outgrowth of social structures e.g., class and gender (Bradshaw-Camball and Murray, 1991).

Incorporating the diferent lenses of power, previous research has identified three distinct modes in which power operates i.e., episodic, systemic, and social integration (Lawrence et al., 2001; Silva and Backhouse, 2003). The episodic mode of power involves the strategic acts by self-interested actors to make others follow a course of action normally not followed, made possible by formal authority and access to scarce resources such as expertise. Systemic mode of power allows one to generate desired outcomes via the use of routines, ongoing practices of the organization, e.g., use of information systems (IS) to discipline employees in accordance with organizational objectives. Social integration mode of power provides the ability for one to influence the behavior of others through the use of rules and norms that govern meaning and membership in organizations. Both the systemic and social integration modes of power may form the basis for the exertion of episodic power by providing the necessary formal and informal conditions for organizational compliance (Silva and Backhouse, 2003). Applying the logic of structuration theory, organizational actors’ use of the diferent modes of power (i.e., episodic, systemic, social integration) draws on the corresponding modalities (e.g., formal authority, scarce resources, discipline, rules and norms of meaning and membership) that alter or reinforce how organizational actors inter-relate to each other in terms of power (i.e., structure of power relations) (see Table 1).

## 2.5. Institutional theory

Institutional theory focuses on the institutional environment surrounding an organization. The theory posits that organizations are driven to incorporate the prevailing rules, values, practices, and logics in the institutional environment in order to increase their legitimacy and survival prospects (Meyer and Rowan, 1977). Previous studies have identified three fundamental dimensions of the institutional environment i.e., regulative, normative, and cognitive (Scott, 1995). The regulative dimension refers to rules that organizations need to comply with to avoid formal or informal sanctions. The normative dimension is related to values and norms that prescribe appropriate patterns of behavior. The cognitive dimension includes institutionalized practices or taken for granted behavioral patterns that express how something is viewed as given. The three dimensions respectively give rise to three distinctive types of isomorphic pressures i.e., coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). Coercive pressure refers to pressure exerted on an organization by others upon whom they are dependent. Normative pressure is associated with professionalization and results from the collective struggle of members of an occupation to define the legitimate conditions and methods of their work. Last, mimetic pressure results from organizations’ standard responses to uncertainty, such as adopting prevalent practices in the industry (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983).

While the traditional emphasis of institutional theory has been on the need of organizations to conform to the institutional environment, there is another perspective highlighting the possibility that organizations compromise and manipulate their institutional environment (Oliver, 1991), i.e., they seek to actively shape their institutional environment. In a structurational sense, organizations may act to conform or attempt to shape the institutional arrangement (i.e., structure of institutional environment) through accepting or manipulating institutionalized rules, values/norms, practices, logics, and the related pressures in the environment (see Table 1).

At this point, it should be noted that there are common elements across diferent perspectives. Particularly, organizational values, assumptions, artifacts, and symbols highlighted in the organizational culture perspective are also included in the notion of routines in organizational learning (Levitt and March, 1988). Routines as repositories of organizational learning may also encompass rules, values, and practices from the normative and cognitive dimensions of the institutional environment (Scott, 1995). Also, rules and norms appear as modalities in the organizational politics perspective. As a result, such elements were categorized into more than one theoretical perspective in our framework (see Table 1). The interweaving of certain elements across the theoretical perspectives further underlines their inter-relationships, which we shall attempt to explicate next.

## 3. A structurational framework of organizational learning

Using the structures, modalities, and actions in Table 1, we explore the inter-relationships among the four perspectives and develop a framework of IT-related organizational change and associated learning. The identification of inter-relationships among the perspectives (Table 1) was informed by extant literature. We first describe the links from the institutional environment to internal organizational elements of memory, culture, and power. These internal organizational elements may in turn implicate organizational learning. We then describe how the occurrence of organizational learning may lead to changes in the internal organizational elements and also implicate the institutional environment. Fig. 1 shows the resultant framework that summarizes the inter-relationships among the elements of the four theoretical perspectives.

## 3.1. Influence of the institutional environment on internal organizational elements

An organization’s institutional environment influences its organizational memory, culture, and power relations. Organizational memory contains routines and schema for thought and action (Walsh and Ungson, 1991) that may include institutionalized practices and values/norms in an industry (R1a). As an organization becomes more isomorphic to institutions in the environment, its organizational memory tends to incorporate more institutionalized practices and values/norms. For instance, in the highly institutionalized legal industry (Scott, 1987), law firms share substantial stocks of knowledge in terms of the industry’s standard practices and procedures for case handling. The buildup of such organizational memory may be facilitated by the implementation of IT for knowledge sharing among these firms (Gottschalk, 2000).

Organizational culture serves as a carrier of institutions (Scott, 1995). Institutions, as systems of legitimate rules, are transported through cultural values to organizational members. Consistent with the observation of culture as transporting institutions, organizational culture has been found to ‘‘imprint” an organization’s early environmental conditions (Stinchcombe, 1965). Hence, organizational culture may be reflective of the organization’s institutional environment (R1b). For instance, public organizations typically have a bureaucratic organizational culture that is reflective of the hierarchical institutional environment (Bozeman, 2000). This culture may in part be induced by IT systems for reporting and approval, to ensure control of employees.

The institutional environment may also influence power relations in an organization. Fincham (1992) notes that power is derived from institutional structures that mediate power relations. Specifically, the existence of institutionalized logics of meaning for organizational interactions (e.g., hierarchies) may facilitate power holders’ eforts to ensure compliance from organization members (Ranson et al., 1980, p. 8) (R1c). Standardized IT used in an industry, such as a system implemented across public organizations that reinforces the prevalent institutionalized logics for interactions (e.g., how subordinates should report to supervisors), may serve to stabilize power relations in these organizations.

## 3.2. Influence of internal organizational elements on organizational learning

Shifting our focus to within an organization, existing organizational memory may facilitate or inhibit learning required for IT-related organizational change. Organizations with established memory face a dificult task when incorporating change amidst continuity (Bartunek, 1993). March (1991) observed that organizational memory can reinforce a first-order learning style that maintains status quo, instead of second-order learning that brings about transformative change (Argyris and Schon, 1978) (R2a). IT may play a salient role in this aspect. As organization members interact with an existing IT over time, a technology-in-practice of routines associated with its use may inhibit members from adopting diferent processes introduced by a new IT. For instance, Orlikowski (1992) noted that standardized work processes stored in organizational memory with the help of CASE tools created a ‘‘trained incapacity” for system developers to explore other ways to perform systems consulting, thus inhibiting second-order learning.

Organizational culture and learning are also closely related. Learning can be considered a cultural artifact, which takes place through the interaction and socialization of shared meanings among individuals (Easterby-Smith and Araujo, 2000). Thus, elements of organizational culture may influence the direction of organizational learning. For instance, organizational members may uphold a culture with the shared value ‘‘we can always do better” and the assumption ‘‘change is always good” that may facilitate second-order learning (Sashkin, 1993). Such a culture that encourages innovative ideas and openly rewards such eforts may be cultivated or reinforced by an IT (e.g., through disseminating information about the award recipients to all employees). In general, organizational culture can be seen as defining the context for organizational learning (Sørensen, 2002) (R2b).

Organizational learning has been acknowledged as a fundamentally political process (Blackler and McDonald, 2000). Power and power relations may provide the social energy that fuels the processes of organizational learning (Lawrence et al., 2005). For instance, organization leaders may use IT to restrict the range of choices available to organization members, thus guiding them in the direction of intended organizational learning (applying systemic mode of power) (Lawrence et al., 2005). Hence, power relations in the organization can have an influence on organizational learning (R2c).

## 3.3. Implications of organizational learning on internal organizational elements

Organizational learning in turn may result in incremental change such as adjustments in procedures (i.e., refinement of organizational memory) (Dutton and Dukerich, 1991), or transformative change such as the search for new routines (i.e., substantial revision of organizational memory) (Argyris and Schon, 1978) (R3a). As organization members learn to use a new IT, a technology-in-practice associated with the processes introduced by the new system emerges. This collective technology-in-practice held by members may gradually be amalgamated into the organizational memory and become a part of the routines for performing organizational tasks.

The occurrence of organizational learning may also influence organizational culture. Organizations learn from experience about which parts of their existing stock of knowledge work for the organization as a whole (Schein, 1990). Specifically, organizational learning may involve adjustment of existing values in the organizational culture, or a fundamental change of existing culture marked by a radical change of core values or basic assumptions induced by the IT (R3b). For instance, a new IT for performance evaluation that requires employees to report their output in concrete figures (e.g., processed 10% more customer complaints compared to the previous year) may lead to the emergence of a result-oriented technology-in-practice. This may eventually change the values of employees related to work performance issues.

Last, organizational learning influences power relations by ascending those who ride on the bandwagon of organizational learning to greater power. For instance, organization members who have learnt to manage new contingencies resulting from the use of a new IT may ascend to power, such as having greater influence in the organization (Stinchcombe, 1990) (R3c).

## 3.4. From organizational learning to the institutional environment and vice versa

Successful organizational learning induced by a new IT may in turn implicate the institutional environment (R4a). Greenwood and Hinings (1988) note that an organization achieving competitive success through adopting a new organizational form would produce pressures on other organizations to adopt the same organizational form. Similarly, institutional pressures from early adopters of an IT to late adopters may be in the form of mimetic pressure (just follow suit), normative pressure (since many others have already implemented the IT), or coercive pressure (forced by tightly-aligned partners to adopt the IT) (Teo et al., 2003). Hence, for the late adopter organizations, there may be a salient direct link from the institutional environment to organizational learning. This is due to the newly formed institutional pressures from early adopter organizations to implement an IT<sup>2</sup> (R5). In conforming to institutional pressures, late adopter organizations may model after the structures of organizational memory (e.g., work practices introduced with the IT) (R4b–R5), organizational culture (e.g., cultural values induced via the use of the IT) (R4c–R5), or power relations (e.g., supervisor-subordinate interaction patterns instituted by the IT) (R4d–R5) of the early adopter that emerged from its IT-related organizational learning.

In summary, we have developed a framework (see Fig. 1) to show how elements from the four perspectives may inter-relate to influence organizational learning during new IT implementation. The application of the proposed framework may sensitize us to the important elements to pay attention to, and enable us to elicit insights from the organizational learning experience associated with an eGovernment initiative in a more systematic manner.

## 4. Research methodology

We conducted an in-depth case study of an eGovernment initiative at the National Library Board (NLB), the library authority of Singapore. According to Yin (2003), the case study strategy is most suitable when the research involves ‘why’ or ‘how’ questions. Our objectives were to uncover in detail, why organizational learning associated with eGovernment initiative was successful in NLB, and how the diferent organizational elements (politics, culture, memory, and institutions) inter-related to afect organizational learning and vice versa. The case study method also allows examination of the phenomenon in a natural setting and helps in understanding its nature and complexity (Benbasat et al., 1987).

This research was designed as a positivist single case study. Although a single case study has limited generalizability, such an approach is appropriate when the case is unique or revelatory, and is useful in the initial stages of research (Yin, 2003). The NLB presents a unique and revelatory case because it is among the first public organizations in Singapore to successfully<sup>3</sup> implement a fully integrated IS to streamline its internal operations and to attain substantial organizational learning through its eGovernment eforts. The system under study is called the Human Resource and Finance Integrated System (HRFIS). Leveraging on the HRFIS and other front-end systems such as its digital library, NLB has transformed from a public library system that lagged behind other developed countries to an international leader within a period of less than ten years.

Additionally, our case study is explanatory (Dube´ and Pare´, 2003) in that it attempts to validate the theoretical framework of Fig. 1. While testing rival theories is appropriate for explanatory case studies (Dube´ and Pare´, 2003, p. 609), our study adopted a diferent approach. Instead of considering the diferent perspectives (i.e., organizational politics, culture, and institutional theory) as rival theories to explain IT-related organizational learning, we attempted to verify their inter-relationships as proposed in the framework. We believe this approach ofers value by contributing to a more holistic understanding of IT-related organizational learning.

## 4.1. Data collection

The data collection primarily involved site visits and multiple interviews with the various stakeholders involved in the HRFIS implementation. We first conducted a preliminary interview with a top management member from NLB to obtain an initial understanding of the HRFIS project. This allowed us to refine the focus of our study as well as expedite the identification of key stakeholders involved in the project. We then conducted a series of in-depth interviews with these stakeholders from diferent levels of NLB that include top management, middle-level management, and operational level employees. Each interview was conducted by at least two researchers and lasted from around 30 minutes to 3 hours. Additionally, we also analyzed existing publications that provided information about the background of NLB, its corporate values and policies, and eGovernment initiatives. These secondary sources of data included archives, press releases, annual reports, as well as other NLB publications. Table 2 provides information about the interviewees, as well as the other sources of data used. Fig. A1 in Appendix A shows the positions of the interviewees in the organizational chart.

Appropriate interview guides were developed based on the framework in Fig. 1. We first reviewed existing literature to search for interview questions to tap on the concepts in Table 1. In cases where these questions were not available, we developed the corresponding interview questions based on the description and dimensions of the concepts. We employed these questions with adjustments based on the interviewee’s nature of involvement in the HRFIS implementation, level of responsibility (managerial versus operational), and inputs from document analysis and previous interviews. The interview transcripts were reviewed for consistency with secondary sources of data, so that the findings could be triangulated for greater reliability (Dube´ and Pare´, 2003; Yin, 2003). The interview guides for management and users are included in Appendix A.

## 4.2. Case description

## 4.2.1. Background of NLB and genesis of HRFIS

In an increasingly knowledge-based economy, the Singapore government recognized that public libraries as ‘‘treasuries of information and knowledge” could play an important role to ensure Singapore’s prosperity. However, public libraries in the early 1990s ofered limited collections of books in poor condition. Services at the libraries were ineficient as borrowing and returning of books could take hours (Hallowell et al., 2001). A survey conducted in 1992 indicated public dissatisfaction with the comprehensiveness and accessibility of public library collections, services, and facilities. Compared to libraries in developed countries such as Scotland, Singapore’s libraries also lagged behind in terms of operational outputs e.g., number of people served per library.

In response to the feedback, a Library 2000 (L2000) committee was set up by the government to review and recommend improvements in public library services for the next 10 years. As a result of the L2000 report, NLB was established in 1995 to revamp the public library system with the mission to expand the ‘‘learning capacity of the nation” and achieve ‘‘a world class library system that is convenient, accessible, and useful” to citizens and organizations. As a statutory board, NLB was given more flexibility and discretion than regular government organizations in its human resource and finance matters, e.g., in allowing for performance and marketbased salary schemes (Hallowell et al., 2001).

First, the L2000 objective was to use technology to provide faster and better services to library visitors through improving the eficiency of NLB’s internal operations. This was even more essential to cope with the expected expansion in staf count and workload at NLB. Second, there was a call by government to public agencies to implement standardized IS in line with Singapore’s strategic plans to achieve integrated eGovernment.

## 4.2.2. Description of HRFIS

HRFIS is an integrated, backend enterprise-wide information system to support the human resource (HR), finance, and other administrative functions of NLB. The system comprises a highly customized enterprise system that is linked to a collaborative system<sup>4</sup>. Executives from finance, HR, and administrative units constitute the power users (approximately 5% of NLB’s 900 staf strength), who use the system for their specialized duties (e.g., recruitment). Apart from the power users, all employees of NLB (from library attendants to management staf in the corporate ofice) use HRFIS for their leave applications, claims and benefits, training needs, and work performance analysis.

To design the system for greater usability, intensive user acceptance tests were carried out before the introduction of each new HRFIS module. During the tests, users were asked to perform certain tasks using the system without any instructions given. Designers then observed the problems faced by the users under such circumstances and evaluated what needed to be done to improve the usability of the module. To further ensure that the system was designed according to users’ needs, owners of afected work processes were engaged in Joint Application Design sessions to specify their requirements to the system consultants. The first phase of HRFIS was designed to automate existing HR and finance operations at NLB while the second phase was intended to improve upon HR development and evaluation.

## 4.2.3. Phase 1: Improving eficiency through HRFIS

The first few modules of HRFIS rolled out consisted of leave, claims and benefits application modules. These modules were designed to cut down approval time for the corresponding HR-related applications. Instead of manually filling up paper application forms and submitting them to the corresponding approving oficer, employees now fill up and submit the forms electronically. Once the form is submitted, an email alert is sent to the relevant oficer for approval. If the oficer is not around, a corresponding notice is sent to the covering oficer instead. This allows the supervisor or covering oficer to quickly approve or reject the application, thereby reducing the time needed for application processing from one or two weeks to a day or two. For applications that involve money such as no pay leave, the system will automatically trigger a pay deduction to the payroll module.

In addition to form submission, employees can also perform a number of self-checks such as the amount of leave available, and the status of their leave or claim application. Prior to HRFIS, it was easy for paper forms to be misplaced. HR staf spent large amounts of time handling call requests from employees to check for their leave applications status, verify their amount of leave available, and pay out petty cash claims. With HRFIS, such mundane work could be done by the employees themselves. This freed HR staf for more value-added tasks, such as improving employees’ welfare and benefits. Messy cash payouts were eliminated as the system was integrated with the payroll module that automatically credited the payment of such claims to employees monthly salary.

Implementation of HRFIS in this phase mainly focused on automation to improve eficiency. However, the significance of this phase was by no means small. By making all levels of employees perform activities that they do regularly (i.e., leave, claims and benefits applications) through the system, it ensured that every employee, including those who did not have IT knowledge previously, now needed to learn how to use a computer and had access to one. The introduction of these modules thus served to prepare employees for complex future modules designed to achieve more substantial learning at NLB.

## 4.2.4. Phase 2: Human resource development and evaluation through HRFIS

Following the rollout of modules for eficiency improvement, subsequent modules of HRFIS were designed to cater to the People Developer process. People Developer is a set of quality standards related to HR development in Singapore. This three-year certification scheme gives recognition to organizations that bring out the best in their people for better business results. The standard ofers organizations a systematic process to review their people practices, develop staf, and improve training efectiveness. Two modules, the Performance Management Programme (PMP) and Training Needs Analysis (TNA), were introduced to achieve these objectives.

The PMP module is an appraisal system aimed to provide more objective assessment of employees’ performance and contribution. It also allows management to monitor employees’ performance more efectively. Prior to PMP, appraisal was merely an end of year exercise where employees filled in a paper form to specify work done. In contrast, the PMP adopts a more comprehensive approach by allowing employees to detail their achievements with reference to specific Key Result Areas (KRAs) and NLB’s competencies for staf members. KRAs refer to work areas in which individual employees are expected to achieve results. These KRAs are derived from corporate goals and objectives, and are cascaded to the library branches and then to the individual employees. For example, a KRA of NLB could be collection development, in which certain targets are set to improve the libraries’ collection (e.g., certain percentage of books requested by customers should be found in the collection). This would be cascaded to library branches that provide diferent book collections (e.g., each branch has to increase the range of books in its collection by a certain percentage). Within each branch, individual employees are then expected to perform collection analysis of their branch to identify the gaps that must be bridged in order to achieve the branch’s objective. NLB’s competencies for staf describe the desired set of employees’ behaviors that demonstrate NLB’s core values, such as ‘‘com mitment to customer service”.

At the beginning of each fiscal year, employees need to plan their entire year’s work objectives and input this plan into HRFIS via the PMP module. They should also indicate any training courses that they think will be helpful to attain their work objectives in order to facilitate corporate budget allocation for training. Employees first discuss the work plan with their supervisors. This is to ensure that their work objectives are in alignment with corporate objectives. Once finalized, the work plan is then routed by the system to the supervisor and counter-signing oficer for endorsement. About three months later, a one-on-one meeting session is held between the employee and the supervisor to review their progress, and changes can be made if necessary. At the end of the year, employees record their past year achievements with respect to the work plan into the system. Based on this information, employees receive due recognition in terms of performance bonuses, salary increments, or promotions.

The TNA module aims to facilitate the incorporation of best practices into NLB via external training courses for employees. The module is closely related to the PMP module. Once employees finalize their work plan, they need to indicate their training needs according to the plan during the annual TNA exercise. The TNA module allows employees to select and apply for courses that they would need in order to attain the necessary proficiency and capabilities for their work plan. For instance, employees could indicate their intention to obtain training in marketing and negotiating skills that may be useful for conducting outreach programs (e.g., to promote the take-up of library membership). In addition, supervisors may nominate their subordinates to attend training courses considered necessary for their work. After the training, employees are required to fill in a post course evaluation via the TNA module to comment on the usefulness of the courses. Half a year later, they are also asked to cite various examples of how the training has helped them in their work. A pop-up reminder will be sent to employees’ computer screen if they have not completed the feedback.

## 4.3. Data analysis

Yin (2003) recommends the use of a general analytic strategy to define priorities for what to analyze and why. In this study, we employ the structurational framework in Fig. 1 to guide both the data collection and data analysis. The framework identifies the organizational elements to examine and their inter-relationships. A case study database as suggested by Yin (2003) was created to organize the data for analysis. This consisted of interview transcripts, secondary sources of data, as well as thematic analysis reports.

Thematic analysis was employed to categorize the data from the transcripts into the structures, modalities, and actions in the framework. Important quotes from the transcripts were noted and used where appropriate in the thematic analysis process. This was done in order to ‘‘bring in the voice of participants in the study” (Creswell, 1998, p. 170) such that ‘‘an external observer can reach an independent judgment regarding the merits of the analysis” (Dube´ and Pare´, 2003, p. 625). Table A1 in Appendix A displays samples of categories and corresponding evidence from the thematic analysis. Based on the thematic analysis, we then tried to make connections between the categories of concepts. Some examples of the resulting logical chain of evidence are presented in Table A2 in Appendix A. The logical chain of evidence permitted an explanation-building analytic strategy (Yin, 2003). Additionally, several in-depth discussions on the analysis of the case were carried out among the researchers involved in the interviews. This helped to produce a more balanced view in the analysis. The case analysis results are presented by HRFIS phase and the inter-relationships of organizational elements in that phase.

## 4.3.1. Case findings based on the structurational framework

4.3.1.1. Influence of institutional environment and internal elements on learning (Phase 1). From institutional environment to organizational memory and organizational learning. The initial stage of HRFIS implementation (i.e., its introduction and the roll-out of the first few modules) faced some resistance from the employees. Resistance before and during the use of new IT systems is a major reason for their failure in organizations. As noted by Lapointe and Rivard (2005), initial forms of apathy and passive resistance such as indiference, lack of interest, and complaints could develop into more hostile forms of resistance, such as active resistive behaviors to stop system use. When we consider the existing organizational memory at the start of the HRFIS project, there was considerable potential for such resistance to occur. Particularly, there was a major gap between the knowledge required by the HRFIS and existing knowledge in NLB that could deter employees’ adoption of the system (Huysman et al., 1994). On hearing that NLB was going to be computerized, there was stress among certain employees, especially the operational level staf members such as library oficers and attendants, who had little or no knowledge of IT. As one library oficer noted, ‘‘we dared not use the computer. . . I would say it’s a phobia . . . we prefer to type using a typewriter, even though there is a computer.” Some people expressed their passive resistance by ‘‘speaking resentfully” of the system (Markus, 1983): ‘‘We were not happy with [HRFIS] because we were not used to computers .”, ‘‘. . . Actually we made a lot of noise.” There was a lack of institutionalized practices of computer usage in the library fraternity as well as among public organizations in Singapore at the time. Thus, the organizational memory contained traditional routines and schemas for library operations (R1a), which were manual in nature. This lack of organizational memory on the use of IT could have led to more active resistance among employees towards the HRFIS and potentially prohibited organizational learning associated with the system (R2a).

From institutional environment to power relations and organizational learning. Foucault (1980) notes that resistances are formed right at the point where relations of power are exercised. In our case, certain factors might have prevented the escalation of employees’ resistance into more aggressive forms. A key factor could be the institutional forces at work that facilitated the exertion of power by the top management of NLB to implement the HRFIS. When NLB was founded as a statutory board, there was a strong thrust from the government to exploit IT to transform the public sector with the launch of the national IT2000 plan (Soh et al., 1993) and eGovernment strategic plans. In line with these plans, the L2000 Committee specified IT as one of the pillars for the nation’s library transformation. In addition, the chief executive appointed to lead the newly formed NLB had a strong IT background having previously served at the National Computer Board (NCB)<sup>5</sup>. He also brought with him five executives from NCB to help him transform NLB. This alignment of the top management’s background with forces from the institutional environment (R1c) may have provided the logics of meaning that granted them power to push intended organizational learning (R2c). A statement from a manager demonstrated this: ‘‘When we rolled out the system, we only knew that the management’s intention was to computerize. . .At that time, I was not aware of the benefits...We just followed the top management’s objec tive to do integration [via HRFIS].”

To promote employees’ buy-in of the system, the management conducted large-scale communication sessions that involved all levels of employees. During these sessions, IT was communicated as a tool that can benefit employees, and a means through which employees can learn and develop themselves. For instance, employees were told that the HRFIS use would be ‘‘enter once, check once, and multiple usage”, which freed them from repetitive work to perform more value-added tasks. Through the sessions, the management provided assurance to employees that there would be no lay-ofs with the implementation of the system, and that the system was there not to replace them but to help them. These positive meanings of the HRFIS communicated to employees also helped clear the obstacles and paved the way for employees’ learning of the system.

From institutional environment to organizational culture and organizational learning. The organizational cul ture of NLB prior to HRFIS implementation might have also played a part in lessening employees’ resistance to the system. Although one would expect the existing organizational culture to resist change, the opposite seems to be true for NLB. Since NLB was formed with the mission to transform public library services with IT, an organizational culture that was receptive to IT and learning was being imprinted. A middle-level manager noted, ‘‘When we turned into a statutory board, learning became a part of our core values that [was] instituted in all of us. . .. When a lot of organizations were moving into IT in Singapore, our people accepted it better.” This observation underscores the need to examine the institutional environment in which an organization is founded, since an imprinted organizational culture that is highly reflective of the environment (R1b) may influence learning associated with IT implementation (R2b). As commented by an employee: ‘‘The culture is one of learning so we open ourselves to learn new things.”

4.3.1.2. Implications of learning on internal elements (Pre-Phase 2). From organizational learning to organizational memory. Due to the lack of organizational memory with requisite IT knowledge for using HRFIS, NLB employees were sent for basic computer courses such as keyboarding and word processing. When a new HRFIS module was rolled out, a representative from each library branch was sent for training in using the module. The representatives were then responsible for training their colleagues in the same branch. Knowledge of using the system was also transferred on a one-to-one basis via ‘‘hand-holding” sessions, where employees with better knowledge of the system guided their peers who required help.

With the initial training and the need to use HRFIS to perform day-to-day activities such as leave, claims, and benefits applications, employees built up knowledge of using the system to make their work processes more eficient. As employees interacted with the HRFIS over time, a technology-in-practice that emphasized eficiency based on automation gradually emerged. The resulting organizational learning led to the refinement of existing routines to make them more eficient (R3a). This refinement in turn translated into time and cost savings. For instance, the approval time required for leave applications was reduced from one or two weeks to a day or two. Many tedious manual tasks were now eliminated. Hence, employees’ work emphasis shifted to ‘‘strategic planning” and ‘‘value adding”. As one of the employees noted, ‘‘Instead of doing all those mundane activities, actually I am doing something smarter.”

From organizational learning to organizational culture.The positive meanings of the HRFIS communicated to employees via the mass communication sessions were reafirmed with employees’ recurrent use of the system in their everyday work. As employees learnt to use the HRFIS and experienced its benefits, they became more comfortable with it. This positive experience of learning to use the HRFIS was shared among the employees themselves during the communication sessions that allowed employees from diferent branches to meet and socialize. The HRFIS now became an integral part of employees’ daily work. Interpretive schemes such as ‘‘IT is good” and an evolving understanding of the values of HRFIS were enacted (R3b). The manager who was unaware of how IT integration would help her work in the beginning now remarked ‘‘. . . people [like me] who [had a] phobia of computers can now understand and appreciate them. . .I can even do a presentation on some of the IT stuf.”

From organizational learning to power relations. With HRFIS successfully assimilated into the work processes of employees and proven to be beneficial to the users (i.e., the enactment of technology-in-practice associated with positive meanings of IT), the top management who steered the system implementation became highly regarded by employees. They were considered as people who have the ‘‘foresight” to lead NLB. As one manager recounted, ‘‘The back-end was an area that he (the chief executive) wanted to improve . . . we did not have the foresight. . . We never thought so much of integration. All these ideas came from the top management.” The management’s position was thus reinforced in terms of power relations (R3c).

4.3.1.3. Influence of internal elements on learning (Phase 2). From power relations to organizational learning. The next phase of HRFIS modules that were introduced for human resource development i.e., the PMP (People Management Programme) and TNA (Training Needs Analysis) modules, benefited from the favourable conditions resulting from the previous phase. The implementation of these modules did not meet much resistance from employees, except that there were some initial complaints about the sluggishness of the system. With their power position stabilized in the previous phase, the top management was ready to implement these new modules that aimed to stimulate organizational learning at a deeper level (R2c). Such learning involved the incorporation of best practices that employees received from external training into NLB’s organizational memory. The assistant director of the Infocomm department noted: ‘‘the key point is not about PMP, it’s not about training, it’s about how you actually bring in best practices, and how you use the system as an enabler for bringing in those best practices.” Systemic mode of power was exercised via the PMP and TNA modules to achieve this goal. Specifically, the TNA module ofers employees’ choices of training courses that are considered most useful in helping them in their work and to attain organizational goals. The post training review exercise provides management with a simple control mechanism to keep track of the acquisition and application of skills learnt by employees. The PMP module allows for efective monitoring of employee’s progress towards achieving personal targets and competencies aligned with desired corporate goals. Consequently, a technology-in-practice that centred on the learning of best practices consistent with corporate goals was enacted as employees used the two modules (PMP and TNA). This further promoted organization members collective learning via the HRFIS.

From organizational memory to organizational learning. As a result of phase 1, a set of rules and resources (structure of organizational memory) related to system use that structured employees’ work around the themes of eficiency was enacted. Such technology-in-practice emphasized value-added work practices and viewed IT as beneficial to personal productivity. The technology-in-practice further encouraged system use, which in turn enhanced employee’s knowledge of HRFIS. Capitalizing on employee’s familiarity with the HRFIS, the management took measures to ensure that the interface of later HRFIS modules was consistent with the previous ones. This helped to facilitate employees’ learning of the new modules (R2a).

From organizational culture to organizational learning. The technology-in-practice that was associated with positive meanings of IT (e.g., IT as beneficial to personal productivity) also induced an organizational culture that embraced IT. This observation echoes Boersma and Kingma’s (2005) finding that technology and organizational culture are in a mutually shaping relationship. With a mindset change among employees, IT was no longer seen as a threat to their job, but a tool that could help them. As an employee noted, ‘‘IT itself is no more fearful to people . . . Right from the very junior staf to the old, we can appreciate IT better.”, ‘‘we are more open to changes (related to the HRFIS system) because we know that it is for our benefit. If we don’t accept the changes we have to do a lot of manual work.” Such favorable views of IT held by employees facilitated subsequent organizational learning (R2b)

4.3.1.4. Influence of learning on internal elements and institutional environment (Phase 2). From organizational learning to organizational memory (R3a). At this stage, the organizational learning that occurred in phase 2 efected more changes on other organizational elements than before. The organizational memory was now enriched with new knowledge and skills acquired by employees through training (R3a). Such knowledge and skills acquired by employees were shared via a number of IT-related avenues that facilitated group interaction and communication (Choe, 2004), e.g., Team Room (a central platform in each library branch for employees to share best practices) and ‘‘Ask Stupid Question Forum” (where employees across NLB seek and exchange information about their work). In addition, the new performance evaluation scheme via the PMP module served to redefine the performance appraisal process in the organizational memory to become more objective, comprehensive, and rewarding (R3a).

From organizational learning to organizational culture. Using the PMP and TNA modules to promote organizational learning in turn strengthened the culture of learning that NLB management wanted to inculcate (R3b). However, with the PMP that cascaded organizational objectives to every level of staf in the form of

KRAs and linked employees’ achievement of these KRAs with their salary and rewards, the culture at NLB also became more result-oriented in nature (R3b).

From organizational learning to power relations. Some employees ascended to potentially more strategic positions with the implementation of the HRFIS (i.e., change in power position relative to others) (R3c). They gained knowledge in IT use in addition to their original scope of work and became more valuable for the organization. As one employee noted, ‘‘I have created more value for my skills through my experience [related to HRFIS].”

From organizational learning to the institutional environment and vice versa. Last, the organizational learning at NLB associated with the HRFIS implementation implicated the institutional environment, specifically other public organizations in Singapore. The success of the HRFIS in helping NLB achieve intended organizational learning has prompted government authorities to request NLB to share its experience and expertise towards implementing similar systems in other statutory boards (R4a). For these public organizations, their eGovernment learning eforts may thus be influenced by the newly formed institutional pressures from NLB (R5). As the influence of organizational learning on the institutional environment just started to emerge when this study was completed<sup>6</sup>, we have yet to observe the implications of the revised organization memory, culture, and power relations of NLB on the institutional environment (R4b, R4c, and R4d).

## 5. Discussion and implications

This study started by identifying the elements of four theoretical perspectives (organizational learning, culture, politics, and institutional theory) that are relevant to IT-related change. Informed by existing literature and using structuration theory as an organizing device, the inter-relationships among the elements were derived in order to develop a research framework for the investigation of IT-related organizational learning. The resulting framework was then validated through a case study of an eGovernment initiative. The findings provide the following implications for research and practice.

## 5.1. Implications for research

The study contributes to research in several ways. First and foremost, we explicate the linkages that exist among the four theoretical perspectives related to change. Past studies seldom employ the four perspectives together in a coherent manner to investigate change and learning induced by IT. This approach thus allows for a more holistic understanding of the phenomenon. In particular, we compare our framework to the two original structurational models in IS (Orlikowski and Robey, 1991 and in general (Giddens, 1979) in order to indicate our contribution.

Structuration theory itself delineates three fundamental dimensions of structure i.e., signification, domination, and legitimation (Giddens, 1979). Parallels can be drawn between the three dimensions of structure and the theoretical perspectives in our study. In particular, organizational culture reflects the structure of signification, organizational politics corresponds to the structure of domination, and the institutional environment is similar to the structure of legitimation. However, while Giddens (1979) acknowledged that the separation between the three dimensions of structure mainly serves analytical purposes and that inextricable links exist between them, such links were not explicated as in our framework.

Similar is the case for Orlikowski and Robey’s (1991) framework where the inter-relationships between the four theoretical perspectives are not investigated. By explicating the inter-relationships between these elements, additional insights may be uncovered. For instance, using the framework in Fig. 1, we found that the institutional environment provides the logics of power relations that facilitated the new management of NLB to direct IT-related learning, despite unfavorable conditions e.g., the lack of IT knowledge among its employees. Such insights may have been omitted if these elements were not inter-related.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

In terms of practical implications, the framework may provide insights to practitioners on how organizational learning associated with eGovernment projects can be achieved. By knowing the inter-relationships among the diferent organizational elements, a more comprehensive strategy to guide intended organizational learning might be devised. Such an intervention strategy could start with an assessment of the existing institutional environment, organizational memory, culture, and power relations to evaluate their potential impact on organizational learning. This can be informed by knowledge from the framework about the influence of institutional environment on internal organizational elements, how internal organizational elements may influence learning essential to bring about intended change, and vice versa in a structurational sense. Further, by knowing the relevant modalities (in the form of interpretive schemes such as schema, facilities such as formal authority, and norms such as cultural assumptions), practitioners can better deploy the necessary resources to attain the desired action or structure related to diferent organizational elements towards achieving intended organizational learning.

More specifically, managers may learn from the measures employed by NLB to ensure the success of HRFIS implementation and associated learning. At the start of the HRFIS deployment, NLB management conducted massive communication sessions to communicate the benefits of the system to all employees. This was supplemented with the ‘‘no lay-ofs” assurance to alleviate employees’ fear. Employees from all sections of NLB were asked to attend the sessions. The aim was to also use the sessions as a venue for employees to share their positive experience of system use. In addition, ‘‘hand-holding” sessions deployed employees with better system knowledge to guide their peers. The dissemination of positive system use experience and knowledge through the employees themselves might be an efective way to enhance learning.

Managers may also consider a multi-phase implementation approach similar to HRFIS for complex IT implementations. In this case, the first phase served to prepare employees with the required IT knowledge for the second phase. The second phase then served to achieve deeper organizational learning through aligning employees’ performance appraisal and training with corporate goals. Deeper learning could be attained by sharing knowledge that employees acquired via training. Specifically, the post training review exercise required employees to provide feedback and cite examples of the usefulness of training received. Sharing of knowledge among employees was also encouraged through platforms such as Team Room and ‘‘Ask Stupid Question Forum”. These measures encouraged learning of the HRFIS by the employees themselves apart from management’s active interventions. These approaches may serve as guidance to other organizations that strive to achieve similar objectives.

## 5.3. Limitations and future work

Several limitations of this study need to be recognized. First, the use of a single case disallows comparison across cases that may help demonstrate the influence of variability in context (Dube´ and Pare´, 2003). However, the study presents a unique case with highly successful organizational learning associated with an eGovernment project that deserves in-depth examination in itself. Future research could nonetheless conduct case studies with guidance of the framework in other organizations. Particularly, cases of failed eGovernment eforts may be examined as these may unveil potentially rich insights that are diferent from the current study.

Second, the public organization examined in this case study operates in the context of Singapore. The relatively high power distance culture (Hofstede, 2001) of Singapore may mean that there is a higher tendency for employees to concede to management’s power. Further, the city state nature of Singapore may also lack the level of complexity faced by public organizations in larger countries, in terms of the requirements and layers of communication (e.g., national, state, and local) that need to be dealt with. Future work may consider validating the framework for eGovernment implementations in countries with larger, federated government structures and with diferent cultural orientations. Nonetheless, the case presents an epitomic gist of the events that unfold with the implementation of an eGovernment initiative and ofers insights without being overwhelmed by such complexities.

Additionally, a context where the technology has stabilized and matured among both the early adopter and late adopter organizations may be investigated to examine the efects of internal organizational elements (memory, culture, and power relations) on the institutional environment. Last, given the extensive literature on the four theoretical perspectives (organizational learning, politics, culture, and institutional theory), our coverage of the corresponding literature is by no means exhaustive. Consequently, certain structures, modalities, or actions related to these theoretical perspectives applied to IT-related change may have been excluded. Future research can build upon this work to investigate other aspects of these theoretical perspectives and validate the framework using other methodologies such as surveys.

## 6. Conclusion

This study constructed a structurational framework of IT-related organizational learning that takes into consideration the relevant theoretical perspectives of organizational culture, politics, and institutional theory. Guided and informed by the framework, we examined an eGovernment efort at a public organization in Singapore. Our study reveals the inter-relationships that exist among the diferent organizational elements in the event of IT implementation. Particularly, the inter-relationships among the diferent organizational elements may serve to explain why more hostile forms of resistance did not occur during the initial implementation of the new IS despite the gap between practices introduced by the system and existing ones (manual versus IT). They can also explain why there was little resistance in the later phase of HRFIS despite the more radical changes introduced by the new modules of the system (e.g., entirely new performance evaluation system).

The framework also shows how changes that occurred in the previous phase resulting from organizational learning may create favorable conditions for the implementation of changes in the subsequent phase. Further, it indicates how the institutional environment channels its influence on organizational learning through internal organizational elements and is in turn implicated by them. Overall, our work may contribute by providing a more holistic understanding of organizational learning associated with eGovernment projects, thus allowing for better IT-related change management strategies to be devised.
