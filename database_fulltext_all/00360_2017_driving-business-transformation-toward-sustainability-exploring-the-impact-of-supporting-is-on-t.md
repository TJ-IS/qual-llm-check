# Driving business transformation toward sustainability: exploring the impact of supporting IS on the performance contribution of eco-innovations

Andre Hanelt,\* Sebastian Busse<sup>†</sup> & Lutz M. Kolbe\*

\*University of Göttingen, Chair of Information Management, Göttingen, Germany, email: ahanelt@uni-goettingen.de; lkolbe@uni-goettingen.de, and <sup>†</sup>UNITY AG, Braunschweig, Germany, email: sebastian.busse@unity.de

Abstract. Information systems (IS) can foster business transformation toward sustainability on a large scale by supporting green technologies, thereby creating hybrid physical–digital solutions that are able to ful<sup>fi</sup>l organizational performance requirements and contribute to sustainable business practices. These supporting IS provide an alternative path to corporate sustainability rather than just contributing to the ‘greening’ of business practices by aiming to improve the organizational performance impact of environmental advantageous innovations, which in turn fos ters their adoption. Employing a multiple case study comprising eight companies that have implemented such eco-innovations (speci<sup>fi</sup>cally electric vehicles) in their business processes, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that the organizational performance contribution of eco-innovations is improved when complemented by supporting IS. This effect is achieved by (1) enhancing the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the business processes in which the eco-innovations are deployed, thus increasing eco-ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and (2) enabling new functionalities, processes and business models that help achieve organizational sustainability goals, thereby driving eco-effectiveness. With these two aspects, we add two functional affordances – technological <sup>fl</sup>exibility and digita eco-innovation – to the existing knowledge base of Green IS and point to an elaborated role of IS in sustainability transformation.

Keywords: eco-innovation, green technology, Green IS, IS impact, organizationa performance, sustainability transformation

## INTRODUCTION

While eco-innovations – new practices that have lower negative effects on the environment than their conventional counterparts do (Rennings, 2000; Pujari, 2006; Halila & Rundquist, 2011) – can help in complying with the institutional environment of the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Butler, 2011), they often suffer disadvantages in a business context when compared with established solutions. This may hold true in terms of increased costs, which negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of business processes (e.g. through the greater maintenance demand of a combined heat and power system or high purchase costs of photovoltaic cells) and thus hinder the adoption of these innovations (Del Rio Gonzalez. 2005). Furthermore, eco-innovations may be less effective than thei conventional counterparts, as exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed in the lower driving ranges of electric vehicles (EVs).

During recent years, the information systems (IS)<sup>1</sup> community has targeted the topic of environmental sustainability (e.g. Chen et al., 2008; Melville, 2010; Watson et al., 2010a; Elliot, 2011; Jenkin et al., 2011; Loeser et al., 2012; Seidel et al., 2013). Although substantial progress has been achieved in sustainable IS research, insights on the impact of Green IS are still lacking (see Malhotra et al., 2013). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, while prior research in this <sup>fi</sup>eld has investigated the potential of IS to contribute to greening regular business procedures, little is known about their potential to improve the organizational performance of new, alternative green business procedures, i.e. eco-innovations (Brooks et al., 2012; Nishant, 2012). This side of corporate sustainability must be explored further, as the advancements in and the increasing pervasiveness of digital technologies (Yoo, 2010) expand ‘the role and relevance of IT in any innovation’ (Nambisan, 2013, p. 216). Therefore, the perspective introduced under the theme of digital innovation (Yoo et al., 2010) – de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the carrying out of new combinations of digital and physical components to produce novel products’ (Yoo et al., 2010, p. 725) or practices (Fichman et al., 2014) – can be of particular value for research on sustainability and IS. Combining physical eco-innovations with the potential of IS spawns hybrid solutions that can better account for the peculiarities of the respective eco-innovation and adapt to the specific organizational circumstances. thus improving the organizational performance of the eco-innovation and opening the door for large-scale adoption.

Therefore, we focus on what we call ‘supporting IS’: IS that are not part of non-IS ecoinnovations (such as EVs) and do not represent a complete Green IS product or service per se. Instead, such IS focus on complementing and supporting the use of eco-innovations, thereby enhancing their impact on organizational performance. To begin exploring this phenomenon and illustrating its mechanisms, we concentrate on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c example of EVs because they are an interesting instance of eco-innovations that, compared with their conventional counterparts, are distinguished by environmental advantages on the one hand and ambiguous economic characteristics – higher purchasing costs or lower performance levels but lower operating costs – on the other (Janssen & Jager, 2002; Sierzchula et al., 2012). Initial research (e.g. Brandt et al., 2012) has demonstrated how IS can enhance the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of electric mobility. However, despite substantial subsidies worldwide. the actual adoption of electric mobility has been slow

Employing the IT business value model by Melville et al. (2004), we investigate whether supporting IS can increase the contribution of the physical eco-innovation of EVs to organizational performance, in terms of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and competitive impacts (Melville et al., 2004). Specifically, the paper addresses the following research questions:

1 What is the impact of supporting IS on the organizational performance of eco-innovations?

2 What are the mechanisms through which this impact occurs?

3 How do organizational factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence the use of supporting IS?

To do so, we employ a multiple-case study comprising eight companies that have integrated EVs into their business processes. Empirically, we <sup>fi</sup>nd a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on organizationa performance. This effect comes not only through optimizing the existing business processes in which the eco-innovations are deployed but also by affording new business processes and business models. Moreover, we identify organizational factors that determine the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and realization of these potentials.

By emphasizing the impact of IS on an eco-innovation’s contribution to organizational performance, we introduce a new perspective on sustainable IS research and add further functional affordances of Green IS (Seidel et al., 2013) to the existing knowledge base. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we show how these supporting IS create technological <sup>fl</sup>exibility for <sup>fi</sup>rms by increasing the degrees of freedom to deploy alternative technologies while ensuring satisfac tory organizational performance and creating windows of opportunity for developing new, sustainable innovations (digital eco-innovations). We thus address two speci<sup>fi</sup>c research gaps within the Green IS <sup>fi</sup>eld of knowledge: First, we contribute to the under-studied <sup>fi</sup>eld of research on the impact of Green IS (Malhotra et al., 2013). Second, we address the underresearched perspective of Green IS in supporting non-IS eco-innovations to leverage their organizational performance impacts.

## IS FOR ENHANCING THE ORGANIZATIONAL PERFORMANCE OF ECO-INNOVATIONS

Businesses worldwide are increasingly required to ful<sup>fi</sup>l stakeholder demands for environmental sustainability (Butler, 2011). Future environmental impacts will largely be determined by business operations and the technologies deployed within them (Elliot, 2011). Therefore, if eco-innovations can be integrated into these operations, there will be substantial gains made toward a better future. This cannot occur, however, if eco-innovations present insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient organizational performance (Melville et al., 2004), as adoption will then remain low (Del Rio Gonzalez, 2005).

In general, prior research on the impact of IS on organizational performance has described IS’ ability to enhance productivity, reduce costs, drive innovation, etc. (Af<sup>fl</sup>erbach, 2015). Inter alia, work on the business value of IT has described how IS can contribute to organizationa performance by supporting other <sup>fi</sup>rm assets, which then leads to increased ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness (e.g. Rivard et al., 2006). However, this potential has not yet been applied to address the speci<sup>fi</sup>c large-scale adoption barriers of physical eco-innovations by improving their organizational performance. Our study therefore attempts to <sup>fi</sup>ll this void by integrating literature on eco-innovations (describing their characteristics and consequences) and sustainable IS research (delineating the potential of IS for sustainable business transforma tion) with selected knowledge from the stream of work on IT business value (concerning the contribution of IS to organizational performance by supporting non-IS <sup>fi</sup>rm resources). In the following, we <sup>fi</sup>rst review the <sup>fi</sup>rst two streams separately and then integrate them to describe the new perspective.

## IS for environmental sustainability

Seidel et al. (2013) describe the double role that IS play in green transformations: they can be seen as ‘both a contributor and a potential solution to environmental degradation’ (p. 1278). A recent review by Malhotra et al. (2013) reveals that substantial progress has been made in conceptualizing and analysing the two related concepts of ‘Green in IS’ (also referred to as Green IT) and ‘Green by IS’ (Jenkin et al., 2011; Kossahl et al., 2012). For instance, Watson et al. (2010a) provide a conceptual model for structuring the sub<sup>fi</sup>eld of Green by IS for the energy sector, entitled ‘energy informatics’, which illustrates how IS can connect the supply and demand sides in intelligent energy systems, thus leading to optimized resource ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Elliot (2011) describes a multidisciplinary framework for understanding and studying ITenabled business transformation and a conceptual model displaying the interdependencies among stakeholders, human behaviour and technologies employed (including IT) as well as the resulting in<sup>fl</sup>uence on environmental quality. Here, the dual role of IS also becomes apparent, as they are described as directly in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the quality of the environment, mediating the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of human behaviour on the environment and vice versa. Moreover, it becomes evident that the second, indirect effect can be further differentiated into one that aims to improve information and communication concerning environmental sustainability (e.g. monitoring and evaluating) and one that seeks to facilitate sustainable human behaviour (Elliot, 2011). Thus, IS afford businesses possibilities for achieving progress toward corporate sustainability (Markus & Silver, 2008).

To explore these potentials, Seidel et al. (2013) conduct an in-depth, interpretative case study and derive four functional affordances, i.e. ‘action possibilities allowed by material properties existent in information systems’ (p. 1279) of Green IS in the context of sustainability transforma tions. When realized, the <sup>fi</sup>rst two affordances – re<sup>fl</sup>ective disclosure and information democra tization – improve organizational sensemaking by allowing for uncertainty reduction and a critical reconsideration of corporate sustainability through the dissemination of sustainability information to relevant stakeholders. The remaining affordances – output management and delocalization – involve the potential to drive sustainable work practices by reducing the nega tive consequences of established business processes or by shifting the locus of their execution so that, e.g., the need to travel is reduced.

Beyond this conceptual work, analytical research has also been conducted. For instance, Corbett et al. (2011) focus on the role of IS in sustainability measurement and provide examples (e.g. vehicle telematics data from a delivery company) that illustrate how IS can contribute to measurement principles for environmental sustainability and thus improve sustainability-related decision making. Although research on the actual design of Green IS is scarce (Malhotra et al. 2013), initial examples have been offered. Hilpert et al. (2013) design an artefact for tracking greenhouse gas emissions in the logistics industry, which, employed in a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, provides management with more detailed and accurate information about the emissions produced by the trucks and thus improves decision-making.

However, research on the actual impact of Green IS implemented in practice on environmental sustainability is practically non-existent (Malhotra et al., 2013). A notable exception is the work of Loock et al. (2013), who conduct <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments revealing the conditions under which Green IS can in<sup>fl</sup>uence human behaviour to reduce energy consumption through providing households with energy usage information and goal-setting possibilities. Moreover, whereas both Green in IS and Green by IS deal with the greening of (IS) products or business processes through the use of IS, less is known about IS’ contribution to improving the organizational performance (Melville et al., 2004) of already green business practices, e.g. green technology. An exception to this is Brandt et al. (2012), who provide evidence that new business models of EV-based virtual power plants can be enabled through IS, which would help to counteract the higher costs of EVs by creating additional revenues. These <sup>fi</sup>rst research efforts suggest the economic potential of IS when combined with eco-innovations. While Green IS research has progressed in explaining the interaction between sustainability-oriented IS and humans (e.g. Melville, 2010) or organizational initiatives (Hedman et al., 2012), there is a lack of understanding concerning the kind of interactions that Green IS have with other (non-IS) green technologies and their impacts. Although initial research has investigated the potential of green IS to complement conventional technology and generate environmental bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Watson et al. [2010b], e.g. describing the case of vehicle telematics leading to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains at UPS), less is known about the impact of Green IS on the organizational performance of eco-innovations in a business context.

Elliot (2011) emphasizes the importance of technologies when considering business transformation toward sustainability, as they are reciprocally connected to human behaviour and affect the environment. However, the author views technology as being inclusive of IT. While this view allows for thinking about hybrid solutions consisting of IS and non-IS technologies, it does not account for how the two parts interact and can lead to reduced environmental degradation and changed human behaviour. Our study takes a closer look at this interaction.

## Business transformation toward sustainability and eco-innovations

Differences among companies’ sustainability efforts, e.g. their amount of sustainability-related research and development when it comes to innovation and technology (Baumgartner & Ebner, 2010), may result from the pressure that management perceives from the corporate environment (Vithessonthi, 2009; Butler, 2011; Seidel et al., 2013). Thus, the institutional environment in<sup>fl</sup>uences sensemaking, knowledge creation and decision making concerning sustainability in organizations, which in turn lead to organizational outcomes, i.e. sustainability efforts (Butler, 2011). Moreover, because of individual and organizational contexts, managers vary in their motivation for and response seeking to sustainability issues (Bansal & Roth, 2000). Following this logic, management’s perception of the need to change – often driven by stakeholder pressure to increase environmental sustainability and management’s ability to recognize it – wil in<sup>fl</sup>uence their decisions on, e.g., whether and how to employ environmentally sustainable innovations (Faucheux & Nicolaï, 2011), which in turn are a core driver of business transformations toward sustainability (Schaltegger et al., 2012).

The adoption of eco-innovations in a business context has both up- and downsides in terms of business bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Del Rio Gonzalez (2005) identi<sup>fi</sup>es several possible positive aspects, including the greater willingness of customers to pay for greener products, fewer resources consumed in processes and intangible advantages, such as improved image, increased motivation and higher product quality. On the downside, they enumerate substantial possible disadvantages – the <sup>fi</sup>rst of which is the high level of uncertainty concerning regulative, technological and organizational efforts due to adopting the innovation. Further disadvantages include the potentially high initial cost of setting up a new green technology and switching from technol ogies already employed. Thus, the ecological advantages can go hand in hand with economic disadvantages, such as higher acquisition costs and/or lower performance levels, thereby hindering their adoption, particularly in business contexts (Janssen & Jager, 2002; Del Rio Gonzalez, 2005; Sierzchula et al., 2012).

These aspects stem from the distinct characteristics of eco-innovations. The differences between eco-innovations and regular ones have been carefully described in the economics literature (e.g. Marin et al., 2015) and can be divided into economic and informational attributes. The economic aspects often involve a different pro<sup>fi</sup>le of investment, i.e. higher initial costs but lower operating costs. Moreover, related research describes an externality problem (Rennings, 2000), as eco-innovations ‘imply a higher collective bene<sup>fi</sup>t but lower or equal private bene<sup>fi</sup>ts than conventional alternatives’ (Bertens & Statema, 2011, p. 18). Because of the general ‘absence of a monetary reward for the improved environmental performance (Marin et al., 2015, p. 673), the economic performance of eco-innovations often lags behind. The informational problems stem from the lack of experience and trust concerning the new technology, which calls for further complementary organizational investments to build the necessary capabilities (Marin et al., 2015). This is particularly relevant because ecoinnovations generally build upon a technological trajectory that is fundamentally different from the former, unsustainable one and thus require greater effort to implement (Bertens & Statema, 2011). These additional efforts might lead the ‘green investment’ to fall short in many cases, leaving the potential for environmental improvement unrealized. This is also supported by empirical research: As Del Rio Gonzalez (2005) <sup>fi</sup>nds, the economic aspects (e.g. high initial investments and long payback times) and the ‘drastic change in the organization/production process’ (p. 34) are the most important barriers to corporate ecoinnovation adoption. Thus, the economic and informational speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of eco-innovations affect a technology’s potential to mitigate environmental problems.

In that regard, according to Carrillo-Hermosilla et al. (2010), there are three types of eco-innovations, which can be categorized according to two dimensions: their impacts on economic and environmental sustainability. Eco-innovations that score low on both dimen sions can be described as a component addition, meaning that they are employed to reduce environmental damage, but the processes in which they are employed are not adapted to their speci<sup>fi</sup>cs. Extra costs are incurred as a result of these ‘end-of-pipe’ technologies (Carrillo-Hermosilla et al., 2010). The second category describes eco-innovations that realize a medium level of impact on economic and environmental sustainability and aim to achieve eco-ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency – ‘the economic value added by a <sup>fi</sup>rm in relation to its aggregated ecologica impact’ (Dyllick & Hockerts, 2002, p. 136) – meaning that they enhance the performance of existing processes, allowing more output to be produced with fewer inputs or less environmental damage (Carrillo-Hermosilla et al., 2010). The third category encompasses ecoinnovations that score high on both economic and environmental sustainability and can be described as ‘system changes’ (Carrillo-Hermosilla et al., 2010). These innovations aim to achieve eco-effectiveness – i.e. change that goes ‘beyond merely reducing negative environmental impact by ending ecological degradation. Seeking an ultimate solution for ecologica problems, eco-effectiveness oftentimes requires a shift of mindset and transformation of busi ness models’ (Chen et al., 2008, p. 188).

As these categorizations reveal, the impacts of eco-innovations are ultimately connected to the degree of change they bring along in their respective implementation contexts, e.g. in terms of business process change (Carrillo-Hermosilla et al., 2010). According to Cavalcante et al. (2011) and as indicated by the system-change category, changes in business processes, however, are linked to changes in the respective business models. As business models can be viewed as a conceptual, intermediate construct between the operational process (including technologies employed) and the strategic, decision-making layer (including corporate sustain ability aspirations) (Al-Debei, 2010), it is an important perspective when studying business transformation toward sustainability. In other words, ‘a company which tries to improve its sus tainability performance has to change its business model’ (Schaltegger et al., 2012, p. 105). The transformational degree of these changes can range from smaller adaptations to fundamental redesigns of business processes (Cavalcante et al., 2011; Schaltegger et al., 2012). Within our study, we investigate the impact supporting IS have on business processes and the organizational performance of eco-innovations.

## Supporting IS’ in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the organizational performance of eco-innovations

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne supporting IS as hardware, software, data and procedures (Silver et al., 1995) that complement the use of an eco-innovation. Supporting IS resources can represent eithe new applications or adaptations of existing IS aiming to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the organizational performance of eco-innovation use. Hence, the concept of supporting IS does not focus on the direct effect of IS on corporate sustainability but rather on the indirect impact that can be realized through enhancing the organizational performance of sustainable technolo gies and innovations.

We thus explore a new facet of Green IS by examining its support of already green technologies or practices in business contexts. With very few exceptions (e.g. Brandt et al., 2012), this perspective remains neglected. In contrast to the Green by IS approach (Kossahl et al., 2012) the new subdomain aims to increase the organizational performance of already green business practices, such as green technology within these business processes. Thus, as the focus of supporting IS lies on increasing organizational performance (Melville et al., 2004) via enhancing the value of other <sup>fi</sup>rm resources (Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005) – ecoinnovations in this case – knowledge from the IT business value stream of work is of particular importance in exploring this perspective. However, it has not yet been applied to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of Green IS (Nishant, 2012), as pointed out by Brooks et al. (2012):

Although IS literature has examined how IS can support various business processes and improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness of organizations, few studies have focused on how Green IS initiatives can improve organizations’ performance by enabling new sustainable practices and processes. (p. 34)

While prior IT business value research has described the potential of IS to support other <sup>fi</sup>rm resources and thus contribute to organizational performance (see Rivard et al., 2006), this perspective remains to be applied for the case of corporate eco-innovation use. In such a situation, eco-innovations could have an enormous impact on business transformation toward environmental sustainability, as their economic characteristics are connected to their large-scale adop tion in business contexts (Del Rio Gonzalez, 2005).

Although little research has been conducted on supporting IS, the evaluation and discussion of IS’ value contribution to overall business success in general is a major <sup>fi</sup>eld of study within the IS community (Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005; Rivard et al., 2006; Wong et al., 2011; Angst et al., 2012; Mithas et al., 2012; Xue et al., 2012; Af<sup>fl</sup>erbach, 2015). Depending on contextual conditions, empirical research on the business value of IT has found a positive impact on organizational performance (Fichman, 2004). Examining prior research and drawing upon the resource-based view (RBV) of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, Melville et al. (2004) present an integrative model of IT business value. Following the RBV (Penrose, 1959; Wernerfelt, 1984), the model depicts how IS resources in<sup>fl</sup>uence ‘both [the] intermediate process level and the organization-wide level, […] comprising both ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency impacts and competitive impacts' (Melville et al.,. 2004. p. 287). Regarding efficiency impacts. Melville et al. (2004 speak of ‘an internal perspective employing such metrics as cost reduction and productivity enhancements in the assessment of a given business process’ (p. 287). The competitive impacts mentioned relate to performance in terms of effectiveness impacts, which in turn ‘denote the achievement of organizational objectives in relation to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s external environment’ (Melville et al., 2004, p. 287).

Even though IS has sometimes been described as directly in<sup>fl</sup>uencing organizational performance, a growing amount of research (e.g. Wade & Hulland, 2004; Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005) has found that the performance effects do not arrive through IS in isolation but rather through the joint operation of IS with other resources (Kohli & Grover, 2008) and the synergies among resources employed within a business process (Nevo & Wade, 2010). Thus, through the targeted and complementary employment of IS, the performance contribution of other resources can be enhanced (Wade & Hulland, 2004; Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005). Although an increasing number of authors have described the importance of this indirect performance impact of IS, it is not yet well understood how it occurs (e.g. Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005).

The study of the performance impact of IS is closely linked to research on IS innovations, as several studies have assessed the performance contributions of single innovative IS or IS investment portfolios as an outcome measure (see Fichman, 2004), and the role of IS for business innovations has been enlarging in recent years (Nambisan, 2013). Here, following the aforementioned direct/indirect debate in IT business value. one must differentiate between the performance impact of IS innovations themselves and the impact of IS on non-IS innovations and resources (Rivard et al., 2006). The impact of IS innovations on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance has been examined, e.g. with reference to innovations of IS <sup>fi</sup>rms or in ISrelated industries (e.g. Ordanini & Rubera, 2010). Recently, the impact of IS on primarily non-IS innovations has also been studied under the theme of digital innovation (Yoo et al., 2010). Yoo (2010), for instance, describes how GM’s IS-based OnStar complements the conventional car and thus creates new revenue streams. Moreover, as Zolnowski et al. (2011) demonstrate with multiple cases from the manufacturing industry, the use of IS components (e.g. sensor technology) in physical innovations (e.g. machines) enables cost cutting (e.g. via decreased maintenance efforts due to enhanced information supply) or additional revenues (e.g. by offering remote services in addition to product sale). In sum, the complementarity argument from the IT business value stream of work (Melville et al., 2004) becomes more important as the complementary use of IS and non-IS resources – driven by advances in and the increasing diffusion of digital technologies and infrastructures (Tilson et al., 2010) – creates ample opportunity to impact organizational performance. Regarding hybrid physical–digital solutions, Porter & Heppelmann (2015) speak of a third wave of IT-driven business transformation that has greater potential to drive productivity and innovation than ever before.

As we want to relate the general principles of IS impacting organizational performance to the case of eco-innovations, we draw on the integrative model of IT business value derived by Melville et al. (2004) as a starting point. Here, in line with other work (e.g. Wade & Hulland, 2004), the performance impact is also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by organizational and environmental factors. Green IS research also maintains that the deployment of Green IS is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by both <sup>fi</sup>rm-internal (e.g. managerial motivation) and -external (e.g. regulation) aspects (e.g. Butler, 2011; Jenkin et al., 2011). Therefore, to adapt the model to our research focus on ecoinnovations, the environmental sustainability perspective must be added, as it is important for managers to be aware of the sustainability requirements that the environment demands the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm to ful<sup>fi</sup>l and to derive strategies for achieving corporate sustainability; these pressures vary among industries.

Moreover, industry characteristics set the stage for the employment of eco-innovations, based on the varying centrality of the respective processes for their business models. For instance, in the public transportation industry, the technology applied for transport is of major importance and thus a main contributor to environmental degradation. Therefore, changing this technology bears both great risks and opportunities. In the business software industry, however the technology used to conduct occasional business trips is not a central aspect of the business model; modi<sup>fi</sup>cations in these kinds of settings would therefore induce only small changes and performance impacts. ## METHOD

## Context: corporate electric vehicle use

Given our aim to explore the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of supporting IS on the organizational performance of eco-innovations in business contexts, we chose a case study research approach focusing on corporate EV use (Benbasat et al., 1987; Gerring, 2007; Yin, 2009). We used a multi-case design comprising eight different cases because selecting such a design allows one to conduct a cross-case analysis and yields more general research results (Benbasat et al., 1987). To qualify as a case company, each company selected had to operate both electric and conventional vehicles within their business processes. To conduct our case studies, we followed the process model of Paré (2004).

## Access and sample

We gained access to the case companies by, <sup>fi</sup>rst, directly contacting employees of severa companies known personally as well as, second, contacting managers who we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed through business-oriented social networking services, such as LinkedIn. We began with a set of three case companies, contacting the managers directly responsible for vehicle operation within the business processes – or at least for the decision that led to using the EVs. It quickly became obvious that we needed to <sup>fi</sup>nd more companies to gain a broader perspective and gather more <sup>fi</sup>eld data and thus a more robust sample. Furthermore, we realized that we needed to observe companies with a varying degree of IS support within their business processes, thus ensuring increased variance in the variable of interest (Bhattacherjee, 2012).

Finally, we selected eight case companies (three initial plus <sup>fi</sup>ve further case companies) across four different business process categories: delivering mail and parcels, business trips, transporting customers (public transportation) and car sharing. Following the diverse case selection approach, we chose one case company per category with a high amount of supporting IS and one with a low amount. A detailed overview of the case characteristics and a summary of <sup>fi</sup>eld data collected are presented in Table 2.

In the following, we describe the detailed analytical process outlined in Figure 2 (Harrison & Rouse, 2014). Along the way, this framework was revised by adapting existing and integrating new constructs when the necessity to do so emerged from the data. Building upon prior research in the <sup>fi</sup>elds of eco-innovation, IT business value and Green IS, we derived a new subcategory of Green IS: supporting IS. To guide and shape (Eisenhardt, 1989; Dubé & Paré, 2003) our investigation of whether different supporting IS resources have different impacts on the organizational performance of eco-innovations in a business context, we created an initial framework (Figure 1) by drawing upon and adapting the integrative model of IT business value of Melville et al. (2004).

## A priori constructs

We included eight a priori constructs in our initial model, which we regarded as tentative (Dubé & Paré, 2003) in the sense that none of the a priori constructs would be ‘guaranteed a place in the resultant theory’ (Eisenhardt, 1989, p. 536). These constructs are de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Table 1.

## Selection of cases (multiple-case design)

We selected eight cases via a diverse case-selection approach (Seawright & Gerring, 2008) that aims to achieve variance along relevant dimensions. Here, we employed a two-stage ap proach. First, we searched for variance with respect to the business context (e.g. the process in which EVs are employed, effectiveness goals of this process, <sup>fi</sup>rm size, and industry sector). We focused on one speci<sup>fi</sup>c country (Germany) to abstract from any law-related, regulatory var iables. Second, for each business process category, we selected a case that could be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as high IS usage and one as low or medium IS usage. To achieve this categorization, we searched for information (e.g. on corporate websites or in practitioner reports) on IS support of the respective processes.

## Preparing data collection

Before we began collecting data, we discussed different kinds of possible business processes and the role IS could play within them. This led to a template based on our initial research model (Figure 1) that guided us during the data-collection phase. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned various characteristics (stakeholder pressure, importance of the business process for the business model, role and function of IS and other resources, etc.) to pro<sup>fi</sup>le IS within the different companies. Furthermore, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned codes and classi<sup>fi</sup>cations (e.g. low/high; /0/+ (see Table 3 for a detailed explanation)) to describe effects on performance and the various levels (process level, company level).

## Interviews and on-site observations

We used introductory, semi-structured, face-to-face interviews with one or two representatives from each company. In total, we conducted 19 interviews including multiple interviews with the same representative(s). Furthermore, we asked each case company whether we could observe the respective business process in practice. During these direct observations (Yin, 2009), the authors took <sup>fi</sup>eld notes, including objective data (organization, place, time, etc.) as well as the sequences of events and interpretations of them following the observation protocol guidelines by Rosenthal (2011). In total, eight different business processes – one per case company – were observed.

## Preliminary analysis

After the interviews and our observations, we discussed and sketched the <sup>fl</sup>ow of activities within the selected business process and the different effects of EVs on the process steps as well as the amount and type of supporting IS being used, again using our prede<sup>fi</sup>ned codes and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. The results (see Table A1 in the Appendix for Case 1 as an example) were then consolidated to increase validity through an iterative process with all authors. Furthermore, we included a feedback loop by sending our results to the interviewees via email, giving them the opportunity to comment on our interpretations. If there were issues mentioned by the interviewees, we discussed them within the author team, adapted the resulting documents and ac tivated the feedback loop again until both the researchers and case company representatives felt that the documentation properly represented the respective business reality.

## Supporting business documents

Additionally, relevant text passages in <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c documents such as annual and sustainability reports, press releases, process documentations and internal training documents were coded analysed to learn about the motivations and goals the case companies were pursuing with their eco-innovation initiatives as well as the speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways in which they conducted them.

## Formal analysis and returning to theory

The differences and similarities of the chosen dimensions in the eight cases were analysed to illustrate the effects of supporting IS’ impact on the organizational performance (Melville et al., 2004) of the eco-innovation usage. In an iterative process, we discussed and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the similarities of the chosen dimensions within the group of authors. Furthermore, by continuously iterating and comparisons between data and theory (Gregory et al., 2015), we clari<sup>fi</sup>ed our initial model and revealed additional aggregate dimensions and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed new emerging constructs.

## RESULTS

Our analysis of the organizational performance impacts of the integration of EV use within business processes revealed three distinct drivers: the use of supporting IS, the organizational aspiration level of eco-innovation use and the proactiveness of the IT stances of the <sup>fi</sup>rms. Figure 3 shows how each of these aggregate dimensions manifested from the data via <sup>fi</sup>rst-order concepts and second-order themes (Harrison & Rouse, 2014). In the following sections, we explore each impact driver and discuss the relevance of our observations and <sup>fi</sup>ndings for the ecoinnovation’s impact on organizational performance. The relationship between supporting IS and the aspiration level determines the mindfulness of the supporting IS usage, an importan phenomenon that we came across in our analysis, and will be discussed later.

## Supporting IS (usage)

There is a quite a variety in the amount as well as the forms and types of IS that complement the eco-innovation use within the business processes analysed. In Case 1, a global player in the mail and logistics industry, supporting IS are used for two main purposes. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is to prepare the vehicle before the delivery trip. Here, the depot management can use an IS that heats the car in advance so it is ready when the driver arrives. Second, after the trip, there is an ISsupported analysis of certain statistics (e.g. battery status) to gain more transparency about the use of technology. We also asked our interview partner (CE1) for possible differences in IS support between the electric and conventional vehicles in their <sup>fl</sup>eet; he replied that they use these ‘speci<sup>fi</sup>c systems for the EVs only’ and that ‘they help a lot to increase the EVs operation range’. Furthermore, we observed a prototype electric truck developed in a joint research project with a large car-manufacturing company, which had a variety of additional IS-enabled features. CE1 highlighted the ‘follow-me’ function, which enables the EV to autonomously follow the delivery personnel wearing a smart wristband. As CE1 states, ‘this functionality on its own boosts the effectiveness of the delivery personnel as they can deliver up to 30% more parcels in the same time as they would without this function’. Interestingly, because of the good perfor mance reported in several press articles, the <sup>fi</sup>rm has received requests to sell the truck with the software solutions to other <sup>fi</sup>rms, e.g. from craft sectors, and is currently contemplating creating a new venture for this business model. Furthermore, Case 1 is experimenting with vehicle-to-grid<sup>3</sup> (V2G) functionalities, which aim to implement a bidirectional connection between the EV and the energy system to generate further revenues by selling the EV’s charging <sup>fl</sup>exibility at electricity markets. Here, IS are mandatory, as they serve as a broker by connecting energy demand and supply.

In contrast, CE2 told us that the <sup>fi</sup>rm does not purchase new EVs but rather converts their conventional vehicles to EVs with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c partner <sup>fi</sup>rm. When asked for supporting IS, Case 2’s interview partner mentioned that they only adjusted the internal vehicle software to enhance the responsiveness of the automatic start/stop system, thus easing the EV use on delivery tours.

In Case 3, we found that the <sup>fi</sup>rm developed speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fl</sup>eet-management software that supports the entire business travel process of their employees when using an EV. First, we observed that the system facilitates a range-sensitive reservation of the vehicle by automatically checking the load status of different vehicles. The system is also able to reserve charging stations en route. CE3a also explained that if the employee is inexperienced with EVs, an e-learning platform is offered. Furthermore, denoted as ‘industry standard’ by CE3a, we also noticed during our observation that the employee conducts the authentication process via RFID card at the vehicle. When returning from the trip, there is an automatic connection to back-end systems for settlement purposes.

However, when analysing Case 4, which also uses EVs for business travel, we found very limited IS support. The process elements, ranging from reservation and driving to charging and settlement, are performed either manually or with basic IS (e.g. calendar functions in mailing programmes). The load-status check and the loading itself are performed manually and therefore require more time. Moreover, time buffers need to be implemented in the planning, as there is often uncertainty about the current battery status. During the trip, there are no special IS assisting the driver, and after the trip, there is no automatic system for settlement and no further means of analysis, e.g. regarding the energy consumption of the vehicle.

The same was observable in Case 5, a regional public transportation company. Not long before our interview and observations, they decided to employ an electric bus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c routes. The entire transportation process is conducted without further IS support, except for one complementary analytical tool monitoring the loading statistics and the battery status at the end of the tour. The regular limitations of EVs (long charging times, limited driving ranges) restricted business process integration, as the electric bus was only used on one adjusted route and, in contrast to conventional buses, was unable to service the route for the duration of the business day.

Case 6, which also operates electric buses, tried to react to this limitation and was involved in a highly innovative research project that assesses the technical and economic feasibility of electric buses in combination with inductive charging. These inductively charged buses – buses that charge their batteries over the air using electromagnetic <sup>fi</sup>elds to transfer the energy – op erate on one speci<sup>fi</sup>c route through the capital city of the operating region. With the integration of these electric buses, the company implements IS solutions that mainly support the charging process; for example, IS are needed to manage the automatic communication between the vehicle and the inductive charging spots that are situated in the ground at selected bus stops. We were able to observe these dedicated charging IS and were told by CE6b, ‘they are used to keep the peak load of all charging processes as low as possible in order to avoid surcharges from the transmission system operator’. CE6a added when speaking about this speci<sup>fi</sup>c IS, ‘it is the key ingredient that enables us to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently operate the electric buses in our transportation network meaning that we can use them equally to conventional ones while keeping the costs at a minimum’.

Case 7 operates a large car-sharing service next to its other business operations, including transportation services via mass transport (e.g. trains). Within their car-sharing <sup>fl</sup>eet, they operate over 300 EVs. We observed a high level of IS support, starting with an application on a mobile device that customers use to <sup>fi</sup>nd the next available vehicle via GPS and receive information about the load status and prices. Moreover, with this information, it is possible to complement the company’s train-based mobility offerings with a <sup>fl</sup>exible solution (EVs) for the ‘last mile’, thus, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, allowing customers a multi-modal end-to-end travel planning on their smartphone. Furthermore, the customers use a near-<sup>fi</sup>eld communication solution to unlock the car and start the vehicle. The settlement of the trip is also completed via the application. On the supplier side, there is a central surveillance system managing the locations and load status of all EVs. CE7a told us, ‘if the load status is under a certain level, an employee is informed, who then drives the vehicle to reload it at a central maintenance station’. Furthermore, when asked about the primary focus of using supporting IS, our interview partner stated

Without the implemented systems, we would not be able to get a grip on managing the large <sup>fl</sup>eet of electric vehicles. What’s even more is that our market research showed us that our cus tomers demand current information about charging levels and driving ranges to reduce their range anxiety. We are very con<sup>fi</sup>dent that without all the additional IS support we could not operate electric vehicles in our <sup>fl</sup>eet with the same service levels as conventional ones. (CE7a)

Moreover, Case 7 also experimentally deployed V2G concepts with parts of their EV <sup>fl</sup>eet.

Case 8, a small car-sharing operator that is still run by its founders, only operates a <sup>fl</sup>eet of 91 vehicles – 10 of which are electric – based at eight different rental stations across a mediumsized German city. When speaking to the company’s manager, we quickly found out that the use of supporting IS is minimal. When the company integrated EVs into their car-sharing <sup>fl</sup>eet, the only additional IS implemented (after some months) were those needed to dynamically monitor the EVs’ charging status and positions.

## Impact on organizational performance

Table 3 displays the results of our assessment concerning the transformative effect, i.e. the effect resulting from the transformation from conventional to EVs within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c case, with respect to the two aforementioned dimensions of organizational performance (Melville et al., 2004) as well as the amount of supporting IS employed and their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the process steps. Here, we differentiate between use time categories re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the actual time the vehicle is used within the business process: pre-use (PU), core use (CU) and post-use (POU).

Moreover, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of supporting IS on EV use can be divided into two categories: 1) IS as an enabler, transforming business procedures to increase organizational goal achievement (effectiveness dimension of organizational performance) and 2) IS as a driver that enhances existing business processes (ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency dimension of organizational performance). We speak about IS as an enabler when the respective functionalities would not be possible without IS (supporting IS as a mandatory resource). In turn, we speak of IS as a driver when the respec tive functionalities are enhanced by the use of supporting IS, although they would also be possible without IS (supporting IS as a supplementary resource).

The <sup>fi</sup>rst category, supporting IS as an enabler, can predominantly be observed in pre-use (PU) process steps. This category makes the deployment of EVs in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c business process feasible and helps transform the existing business model or even create a new one through new IS-enabled functionalities. When comparing two cases within a business process category (case companies in which we observed the same business process, e.g. delivering mail and parcels; see Table 2), we were able to observe a positive difference in the transformative effect on the eco-innovation’s contribution to effectiveness, i.e. organizational goal achievement (Melville et al., 2004), between the cases with a relatively low amount of supporting IS and those with a relatively high amount. In the latter, supporting IS appeared to trigger additional functionalities that increased organizational goal achievement with respect to the business context. An example is the supporting IS-enabled V2G functionality in Cases 1 and 7, which aims to generate additional returns. Another example is the integrated software solution targeted at EV use for business travel by Case 3, as described by CE3b:

As our scattered business sites lead to a high volume of business travel between the sites we do have to <sup>fi</sup>nd solutions that are ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and thus cost-minimized and green at the same time. We believe that our developed software can achieve exactly that! We are even thinking about making a product from this and selling it to our customers who already started asking for this when they saw our EVs driving around. (CE3b)

The second category, supporting IS as a driver, was predominantly observed in core use (CU) process steps. This category improves the performance of speci<sup>fi</sup>c process steps by enhancing information <sup>fl</sup>ow or automating functions, without being essential for enabling the process. We found that the business process performance, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency dimension (Melville et al., 2004), can increase when the amount of supporting IS positively differs in a given business process category. Here, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that supporting IS lower the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency losses (i.e. higher costs) that come along with the integration of an eco-innovation. Examples suggest ing a higher ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency driven by supporting IS can be found in Cases 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 and 8, in which supporting IS help to manage the charging process after or during EV use (Table 3). Furthermore, in Cases 1 and 3, IS may boost ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency by automatically applying the charging costs to the user or department, while in Case 6 an IS replaces manual work, automatically monitoring the (charging) condition of the bus and alerting the control centre if the charging or other state conditions of the bus differ from its planned state. We thus observed that supporting IS do in fact enhance ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency by connecting technology, EVs and charging infrastructure as well as human resources. This connection comes via applying supporting IS in automating tasks and supplying employees with relevant information. As one interviewee from Case 3 asserted, ‘Our special e-learning tool prepares the employees for EV use in advance. When they actually use it, they have no problems at all and can start right away’ (CE3a)

Overall, the impact of IS on the performance of eco-innovations in a business context is positive. By the mechanisms described, IS are an important ingredient in helping eco-innovations reach a better – and perhaps critical – performance level (Figure 4).

Nevertheless, we found differences in the realization of these potentials, even with companies in the same industry, indicating the existence of other relevant factors. For instance, with both companies using EVs for business travel, Case 3 with its high motivation to reduce carbon emissions from business travel implements a variety of supporting IS, while Case 4, aiming to use EVs simply as marketing displays, does not employ dedicated IS.

To reduce travel-induced emissions, the <sup>fi</sup>rm has already been using videoconferencing tools for years. It has also begun to implement EVs into its portfolio of mobility solutions. After an initial testing project with selected employees, the vehicles were made accessible for all employees interested.

For Case 4, our interview partners asserted that sustainability, in its environmental sense, is a major topic for the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Since 2007, the company has delivered its customers with electricity solely from renewable sources and since 2010, solely with climate-neutral gas. Sustainability is viewed as an opportunity to compete with major national or international energy suppliers. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>rm is very engaged in promoting green issues in their corporate communica tions (e.g. by displaying a ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 } { \mathsf { - s a v i n g } }$ calculator on their website). The company employs a small number of EVs for two main reasons. First, they want to further expand their green reputation; EVs are a visible means of spreading this image. Second, they want to gain insights into the consequences of using EVs for the grid to determine whether there are interesting business models that could arise in the future. The vehicles are mainly used for business travel to customers. Nevertheless, CE4a told us that the management ‘does not believe in a short term EV breakthrough’, and because of that, they ‘are not willing to spend too much cash on this technology yet’. Hence, they ‘want to operate them as cheaply as possible while using them as “green testimonials” ’ (CE4a).

Case 5, which perceives itself as a major contributor to the sustainability of the community through their services offered, takes a similar position. CE5a explained that, as they consider their core business to be a sustainable means of transportation, they are ‘very interested in communicating this rather than actually changing [its] business’. Because the company’s management wants to keep their business as is, they ‘are unconvinced to adopt new, sustainable technologies, mostly due to the additional infrastructure required’ (CE5a). CE5a stated further that they are therefore also ‘unconvinced by federal programs that support the use of EVs’. Instead, they maintain that ‘the government should further support public transportation as a whole’ (CE5a). Nevertheless, management feels the need to follow certain trends in order not to bypass relevant developments as revealed by CE5a: ‘We are skeptical about EVs, but we want to get a foothold in the whole thing and participate in designing the “rules of the game” ’.

Like Case 5, Case 6 perceives itself as a major contributor to the sustainability of the community, as can be found in a central statement on the company’s website. Accordingly, our interview partners told us that the company is involved in several publicly funded research projects that aim to innovate public transportation. This might be due to the academically very strong technical university that is situated in the operating region of the company, as according to the interview partners, ‘the connection between the company and the university has historically been very close’ (CE6b). Both interview partners emphasized that the company per ceives itself as innovative and pursues the strategic goal of being technologically ahead of its competitors in order to be ‘a key component of the community’s sustainability agenda’ (CE6a).

Regarding Case 7’s sustainability strategy, our interview partners declared that with their high-volume transportation business, sustainability is an important topic for the company because they are always in the public eye. CE7b mentioned that their market research clearly in dicates that ‘customers demand means of sustainable transport and show less price sensitivity when they believe that they do not harm the environment while commuting’. Therefore, the corporation continuously tries to enhance the environmental performance of its products and services. Recently, the <sup>fi</sup>rm announced that in the future, all long-distance trains would be powered completely by electricity from renewable energy resources. For their car-sharing service, ‘the large-scale integration of electric vehicles has not been a question at any point in time (CE7a). Nevertheless, CE7a also maintained, ‘the economically successful operation of the EVs within [their] <sup>fl</sup>eet was always of utmost importance as [they] are not able to operate this large <sup>fl</sup>eet of EVs just for marketing purposes’.

Despite their small <sup>fl</sup>eet of 81 vehicles – 10 of which are electric – we heard almost the same statement from Case 8’s general manager (CE8a), telling us that they consider both economic and ecological dimensions in their understanding of sustainability. Nevertheless, he also stated that the economic one is clearly the priority, as it is considered to be essential in guaranteeing the survival of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Cost coverage is therefore seen as economically sustainable, while low CO emissions are perceived as being environmentally sustainable. However, despite constant growth and professionalization, the company still feels a special connection to the environment and is therefore considering transforming all or part of its <sup>fl</sup>eet to EVs in the near future.

Taking all these observations into account, we noticed that <sup>fi</sup>rms that put a high emphasis on corporate environmental sustainability in terms of their sustainability strategy, such as Cases 1, 3, 5 and 7, undertake greater efforts when deploying EVs and supporting IS than those with a less aspirational sustainability strategy. For instance, this is expressed in how open the companies are towards innovative research projects (e.g. Cases 1 and 6) revolving around the EVusage. This effect appears to be even stronger when the business process in which EVs are employed is more important for the business model. Therefore, we assume that the relevance of the sustainability strategy and the perceived business process importance determine a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s aspiration level (March & Simon, 1958; Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) for integrating the eco innovation into their business processes. This aspiration level can be described as a reference point of the desired organizational performance an eco-innovation must deliver and thus in<sup>fl</sup>uences the effort in the eco-innovation implementation (Table A2). An organization with a pro-active sustainability strategy and a high perceived importance of the respective business process for future business will set higher goals with respect to the contribution of the ecoinnovation to organizational performance and therefore devote more effort to its implementation. On the other hand, a <sup>fi</sup>rm that uses the eco-innovation in a less important process and/or has a less ambitious sustainability strategy would not engage very much when it comes to the effort in the eco-innovation implementation.

## Proactiveness of IT stance

Beyond organizational aspirations, we found a further interesting in<sup>fl</sup>uential factor we initially came across when interviewing CE2, who expressed his dissatisfaction with EV usage in the company:

I am absolutely not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the current EV integration. You feel our high sustainability goals we set for ourselves at every corner of your daily work – from a large section on our intranet website, to all our marketing activities – but we do not get EVs on the road on a large scale. What is even more disappointing is that even the few EVs we really operate are not used as much as they could be. I have a strong feeling that we set our goals way too high for what we are able to really deliver with our business operation. (CE2)

Despite this dissatisfaction, Case 2, in contrast to Case 1, did not identify signi<sup>fi</sup>cant potentia to enhance the use of EVs via supporting IS.

When further analysing the cases, we found substantial evidence that the way in which <sup>fi</sup>rms attend to IS when it comes to innovation in general plays an important role in this regard. This <sup>fi</sup>nding became most obvious in Case 3: ‘As a business software company, when we think about innovation, we always think about IT innovations. This applies for sustainability actions as well'. (CE3a). As a conseguence of this positive posture towards IT, the company designed a sound solution that connected all necessary steps in the business process (business trips) in which the EVs were deployed, making the process smooth for the users and transparent for managers. Interestingly, CE3a commented when showing us their independently developed, highly integrated <sup>fl</sup>eet-management software that the company wants to make sure that it excels in operating the EVs in their <sup>fl</sup>eet, and they therefore ‘invest a lot in process optimization and of course IS to optimally integrate them in the business processes’. In contrast, in Case 4, which also deploys EVs for business trips, there were no such aspirations. Instead, only standard software for of<sup>fi</sup>ce purposes, particularly the calendar functionality, is used to reserve vehicles.

Thus, while all the case companies we analysed use IS somehow in their business processes, the way they perceive the role of IS for their businesses varies substantially. For instance, Case 7 has opened an innovation lab as a separate organizational unit to proactively consider digital innovations. When they initially implemented EVs in their car-sharing operations, they were experimenting with mobile applications that could ease the use of EVs for the customers. Case 8, on the other hand, introduced the EVs without even offering the option to book these vehicles on their website, as they did not know how to handle the uncertainty concerning the driving range. At our <sup>fi</sup>rst visit, we observed that customers had to reserve these vehicles via telephone or needed to come to the of<sup>fi</sup>ce. Only when they heard from other competitors that had information on the battery capacity of the vehicle displayed in their online booking systems did they react; not possessing any advanced IT skills themselves, however, they hired an IT consultancy that implemented sensors and devices in the vehicles and programmed the software.

In sum, there were substantial differences in how <sup>fi</sup>rms attend to IT when it comes to inno vation. For the IT-company in our sample, it is rather natural to reason about IT in innovation, which also happened when they were thinking about implementing EVs. Other companies, which, in general, were reluctant to use much IT for innovation, were also not very interested in it for supporting the EVs. Thus, a proactive search for ways to exploit and explore business by the means of new IT, referred to as proactive IT stance (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011), also in<sup>fl</sup>uences the amount and kind of supporting IS (Table A2). That is, we suggest that the aforementioned potentials of supporting IS can only be realized through proper implementation if management is open to and aware of the opportunities offered by innovative IS usage.

## DISCUSSION OF FINDINGS

When taking an aggregated view of our cases, the employment of supporting IS appears to have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the performance level that an eco-innovation reaches in a given business process category (Figure 4). These impacts result from two mechanisms. Although the degree may vary, when implementing eco-innovations, there regularly emerge changes in the existing business processes necessary to operate them (e.g. charging an EV). These changes, in isolation and without supporting IS, where eco-innovations are often used as ‘end-of-pipe’ technologies, result in extra costs (Carrillo-Hermosilla et al., 2010). Here, the <sup>fi</sup>rst mechanism sets in, as business process performance is positively affected by replacing or reducing the human labour needed to operate EVs in the respective processes, e.g. charging. Moreover, information is provided to increase certainty about the feasibility and most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient use of EVs, which further reduces costs, e.g. by using learning tools in advance.

Aside from that, we have witnessed that eco-innovations have speci<sup>fi</sup>cs that allow for entirely different applications, which create an even larger change in the system within which they are employed (Carrillo-Hermosilla et al., 2010). Here, as a second mechanism, supporting IS trig ger completely new functionalities, business processes and even business models that bundle physical and digital elements and relate to environmental sustainability, e.g. the V2G implementations or the new business model of selling an eco-friendly truck with supporting IS in Case 1.

In sum, these impacts on organizational performance remind us of IS’ three strategic roles described in prior works: automate, informate and transformate (Zuboff, 1988; Schein, 1989; Dehning et al., 2003). The observed ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency impacts can be related to the role of automation (automating business processes by replacing human labour) as well as information (informing employees and management about current business activities). Here, organizational savings (e.g. reduced overhead, lower resource consumption) are created through information technologies that reduce the need for human interaction (e.g. automatic data exchange) and improved information processing capabilities (e.g. increased planning accuracy) (Chen et al., 2008). Thus, supporting IS create complementarities (Wade & Hulland, 2004; Zhu, 2004) by easing the use of eco-innovations in business processes through increased connectivity among all resources required in the respective processes. Hence, we propose the following:

Proposition I Through information and automation, supporting IS drive connectivity and thus positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the integration of eco-innovations with other organizationa resources.

Competitive (Melville et al., 2004) or innovative impacts are in turn related to IS’ role of trans formation, i.e. transforming existing business and industry processes and relationships (Dehning et al., 2003). Hence, we propose the following proposition:

Proposition II Through transformation, supporting IS enable new sustainable business processes (and business models).

Thus, with supporting IS complementing the physical eco-innovation, both performance aspects described by Melville et al. (2004) are affected. We <sup>fi</sup>nd empirical evidence for the promising statements of recent research on the potential of hybrid physical–digital solutions for productivity and innovation (Porter & Heppelmann, 2015). From the results depicted in Table 3 and Figure 4, we propose the following:

Proposition III Supporting IS positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the performance impact of the eco-innovation via the integration with other resources as well as the realized business mode change.

We found that managerial awareness of the potential of supporting IS varied substantially among the cases. While an af<sup>fi</sup>nity for IS in general helps to identify the possible contributions, this does not mean that a high level of supporting IS is always necessary. Adapting existing business processes or creating new ones is associated with changes in the business model (Cavalcante et al., 2011). Although a high degree of business model change can be desirable in organizations that place signi<sup>fi</sup>cant emphasis on sustainability and thus have a strong organizational aspiration to integrate the eco-innovation into their business process while in creasing its impact on organizational performance, this is not the case in all <sup>fi</sup>rms.

While our observations reveal that a greater level of organizational aspiration (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) for the eco-innovation use and a proactive IT stance (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011) leads to a greater amount of supporting IS, we also found evidence that the positive impact of IS on the eco-innovation’s contribution to organizational performance is best achieved i the amount of supporting IS matches the organizational aspiration level. This echoes the concept of organizational mindfulness (Fiol & O’Connor, 2003); we view a mindful use of supporting IS as one ‘with reasoning grounded in its own organizational facts and speci<sup>fi</sup>cs’ (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004, p. 559). Thus, this mindful employment of supporting IS requires both an awareness of the potential for ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness as well as contextually differentiated reasoning (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004).

This was most obvious in Case 1, which employed EVs in a much more effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient manner than Case 2 did. In both cases, EVs were deployed in a business process that was of major importance for the delivery companies. Together with a highly ambitious sustainability strategy, this leads to a high organizational aspiration to integrate the eco-innovation into their business processes while increasing the eco-innovation’s impact. Therefore, to be mindful, both case companies should theoretically use a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of supporting IS to optimize the eco-innovation’s performance impact. However, only Case 1 employed supporting IS mindfully, i.e. an amount of supporting IS that matches the high organizational aspiration for the ecoinnovation use. The amount of supporting IS in Case 2 was too low and thus not mindful resulting in the eco-innovation’s performance impact being below the desired amount that stems from the company’s organizational aspiration level. Therefore, Case 2 experiences ‘costs of being green’ (Hoffman & Woody, 2008) when integrating the eco-innovation in their business process instead of increasing the ‘payoffs of being green’.

Combining the insights we draw from our data in terms of IS’ impact on performance (Figure 4) with the previous categorization of the case companies regarding a mindful or not mindful use of supporting IS (Figure 5), we can reveal a positive relationship between these two factors (Figure 6). Our data discloses that the mindful use of supporting IS leads to a higher performance impact of the eco-innovation. As an example, Case 8 has a high organizational aspiration level but a very low – and thus not mindful – amount of supporting IS. Case 7, which has the same business model (car sharing), also has a high aspiration level but at the same time uses a high – and thus mindful – amount of supporting IS. Analysing our case study data, it be comes obvious that Case 7, which mindfully uses supporting IS, registers a higher performance of the eco-innovation. However, Case 8 is a rather small company that does not have the same <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fl</sup>exibility as Case 7. Nevertheless, we were also able to observe this effect for the delivery service business process category (Cases 1 and 2).

The two other business process categories – business travel (Cases 3 and 4) and public transportation (Cases 5 and 6) – only include cases with a mindful supporting IS integration. Nevertheless, in these cases, we were also able to observe a performance-optimization poten tial. Case 4, for example, exhibits low organizational aspiration and a low amount of supporting IS and thus acts mindfully. Hence, Case 4 follows a supporting IS deployment decreasing the costs of being green. However, if the company manages to enter a process in which it changes its organizational aspiration level and at the same time increases the amount of supporting IS by the same degree, it would have the potential to increase the eco-innovation’s performance impact, increasing the payoffs of being green. The same effect holds true for the public transportation business process category (Cases 5 and 6).

This leads us to formulate the following proposition:

Proposition IV When the organization deploys an amount of supporting IS matching the aspiration level for eco-innovation use, thus acting mindfully, the realized performance impact will be enhanced.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings presented previously indicated that we needed to adjust our initial research framework (Figure 1), in which we explicitly assumed that the context in which the relevant pro cesses were embedded would be rather similar, at least in the same industry. Nevertheless, while conducting the case analysis, it became clear that several organizational factors played a major role. Our observations suggest that the kind of sustainability strategy a <sup>fi</sup>rm adopts is noteworthy (Baumgartner & Ebner, 2010). We found signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different approaches to sustainability depending on management’s perception of the need to change in response to stakeholder pressure (Butler, 2011). Through the variances of perceptions by companies within a business process category, we learned that it is not only a matter of sensing this need but also that differences occur in how it is interpreted, which is related to prior knowledge and regarded in decision making. The resulting sustainability strategy and the importance of the business process in which the EVs are employed determine a company’s organizational aspiration leve (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) with respect to the implementation of the eco-innovation. In turn, this manifests itself in the efforts invested for eco-innovation integration. including the kind and amount of supporting IS used. However, with regard to the latter, the potential must be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by management. The aforementioned cognitive processes not only relate to the sustainability pressures in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s environment but also to the perception of the possibilities that are afforded by the digitalization of more and more areas of (business) life (Gregory et al., 2015), which is ultimately expressed in the attitude toward using IT for innovating the business i e the proactiveness of the IT stance (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011). Together with the eco-innovation and other IS and non-IS resources, supporting IS in<sup>fl</sup>uence both organizational performance dimensions mentioned by Melville et al. (2004). In doing so, there emerge changes to existing processes and the creation of new ones. These business process changes represent the realized business model changes (Cavalcante et al., 2011).

Furthermore, it emerged that the industry characteristics were not the determining factors. Therefore, we further elaborated the focal-<sup>fi</sup>rm layer and reframed the outer layer as the speci<sup>fi</sup>c institutional environment. The performance impacts of supporting IS described previously are located on the operational, business process layer. Figure 7 depicts our <sup>fi</sup>nal conceptual framework. ## CONTRIBUTIONS

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of Green IS to deal with the role of IS indirectly contributing to environmental sustainability via enhancing the organizationa performance of green technology. Our results indicate that IS in combination with ecoinnovations create hybrid solutions, containing physical and digital elements, that allow for com mercial and professional eco-innovation deployment on a larger scale than without these IS. Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that supporting IS enable the creation of completely new sustainable procedures. Thus, following the RBV, there emerge complementarities in that supporting IS enhance the value of the other resources (the eco-innovation) for the <sup>fi</sup>rm, leading to an overall positive impact on organizational performance (Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005; Rivard et al., 2006). As this makes it more likely that more eco-innovations will be deployed (Del Rio Gonzalez, 2005), supporting IS can be a major driver in business transformation toward sustainability (Elliot, 2011), as we elaborate in the following.

## New functional affordances of green IS: technological <sup>fl</sup>exibility and digita eco-innovation

Supporting IS extend the functional affordances of Green IS derived by Seidel et al. (2013) described previously. Although also targeting the category of sustainable practicing, supporting IS go beyond the output management and delocalization of affordances, as they allow for the employment of environmentally friendly technologies in an economic way. In doing so, they offer ‘technological <sup>fl</sup>exibility’, i.e. the economical use of alternative technologies in the business con text, which in turn has transformative effects. As our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate, supporting IS – through the information and automation roles – drive business process performance by creating connectivity and integration among users, technologies and infrastructures. In doing so, they add a digital layer to the physical activities in business processes, thereby providing employees as well as management with enhanced information and thus reduced uncertainty. As a result, the business processes become disentangled from the physical components, e.g. established technologies, employed within them. This increased certainty about the process activities allows for the targeted implementation of technological alternatives with a reduced negative impact on the environment, i.e. eco-innovations. Thus, supporting IS help to provide an infrastructural (Tilson et al., 2010) background in which the eco-innovation can be employed as ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently as possible. By doing so, supporting IS in combination with eco-innovations can replace existing unsustainable technologies without weakening business process performance (Melville et al., 2004). This technological <sup>fl</sup>exibility afforded by supporting IS is in turn essential for the further corporate adoption of eco-innovations on a larger scale (Del Rio Gonzalez, 2005) and drives eco-ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, i.e. the ‘production of economically valuable goods and services while reducing the ecologica impacts of production’ (Carrillo-Hermosilla et al., 2010, p. 1076).

However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that supporting IS also constitute another functional affordance of Green IS, which we, based on Yoo et al. (2010), call ‘digital eco-innovation’, i.e. the carrying out of new combinations of digital and physical components to produce novel, environmentally sustainable outcomes (products, services, business models). When eco-innovations are implemented in existing businesses, they often produce extra costs as additional efforts become necessary (Carrillo-Hermosilla et al., 2010). As described previously, when supporting IS are deployed mind fully with the eco-innovation, these ‘negative’ adaptations of business processes can be reduced, and ‘positive’ changes, i.e. the creation of new sustainability-related procedures, become possible. By adding software-based capabilities (Yoo et al., 2010), new functionalities are enabled that utilize the potentials and speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of physical eco-innovations and thus create entirely new sustainable business activities that drive organizational goal achievement. Concerning the typology of different eco-innovations (Section 2) by Carrillo-Hermosilla et al. (2010), EVs combined with supporting IS can be transformed from being end-of-pipe-technologies toward the system change category and thus toward eco-effectiveness. This implies not only a reduction of the negative impacts of conventional vehicle use but also emphasizes the positive impacts of the new functionalities and processes enabled by supporting IS. New processes and business models that are sustainable from the beginning become attainable, representing a major contribution to sustainability transfor mation (Schaltegger et al., 2012; Seidel et al., 2013).

## Characteristics and managerial awareness of elaborated role of green IS in business transformation toward sustainability

According to prior, literature. IT-enabled business transformation is associated with fundamenta changes to core business processes (e.g. Besson & Rowe, 2012; Lucas et al., 2013). This also holds true for sustainability transformation (Seidel et al., 2013). The majority of Green by IS instances reported in prior literature are concerned with monitoring and evaluating sustainability aspects of existing procedures (Elliot, 2011; Jenkin et al., 2011) or describe single systems for speci<sup>fi</sup>c purposes, e.g. delocalization by a video conferencing tool (Seidel et al., 2013). Because of the generally smal adaptations in business that result from these green IS instances, the degree of business transforma tion often remains low. The enhanced integration and connectivity of eco-innovations with other resources in the business processes – achieved through supporting IS’ automate and informate roles (Dehning et al., 2003) – are related to a low-to-medium degree of IT-enabled business transformation as they allow for interconnected and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently executed business processes (Venkatraman, 1994). However, through the transformate role (Dehning et al., 2003), whereby the business subjects (e.g. selling energy services instead of only delivering parcels) and partners (e.g. electricity brokers) are also changed by the potential of supporting IS, the revolutionary degrees of IT-enabled business transformation are achieved as well (Venkatraman, 1994).

By doing so, supporting IS and the knowledge and capabilities connected with them can act as a pivotal factor, i.e. an important transformation driver that incorporates a changed role and a new perspective for Green IS research. Research thus far has often described the direct potential of, e.g., new IS applications regarding the environmental footprint of the <sup>fi</sup>rm by either reducing the negative impacts of IS themselves or non-IS business practices (Faucheux & Nicolaï, 2011). In this way, Green IS act as an operand resource in organizational transformation toward sustainability (Seidel et al., 2013). However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that there might be another role of Green IS (speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, supporting IS), in which it operates on other resources (EVs in our case) to create entirely new systems, thus acting as an operant resource in the transformation process (Nambisan, 2013). This role as a trigger for sustainable innovation implies an even bigger impact on achieving sustainability and offers ample opportunity for future research in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of Green IS.

Moreover, our study also describes the importance of managerial awareness of this expanded potential of Green IS. To us, this is part of a larger development that implies that managers of all kinds – also in non-IS industries – should consider IS when they innovate or transform the business (Nambisan, 2013). The affordances of digital (eco-)innovation create substantial deviations in managerial thinking and organization in non-IS industries (Yoo et al., 2012). As our study emphasizes, it is not only the eco-innovation of EVs and the respective business contexts that determine the performance consequences but also the complementary use of supporting IS. Consequently, an eco-innovation might display poor organizational performance on its own but with a mindful level of supporting IS could be a bene<sup>fi</sup>cial alternative. In turn, supporting IS that is too extensive might diminish business performance in contexts in which they are not of particular importance. Identifying and adapting the potential of Green IS constitutes an important managerial capability that must be further differentiated by future research. For Green IS, as a young domain, this may be helpful as it helps in avoiding the in<sup>fl</sup>ation of expectations and highlights the importance of context for the employment of Green IS. We thus provide important organizational contingency factors to take into account when considering the under-researched theme of Green IS’ impact.

## IMPLICATIONS FOR MANAGERIAL PRACTICE AND LIMITATIONS

When judging eco-innovations, there should be a further categorization between costs of being green and payoffs of being green called ‘payoffs if implemented mindfully with supporting IS’. The concept of mindfulness is of particular value, as it does not emphasize the actual use of

IS for every <sup>fi</sup>rm in every situation, which could lead to disillusionment and disappointment but instead underscores the consideration of IS and the careful assessment of the opportunities and risks involved for the speci<sup>fi</sup>c organization – a major implication for business managers. Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that with supporting IS, businesses can achieve both an economical substitution of prior unsustainable technologies as well as a more profound innovation of the respective system. Hence, supporting IS can help managers to realize not only the compliance aspect of sustainable business transformation but also the chances of proactive innovation toward new environmentally and economically sustainable business practices.

We thus help managers to avoid being too focused on their established paths and logics, which could result in an evaluation of sustainable technologies that falls short of the hidden potential possibly enabled by supporting IS. On the other hand, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that supporting IS need to be deployed mindfully. We therefore do not advocate that every <sup>fi</sup>rm em ploy as much supporting IS as possible; instead, its potential should be carefully investigated, and then scarce resources be allocated according to the most critical business areas. In such a case, other kinds of Green IS (or Green IT) might be a reasonable option.

However, several limitations are connected to our study. First, our qualitative study covers only one particular eco-innovation, which was necessary in order to explore the relevant interrelations in detail. Therefore, future research could test our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by examining other ecoinnovations. Furthermore, our propositions should be tested quantitatively on a large amount of survey data to account for signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and reliability. Moreover, testing our model in a cross-country case study would re<sup>fi</sup>ne our results.

## CONCLUSION

Supporting IS provide an alternative path to corporate sustainability rather than just contributing to the greening of business practices. Our results indicate that in combination with eco-innovations, they create hybrid solutions, containing physical and digital elements that allow for commercial and professional eco-innovation deployment on a larger scale than without these IS. Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that supporting IS enable the creation of completely new sustainable procedures. With these two aspects, we add two functional affordances – technological <sup>fl</sup>exibility and digital eco-innovation – to the existing knowledge base of Green IS. Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd the realization of supporting IS potential is dependent on the organizational aspiration level for the eco-innovation use and the proactiveness of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT stance. A mindful use of supporting IS can transform eco-innovations from additional cost factors into important contributors to current and future business success, highlighting the pivotal role of supporting IS in sustainability transformations.

## Biographies

Andre Hanelt is head of the Digital Transformation Research Center (DTRC) at the Chair of Information Management at the University of Göttingen, where he also obtained his PhD. His research interests relate to the transformative impacts of IS and the implications for organizations on their journey towards conducting sustainable business in a digitalized business environment. His research has been published in leading international conferences (e.g., International Conference on Information Systems) and international journals, including International Journal of Innovation Management and Information Systems Journal.

Sebastian Busse is a managing consultant at UNITY AG, the management consultancy for future-oriented corporate management. He advises his industry clients regarding IT strategy and digital transformation. As a guest lecturer at TU Braunschweig Sebastian teaches students in developing digital business model innovations. He obtained his PhD at the Chair of Information Management at University of Göttingen where he successfully established and headed the university’s Sustainable Mobil ity Research Group. His research has been published in leading international conferences (e.g., International Con ference on Information Systems, European Conference on Information Systems).

Lutz M. Kolbe is a full professor and Chair of Information Management at the Georg-August-University of Göttingen, Germany, since 2007. At the Technical University Freiberg, Germany, he earned his doctorate in Business Administration. Subsequently, he led Deutsche Bank’s IT think tanks in Frankfurt, New York, and San Francisco. In 2002, he moved on to the Institute of Information Systems at the University of St. Gallen, Switzerland, as head of several competence centers. He was also a visiting scholar at the University of Rhode Island, New York Univer sity, and IBM Research Almaden (USA). His main research interests are IT Innovation Management and Sustainable Information Management. His research has been published in leading international journals, including Energy Policy, Information Systems Journal. and MIS Quarterly Executive.
