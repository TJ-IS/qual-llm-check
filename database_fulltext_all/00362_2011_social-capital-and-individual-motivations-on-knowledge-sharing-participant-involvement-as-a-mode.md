# Social capital and individual motivations on knowledge sharing: Participant involvement as a moderator

Hsin Hsin Chang \*, Shuang-Shii Chuang

National Cheng Kung University, Department of Business Administration, Institute of International Business, Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 8 December 2009 Received in revised form 16 July 2010 Accepted 19 September 2010 Available online 1 December 2010

Keywords: Social capital Knowledge sharing Individual motivations Participant involvement Virtual community

## A B S T R A C T

The Internet is a communication channel that allows individuals to share information and knowledge. However, it is not obvious why individuals share knowledge with strangers for no apparent benefit. What are the critical factors influencing such behavior? To attempt to understand this paradox, we combined the theories of social capital and individual motivation to investigate the factors influencing knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community, applying a participant involvement concept to analyze the moderating effects of individual motivation on knowledge sharing behavior. By analyzing the results of a survey using a questionnaire, we found that altruism, identification, reciprocity, and shared language had a significant and positive effect on knowledge sharing. Reputation, social interaction, and trust had positive effects on the quality, but not the quantity, of shared knowledge. Participant involvement had a moderating effect on the relationship of altruism and the quantity of shared knowledge. Theoretical and managerial implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The Internet has become an inexpensive medium that enables millions of people worldwide to exchange information and knowledge. It has drastically changed the scope, boundaries, and dynamics of social interactions. It provides a platform to support individual communication unrestricted by the constraints of time and space. Moreover, online social networks have become a part of the lives of users, serving to satisfy the desire to interact with and help others. According to Breslin and Decker [8], the 10 most popular domains account for about 40% of all page views on the Web, and nearly half of those views are from two social networking sites: MySpace and Facebook. In Taiwan, although the most used Internet application is still webpage searching and surfing, participating in virtual communities has become one of the major user activities.

Due to the pervasiveness of virtual communities, recent studies have focused on communities of practice in organizations [19], specific professional virtual communities [10], and educational learning communities [22]. In addition, Algesheimer et al. [1] considered the value of virtual communities in business.

Although people can obtain abundant information and knowledge from communities, there is no guarantee that they will share their knowledge without expecting a return. According to Bock et al. [6], individuals contribute knowledge only if their expected benefits overweigh the costs. In addition, spontaneous knowledge sharing behavior can be regarded as organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) [30].

Many authors have used TAM (the technology acceptance model) to explain the behavior of users, but we decided to apply social capital theory (SCT) to explain participation in online social networks [26]. Based on our literature review, we felt that a network of relationships in online communities could provide the resources that facilitate knowledge sharing between participants. Therefore, we attempted to enhance the notion of social capital by applying it to general virtual communities.

However, there has been little research on virtual communities examining the moderating effect of involvement. Our study applied the concept of involvement as a moderating construct to analyze how it affects the influence of individual motivation toward knowledge sharing behavior in virtual communities.

The main objectives of our study were to (1) investigate participant behavior and participants’ interactive relationships within virtual communities and (2) incorporate both individual and organizational perspectives to determine their effect on knowledge sharing. These objectives were proposed to help in answering three research questions:

(1) Why are people willing to share personal knowledge and participate in a virtual community?

(2) How can individual motivation and social capital facilitate knowledge sharing behavior in the context of a general virtual community?

(3) How can participant involvement moderate the relationship between individual motivation and knowledge sharing behavior?

## 2. Theoretical background and research hypotheses

People form groups and communities for reasons that include sharing their experiences, among many others. Rothaermel and Sugiyama [21] defined a community as a group of people meeting to perform a mutual obligation or to share particular interests. Muniz and O’Guinn [17] mention community as an important concept of sociology and identified three community factors: consciousness and intrinsic connection to other groups, shared rituals and tradition, and a shared sense of moral responsibilities. Virtual communities are developed around affinities, shared interests, professional disciplines, common practice, and values. They are formed and evolve through the participation and interaction of participants [18]. A virtual community is totally different from a conventional one; participants interact with each other via a communication system instead of face to face. In addition, communities do not have concrete reward systems to foster knowledge sharing; therefore, individual motivation is necessary to sustain participation.

Thus, a virtual community is a social network, such as bulletin board system (BBS) and an online discussion forum, among others. It is not limited by space and time, and participants in a virtual community have common interests and characteristics that lead them to interact with each other regularly via cyberspace. Mutual interaction and communication and a long-term relationship including shared content are the keys for a sustainable virtual community.

## 2.1. Knowledge sharing

Virtual communities provide an opportunity for users to interact and form relationships. Knowledge is accrued by integrating information, experience, and theory. Tacit knowledge can only be shared by interpersonal means while explicit knowledge can be delivered via technology-driven or structured processes. Individuals will participate in a virtual community to share or exchange knowledge if the personal perceived benefit outweighs the perceived loss of valuable knowledge.

Furthermore, knowledge sharing requires the effort of the individuals who do the sharing and are involved in the social process.

## 2.2. Social capital theory

Social capital has been conceptualized as the sum of the assets or resources embedded in the networks of relationships between individuals, communities, networks, or societies. It exists through interpersonal relationships among individuals. Therefore, social capital is embedded in the relationships between individuals and their connections with their communities.

The structural dimension deals the overall pattern of relationships found in organizations. It describes the impersonal configuration of linkages between people or units and the extent to which people in an organization are connected with one another [7]. The relational dimension deals with the nature of the connections between individuals in an organization. The key facets of this dimension are trust, norms, obligations, expectations and identification. The cognitive dimension of social capital concerns the extent to which people in a social network share a common perspective or understanding. The critical resources of this dimension may be shared language and codes.

Following the work of Chiu’s et al., our study adopted social interaction as the variable of structural social capital, trust, identification and reciprocity as those for relational social capital, and shared language for cognitive social capital. Thus, we could examine how these embedded resources affected the operation of a virtual community. More specifically, social interaction was used to examine the effects of network ties, the utilization of trust, identification, and reciprocity in representing the quality of network relationships and with regard to the use of a shared language to determine the degree of understanding among members.

## 2.2.1. Social interaction for knowledge sharing

In addition, network-related social relationships provide information channels that reduce the time and effort required to gather information. Knowledge sharing is relatively easy to achieve and sustain when networks have strong connections and direct ties between their members.

Chiu et al. argued that social interaction ties among members of a virtual community provided a cost-effective way to share knowledge. The more these social interactions build, the greater the intensity, frequency, and breadth of the knowledge exchanged [29]. Therefore, we posited that social interactions can enhance knowledge sharing behavior:

H1a. Social interaction from participation has a positive effect on the quality of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

H1b. Social interaction from participation has a positive effect on the quantity of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

## 2.2.2. Trust, identification and reciprocity for knowledge sharing

Relational social capital is the affective nature of the connections among individuals that facilitates knowledge exchange. Wasko and Faraj [25] decided that relational capital exists when individuals have a strong identification and trust in a network.

Trust is an important antecedent of cooperation, resource acquisition, and knowledge sharing in virtual communities [20]. When relationships are high with regard to trust, people are more willing to engage in social exchange and cooperative interaction. Inter-personal trust is important in creating an atmosphere for knowledge sharing. Chiu et al. considered trust as a generally accepted set of values, norms, and principles followed by members of a virtual community that increases knowledge sharing. Therefore, we posited that a higher level of trust will lead to increased knowledge sharing.

H2a. Trust from participation has a positive effect on the quality of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

H2b. Trust from participation has a positive effect on the quantity of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

Identification is the process whereby individuals see themselves as grouped with another person or set of people. It reflects individuals’ efforts to express and present themselves to others, and it enhances the degree of knowledge contribution [16]. Dholakia al. [13] suggested that identification is an individual’s willingness to maintain a relationship with a virtual community. Chiu et al. further verified that identification in a virtual community is essential for knowledge sharing. Therefore we made two more hypotheses:

H3a. Identification from participation has a positive effect on the quality of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

H3b. Identification from participation has a positive effect on the quantity of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

Knowledge sharing is also facilitated by a strong sense of reciprocity: favors given and received and a strong sense of fairness. If the invested efforts in knowledge sharing can be reciprocated, members are motivated to contribute more. Finally, Chiu et al. also demonstrated how norms of reciprocity are related to knowledge sharing. Thus:

H4a. Reciprocity from participation has a positive effect on the quality of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

H4b. Reciprocity from participation has a positive effect on the quantity of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

## 2.2.3. Shared language for knowledge sharing

Cognitive social capital involves a shared understanding among individuals, such as a shared language and codes. This results in mutual knowledge [11], which can increase the likelihood of understanding between members because it allows them to formulate their knowledge as a result of knowing what the receiver does and does not know. This also prevents others from gathering the information. Individuals in virtual communities make use of shared understanding to build their own vocabulary, enhancing their efficiency of communication. Thus:

H5a. Shared language from participation has a positive effect on the quality of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

H5b. Shared language from participation has a positive effect on the quantity of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

## 2.3. Individual motivation and knowledge sharing

Knowledge belongs to individuals, and thus knowledge sharing in virtual communities is far from spontaneous, especially when no rewards are given. It cannot be forced but rather can only be encouraged and facilitated.

Knowledge sharing is more likely to occur when individuals are motivated. However, participants risk losing their unique knowledge or reputation if the shared content is unreliable. With no rewards to compensate participants’ sharing costs, the intention to share knowledge will decline.

Knowledge contributors benefit from showing others that they possess valuable expertise, their self image is improved, and they receive recognition as an expert [3], thereby building a reputation. As a result, these personal benefits are the key motivational factors for individuals to share knowledge [15]. Reputation and altruism are the main perceived benefits that enhance knowledge sharing behavior in virtual communities. Thus, we conclude that establishing a reputation and enhancing status are the important factors stimulating participants to provide content by responding more frequently and more intelligently.

According to Yu and Chu, ‘‘spontaneous assistance can be regarded as OCB (organizational citizenship behavior)’’. When a group of individuals work to achieve a goal, they increase their altruistic behavior (helping others without expecting anything in return) [23]. Wasko and Faraj showed that knowledge contributors gain satisfaction by demonstrating their altruistic behavior.

Based on these arguments, we posited:

H6a. Reputation from participation has a positive effect on the quality of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

H6b. Reputation from participation has a positive effect on the quantity of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

H7a. Altruism from participation has a positive effect on the quality of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

H7b. Altruism from participation has a positive effect on the quantity of knowledge sharing behavior in a virtual community.

## 2.4. Participant involvement and knowledge sharing

It is important to know the level of participation of from those who support a community; higher participation means a higher level of involvement.

Researchers; e.g., [12,14], have agreed that there are two main sources of involvement: an enduring perspective, which refers to intrinsic or stable sources, and a situational source, which is specific but temporary. This implies that different types or groups of participants have different roles in a virtual community.

We therefore investigated the impact of involvement on knowledge sharing behavior. Knowledge sharing should be strongly influenced by an individual’s willingness to engage in online interaction [27]. Moreover, different members are significantly different in their interaction frequency. The more participants are involved in the activities of a community, the more intrinsic benefits they will perceive. Studies have suggested that frequently visiting a virtual community and interacting during discussions [28] enhances the levels of participation and involvement.

This intrinsic behavior is more likely to accrue in individuals who actively participate and help others [24]. By extending the concept of involvement, different levels of participant involvement contribute different degrees of content and knowledge. Therefore, participation in virtual community activities may also influence member behavior [2]. They can share information and experience to achieve group cohesion [9]. Thus, we felt that participant involvement had a moderating effect on the relationship between individual motivations (i.e., reputation and altruism) and knowledge sharing. This led to the following hypotheses:

H8a. Reputation has a stronger positive effect on quality of knowledge sharing behavior when members have a higher level of participation involvement in a virtual community.

H8b. Reputation has a stronger positive effect on quantity of knowledge sharing behavior when members have a higher level of participation involvement in a virtual community.

H9a. Altruism has a stronger positive effect on quality of knowledge sharing behavior when members have a higher level of participation involvement in a virtual community.

H9b. Altruism has a stronger positive effect on quantity of knowledge sharing behavior when members have a higher level of participation involvement in a virtual community.

## 3. Conceptual framework and research design

## 3.1. Conceptual framework

Our research framework is depicted in Fig. 1. The quality and quantity of knowledge sharing was our dependent variable. Social capital theory was used to examine the effect of interpersonal relationships through the structural dimension (social interaction), relational dimensions (trust, identification, and reciprocity), and the cognitive dimension (sharing language) serving as antecedents assumed to have a positive effect on the dependent variable. The two individual motivation factors (reputation, and altruism) were also expected to have a positive effect on the dependent variable. Also, participant involvement was assumed to be a moderator that affected the relationships between individual motivations (reputation, and altruism) and the dependent variable.

## 3.2. Research methodology

## 3.2.1. Construct definitions and measurement development

In order to test the proposed hypotheses, we used measurement items found in related studies. Definitions of these constructs and items are summarized in Table 1. The proposed hypotheses were then empirically tested by developing and using a questionnaire. For all the measurement items, a seven-point Likert scale was adopted, with anchors ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). All items are listed in Appendix A.

## 3.2.2. Pilot test

The preliminary questionnaire had 51 items and was sent to people who had participated in virtual communities. Altogether, 45 participants filled out the questionnaires, but three questionnaires were found to be invalid and thus were eliminated. The results were analyzed, and nine items were deleted because their scores were below the normally acceptable reliability thresholds (Cronbach’s a < 0.6). IR03, AL02, AN02 and AN03 were ambiguous, as noted by some respondents. Therefore, we change IR03 ‘‘Participating in the virtual community can learn knowledge to enhance my reputation in the professional field,’’ to ‘‘Participating in the virtual community can enhance my reputation in the professional field.’’ Also AL02, ‘‘The knowledge shared by members in the virtual community is easy to understand’’, became ‘‘The knowledge shared by members in the virtual community is understandable’’. AN02, ‘‘Members can obtain abundant content and knowledge from the virtual community,’’ was altered to ‘‘Members can obtain considerable content and knowledge from discussions in the virtual community’’, and AN03, ‘‘There are a lot of people viewing and providing feedback to discussed topics and content in the virtual community’’ was divided into two parts: ‘‘There are a lot of people viewing discussions in the virtual community’’ and ‘‘There are a lot of people providing responses to discussions in the virtual community’’. Thus, there were 42 items in the final version.

## 3.2.3. Data analysis procedure and methods

The formal questionnaire was posted on a survey website (http://qqq.cht.com.tw). The link to this website site was also posted on several BBSs, online discussion forums, and weblogs so as to deliver the questionnaire to members participating in virtual communities. All virtual community members and participants were considered valid if they answered the questionnaire.

## 4. Results of data analysis

## 4.1. Measurement assessment

The t-value of the items ranged from 8.54 to 27.4, exceeding the significance level. However, the factor loading for AL01 (0.48) was lower than the recommended value 0.5; therefore, we excluded it in further analyses.

We used a CFA to test the validity of the measures. As shown in Table 3, the results supported convergent validity for all measures: all estimated loadings were significant. Chi-square normalized by degrees of freedom (CMIN/DF) was 2.36, which is less than the maximum of 3, and the CFI, the GFI, and the RMSEA were satisfactory (0.90, 0.77 and 0.069, respectively). Cronbach’s alpha of the constructs exceeded the normally accepted 0.7 threshold. The minimum reliability (CR) of these measures was over the threshold of 0.7. In addition, the AVE across the constructs exceeded 0.5. As Table 3 shows, the smallest AVE of the constructs was 0.59. The discriminant validity of the research instrument was assessed by checking that the square root of the VE from the construct was greater than the correlation shared between the construct and other constructs in the model (see Table 4).

## 4.2. Hierarchical regression analysis

We tested all the hypotheses using hierarchical regression analysis: we averaged all scales to form composites. Once these had been formed, the constructs of the independent variables were mean centered to avoid the potential of multicollinearity in the interaction terms. Then interaction terms were estimated by taking the product of the mean-centered constructs. (It is favorable when the variance inflation factor (VIF) scores are well within the cutoff of 10.) In order to examine the hypothesized main and interaction effects, we used a two-tailed test to ensure the significance of the standardized coefficients [5].

Each dependent variable used six models to test the hypotheses. Some variables had been found to be significant in earlier models (Models 1–4) but became insignificant in Model 6, which contained every variable. Thus we inferred that the theoretically supported relationships might be reversed because of multicollinearity. In addition, the variables were significantly correlated, see Table 5, and the VIF range increased in the full model, see Table 6.

Model 1 estimated the relationships between individual motivations and the quality and quantity of shared knowledge. This showed that KS had a positive and significant effect on quality but it had no significant effect on the quantity of KS; therefore, our results supported H6a but not H6b. In addition, both H7a and H7b were fully supported, as the effects of altruism on the quality and quantity of KS were positive and statistically significant.

Model 2 included social interaction; it therefore tested the relationships between structural social capital and the quality and quantity of KS. Our results showed that H1a was supported, but social interaction had no significant effect on the quantity of KS; thus, H1b was not supported.

Model 3 added the three relational social capital variables to test their effects on the quality and quantity of KS. H2a was supported by the positive and significant effect of trust on quality of KS, but H2b was not. Moreover, H3a and H3b were supported by the positive and significant effects of identification on the quality and quantity of KS; H4a and H4b were also supported.

Model 4 included shared language in order to see its effects on the quality and quantity of KS. The results fully support H5a and H5b.

## 4.3.1. T-test analysis for gender

There was no statistical difference between male and female groups except for reputation and shared language. Thus, gender affects member perception regarding gaining reputation and also influences the way which people use shared language to communicate. We used a t-test analysis to see if people’s sharing experience had any impact on each construct. The means of the two groups for each variable were statistically different. Thus, sharing experience has no effect on reputation, trust, reciprocity, and shared language. However, the experience of sharing knowledge affects the perception of altruism, social interaction, identification, the quality of KS, the quantity of KS, and participant involvement. It is likely that members with experience in sharing knowledge somehow have a greater degree of motivation and create more social capital in communities.

## 4.3.2. One-way ANOVA

(1) Age: The study found no statistical difference between any two groups for each dependent variable. It seems that age had no impact on our research structure.

(2) Days per week of using the Internet: Except for the quality of KS, there was no statistical difference between any two groups. Specifically, the study found that there were some differences between the two groups with regard to the quality of KS. According to the Scheffe post hoc test, the mean of less than one day was significantly higher than the mean of one to four days per week although the results might imply that people rarely using the Internet will believe that the information that they gather in the community is of higher quality than that garnered elsewhere.

(3) Hours per day of using Internet: No difference was found between any two groups for any variable. Thus, use per day had no influence on the research outcome.

(4) Most used virtual community: There was no statistical difference between the two groups for each variable. Although the F-values of identification (2.43) and shared language (3.06) were significant, we did not find differences between any groups for these variables.

## 5. Conclusions and recommendations

## 5.1. Discussion

Our study verified the relationships of the theoretical model, and most of the hypotheses were statistically supported. Contrary to our expectation, reputation was not found to motivate members to increase the quantity of knowledge sharing. Knowledge contributors seemed more concerned with increasing the quality of shared knowledge. Although our research sample was not all from anonymous communities, members generally used nicknames instead of their real names. In fact, they rarely knew other participants’ real identities. Apparently intrinsic benefits (i.e., altruism) are more influential than extrinsic rewards (i.e., reputation) in determining online knowledge sharing.

Social capital developed in virtual communities plays an important role in knowledge sharing. When members have intensive interactions and trust one another, they tend to share reliable knowledge. Members who perceive fairness, experience a sense of belonging, and who share the same language may provide more high-quality knowledge. However, social interaction and trust are two variables that do not increase the quantity of knowledge shared. Coordinators are members who organize events and connect community members, so their task is to manage the performance of knowledge sharing.

Contrary to our assumptions, when considering participant involvement, reputation was not positively related to overall knowledge sharing behavior, and altruism was not positively related to the quality of knowledge. Although individuals in networks of practice are capable of sharing a great deal of knowledge, the intensity of user involvement is largely confined to the volume of communication [4]. The same situation is also found in the relationships among altruism, knowledge quality and participant involvement.

## 5.2. Conclusions

The objective of our study was to investigate participant behavior and participants’ interactive relationships from both individual and organizational perspectives. We found that individual motivation motivates participants to contribute quality knowledge to the community. From an organizational perspective, the dimensions of structure (social interaction), relation (trust, identification, and reciprocity), and cognition (shared language) help participants contribute both quality and quantity of knowledge to the community. Also, participant involvement only has a moderating effect on the relationship between altruism and the quantity of knowledge sharing behavior.

## 5.3. Implications

## 5.3.1. Theoretical implications

Our study contributed an overall conceptual understanding by combining perspectives from the personal cognition and community relationships. From the standpoint of the individual, there are factors that motivate people to participate in social exchanges. Facets of social capital are found to explain motivation leading to knowledge sharing.

We focused more on the general virtual community, which has diversified topics and discussions and in which relationship among members are weak. This provided an important understanding of the core elements that motivate and facilitate knowledge sharing. More specifically, our findings implied that intrinsic rewards, the relational social capital, and shared language are the main factors influencing participants to share knowledge. Our study adopted self-report scales using measurement items from prior studies.

## 5.3.2. Managerial implications

People interested in developing and sustaining knowledge sharing in virtual communities may develop substantial mechanisms that can motivate members to participate in collective actions. For example, managers can design reward systems to provide evidence of member participation. These may be extrinsic rewards, such as value-added points. When sharers provide high quality knowledge, reward systems or receivers may offer valueadded points that can attest to their effort and performance.

Secondly, managers in virtual communities may hold periodic face-to-face meetings to introduce participants to one another, thus helping to enhance social interaction and build trust.

Finally, participants with a higher level of involvement can contribute more value to a virtual community. Our results indeed confirm that participant involvement is likely to enhance the quantity of members’ knowledge contribution. Managers should motivate members to participate in community activities eagerly and often. In order to generate core members, mangers should aim at individuals who have longer tenure and experience in the community.

## 5.4. Research limitations

There were several limitations to our study, the first of which is the sample composition. Most of the data was from students who fell into a young age group. However, young people are in fact dominant on the Internet.

Secondly, virtual communities, in which topics are diverse, may not be generalized to other types of communities.

Thirdly, we conducted research using a self-motivated questionnaire. Community members may behave differently when they participate in a different forum (such as a game and Wikipedia), and this may cause bias with regard to both knowledge quality and quantity.

Participants who do not share their knowledge become lurkers in the community, and they are not very motivated to add knowledge. They choose to participate only to receive free content and knowledge. Participants sometimes cannot recognize the motivation for their behavior.

## Appendix A. Questionnaire: measured items

Social interaction (source: [10,29])

SI01: I maintain close social relationships with some members in the virtual community.

SI02: I spend a lot of time interacting with some members in the virtual community.

SI03: I have frequent communication with some members in the virtual community.

SI04: I know some members in the virtual community on a personal level.

SI05: I am not interested in participating in the virtual community.

Trust (source: [10,20])

RT01: Members in the virtual community will not take advantage of others even when the opportunity arises.

RT02: Members in the virtual community will always keep the promises they make to one another.

RT03: Members in the virtual community would not knowingly do anything to disrupt the conversation.

RT04: Members in the virtual community behave in a consistent manner.

RT05: Members in the virtual community are truthful in dealing with one another.

Identification (source: [10])

ID01: I feel a sense of belonging toward the virtual community. ID02: I have a feeling of togetherness or closeness in the virtual community.

ID03: I have a strong positive feeling toward the virtual community.

ID04: I am proud to be a member of the virtual community. Reciprocity (source: [10,26])

RR01: It is fair to help each other in a virtual community.

RR02: I know that other members in the virtual community will help me, so it’s only fair to help other members.

RR03: I believe that members in the virtual community would help me if I needed it.

Shared language ([10])

SL01: Members in the virtual community use common terms or jargons.

SL02: Members in the virtual community use an understandable communication pattern during discussions.

SL03: Members in the virtual community use understandable narrative forms to post messages or articles.

Reputation (source: [26])

IR01: I earn respect from others by participating in the virtual community.

IR02: I feel that participation improves my status in the virtual community.

IR03: Participating in the virtual community can enhance my reputation in my professional field.

IR04: I can earn some feedback or rewards through participation that represent my reputation and status in the virtual community.

Altruism (source: [26,30])

IA01: I am willing to help other participants in the virtual community.

IA02: I like to help other participants in the virtual community. IA03: I feel happy to support other participants to solve problems in the virtual community.

IA04: I enjoy helping other participants in the virtual community since it results in my own achievements.

Quality of shared knowledge (source: [10,26])

AL01: The knowledge shared by members in the virtual community is understandable.

AL02: The knowledge shared by members in the virtual community is accurate.

AL03: The knowledge shared by members in the virtual community is complete.

AL04: The knowledge shared by members in the virtual community is reliable.

Quantity of shared knowledge (source: [10,26])

AN01: New content and knowledge are shared or posted frequently in the virtual community.

AN02: Members can obtain abundant content and knowledge from the virtual community.

AN03: There are a lot of people viewing discussions in the virtual community.

AN04: There are a lot of people providing responses to discussions in the virtual community.

Participant involvement (source: [12])

PI01: I am interested in participating in the virtual community (VC).

PI02: It is pleasurable and enjoyable for me to participate in the virtual community.

PI03: Participating in the VC can help me to express who I am since others can understand what I am interested in.

PI04: It is important for me to participate in the virtual community.

PI05: I spend a lot time and effort participating in the virtual community actively.

PI06: I eagerly reply to postings by help-seekers in the virtual community.
