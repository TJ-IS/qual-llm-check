# Competing “Creatively” in Sponsored Search Markets: The Effect of Rank, Differentiation Strategy, and Competition on Performance

Animesh Animesh

Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, Quebec H3A 1G5, Canada, animesh.animesh@mcgill.ca

Siva Viswanathan, Ritu Agarwal

Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742 {sviswana@rhsmith.umd.edu, ragarwal@rhsmith.umd.edu}

lthough efficiency-enhancing features of online markets have been well studied, much less is known about firms’ differentiation strategies in these competitive markets or the outcomes of such differentiation. This study examines competition among firms in online sponsored search markets—one of the fastest growing and most competitive of online markets. We develop and test a model that predicts the clickthrough rate (CTR) of a seller’s listing in a sponsored search setting. Drawing on consumer search theory and competitive positioning strategies, we theorize that CTR is jointly driven by a seller’s positioning strategy as reflected by the unique selling proposition (USP) in its “ad creative,” by its rank in a sponsored search listing, and by the nature of competition around the focal firm’s listing. We use data from a field experiment conducted by a leading firm in the mortgage industry where the firm varied its rank and USP dynamically. Results suggest that sponsored search listings can act as effective customer segmentation mechanisms, consistent with a model of consumer search in directional markets. We further find that the effect on CTR of a firm’s positioning strategy and its rank in a listing is strongly moderated by its ability to differentiate itself from adjacent rivals. We discuss the impli cations of our findings for sellers’ strategies in sponsored search markets and for extending the understanding of consumer search behavior in directional markets.

Key words: online competition; online differentiation; e-commerce; Internet marketing; online search behavior; sponsored search

## 1. Introduction

Online marketplaces have significantly transformed traditional commerce, influencing buyer-seller interactions across many industries. Among the most widely discussed effects is their role in lowering buyers’ search costs and the resulting impact on market efficiency (e.g., Alba et al. 1997, Bailey et al. 1999, Bakos 1997, Bakos et al. 2005, Smith 2001, Smith and Brynjolfsson 2001). However, though it is true that lower search costs for consumers have led to increased price competition in online markets, it is also well known that firms in competitive settings successfully differentiate themselves from their rivals to reduce price pressures. Such positioning and differentiation strategies have been extensively studied in traditional settings (Deephouse 1999, Economides 1986, Netz and Taylor 2002) but, suprisingly, very few studies (for exceptions, see Bakos et al. 2005, Kuksov 2004, Lynch and Ariely 2000) have examined the differentiation strategies adopted by sellers to counteract the increased competition in online markets or the effectiveness of such differentiation. In this paper, we investigate the outcomes of differentiation on price and quality attributes for a focal firm competing for customers in one of the fastest growing and most competitive online markets—sponsored search advertising.

In online sponsored search (also known as “paid search” or “pay for placement” or “keyword”) markets, firms compete to be listed at the top of search results generated in response to a user’s query (keyword search) on search engines such as Google and Yahoo!. Sponsored search markets have evolved to become a dominant mechanism for customer acquisition online, with firms reallocating significant proportions of their advertising budgets from traditional media to online sponsored search. In 2005, for instance, sponsored searches accounted for more than

40% of the total online advertising dollars spent by companies in the United States (Markoff and Ives 2005). These markets exhibit a number of unique characteristics that make them ideal test beds for understanding the effectiveness of online differentiation strategies. As noted earlier, the intensity of competition in these settings is substantial and is reflected in the staggering costs for the top slots for several product keywords. Firms are willing to incur these costs because sponsored search markets allow them to precisely target consumers who are actively searching for specific products or services. In addition, online sponsored search markets use a pay-per-click pricing mechanism in contrast to most traditional advertising formats that adopt a lump sum pay-per-exposure pricing mechanism. This provides an opportunity for numerous smaller, less-established firms to compete with their larger, well-established counterparts, diminishing the role of brand name as a significant differentiating factor and intensifying competition for the top slots.

Finally, and most significantly, the ordered listing of advertisers in sponsored search markets creates an information environment such that consumers tend to browse through listings sequentially in the order they are listed.<sup>1</sup> This “sequential search” creates a directional market,<sup>2</sup> where firms listed at the top have an advantage over firms appearing lower in a listing (Arbatskaya 2007). The directional nature of online sponsored search markets and the resulting sequential nature of consumer search further heightens competition for the top ranks and the associated cost to firms for using these markets.

Given the nature of this heightened online competition, it is evident that firms will attempt to craft alternate differentiation strategies. Firms competing in traditional contexts may rely on factors such as physical location and appearance but these factors are not typically significant sources of distinction in the online world. Furthermore, questions related to the strategies firms adopt while competing online and how effective these strategies are remain, for the most part, unanswered. To the extent that sponsored search markets constitute a significant forum for customer acquisition, and to the degree that the strategic significance of these markets is likely to grow, it is clearly important to gain a better understanding of competition in these online contexts.

To date, extant research on sponsored search has focused primarily on designing optimal rank allocation mechanisms or on developing bidding strategies for sellers to obtain higher ranks in a search listing. Much of the research in this area builds on earlier work in the design of auctions and optimal bidding strategies for firms (Feng et al. 2007, Kitts and Leblanc 2004) and largely treats sellers as homogenous bidders. This body of research ignores other strategic choices made by competing sellers, thereby yielding findings that may not reflect the true reality of this phenomenon. Furthermore, outcomes in sponsored search settings are jointly determined by seller strategies and consumer behaviors. In addition to offering limited understanding about the characteristics of bidders, prior research has failed to integrate work on consumer search behavior into the sponsored search context to explore the implications of these behaviors for seller strategies.

The focus of this study is the unanswered questions about sellers’ positioning strategies, consumers search behaviors, and the ensuing market dynamics in sponsored search settings. Drawing on the consumer search, directional markets, and competitive positioning literatures, we identify factors that influence the performance of a focal firm’s advertisement (as measured by clickthrough rates (CTR)) over and above the effect of its rank in a listing. We use data from a field experiment to examine how a seller’s positioning strategy (i.e., its unique selling proposition (USP)), as highlighted in its “ad creative,” influences the effectiveness of its listing in the sponsored search market. We also examine the performance effects of the interaction between a firm’s positioning strategy and its rank in a sponsored search listing. The primary context for the study is a firm in the mortgage industry. Though the generalizability of the findings to other settings is in need of further empirical verification, the study is among the first to examine positioning strategies and competition in a sponsored search market.

Our results are consistent with a model of consumer search in directional markets and suggest that sponsored search listings can act as an effective customer segmentation mechanism. We further find that the effects on CTR of a seller’s positioning strategy and rank in the listing are strongly moderated by the seller’s ability to differentiate itself from its rivals. Overall, the findings of this study extend the understanding of consumer search behavior in directional markets and yield important implications for sellers ad positioning and rank allocation strategies in sponsored search markets.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a brief overview of the sponsored search advertising context. Section 3 reviews sponsored search literature and other relevant literature from economics, marketing, and information systems that have studied consumer search, and also examines competitive and positioning strategies. In $\ S 4 ,$ we present research hypotheses. The research methodology is described in §5, followed by results in §6. Section 7 provides a discussion of the findings and their implications, and §8 concludes the paper.

## 2. Research Context: Sponsored Search Markets

As noted earlier, sponsored search is the fastest growing of all online advertising formats including display advertising, search advertising, sponsorship, referral, e-mail, rich media, slotting, and classifieds. According to a recent survey, search advertising was a \$6.80 billion industry in 2006 (approximately 40% of total U.S. online advertising spending that year and approximately 3% of the \$240 billion U.S. overall advertising industry) and that spending is expected to more than double to \$14.9 billion in 2010 (eMarketer 2007).

A unique aspect of sponsored search is the integration and coevolution of search and advertising business models. Though it is well-understood that search and advertising are two sides of the same coin (the objective of each being to match buyers and sellers), the ability of firms to target their advertising to consumers or the ability of consumers to selectively seek out advertisements has been limited by the constraints imposed by traditional media. In contrast, sponsored search mechanisms enable firms to target consumers who are actively searching for products or services offered by those firms. Given that over 67% of online consumers seeking information use search engines as part of their purchase process (iCrossing 2005), the integration of search and advertising is appropriate.

A typical sponsored search advertisement contains a title line and 2 lines of description, generally between 25 and 45 characters in length, followed by the website address (i.e., URL) of the advertiser. This text, also referred to as the “creative,” plays an important role as it conveys information about the firm and its offerings to potential consumers (see Figure 1 for examples of ad creatives used by different advertisers selling digital cameras). In essence, the creative conveys the positioning strategy of the seller in this market, i.e., the seller’s unique selling proposition (USP). Sellers select keywords that are relevant to their product or service and bid on them for enhanced placement $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ higher rank) of their advertisements in sponsored search results. The higher the bid, the higher the seller’s message appears in a sponsored search list. This should lead to more sales leads, i.e., clickthroughs (Kitts and Leblanc 2004) and, consequently, greater sales.

Major search engines such as Google and Yahoo! conduct auctions to price advertisements on various ranks in their sponsored listings. Each sponsored listing corresponds to a specific keyword and can be considered as a separate auction market. A bid in an auction represents the amount the seller is willing to pay for every click on its advertisement. The seller pays only for clickthroughs $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ when a user clicks on an advertisement link and visits the seller’s website) and is not charged for exposures or impressions. The clickthrough rate (CTR), which is defined as the total number of clicks received by an ad divided by the total number of times the ad was shown, is a measure of the effectiveness of the ad in attracting consumers to a seller’s website. Because sellers can change their bids in real time, their rank in a listing may change dynamically depending on the rank ordering of the current bids. Thus, a seller cannot lock in a rank for a keyword and has to constantly monitor and actively manage the bidding process. To make it easier for sellers to bid for a specific rank, auctioneers and third parties provide automated bidding tools to adjust bids to maintain a rank in the listing subject to the seller’s maximum bid constraint.

As evident from the brief description above, sponsored search markets require significant strategizing by sellers. Seller strategies need to be shaped not only by the normative implications of auction design but also by the expected response of end consumers to the adopted differentiation strategy. Yet, research providing insight into the implications of these relatively new markets for sellers’ strategies is limited. Our study seeks to fill this gap by examining the effects of a firm’s positioning and differentiation strategies in a sponsored search market on the performance of its sponsored search listings.

## 3. A Conceptual Model of Ad Performance

The conceptual model guiding our study is shown in Figure 2. The performance of a firm’s advertisement in a sponsored search listing is the observable consumer behavior in the form of a clickthrough; representing the culmination of a search process. The search process, in turn, takes place in a context characterized by the strategic decisions made by the seller with regard to ad rank and USP together with the competitive landscape within which the ad is displayed. Here, we will briefly review relevant literature that provides the theoretical foundation for the proposed conceptualization. First, we discuss extant literature on sponsored search listings and underscore the need to adopt a consumer search perspective to understand outcomes in sponsored search markets. Next, we review consumer search literature, focusing on consumer heterogeneity in terms of search intensity and preferences for price versus quality that would affect how many and which ads a consumer samples. Finally, we examine literature on competition, focusing specifically on directional markets to highlight the impact of positioning strategies of competitors and consumer search on seller performance.

## 3.1. Sponsored Search Listings and Consumer Search

Research on sponsored search advertising is at a nascent stage of development. As noted earlier, current research in this field predominantly adopts an “auctions” perspective to examine the interaction between the search intermediary that conducts the sponsored search auction and sellers that bid in these auctions. One stream of research focuses on designing better rank and position allocation mechanisms from the perspective of the search intermediary (Aggarwal et al. 2006, Edelman et al. 2007, Feng et al. 2007, Lahaie 2006, Varian 2007) while a second investigates optimal bidding strategies from the perspective of sellers (Borgs et al. 2006, Kitts and Leblanc 2004, Rusmevichientong and Williamson 2006). In addition, a few studies examine the market dynamics that result from these sponsored search auctions (Animesh et al. 2010, Edelman and Ostrovsky 2007). Research examining consumer search behavior in a sponsored search setting or the implications of sponsored search mechanisms for seller strategies is even more limited. In one of the few scholarly studies on consumer behavior in sponsored search, Jansen and Resnick (2005) examine the perceptions of searchers toward sponsored search listings and find that, compared to organic search listings, consumers are skeptical of sponsored search results and trust them less.

Research in contexts other than sponsored search nonetheless provides useful insights into consumer behavior online. Significant work in economics and information systems suggests that a consumer’s search cost has an influential impact on the price, product positioning, advertising, and other strategic decisions of a firm (Bakos 1997, Stigler 1961). Search costs also determine the competitive dynamics and structure of a market (Stahl 1982) as does consumer welfare (Salop and Stiglitz 1977). Given that the Internet and online marketplaces have significantly lowered a consumer’s search cost, researchers have employed models of consumer search to examine the implications of online markets on various stakeholders (Alba et al. 1997, Lal and Sarvary 1999, Smith and Brynjolfsson 2001). Because consumers using sponsored search markets incur search costs that influence their behavior and market outcomes (Arbatskaya 2007), a consumer search perspective provides an appropriate framework to study the implications of different seller strategies in sponsored search markets.

## 3.2. The Nature of Consumer Search

Two key aspects of the search process are relevant to our conceptualization. One is how much consumers are willing to search (i.e., their search intensity) and the second is the nature of the search process in terms of the specific information sought.

3.2.1. Determinants of Search Intensity. The economics and marketing literatures use a cost-benefit framework to understand consumers’ information search and choice behavior (Ratchford 1982, Stigler 1961). Researchers suggest that consumers differ in terms of their search intensity, i.e., the time and effort spent in searching for sellers in a market. Ratchford (1982) observes that higher relative valuation and large dispersion of prices increase the gains from search, thus leading to greater search. He suggests that education, a proxy for search efficiency, has a positive impact on search whereas income, a proxy for opportunity costs of time, has a negative impact on search intensity. Using a survey methodology, Ratchford and Srinivasan (1993) also find that search intensity (measured as the time spent searching for price) increases with the perceived benefits of search whereas it decreases with consumer knowledge and hourly wage.

Schmidt and Spreng (1996) provide an extensive review of research on the determinants of search intensity and develop a model integrating the psychological search and economics of information (EOI) literatures. They propose that the individual, situational, and environmental variables determining search are mediated by ability to search, motivation to search, and the cost and benefits of search. Such a cost benefit framework has also been employed in online markets (Klein and Ford 2003) and, thus, should be relevant within the sponsored search context. Although consumers will doubtless vary in their search intensity, they are likely to search for information on aspects of the offerings that meet their differing needs.

3.2.2. Price Search vs. Quality Search. A fundamental tenet underlying descriptive and normative models of consumer search is that consumers differ in terms of their needs and product attribute preferences (Bell and Lattin 1998). The two most important attributes of a product or service are price and quality, and consumers differ in the weight that they attach to these attributes in their purchase decisions (Levin and Johnson 1984, Wolinsky 1983). As a result, the type of information (i.e., price versus quality) sought and acquired by consumers depends on their product attribute preferences. For example, qualityseeking consumers have a higher willingness to pay for any quality level as compared to consumers with lower valuations for quality (Desai 2001, Wolinsky 1983). Consequently, given a choice between different types of sellers, consumers with a higher valuation for quality are more likely to search for high-quality sellers than low-price sellers.

Of particular importance to our study is the fact that the differences in preference for quality versus price are known to affect a consumer’s search cost because collecting and comparing quality information is more costly than collecting and comparing price information (Nelson 1970). Today, Internet technologies alter the cost of collecting price and quality information and thus significantly affect consumer behavior (Smith 2001). For instance, research on consumer search behavior in online environments suggests that the differential search cost for obtaining quality information vis-à-vis price information may affect price sensitivity (Diehl et al. 2003, Lynch and Ariely 2000). Given the importance of the price and quality attributes from consumers’ as well as sellers’ perspectives, we focus on differences across consumers in terms of their price versus quality preferences in their searches.

## 3.3. Positioning Strategies and Competition in Directional Markets

Sellers in a market compete with each other by employing different positioning strategies to attract their target consumer segments. There is an extensive literature on the impact of competitive positioning on firms’ performances in traditional nondirectional markets (Deephouse 1999, Economides 1986, Netz and Taylor 2002). Researchers examining such traditional markets or media generally assume that consumers sample firms randomly while searching a medium such as the Yellow Pages (Perry and Wigderson 1986). However, the sponsored search medium creates an environment where the order in which the firms are searched is known, i.e., the order in which the firms are searched is a function of the rank of the firm on a listing (Arbatskaya 2007). The ordered presentation format limits the freedom and flexibility that a consumer may have in browsing advertisements. In related search contexts, researchers have found that the ordinal ranking of firms is strongly related to a consumer’s likelihood of visiting a firm and purchasing from it (Ellison and Ellison 2004, Smith and Brynjolfsson 2001). This pattern of search where consumers find it natural to search from top to bottom of a listing creates a directional market—a market where a consumer’s search cost or transportation cost to visit a seller in the market depends not only on the distance between the consumer’s location and the location of the firm but also on the direction in which the firm is located (Nilssen 1997). For example, a parking garage on a one-way street represents a directional market where it is more costly for a driver to return to the garage located behind her versus going to a garage ahead, irrespective of the distance between the garages (Lai 2001). Another dimension on which markets may exhibit directionality is temporal space, as in markets for radio or television programming and transportation services such as airline flights. Here, it might be less costly for a consumer to wait awhile, relative to her ideal time, than to arrive earlier (or later, depending on the context) to receive service (Cancian et al. 1995, Nilssen 1997).

In sponsored search markets, the directionality is an outcome of cognitive burden as it is cognitively “costlier” for a typical consumer to visit sellers at the bottom of a list before visiting sellers at the top of a listing. This directionality, where consumers search the sellers in a market in the order in which their ads are displayed in a listing, affects the distribution of consumers that each firm encounters. As one might expect, both the strategies employed by firms as well as the resulting competition dynamics in such an ordered medium are likely be different from those in the traditional medium (Arbatskaya 2007, Perry and Wigderson 1986). Much of the consumer search literature addresses behaviors in traditional nondirectional search markets with one notable exception (Arbatskaya 2007). Employing an analytical approach, Arbatskaya examines the impact of ordered search on competition. She suggests that in a directional market, price dispersion will exist and sellers lower on the list will charge a lower price; thus, consumers with lower search costs will search longer and obtain better deals.

In summary, the unique attributes of sponsored search markets and the nature of online competition in these markets require firms to seek appropriate means to differentiate themselves from their rivals. Prior research has suggested that consumers vary in the intensity with which they search, and have heterogeneous preferences in regard to the product attributes (price or quality) they value. To the extent that sponsored search markets are inherently directional in nature, consumer search behavior is also dictated by the order in which firms are listed in the search results. We use these insights from prior work to develop testable hypotheses in the next section.

## 4. Research Hypotheses

Specific instantiations of the conceptual constructs are depicted in the research model (see Figure 3). The key outcome of interest is the performance of the focal firm in the sponsored search market, operationalized as the CTR of its ad. Theoretical arguments for each of the proposed relationships are provided below. Note that the actual process of consumer search behavior is outside the scope of our theoretical and empirical analysis. Our goal is to use prior theory from consumer search behavior as the foundation for developing predictions related to the interaction between consumer behavior and the information environment created by the sponsored search listing, the competitive landscape, and sellers’ positioning strategies.

## 4.1. Sequential Search

The typical format of a sponsored search listing is an ordered display of firms along with their advertising messages. It has been observed that when presented with an ordered list of items, individuals tend to browse through them sequentially in the order in which they are listed. For instance, a recent eye motion study shows that searchers scan a listing from top to bottom and they pay more attention to the advertisements appearing at the top of the listing (Sherman 2005). As noted earlier, this sequential search creates a directional market, where firms listed at the top have an advantage over firms appearing lower in a listing. In addition, consumers are likely to vary in their search intensity because of differences in perceived search costs and benefits (Stigler 1961). Thus, though all consumers may search sequentially from the top to the bottom, consumers with higher search costs (or lower search intensity) will restrict their search to the top sellers in a list. By contrast, consumers with lower search costs (higher search intensity) are more likely to search further down a listing. Given the tendency for consumers to search sequentially and the heterogeneity in consumer search intensity, more consumers are likely to click on sellers with a higher rank or placement. We therefore test the following hypothesis.

## 4.2. The Impact of a Firm’s Unique Selling Proposition (USP)

In addition to the rank of an advertisement, we expect the nature of the message conveyed in an ad’s creative to have an impact on consumer behavior. Although a naive sequential search strategy might be realistic in simple settings, it is likely to be less so in a more complex and high-involvement setting such as shopping or purchase. In a shopping situation, consumers typically seek products and services that meet their needs. As discussed earlier, consumers can be broadly classified as price seeking or quality seeking based on their preferences for price and quality (Desai 2001, Levin and Johnson 1984, Wolinsky 1983). A price-seeking consumer is more likely to search for low-priced offerings while a quality-seeking consumer is likely to search for high-quality offerings. Thus, though all consumers may search sequentially through the ordered listing, consumers with specific preferences for certain attributes will filter out those sellers that do not appear to provide the attribute sought. In other words, a consumer who is quality seeking has a higher likelihood of visiting sellers that signal higher quality while a price-seeking consumer is more likely to sample sellers that signal lower prices. Because firms use their ad creative to differentiate themselves and convey their unique selling proposition to potential customers, consumers will select sellers whose USP signals the attributes they value. The number of potential consumers who may click on an ad will be a function of the number of consumers in the market who seek the same USP as advertised by the seller. As a result, the seller’s USP as highlighted in the ad creative, in addition to its rank in the listing, would affect the clicks received on its sponsored search advertisement. We therefore test the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2A (H2A). The CTR of a seller in a sponsored search listing is influenced by the seller’s positioning strategy as highlighted by the USP in its ad creative.

## 4.3. The “Filter” Effect of a Sponsored Search Listing

Given the directional nature of the sponsored search market, the effectiveness of a firm’s positioning strategy (USP) is likely to be moderated by its rank in the listing. Prior research suggests a correspondence between consumers’ needs and search intensity, stemming from differences in perceived costs and benefits of search (Nelson 1970). For instance, search costs and benefits for a consumer are a function of the consumer’s individual characteristics, i.e., income, education, etc. (Ratchford 1982, Ratchford and Srinivasan 1993, Stigler 1961), as well as the product attribute that is being searched, i.e., the cost of searching and comparing different attributes and the utility derived from the attributes (Andrews and Currim 2002, Schaninger 1981). Therefore, consumers searching for product attributes who obtain greater net marginal benefit from additional searches or have a lower opportunity cost of time because of their individual characteristics would exhibit higher search intensity (Schmidt and Spreng 1996). Because consumers search a listing sequentially from top to bottom, it follows that those with higher search intensity will search further down a listing whereas those with lower search intensity would restrict their search to the top few positions. Thus, the proportion of consumers with a specific product attribute preference will vary as we move down a listing. We label this phenomenon that changes the composition of consumers likely to see a seller on a sponsored search listing as the sponsored search filter (see Figure 4). Because the proportion of consumers searching for a specific product attribute varies across different ranks because of the sponsored search filter effect, the performance of a seller’s USP would depend on the rank of the seller in the listing. Therefore, we test the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2B (H2B). The effect of a seller’s USP on CTR is moderated by the rank at which the seller appears in the sponsored search listing.

H2B asserts a general interaction between the seller’s USP and the rank of the seller in the listing. Next, we develop specific predictions about the differential effect of price and quality USPs at different ranks. In general, price-seeking consumers are likely to have higher search intensities than quality-seeking consumers for at least three reasons. First, research suggests that quality information is more expensive to collect and process as compared to price information (Nelson 1970) and, therefore, searching for quality is likely to be more time-consuming than searching for price. Assessing and comparing the quality of sellers in an online environment as compared with offline settings is exacerbated by the fact that other “physical” signals of quality are not easily available. By contrast, price comparison across sellers online does not rely on qualitative factors such as store appearance and location and is thus more objective and easier. Therefore, quality-seeking consumers would not consider it worthwhile to search further and will be more likely to restrict their searches to sellers in the top ranks.

A second reason that price-seeking consumers are expected to search more arises from the relationship between income, search cost, and preferences of a consumer. Research suggests that income is associated with preferences for higher-quality products. According to the “relative income hypothesis,” income is linearly and positively related to consumption of expensive or high-quality products (Schaninger 1981). High-income consumers have a higher willingness to pay and are less price sensitive across various product categories (Ainslie and Rossi 1998), and low-income consumers who have a lower willingness to pay typically search for low prices (Andrews and Currim 2002). Income has also been identified as a determinant of search costs. As might be expected, the marginal cost of an incremental search effort (i.e., the opportunity cost of time) is higher for a consumer with a higher income (Ratchford 1982, Ratchford and Srinivasan 1993, Stigler 1961). Given the existence of a sponsored search filter effect, a larger proportion of consumers who reach the bottom ranks in the listing would have a lower willingness to pay. To the degree that consumers with a higher willingness to pay (the higher-income group) attach greater weight to the quality dimension than the price dimension vis-à-vis the low-income group, firms at higher ranks should receive a larger proportion of quality-seeking consumers as compared to firms at lower ranks.

Finally, consumers may believe that sellers at the top positions are of higher quality because of either an awareness of the sponsored search mechanism along with an advertisement signaling beliefs or a lack of knowledge about the distinction between organic and sponsored ads and a transfer of positive experience from top-ranked results on the organic search listing to the sponsored listing. As a result, quality-seeking consumers would concentrate their search efforts on the sellers appearing at the top of the listing, and a larger proportion of consumers who reach the bottom ranks in the listings are likely to be price seekers. We therefore test the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2C (H2C). The relative performance (i.e., CTR) of a price USP as compared to a quality USP improves at lower ranks in the sponsored search listing.

## 4.4. The Impact of Competition

Given limited attention and memory, consumers are more likely to compare adjacent sellers offering the attribute that is being sought before visiting a seller’s website. As stated earlier, consumers are expected to click on the seller with a USP that matches their preferences. To the extent that a consumer’s comparison set consists of adjacent sellers in a listing, the constitution of this comparison set should influence the CTR received by a focal firm. In particular, the greater the number of firms with an identical USP within the comparison set, the lesser the differentiation and the higher the competitive intensity. Assuming that consumers select a subset of sellers from a group of adjacent sellers employing a similar USP while searching sequentially from top to bottom in the listings, because of market fragmentation, the presence of higher competitive intensity in the seller’s USP space will lower the clickthrough received by the firm. Hence, we come to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3A (H3A). In a sponsored search listing, higher competitive intensity (i.e., more firms around a focal firm that are using the same USP) for a USP will lead to lower CTR for a seller with that USP.

Finally, because quality-seeking consumers have higher search costs and lower search intensity (Nelson 1970), they will sample fewer sellers from their comparison set vis-à-vis price-seeking consumers who have lower search costs. Therefore, the effect of competition will be more pronounced for a quality USP than for a price USP. We illustrate the logic underlying this effect with an example. Consider an extreme case where price-seeking consumers are willing to search all firms on a list whereas quality-seeking consumers are willing to search only one firm on the list. If there is only one seller on the list with a quality USP, then all the quality-seeking consumers will click on that seller. However, if there is a second seller competing on a quality USP, then, ceteris paribus, the focal seller’s CTR will be reduced because of market fragmentation. On the other hand, clicks on a seller with a price USP would not be affected even if there is one more seller using a price USP because price-seeking consumers would click on both of them for price comparison. Formally, this leads us to predict the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3B (H3B). The impact of competition is stronger for a seller using a quality USP relative to a seller using a price USP.

## 5. Data and Methodology

## 5.1. Empirical Context

Our analysis is based on data from a field experiment conducted in conjunction with a firm in the mortgage industry (we will call it “FocalSeller”) that bids for placement on one of the largest sponsored search markets. The key strategic choices facing the firm are the rank at which it will be displayed in the listing (determined by the amount it bids) and the specific differentiation it will highlight in the text message accompanying its advertisement. FocalSeller randomly varies the keywords and associated USP it utilizes in the market, and changes its bids for placement dynamically. The randomized variation of FocalSeller’s strategies (rank and USP) helps achieve the same objective as a random assignment of subjects to treatment and control samples in an experiment (Hofacker and Murphy 2005, Michael and Peter 2005, Murphy et al. 2006). Through this experimentation, the objective is to find the optimal rank that maximizes its CTR for a given keyword. The data set contains FocalSeller’s bids for the top 36 (based on clickthroughs) keywords (e.g., loans, mortgages, home loans, etc.) and the ranks obtained in the sponsored search listings corresponding to each of those bids.

FocalSeller provided us with data for a period of 3 months in 2006. As depicted in the research model, the key predictors of interest are FocalSeller’s rank in the sponsored search listings and the USP as highlighted in its ad creatives. The dependent variable is the CTR obtained. Bids were updated frequently and resulted in different ranks over time and for different keywords. In addition, the ad creative (i.e., 2 lines of text advertisement with 30 to 40 characters in each line) to be displayed on the listings used by FocalSeller was varied over time and across keywords. Two broad positioning strategies were chosen—price and quality—and the ad creatives that were employed highlighted either a price or a quality USP. Ad creatives using a quality USP generally included words such as “customer satisfaction,” “trusted,” “secure and confidential,” and “quick and easy” whereas price USP ads used phrases such as “no closing cost,” “low rates,” “lowest price,” “low interest,” and “compare offers.” For each of the strategic choices made by FocalSeller with regard to rank and ad USP, we obtained the corresponding performance data, i.e., clickthrough rate.

To measure competition, we further collected data on the USP of competitors in the sponsored search listing on the selected keywords. The competition data were compiled by extracting keywords in the sample from the search engine result page every two hours. Then, using content analysis and identifying a set of words that captured the essence of price and quality USPs, we classified the ad creatives used by the competitors as one of the following—price USP, quality USP, both price and quality USP, or neither price nor quality USP. Four marketing experts who were blind to the objective of the research study were asked to classify a sample of ads into quality and price USPs. The agreement of the experts for the USP classification was assessed using interrater reliability tests for nominal data (Hughes and Garrett 1990). We obtained a Cohen’s kappa agreement index of 0.69, indicating a high degree of interrater reliability (Landis and Koch 1977).

We used different “windows of competition” to identify competitors adjacent to the seller. We created multiple competition indices based on different windows to test for the robustness of the results. For the smallest window, we used one advertisement above and one advertisement below FocalSeller’s advertisement to define adjacent competitors. The competitive intensity for a USP is obtained by dividing the number of advertisements using that USP by the number of adjacent ads. We include the hybrid ads (i.e., those ads that contain both price and quality USP) in the calculation of the price competition index as well as quality competition index.

Finally, although the CTR, by virtue of being normalized, takes into account differences in clicks due to differences in impressions, it still suffers from some measurement issues. We assume that the CTR obtained by a seller is a reliable estimate of the true effectiveness of a seller’s ad. However the estimate of effectiveness is not reliable if the number of impressions obtained by an advertisement is small. We thus weight each observation in the regression model such that observations with higher impressions get a larger weight when the least square errors are minimized in the regression analysis. We assume that after a threshold number of impressions, the CTR estimate would be very close to the true value. ## 6.1. Robustness Test

Our main data set comes from the mortgage industry. This choice is primarily driven by the popularity of online mortgage services, which provides us with a sufficient number of clickthroughs to perform the empirical analyses. Although there is no theoretical reason to expect that the relationships would not hold in other settings, to test the robustness of these findings across industries, we obtained an additional data set from an e-retailer in the home furnishing business. This e-retailer is included in the top 500 e-retailers list compiled by Internet Retailer<sup></sup> (Internet-Retailer.com). We collected data on the rank of the e-retailer’s ads in the listing and the USP used in those ads (i.e., quality or price) as well as the CTR obtained by each ad. However, unlike FocalSeller in the mortgage industry, we could not collect data about competitive ads around those of this e-retailer’s. Therefore, we were unable to test the effect of competition intensity in this analysis. For rank and USP, we find consistent results, which thus increases our confidence in the generalizability of the findings (see Figure 8 and also Table A.2 and in the appendix).

For our main analysis, we used the smallest “window of competition,” comprised of one seller above and one below the focal firm (FocalSeller). To test the robustness of the results, we used larger windows of three sellers above and below as well as all the sellers above the FocalSeller to create the competitive intensity variable. The results are consistent across these different “windows of competition” (see Table A.1 in the appendix). The sign of all the coefficients (except the main effect of USP) are consistent. However, we find that the three-way interaction involving competitive intensity loses significance as we use a larger window of competition. Furthermore, the R-squared values are also lower when using the larger window. This suggests that a larger window weakens the results by adding noise to the competitive intensity variable (as reflected in the p values and R-squared values of the regression reported in Table A.1, which use alternate measures of competitive intensity). Note that we are examining behavior at the aggregate level; therefore, it is appropriate to focus on the lowest common denominator (i.e., a comparison window for computing competitive intensity that would be valid for the largest possible set of consumer search patterns). Choosing the window size of / 1 ad ensures that we remain within the comparison window likely to be chosen by the majority of consumers.

## 6.2. Limitations

Prior to discussing the implications of the findings in §7, we acknowledge the limitations of our study. Our empirical analysis is restricted to data from a single firm and, although we performed additional tests for robustness across industries, the generalizability of the findings to other settings would need to be empirically verified. This study being the first of its kind, we consider it to be exploratory in a new context that does need further work to generalize the results across different industries. Nevertheless, note that our unit of analysis is not the firm but rather the specific differentiation strategy, i.e., USP, deployed at a given rank. Our focal firm experimented with both these key strategic variables, permitting us to test the assertions in the research model. Moreover, the use of a single firm allowed us to control for a number of other firm-specific factors such as brand name (Wu et al. 2005) that might affect the outcomes of interest in our study. By focusing on a single firm, we effectively isolated the impact of the firm’s competitive and positioning strategies in these markets. We believe the results would also hold in other industries, although the strength of the relationships may vary. Extending this study to encompass other sectors would be an interesting avenue for future work.

It is possible that the consumers in the markets that we examined in this study are different from those in other markets, which may lead to selection bias. We note that selection bias should not invalidate the results of this study, though it may diminish the generalizability of the findings.<sup>6</sup> A third potential limitation of the study is that we measure only CTRs rather than actual purchases; nonetheless, CTRs are considered to be one of the primary performance metrics by practitioners (Search Engine Marketing Professional Organization 2006; Otlacan 2009). To the degree that different product categories may have different conversion rates, it would be important for future researchers to understand the effect of firms’ positioning strategies on conversion rates and sales. Fourth, though we are able to observe consumers’ actions (i.e., their click throughs), we do not directly observe individual consumers’ search behaviors or their preferences. Finally, the sellers in the listing may vary in terms of their brand equity, which may be related to consumers’ perceptions about the quality of the seller. Lack of information about the brand equity of competitors relative to the focal firm may increase noise in the measure of competitive intensity. Complementary studies employing behavioral laboratory experiments should validate our assumptions.

## 7. Discussion and Implications

Our goal in this paper was to extend understanding of the performance of a seller’s differentiation strategy in sponsored search listings by moving beyond a unidimensional focus of ad rank as the determinant of performance. We examined how a firm’s positioning strategy (its USP) as highlighted in its “ad creative,” the rank of the ad in a sponsored search listing, and the competitive intensity around a focal firm’s ad interact to influence the effectiveness of the sponsored search ad. Results largely support our predictions and suggest that these factors are indeed important strategic variables for firms competing in these markets.

The study makes several contributions. It integrates consumer search theory with the directional markets literature to provide insights into consumer search and sellers’ differentiation strategies in sponsored search markets. We proposed and found evidence that the sequential nature of search in such markets results in sponsored search listings acting as consumer segmentation filters. The number of consumers who click at the top is larger than those who click at the bottom, as might be expected, but a striking finding is that consumers who reach the bottom may be more homogenous in certain characteristics such as search intensity, income, product attribute preferences, etc. The findings provide indirect evidence of consumer search patterns in sponsored search markets and can assist analytical modelers and auction designers in developing more realistic models.

In addition, the study yields several managerial implications. First, we reaffirm the widespread belief that a higher ranking of an advertisement leads to better performance (i.e., more clickthroughs). However, strikingly, an the ad’s rank in the search results explains only a small (around 5%) proportion of the variance in clickthrough rates that a firm obtains. A key implication of this finding is that the forecasting models used by search engine marketers and advertisers that only include rank as a predictor of clickthroughs would not only be misleading but may also result in suboptimal bidding strategies.

Our results indicate that both the focal firm’s USP as well as the competitive intensity around its position have a significant impact on its advertisement’s performance. In general, price USP performs better than quality USP at lower ranks. This finding, which is consistent with recent empirical results showing that topmost ads may not always be optimal (Agarwal et al. 2008), suggests that the advertisers (or search engine marketers advertising on behalf of their clients) who typically treat rank and USP as independent decisions should seek to jointly optimize these two decision variables.

A second implication is the need for sellers to build Web-based information systems capable of capturing the rank of an ad in a sponsored search listing at which a consumer clicked to visit that seller’s site. This information can then be used in conjunction with consumer demographics and psychographics (if available) to obtain insights into the differences between the profiles of consumers who encounter a seller’s ad at different ranks in a listing and to make sharper predictions about consumer purchase behavior.

Third, a seller would find it useful to customize his landing page (i.e., the Web page that a consumer is taken to when she clicks on an ad in a sponsored search listing) for ads shown at different ranks. For example, if a seller’s ad is appearing at the bottom rank, then the seller can customize his page to fit the information needs and preferences of a price-seeking consumer profile and may even post lower prices. By contrast, if a seller is listed at the top, he can afford to set higher prices and customize his site to cater to quality-seeking consumers. Furthermore, a seller can dynamically set the price on a landing page based on the rank at which the ad linked to this landing page appears on a listing. Finally, a high-quality seller may benefit more from bidding higher to appear at the top of a listing because a majority of the consumers who are quality seeking may not even see the seller if his ad appears at the bottom of a listing. Sellers competing on price can, however, afford to be at the lower ranks. Although this strategy would lower their clickthrough (because of the lower rank), it would draw a more homogenous set of consumers (in terms of their search intensity and other factors that determine search intensity or are related to search intensity) to sellers’ websites. Thus, sellers may obtain higher sales by developing more targeted marketing strategies.

The finding that quality-seeking consumers tend to be concentrated at the top of a listing also has implications for the design of sponsored search mechanisms. Search engine intermediaries need to design ranking mechanisms to ensure that sellers at the top of listings are of high quality. Otherwise, in the long run, at equilibrium, quality-seeking consumers may stop using sponsored search results.

Interestingly, we also find that quality USP is strongly affected by competitive intensity. As expected, the effect is negative at the top ranks but reverses at the bottom ranks. Our proposed explanation suggests that this is because of price-seeking consumers clicking on the quality USP ads when they do not have enough price USP ads to sample at the bottom. This may, however, have implications for the conversion rate, i.e., the proportion of clicks that generate a sale of the quality ads when they are at the bottom amidst high-quality USP competition. It is possible that in such an environment, the conversion rate of an ad may be lower. One important implication of this finding is that advertisers, especially those who are using a quality USP, should develop information systems to monitor the competition on sponsored search listings to better predict fluctuations in their CTR and to make more informed decisions about what ranks to obtain for their ads for different keywords in the advertiser’s keyword portfolio. As opposed to just the rank of the ad, the advertisers should also include historical competitive intensity, USP, and the ad’s rank to forecast future CTR to obtain better forecasting estimates and to arrive at an optimal bidding strategy for sponsored search auctions.

## 7.1. Future Research

Several promising new avenues for research in this nascent field emerge from this study. First, researchers can build on this study by using complementary research methods. Controlled lab experiments can be designed to directly examine the consumer behavior that was inferred in this study and to address some of the limitations described earlier. Consumer surveys can also be conducted to identify the profile of consumers who search until the bottom of the listing and how this profile differs from those who stop at the top. Furthermore, if sellers start tracking the ranks at which consumers saw ads, researchers can use this secondary data to triangulate findings about consumer search behavior in sponsored search listings.

We examined competition in terms of the USP on which the firms were competing. Future research can develop alternative metrics for competitive intensity (for instance, competition based on a firm’s brand equity). It is possible that competition from firms that have greater market share (or brand recognition) than the focal firm would lower the performance of the focal firm’s advertisement. Finally, researchers can analyze data collected from firms in different industries to examine the sensitivity and robustness of the findings.

## 8. Conclusion

Researchers believe that differentiation strategies employed by firms and the resulting competitive dynamics in directional markets may be different from those created by traditional formats (Arbatskaya 2007, Perry and Wigderson 1986). However, very few empirical studies have examined the nature and outcomes of competition in such online markets and the various differentiation strategies adopted by firms competing in an environment characterized by very high competitive intensity and a growing number of rivals. The exclusive focus on the rank of firms in sponsored search markets as a driver of performance evident in the research literature as well as in practice fails to account for the unique characteristics of sponsored search markets and the ability of firms to differentiate themselves in such contexts. In contrast to extant research that has adopted an auctions perspective to understand the dynamics between search intermediaries and sellers, in this study we adopted an alternative consumer search perspective. Findings from this study can aid firms in optimizing their sponsored search strategies. Tracking and accounting for a range of variables (in addition to rank) in forecasting models can improve the prediction accuracy of clickthrough rates. The results of this study are likely to be useful not only for managers seeking to analyze the competition and craft their best responses to their competitors’ positioning strategies. The results also extend existing research on directional markets to a new and emerging empirical context. Finally, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to theorize and examine the impact of positioning strategies and competition in directional markets such as sponsored search.
