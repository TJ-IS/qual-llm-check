Abstract: This paper investigates the information sharing issue in a simple supply chain with one manufacturer and one retailer. The market demand might be subject to disruption, but the retailer can get access to some signals from social media platforms to better forecast the market state (regular or disrupted) even though the signals might be not fully reliable. Different from the traditional information sharing literature, we incorporate the shutdown policy into our model. We first characterize the equilibrium outcomes under both with- and without-information sharing cases and then examine the players’ preferences over information sharing or not. It is shown that when the level of information reliability is relatively low, the manufacturer prefers the information sharing cases, whereas the retailer prefers the no-sharing case, and

#

information sharing does harm to the whole channel. In addition, our results show that as the level of information reliability increases, the manufacturer benefits more but the retailer and the whole channel lose more from information sharing. Contrary to the conventional wisdom, when the level of information reliability falls into an intermediary interval, the retailer will have an incentive to share information, and information sharing will benefit the manufacturer and the whole supply chain. However, the benefits from information sharing decrease as the level of information reliability increases. When the level of information reliability is sufficiently high, both firms are indifferent between the information sharing and no-sharing cases. Additionally, the incorporation of the shutdown policy has important implications for the information sharing issue, and thus, the policy should not be overlooked.

Keywords: Information Sharing; Shutdown Policy; Market Disruption; Information Reliability

## 1. Introduction

Social media technologies such as Facebook, Twitter, and WeChat are becoming a reliable platform through which customers communicate their opinions and share product- or service-related information in a timely manner (Osatuyi 2013, Shang et al. 2017). Prior literature confirmed that these online-shared product or service opinions and experiences have a significant influence on customers’ purchasing decisions, and this in turn affects the retailers’ decisions and profits (Liu et al. 2016). As a result, retailers, being closer to the consumers, are increasingly using social media technologies to interact with their customers to learn their specific demand, which provides them an information advantage over their manufacturers (Nalca et al. 2017). A natural question arising here is that whether these retailers are willing to share their private information with their manufacturers?

In academia, information sharing has garnered greater research attention in recent years and has been regarded as an effective approach for reducing the bullwhip effect and restoring the system efficiency in the supply chain (see, Lee et al. 1997, Bian et al. 2014). However, most of previous studies on information sharing have focused on investigating the types of information shared and the gains from sharing (Croson and Donohue 2006). Furthermore, these studies implicitly assume that firms that share information are willing to do. However, incentives for information sharing

#

in a decentralized supply chain are still an issue. To this end, we consider a simple supply chain consisting of an upstream manufacturer (a female) and a downstream retailer (a male), where the former offers a linear price contract and the latter responds by placing an order. Moreover, the retailer possesses private market demand information. It is well established in the literature that information sharing makes double marginalization more pronounced, thus benefiting the manufacturer, but incentive to share his proprietary information with the manufacturer (see Tan et al. 2016). On the other hand, information sharing is widely observed in practice, usually initiated by retailers. To identify the drivers of voluntary information sharing, some studies investigate the impacts of environmental factors such as competition, contract format, production (dis)economy, and production mode (make-to-order or make-to-stock) on information sharing incentives (see, Li 2000; Zhang 2002; Ha and Tong 2008; Ha et al. 2011; Li and Zhang 2015; Shang et al.2016). To the best of our knowledge, however, there is little literature incorporating the shutdown policy (we will explain this policy in Section 2) into the information sharing studies.

Considering the prevalence of information screening models in voluntary information sharing issues on one hand, and the importance of shutdown policy in these models on the other hand (see, Ha 2001), this paper attempts to investigate whether and when the retailer has an incentive to share his private demand information in a simple supply chain by incorporating the shutdown policy. We focus on ex ante information sharing commitment, where a retailer decides whether to share information before he observes the content of information and the shared information is credible. According to the information state (information asymmetry state where the retailer’s private information is not shared and thus the manufacturer only has a prior information and information symmetry state where the retailer shares his private information) the manufacturer faces, she has the right to decide whether to trade with the retailer, and if trading, how to set her wholesale price. Compared with the information sharing literature, the incentive for the retailer to share information in our paper becomes more complex because of the introduction of shutdown policy. The research questions posed in this paper include the following: How do the manufacturer’s decisions on whether to trade and wholesale price interact with the retailer’s information sharing strategy? When does the retailer have an incentive to

share information and how does his decision on information sharing vary with his information reliability level? Under what conditions will the supply chains as a whole profit from information sharing?

We study these questions in a simple supply chain setting consisting of a manufacturer and a retailer, where the former sells products to consumers through the latter. The market demand might be subject to market disruption caused by some unplanned and unanticipated events such as product quality or social responsibility scandals (Xia et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2017). Because of being closer to the market signals about the market state (regular or disruption), and thus, the retailer can acquire more accurate market information than the manufacturer, although this information might be imperfect. We use the phrase “information reliability” to denote the accuracy of the retailer’s signal. The market state information is the retailer’s private information. Therefore, the higher the information reliability level is, the higher information advantage the retailer has. Before observing the market signal, the retailer needs to decide whether to commit to sharing his signal with the manufacturer. If sharing, the information between the manufacturer and retailer will be symmetric. Otherwise, there will be information asymmetry between them. The higher the level of information reliability, the higher the level of information asymmetry between the two players. Based on the retailer’s decision about information sharing, the manufacturer decides whether to trade with the retailer, and if trading, how to set the optimal wholesale price. After that, the retailer places an order before perfectly knowing the market demand information.

Based on these settings, we have several interesting findings. First, when the information reliability level is relatively low, on average, the manufacturer under the no-information sharing case has to distort her wholesale price downward for the trade to be always carried out (i.e., the wholesale price is applicable and valuable to the least efficient retailer-type, and thus no retailer is shut down), whereas the manufacturer under the information sharing case can tailor her wholesale price to different signals shared by the retailer. Therefore, under this situation, no shutdown occurs and double marginalization is aggravated; thus, we reiterate the well-established result: information sharing benefits the manufacturer but does harm to the retailer and whole supply chain. Moreover, as the information reliability level increases, the manufacturer benefits more from information sharing, but the retailer and whole supply chain lose more. Second, when the information reliability level is

#

intermediate (falling into a certain interval), the manufacturer under the no-information sharing case will shut down (i.e., give up) the least efficient retailer-type because the benefit from trading with this retailer-type cannot offset the loss owing to the distortion of wholesale price. By contrast, the manufacturer under the information sharing case can still trade with all retailer-types. Owing to the extra benefit from the least efficient retailer-type, information sharing provides benefits to all the parties in supply chain and thus the whole supply chain. However, as the information reliability level increases, the additional benefit will diminish. Third, when the information reliability level is sufficiently high, the manufacturer under th information sharing case will also shut down the least efficient retailer-type, just as the manufacturer under the no-information sharing case does. As a result, in this situation, information sharing or not makes no difference.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides reviews on the related literature. Section 3 provides a description of our model and the information structure and provides some basic results for a wholesale price contract. Section 4 provides a derivation of the optimal equilibria for a wholesale price contract under the cases without and with information sharing, respectively, and further investigates the impacts of information sharing by comparing these results. Section 5 provides information on numerical experiments conducted to check the validity of our results. Section 6 provides conclusion of the paper.

## 2. Literature review

Our work is closely related to the following two streams of literature: showdown policy in information screening games and information sharing in supply chains. In the following subsections, we review relevant literature in these areas.

## 2.1 Shutdown Policy

A shutdown policy, under the optimal scheme for information screening or principal–agent models, allows the uninformed party (i.e., principal) to set a threshold level on the asymmetric information to determine whether a contract should be offered to the informed party (i.e., agent). Specifically, assuming the production cost information to be asymmetric, the informed party will be induced to accept the contract if his true cost is lower than the given threshold level and not to accept the contract if it is higher. We refer to the former (accepting the contract) as an efficient agent and the latter (not accepting) as an inefficient agent. For more details on the

shutdown policy, refer to Laffont and Martimort (2002, Sect. 2.3.2). Moreover, Baron and Myerson (1982) and Ha (2001) prove the optimality of the shutdown policy for information screening models.

In most textbooks and academic work on information screening and principal–agent models, the shutdown policy is often ignored, i.e., the principal would offer a contract to trade with any type of agent, no matter whether the agent is efficient or not. One possible reason for this is that the introduction of the shutdown policy can make the screening problem more difficult to mathematically manipulate, see Rajan and Saouma (2006) and Liu and Ҫetinkaya (2009) for more details. However, a few studies in the operations management field allow the principal not to contract with the inefficient agent type and lead to some interesting results; see, e.g., Porteus and Whang (1999), Corbett and de Groote (2000), Corbett et al. (2004), Liu and Ҫetinkaya (2009), and Wang et al. (2015). However, these studies focus on designing the optimal screening contract under information asymmetry. Differently, we further investigate the information sharing issue based on the derived optimal contract.

## 2.2 Information Sharing

The survey by Chen (2003) points out two categories concerning information sharing issues in supply chains. The first category takes the perspective of a central planner who acquires information to improve the performance of the whole supply chain, hence assuming no competition among supply chain members. For example, Lee et al. (2000) evaluate the value of sharing demand information in a simple two-level supply chain with nonstationary end demands. Their analysis suggests that the value can be quite high, especially when demands are significantly correlated over time. By comparing the performances of two scenarios of whether or not a supplier shares his lead time information with his retailer, Chen and Yu (2005) quantify the value of lead time information in a similar supply chain setting. However, these studies fail to address whether these benefits can be achieved under a decentralized setting.

The second category is regarding supply chains where not all players have the symmetric information. Information asymmetry prompts members of the supply chain to engage in signaling or screening. In some supply chain scenarios, information is unverifiable, and as a result, signaling might arise. For instance, Cachon and Lariviere (2001) consider a supply chain setting consisting of a manufacturer and a supplier in which the manufacturer who privately learns the demand forecast might signal the

#

information to the supplier through capacity distortion. Similarly, Özer and Wei (2006) discuss the signaling role of advance purchase contract, where the supplier requires a quantity commitment to hold the manufacturer accountable for her private forecast information. Such signaling games also have been studied by Kong et al. (2013) and Li et al. (2014), which incorporate the downstream competition compared to risk aversion in Jiang et al. (2016). In addition to costly signaling, several recent studies have investigated whether supply chain parties can truthfully share information through costless signaling — cheap talk. Chu et al. (2016) and Shamir and Shin (2016) find that credible information dissemination using cheap talk is possible when conflicting trade-offs emerge (e.g., announcing a large demand forecast might not only induce large capacity investment but also trigger a price increase or downstream competition). However, these papers focus on how to derive stable separating and pooling equilibria from the perspective of the informed party.

Some other papers employ screening games to check the feasibility of voluntary information sharing. For this issue, it is generally accepted that the upstream manufacturer prefers having access to the downstream retailer’s private information, whereas the retailer prefers to keep this information private. For example, for a single-manufacturer, single-retailer supply chain, Ha and Zhang (2017) show that, under linear price contracts, information sharing increases the manufacturer’s profit at the expense of the retailer and the whole supply chain. To identify the drivers of voluntary information sharing, Li (2002) and Zhang (2002) show that the downstream sellers might be willing to share their private demand information with their common supplier in a competition setting by charging side payment or information exchange price. Ha and Tong (2008), Ha et al. (2011), and Shang et al. (2016) reveal that information sharing can be influenced by the contract choice or the production (dis)economy. In view of the assumption of make-to-order in these above-mentioned papers, Li and Zhang (2015) consider a make-to-stock setting and find the retailer has an incentive to share his information with the manufacturer when the magnitude of demand uncertainty is intermediate. Our study falls in this screening game field, but differs from this literature in that we focus on the impact of information reliability on information sharing. Moreover, these information sharing models mentioned above employ a linear-expectation information structure without incorporating the shutdown policy, whereas our work adopts a discrete information structure by considering the shutdown policy (the policy is critical to arriving at our conclusion).

Similarly, there are many empirical studies on information sharing. Based on

#

surveys, these papers focus on the benefits and willingness to share information. For example, Chengalur-Smith et al. (2012) investigate the effect of information sharing and business systems leveraging on business benefits through web-based supply chain applications. As for willingness to share information, Du et al. (2012) indicate that it is conducive to information sharing by enhancing commitment, trust, coordination, and interdependence with their partners, and Shin et al. (2007) show that social network structures have a significant influence on information sharing, whereas Zimmer et al. (2010) argue that information relevance is a critical antecedent to information disclosure and that both relevance and trust could alleviate perceptions o risk associated with disclosure, thereby increasing firms’ intentions to disclose information. In this paper, we build an analytical model to examine the effect of demand information reliability on willingness to share information.

## 3. Model Setup

We develop a parsimonious supply chain model consisting of a manufacturer and a retailer, where the former sells products to consumers through the latter. The manufacturer has a constant marginal production cost c. For convenience, we refer to the manufacturer as “she” and the retailer as “he”. All members in the channel are uncertain about the market state (regular or disruption), and we explore the impacts of the downstream player’s forecasting capability about market state on information sharing decisions. Owing to long lead time, the retailer has to place an order before perfectly knowing the market state. However, he can collect relevant information to mitigate his uncertainty in advance. For example, with the advance of information technology, retailers routinely and efficiently acquire rich market data related to point-of-sales or online product reviews, and the data are useful for a retailer to analyze buying trends (Keifer 2010), which could result in the retailer’s information advantage relative to the manufacturer.

Let $\theta _ { \mathrm { m } }$ denote the market state, only taking two possible values, and its prior distribution is modeled as the state being regular (m = r) with probability and being disrupted $( \mathbf { m } = \mathrm { d } )$ with probability $1 - \gamma ,$ which is common knowledge for all players. When the market state is regular, the demand $\tilde { D }$ is newsvendor-type, i.e., the retailer resells the products purchased from the manufacturer at w per unit to the consumers at p per unit and disposes the unsold products at v per unit $( \nu < c )$ . Assume that $\tilde { D }$ has a distribution on support $[ d _ { \operatorname* { m i n } } , d _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } ]$ with cumulative distribution function F(), probability density function $f ( \cdot )$ , mean $\mu ,$ and standard deviation . However, when

the market state is disruption, the demand will terminate, and the stocked products will have no value. This means that when the disruption occurs, no consumer will buy products from the retailer, and thus, the retailer cannot obtain profits from the stocked products. We make this extreme assumption for two reasons. The first one is for the sake of mathematical tractability. Second, although this assumption is very strict, it is not entirely implausible in reality. For example, in 2017, as South Korea deployed the THAAD (i.e., Terminal High Altitude Area Defense) system, Chinese people began to resist the Korean enterprises. Lotte Mart, a well-known retailer of Korea, had lost the whole market in China because of this unanticipated event (or disruption) and had to withdraw from China several months later.

Because of being closer to the market, the retailer can use the market information generated by his information system to gain information advantage relative to the manufacturer. The information system may not be perfect (i.e., noisy) in predicting the state of the world ex ante, but it does predict the market better than what is implied by the prior distribution on $\theta _ { \mathrm { m } }$ . Thus, the information system can be interpreted as a knowledge system, which provides better information to the retailer than his prior belief in the sense of the match of the belief (after getting the information) to the actual ex post state that will be realized (see, for example, Chen et al. 2001). The output of the information system is a signal (to the retailer) that indicates whether $\theta _ { \mathrm { m } } = \theta _ { \mathrm { r } }$ or $\theta _ { \mathrm { d } } .$ , which in general may not be fully reliable. The reliability of the information system is modeled as follows: Let $\theta _ { \mathrm { r } }$ and $\theta _ { \mathrm { d } }$ refer to the true state of demand, either regular or disruption, and let $s _ { \mathrm { r } }$ and $s _ { \mathrm { d } }$ denote the signals indicating that the market state will be $\theta _ { \mathrm { r } }$ and $\theta _ { \mathrm { d } }$ , respectively. $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { s }$ in Chen et al. (2001), we define the reliability of the information system (or signal) by a measure $\rho \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ We assume that the manufacturer can get access to the reliability of signal $\rho$ based on historical data, although she does not learn about which signal the retailer receives, s<sub>r</sub> or $s \mathrm { d } .$ Our formulation has the following properties:

(i) When = 1, the signal is perfectly reliable in the sense that $\mathrm { P r } ( s _ { \mathrm { r } } | \theta _ { \mathrm { r } } ) = \mathrm { P r } ( s _ { \mathrm { d } } | \theta _ { \mathrm { d } } ) = 1$ On the contrary, when the signal is completely unreliable and thus $\rho = 0$ , the signal has no improvement over the a priori, i.e., $\operatorname* { P r } ( s _ { \mathrm { r } } | \theta _ { \mathrm { r } } ) = \gamma$ and $\operatorname* { P r } ( s _ { \mathrm { d } } | \theta _ { \mathrm { d } } ) = 1 - \gamma .$ This means that the retailer has no information advantage.

(ii) The signal is unbiased in the sense that the unconditional probability of a signal indicating market to be regular (or disruption) is equal to the true prior probability of market being regular (or disruption), i.e.,

$$
\operatorname * {P r} \left(s _ {\mathrm{r}}\right) = \operatorname * {P r} \left(\theta_ {\mathrm{r}}\right) \operatorname * {P r} \left(s _ {\mathrm{r}} \mid \theta_ {\mathrm{r}}\right) + \operatorname * {P r} \left(\theta_ {\mathrm{d}}\right) \operatorname * {P r} \left(s _ {\mathrm{r}} \mid \theta_ {\mathrm{d}}\right) = \operatorname * {P r} \left(\theta_ {\mathrm{r}}\right),\tag{1a}
$$

$$
\operatorname * {P r} \left(s _ {\mathrm{d}}\right) = \operatorname * {P r} \left(\theta_ {\mathrm{r}}\right) \operatorname * {P r} \left(s _ {\mathrm{d}} \mid \theta_ {\mathrm{r}}\right) + \operatorname * {P r} \left(\theta_ {\mathrm{d}}\right) \operatorname * {P r} \left(s _ {\mathrm{d}} \mid \theta_ {\mathrm{d}}\right) = \operatorname * {P r} \left(\theta_ {\mathrm{d}}\right).\tag{1b}
$$

(iii) A regularity property:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (s _ {\mathrm{r}} | \theta_ {\mathrm{r}}) + \operatorname * {P r} (s _ {\mathrm{d}} | \theta_ {\mathrm{r}}) = 1,\tag{2a}
$$

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (s _ {\mathrm{r}} | \theta_ {\mathrm{d}}) + \operatorname * {P r} (s _ {\mathrm{d}} | \theta_ {\mathrm{d}}) = 1.\tag{2b}
$$

Given the unbiasedness and regularity properties in Equations (1) and (2), we can derive the following conditional probability as

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (s _ {\mathrm{r}} | \theta_ {\mathrm{r}}) = 1 - (1 - \rho) (1 - \gamma), \quad \operatorname * {P r} (s _ {\mathrm{d}} | \theta_ {\mathrm{r}}) = (1 - \rho) (1 - \gamma),
$$

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (s _ {\mathrm{r}} | \theta_ {\mathrm{d}}) = (1 - \rho) \gamma , \quad \text { and } \operatorname * {P r} (s _ {\mathrm{d}} | \theta_ {\mathrm{d}}) = 1 - (1 - \rho) \gamma .
$$

Based on Bayes’ formula, the retailer can compute the posterior probability as

$$
P _ {\mathrm{r}} \equiv \operatorname * {P r} (\theta_ {\mathrm{r}} | s _ {\mathrm{r}}) = 1 - (1 - \rho) (1 - \gamma),\tag{3a}
$$

$$
P _ {\mathrm{d}} \equiv \operatorname * {P r} (\theta_ {\mathrm{r}} | s _ {\mathrm{d}}) = (1 - \rho) \gamma .\tag{3b}
$$

Note that, $P _ { \mathrm { r } } \geq P _ { \mathrm { d } }$ . The detailed definition of $\rho$ and the derivation of the posterior probability is relegated to Appendix A. Note that for $\mathrm { a n y } \rho > 0$ , the signal is always informative in the sense that $\operatorname* { P r } ( \theta _ { \mathrm { f } } | s _ { \mathrm { r } } ) > \operatorname* { P r } ( \theta _ { \mathrm { r } } )$ and $\operatorname* { P r } ( \theta _ { \mathrm { d } } | s _ { \mathrm { d } } ) > \operatorname* { P r } ( \theta _ { \mathrm { d } } )$ . This implies that having obtained the signal, the retailer will not ignore the signal, as it improves the probability of being correct. $\operatorname { A s } \rho$ increases toward 1, the reliability of the signal improves and the probability of wrong prediction of the market state decreases. However, as long as $\rho$ is less than 1, there is a positive probability that the retailer ends up with a mismatch between the signal and the realized state. Based on this setting, we will construct a game theoretical framework to examine the information sharing decision between the manufacturer and the retailer.

The timing of the game model is as shown in Figure 1. First, before the retailer observes the market signal $s _ { \mathrm { m } } ( \mathbf { m } { \in } \{ \mathbf { r } , { \mathrm { d } } \} )$ , the retailer decides on whether $s _ { \mathrm { m } }$ will be shared. Second, the retailer receives signal $s _ { \mathrm { m } }$ from his information system, which leads to his information advantage relative to the manufacturer whenever the reliability of signal $\rho$ is larger than zero. Third, the manufacturer offers a contract to the retailer, and the retailer responds by placing an order. Finally, the true state of market is revealed. At this point, if the market is regular, the retailer behaves like a newsvendor, sells according to the demand but only up to the quantity stocked, and salvages the excess stock. If the market is disruption, the retailer will earn nothing

from the stocked goods.

## 4. Wholesale Price Contract

To facilitate the analyses of subsequent information asymmetry issues, we first consider a simple wholesale price contract with information symmetry where the prior distribution on the market state is the only information the manufacturer and the retailer can acquire; that is, there is no extra signal helpful for the retailer (and/or the manufacturer) to mitigate his market state uncertainty. Under the simple wholesale price contract, the retailer responds by placing an order of $q$ after the manufacturer announces a wholesale price w. Note that the prior distribution is that the market state being regular $( \theta _ { \mathrm { { m } } } = \theta _ { \mathrm { { r } } } )$ with probability and being disruption $( \theta _ { \mathrm { { m } } } = \theta _ { \mathrm { { d } } } )$ with probability 1– . Given w, the retailer’s expected profit can be expressed as

$$
\Pi_ {\mathrm{R}} (q | w) = \gamma \left\{p q - (p - v) E [ (q - \tilde {D}) ^ {+} ] \right\} - w q,\tag{4}
$$

where $E [ ( q - \widetilde { D } ) ^ { + }$ ] represents the retailer’s expected sales under the regular market state. Obviously, the retailer’s problem of making stocking decision to maximize his expected profit in Equation (4) is equivalent to a classical newsvendor issue with underage cost p–w/ and overage cost $w / \gamma { - } \nu .$ . Therefore, the optimal order quantity can be derived as

$$
q ^ {*} (w) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} F ^ {- 1} \left(\frac {p - w / \gamma}{p - v}\right), & \text { if } w \leq \gamma p \\ 0, & \text { otherwise } \end{array} \right..\tag{5}
$$

That is, the relationship between w and q can be rewritten as

$$
w (q) = \gamma \Big [ p \overline {{F}} (q) + v F (q) \Big ],\tag{6}
$$

where $\overline { F }$ is the complementary function of F. Furthermore, the manufacturer’s profit can be given as

$$
\Pi_ {\mathrm{M}} (q) = \left[ \gamma (p - v) \overline {{F}} (q) - (c - \gamma v) \right] q.\tag{7}
$$

We follow the logic as in Lariviere and Porteus (2001) and Tilson (2008) to derive the optimal outcomes, which are given in Lemma 1.

Lemma 1. If the distribution F has an increasing generalized failure rate (i.e., IGFR) with a finite mean and support $[ d _ { \mathrm { m i n } } , d _ { \mathrm { m a x } } )$ , the manufacturer’s optimal order quantity $q ^ { * }$ is uniquely determined by the first-order condition

$$
\overline {{F}} (q) \big [ 1 - g (q) \big ] = (c - \gamma v) / [ \gamma (p - v) ],\tag{8}
$$

and the corresponding optimal wholesale price $w ^ { * }$ can be given by

$$
w ^ {*} = \left[ c - \gamma g \left(q ^ {*}\right) \right] / \left[ 1 - g \left(q ^ {*}\right) \right],\tag{9}
$$

where $g ( q )$ is the generalized failure rate of the distribution F and defined as

$$
g (q) = q f (q) \big / \overline {{F}} (q).\tag{10}
$$

The proof of Lemma 1 is relegated to Appendix B.

With the above results, we analyze the information sharing issue when the retailer can observe the signal about the market state.

## 4.1 Without Information Sharing

Consider first the case where there is no information sharing; hence, information asymmetry arises between the manufacturer and retailer. The sequence of events is as follows. First, the retailer observes the signal about the market state, s or s , which is his private information. Second, the manufacturer charges a wholesale price w/unit based on the prior information about the market state; the retailer responds by declaring an order quantity $q$ according to his knowledge about market state updated by the signal observed by him at the initial step and pays the manufacturer wq. We find the equilibrium solutions using backward induction. Hereafter, we refer to the retailer receiving the signal $s _ { \mathrm { m } }$ as the s -type retailer, m {r, d}.

Note that as long as the level of information reliability $\rho$ is less than 1, there still exists a positive probability that the realized market state is regular even if the signal that the retailer observes is the disruption one $s \mathrm { d } .$ . This implies that the retailer can still place an order $q > 0$ after he observes $s _ { \mathrm { d } } .$ . Therefore, the manufacturer’s problem can be formulated as follows.

$$

information knowledge of the market state (regular or disruption). As the manufacturer does not know the actual market state, she has an information disadvantage over the retailer. ## 4.3 When to Share Information

In this section, we will investigate whether to share information between the manufacturer and retailer by comparing the results in Lemmas 2 and 3. The following Proposition 1 gives the comparison of profits for the manufacturer, the retailer, and the whole channel.

Differently, whether the retailer and the whole channel benefit from information sharing depends on the information reliability level $\rho .$ When $\rho { \le } \rho ^ { N }$ , information sharing makes the retailer lose his information advantage relative to the manufacturer and stops him from grabbing the information rent. Therefore, the retailer becomes worse off. In the case of information asymmetry, the manufacturer sets the wholesale price by making a tradeoff between cutting prices to induce the s<sub>d</sub>-type retailer to place an order and increasing prices to serve only the s<sub>r</sub>-type retailer, where the former can make her to get a slice from the enlarged system profit (larger expected order)

whereas the latter can make her to obtain a higher profit margin (note that, $w \mathrm { I I } ^ { N }$ is larger than $w \mathrm { I } ^ { N } ,$ $w \mathrm { I I } ^ { N }$ is increasing in the information reliability level $\rho$ and $w _ { \mathrm { { I } } } ^ { N }$ is decreasing in $\rho )$ . When $\rho ^ { N } { < } \rho { \leq } \rho ^ { S }$ , the manufacturer would give up the s<sub>d</sub>-type retailer and charge a wholesale price $w \mathrm { I I } ^ { N }$ only for the $s _ { \mathrm { r } } .$ -type retailer. This implies that $w _ { \mathrm { I I } } ^ { ~ N } = w _ { \mathrm { I r } } ^ { ~ S } = w _ { \mathrm { I I r } } ^ { ~ S }$ . Given that the manufacturer under information symmetry still offers a $w _ { \mathrm { H } } { } ^ { S }$ to the s<sub>d</sub>-type retailer, the retailer can obtain an extra profit from the signal $s \mathrm { d } \cdot$ Therefore, the retailer benefits from information sharing. Accordingly, the whole channel does so.

In the case of information symmetry, when $\rho { > } \rho ^ { S } ,$ , the increase in $\rho$ makes the manufacturer more confident about the disruption market state if the signal is $s _ { \mathrm { d } } ,$ , and therefore, the manufacturer only serves the s<sub>r</sub>-type retailer (note that ${ q _ { \mathrm { I d } } } ^ { S } = 0 \mathrm { a t } \rho = \rho ^ { \mathrm { S } } )$ Therefore, there is no difference between with information sharing and without information sharing.

## 6. Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we have examined the issues of information sharing in a supply chain with one manufacturer and one retailer whose market demand is subject to market disruption with certain prior probability. The interaction between the manufacturer and retailer is characterized by a simple wholesale price contract. The retailer can use his information system to predict the state of market (regular or disrupted), and thus, the retailer can acquire more accurate disruption information than the manufacturer, which gives him an information advantage over the manufacturer. The retailer’s forecast might be not fully reliable, and thus, we use a phrase “information reliability” to denote the forecasting accuracy. We first derive the equilibrium outcomes for the without- and with-information sharing cases by incorporating the shutdown policy and then conduct a comparison to investigate the preferences of each player regarding these cases. This paper leads to the following several interesting findings.

First, under the without information sharing case, the manufacturer’s expected profit is quasi-convex at the information reliability level $\rho ,$ and the expected profits of both the retailer and whole channel are piece-wise increasing functions of $\rho .$ . This

result is somewhat interesting and counterintuitive because it indicates that when the information reliability exceeds a certain threshold, the manufacturer actually can benefit from the enlarged level of information asymmetry (note that, the higher the level of information reliability, the higher the level of information asymmetry between the two players).

Second, under the information sharing case, the expected profits of the retailer and whole channel are continuous in the whole range of $\rho ,$ , which is different from those of the without information case. All the members and the whole channel benefit more accurate decisions.

Third, our results show that the preferences of each player over the information sharing depend on the level of information reliability and the shutdown policy. If we do not consider the shutdown policy, the manufacturer always prefers the information sharing case, whereas the retailer and the whole channel prefers the without information sharing case, no matter what the information reliability is. However, when the shutdown policy is considered, the above conclusion might not be fully true. Specifically, when the level of information reliability is relatively low, the players preferences are the same as those in the no-shutdown policy situation. However, when the level of information reliability falls into a certain interval (i.e., higher than a lower bound but lower than an upper bound), both the manufacturer and retailer prefer the information sharing case. Unfortunately, their benefits from information sharing decrease as the level of information reliability increases. When the level of information reliability further increases and exceeds a certain threshold, both firms are indifferent between sharing and no-sharing cases.

Although we have derived some meaningful results, this paper can also be extended in several directions. First, for mathematical tractability, we assume the market demand will terminate, and the stocked products will have no salvage value when the market disruption occurs. Although this situation is indeed possible in the real world, we will consider a more common situation in the future work where the market demand shrinks from the regular $\tilde { D }$ to $\alpha \tilde { D }$ after disruption, $\alpha \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ Second, this paper focuses on a simple wholesale price contract to characterize the interactions between the manufacturer and the retailer. Actually, we have also conducted the analytical and computational work for the menu of contracts (the most sophisticated contract formats). Our preliminary results show that the retailer is unwilling to share his private information, no matter what the level of information

reliability is. Although the conclusion in the menu of contracts is different from that in the wholesale price contract, our work is still valuable. First, the previous studies on information sharing issues also use the wholesale price contract. However, our work obtains results different from the results in these studies. This underlines the necessity of incorporating the shutdown policy when investigating the information sharing issues—often at the expense of analytical tractability. Second, as we stated before, wholesale price contract is the most used contract format in the real world. Therefore, our conclusion can be applied to most situations in reality. Nevertheless, in the future work, we will try a wider spectrum of contract formats such as return policy and revenue sharing to investigate whether the contract formats have an effect on the players’ preferences over information sharing. Last but not least, in the future work, we will also investigate what will happen if the information shared by the retailer is not credible.

Appendix A. The definition of the retailer’s forecasting capability $\rho$

According to Chen et al. (2001), the accuracy of a signal received by the retailer should be defined as

$$
I = \frac {\operatorname * {P r} \left(\theta_ {\mathrm{r}}\right) \operatorname * {P r} \left(s _ {\mathrm{r}} \mid \theta_ {\mathrm{r}}\right) + \operatorname * {P r} \left(\theta_ {\mathrm{d}}\right) \operatorname * {P r} \left(s _ {\mathrm{d}} \mid \theta_ {\mathrm{d}}\right)}{\operatorname * {P r} \left(\theta_ {\mathrm{r}}\right) + \operatorname * {P r} \left(\theta_ {\mathrm{d}}\right)},\tag{A1}
$$

When the retailer can correctly forecast the market state, we must have $\operatorname* { P r } ( s _ { \mathrm { r } } | \theta _ { \mathrm { r } } ) =$ $\mathrm { P r } ( s _ { \mathrm { d } } | \theta _ { \mathrm { d } } ) = 1$ , and hence, $I _ { \mathrm { m a x } } = 1$ . When the signal received by the retailer is uninformative, we must have

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (s _ {\mathrm{r}} | \theta_ {\mathrm{r}}) = \gamma \text {   and   } \operatorname * {P r} (s _ {\mathrm{d}} | \theta_ {\mathrm{d}}) = 1 - \gamma ,
$$

and hence, $I _ { \operatorname* { m i n } } = \gamma ^ { 2 } { + } ( 1 { - } \gamma ) ^ { 2 }$

Of course, the retailer’s forecasting capacity ultimately depends on his capability of gathering and processing individual market information. To measure this capability, we define the retailer’s forecasting capability $\rho$ by rescaling I upon [0,1] with the following identity

$$
\rho = \frac {I - I _ {\min}}{I _ {\max} - I _ {\min}}, \text { i.e., } I = \rho + (1 - \rho) [ \gamma^ {2} + (1 - \gamma) ^ {2} ].\tag{A2}
$$

where $\rho$ is the index measure for the retailer’s ability to forecast the market state (regular or disruption). When $\rho = 0$ , the retailer does not have valuable information at all; hence, $I = I _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ . When $\rho = 1$ , the retailer can distinguish the market states perfectly; hence, $I = I _ { \mathrm { m a x } } = 1$

## Appendix C. Proof of Lemma 2

Owing to $P _ { \mathrm { r } } > P _ { \mathrm { d } } .$ , the retailer can place an order $q > 0$ , no matter what the signal observed by him is whenever the wholesale price w is lower than $P _ { \mathrm { d } P }$ . $$

$$

when the signal is $s \mathrm { d } ,$ , his order quantity is

According to the requirement $^ { \mathrm { e } } w \leq P \mathrm { d } p ^ { \mathrm { 7 } }$ , the condition under which Situation I can apply is

$$

Situation II. $^ { \mathrm { < < } } P _ { \mathrm { d } } p < w \leq P _ { \mathrm { r } } p ^ { \mathrm { > } }$
