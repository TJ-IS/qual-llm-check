Abstra ct: Application-based pricing is common in telecommunications. Wireless carriers charge consumers more per byte of traffic for text messages than they do for wireless surfing or voice calls. Such pricing is possible because carriers and handset manufacturers have the ability to tag and meter each application. While tagging and metering are possible in the case of closed platforms such as iPhone, they are not in the case of open platforms such as Android. Android is open source with open application programming interfaces, and anyone can develop applications for it. Because the carriers have little control over applications, Android is inherently disruptive of differential pricing across applications. Users and neutrality advocates support Android, believing that it can increase consumer surplus by disrupting differential pricing. However, we show that the equilibrium under differential pricing is different from the equilibrium under open platforms, and it is particularly so with regard to the sets of consumers served and the quantities consumed. With open platforms, certain consumers are either not served or they are served a quantity that is less than what they would be served under differential pricing. Consequently, the consumer surplus and the social surplus are often lower with open platforms. Similarly, firms are expected to prefer differential pricing. We show that this expectation is also not true under certain circumstances in which open platforms and neutral pricing work like a quasi-bundle.

Key word s a nd phra ses: net neutrality, nonlinear pricing, open platforms, quasibundling, wireless services.

Al com unica tions in a m od ern cel phone system a re d igital . Voice is converted to a digital data stream using advanced codecs (compressor-decompressors) with realtime compression and decompression. Despite this common communication mode, the price of digitized traffic (per megabyte of data transmitted) varies widely from one application to another. For instance, we computed the prices for a national carrier in the United States using a data rate of 100 kilobytes per minute for voice and 100 bytes per message for SMS (short message service) text messages: A price of \$0.10 per minute for voice turned out to be equivalent to a price of \$1 per megabyte, while a price of \$0.20 per message for SMS text turned out to be equivalent to \$2,000 per megabyte. This kind of discrimination based on application (or traffic type) is also seen in other areas of telecommunications, for example, cable traffic and broadband Internet traffic are charged different rates [14].

This wide variation in prices is made possible by the close association between cell phone manufacturers, phone software producers, and carriers, which enables applicationbased pricing because the carriers enjoy the ability to tag and meter each application separately. Open platforms have the potential to disrupt this capability. Anyone can develop and distribute new applications for open platforms such as Android. Carriers supporting open platforms may not have the ability to restrict these applications or the ability to tag them for the purpose of metering them separately. They can still meter the total digitized traffic and charge a tariff that depends on this traffic, however.

Many in the United States, including many supporters of net neutrality, welcome open platforms such as Android. They believe that these open platforms benefit consumers by curbing the practice of differential pricing. According to them, differential pricing of any form based on the source, destination, or traffic type only enriches carriers at the expense of consumers [17]. Walt Mossberg, a popular Wall Street Journal columnist, claims:

We need a wireless mobile device ecosystem that mirrors the PC/Internet ecosystem, one where the consumers’ purchase of network capacity is separate from their purchase of the hardware and software they use on that network. It will take government action, or some disruptive technology or business innovation, to get us there. [12]

Despite the popularity of this view against differential pricing and the advent of disruptive technologies such as Android, the literature on pricing and economics of information has not critically examined the merits and demerits of the application-based pricing prevalent in the United States. Our intention is to fill this void in the literature.

We note that open platforms also have an impact on innovation, particularly with regard to the development of new applications. The freedom to develop applications spurs development of new applications for them. We do not analyze the innovation issue in this paper. We analyze the first-order effect of open platforms on the prevalent practice of differential pricing across applications.

To understand how the disruption of closed platforms and application-based pricing affects carriers, consumers, and the society as a whole, we compare two price regimes. The first regime, which is often the case with closed platforms, involves pricing different applications differently. Carriers in the United States often use such pricing. For example, consider the pricing practice of a leading carrier. For voice calls, it currently offers consumers several options, including the option to choose a plan that costs \$80 a month and includes 1,000 minutes of call time and another that costs \$60 a month but includes only 700 minutes. Consumers can also choose between purchasing 300 text messages for a price of \$5 a month or 1,000 text messages for \$10 a month. For each application, consumers thus have the option of picking a suitable plan from a menu of plans. In the case of open platforms, a carrier would simply price the traffic consumed while leaving to the consumer the decision to allocate the purchased traffic between applications according to his or her preferences. An Android plan that charges a monthly access fee of \$60 in addition to a \$1.99 charge for every megabyte of data transmitted is a good example of this regime.

We model a monopolist’s tariff design problem assuming it faces a heterogeneous market with two consumer types. We carry out the comparison of the two pricing regimes assuming that each regime employs nonlinear pricing [7, 10, 13, 15, 16]. Nonlinear pricing is highly relevant to wireless services. The most commonly used wireless tariff looks as follows: “The charge is p dollars per month for up to n units of usage, and usage over n units will be billed at r dollars per unit.” Such “fixed up to” (FUT ) pricing has three components: a fixed fee of p dollars, a free call time allowance of n units, and an over-limit rate of r dollars per unit. Masuda and Whang [11] show that no consumer in the equilibrium goes over the limit when the number of consumer types is finite and the monthly usage of a consumer type is deterministic. Further, there is empirical evidence suggesting that this is largely true: only a minority goes over the limit [9]. Given these analytical results and empirical evidence, we simplify the FUT pricing to a simple nonlinear price schedule.

We examine two kinds of consumer heterogeneity in a two-application setting. In the first case, one class of consumers is willing to pay more than the other class for both applications. Throughout this paper, we refer to consumers with higher willingness to pay as the high type and those with lower willingness to pay as the low type. So in the first case, the same consumer class is the high type for both applications. In the second case, one class of consumers is willing to pay more for one application while the other class is willing to pay more for the other application—that is, different consumer classes are high types for different applications. It is imperative for us to note that the two cases of consumer heterogeneity described here are different from vertical or horizontal differentiation models: unlike the situations modeled in the product differentiation literature [4], a consumer in our model does not choose between applications and can, in fact, use both.

The objective of this research is to show that common beliefs regarding the impact of open platforms on producer, consumer, and social surpluses often do not hold. We find that the prevalent view, that consumers favor open platforms, holds only in certain circumstances. For example, in the case where the same class of consumers is the high type for both applications, closed platforms lead to a higher consumer surplus in markets characterized by a moderate-to-large fraction of high-type consumers. This situation occurs because low-type consumers often get partly or fully frozen out of the cell phone market with open platforms and traffic-based pricing, even though they get access to voice plans when application-based pricing is used. As we know from the theory on second-degree price discrimination [13], when low-type consumers consume more, it increases the surplus that goes to the high type. Further, because closed platforms also lead to a higher producer surplus in this situation, the social surplus is also higher with closed platforms.

Turning to the producer, we find that open platforms can increase profits when different classes of consumers are high types for different applications. This happens because traffic-based pricing is essentially quasi-bundling. With quasi-bundling, each consumer picks his or her bundle composition [5, 8]; specifically, in the case of traffic-based pricing, each consumer decides how to divide the purchased traffic between applications. In contrast, in the case of bundling, the seller chooses the bundle composition [1, 3]. Just as bundling becomes effective when consumer preferences exhibit an inverse relationship [15], we find that quasi-bundling or traffic-based pricing also becomes effective when different consumer classes are high types for different applications. This is precisely the reason that a carrier finds open platforms profitable under certain situations, contrary to what many in the United States currently expect.

To sum up the discussion, this work contributes to the literature by explaining the disruptive effects of open platforms on differential pricing. We present a novel model of traffic-based pricing and explain why consumer and social surpluses can be lower with traffic-based pricing. Further, we contribute to the literature on quasi-bundling by showing that open platforms can increase the producer surplus. All these results are new and unexpected, and therefore they also augment the existing literature on nonlinear pricing.

Next, we introduce the model and discuss the assumptions. In the third section, for the case where the same class of consumers is the high type for both applications, we derive solutions and compare closed platforms using application-based pricing with open platforms using traffic-based pricing. We do the same in fourth section for the case where different classes of consumers are high types for different applications. And we consider in the fifth section the fact that closed platforms allow both forms of pricing—application-based and traffic-based—as well as bundling, while open platforms only allow traffic-based pricing. In the concluding section, we provide a rich discussion of our primary findings, their implications, and the limitations of this research.

## Model Preliminaries

When a ca rrier em pl oys cl osed pla tf orm s, it announces a separate nonlinear pricing schedule for each application. We use the term plan to refer to an item on a schedule.

If a carrier offers a plan $( p _ { c } , n _ { c } )$ for an application, say C, it means that a consumer can purchase $n _ { c }$ units of that application for $p _ { c }$ dollars. On the other hand, when the carrier uses open platforms, it uses one nonlinear pricing schedule to price the traffic consumption—it offers plans of type $( p , t )$ , which means that a consumer can purchase t bytes of traffic for p dollars.<sup>1</sup> After the decision to purchase, each consumer allocates the purchased traffic to different applications based on his or her own preferences for those applications. Taking the traffic of each application as given, we can normalize the units for application-based pricing to be the same as that of traffic. For example, we can express the plan $( p _ { c } , n _ { c } )$ discussed above also as $( p _ { c } , t _ { c } )$ , where $t _ { c } = n _ { c } \tau _ { c }$ and $\tau _ { c }$ is the average traffic generated per unit of application C. Such normalization to a common unit makes it easier for us to compare the two pricing regimes.

As already mentioned, we model a two-application scenario. (All the notations used in our modeling in this paper are included in Appendix A.) We call them C (calling) and M (messaging). We examine a market with two types of consumers, types 1 and 2, whose fractions are, respectively, $1 - \lambda$ and $\lambda ,$ where $0 < \lambda < 1$ . Let consumer type i’s reservation price for application j be $\nu _ { i j } ( t ) , i = 1$ or 2. We now state our assumptions. Following recommendations of prior empirical research [6], we assume a square root form for the reservation prices.

Assumption 1 (Consumers’ Reservation Prices): The following functional forms are assumed for consumer type 1 and 2’s reservation prices for calling, C, and messaging, M:

$$
\begin{array}{c} {v _ {1 C} (t) = 2 \alpha_ {C} \sqrt {t}, v _ {1 M} (t) = 2 \alpha_ {M} \sqrt {t}} \\ {v _ {2 C} (t) = 2 \alpha_ {C} \theta_ {C} \sqrt {t}, v _ {2 M} (t) = 2 \alpha_ {M} \theta_ {M} \sqrt {t},} \end{array}
$$

and $\mathfrak { a } _ { c } , \mathfrak { \theta } _ { c } , \mathfrak { a } _ { u } ,$ , and $\theta _ { { } _ { M } }$ are all positive.

Note that ${ \theta } _ { i } > 1$ signifies that consumer type 2 is the high type for application $j ,$ and $\theta _ { { \scriptscriptstyle i } } < 1$ signifies that consumer type 1 is the high type. We consider both possibilities. This setting with two consumer types—a high type and a low type—resembles a differentiated market. But it is in fact different. For example, in a vertically differentiated market, a seller offers two competing goods—a high-quality good and a low-quality good—with each consumer deciding between the two according to his or her preference for quality. Similarly, in a horizontally differentiated market in which a seller offers two competing goods, each consumer opts for the one that minimizes his or her “fit cost.” In our case, a consumer may buy both applications.

Following the literature on bundling [2], we also assume that the total willingness to pay for the service is the willingness to pay for calling plus the willingness to pay for messaging. We do so in order to separate out issues pertaining to synergy and substitutability between applications. One can argue that synergies are possible or that having one application may in fact make the other less valuable as they both offer ways of communicating. However, incorporating such factors in our model would clutter the comparison of closed and open platforms, biasing the results one way or another depending on whether we assume synergy or substitutability. We therefore do what the bundling literature has done in order to simplify the problem and to focus on the issue at hand.

Assumption 2 (The Carrier’s Marginal Cost): The carrier’s marginal cost is k dollars per byte of traffic.

We use a constant marginal cost in order to separate out the issues related to scale economy. We therefore do not examine issues on the cost side such as congestion, modeling that typically requires assuming a convex cost function (equivalently, increasing marginal costs). We focus on the revenue side, on the comparison of application-based pricing and traffic-based pricing, without cluttering our analyses with issues on the cost side. For the same reason, we also normalize the fixed cost to zero because we consider it sunk and not relevant to the pricing decision.

## Closed Platforms

The carrier offers the schedule $( ( p _ { _ { 1 C } } , t _ { _ { 1 C } } ) , ( p _ { _ { 2 C } } , t _ { _ { 2 C } } ) )$ for calling, and the schedule $( ( p _ { _ { 1 M } } , t _ { _ { 1 M } } ) , ( p _ { _ { 2 M } } , t _ { _ { 2 M } } ) )$ for messaging. When each consumer type self-selects, as we know from the theory of second-degree discrimination [13], optimal policies for the seller occur when the low-type consumer is indifferent between purchasing and not, and the high-type consumer is indifferent between purchasing the plan aimed at the low-type consumer and its own plan.

The individual rationality (IR) or participation constraints for the low type (type 1) are

$$
v _ {1 j} \left(t _ {1 j}\right) \geq p _ {1 j}, \quad j \in \{C, M \}.\tag{IR1}
$$

The incentive compatibility (IC) constraints for the high type (type 2) are

$$
v _ {2 j} \left(t _ {2 j}\right) - p _ {2 j} \geq v _ {2 j} \left(t _ {1 j}\right) - p _ {1 j}, \quad j \in \{C, M \}.\tag{IC2}
$$

When the IR1 and IC2 constraints are equalities, we can use them to solve for $p _ { 1 j }$ and $p _ { 2 j } ,$ and we can express the carrier’s profit maximization problem as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \max \Big (\big (v _ {1 C} \left(t _ {1 C}\right) + v _ {1 M} \left(t _ {1 M}\right) \big) + \lambda \big (v _ {2 C} \left(t _ {2 C}\right) - v _ {2 C} \left(t _ {1 C}\right) \big) + \lambda \big (v _ {2 M} \left(t _ {2 M}\right) - v _ {2 M} \left(t _ {1 M}\right) \big) \\ - k \big (1 - \lambda \big) \big (t _ {1 C} + t _ {1 M} \big) - k \lambda \big (t _ {2 C} + t _ {2 M} \big) \Big) \\ \text { subject   to } t _ {1 C}, t _ {1 M}, t _ {2 C}, t _ {2 M} \geq 0. \end{array}
$$

This figure also demonstrates that wherever closed platforms lead to a higher consumer surplus, they do so while also leading to a higher producer surplus. Thus, open platforms lead to a lower social welfare in many circumstances. Only in U1, which is characterized by a few high-type consumers and a large number of lowtype consumers, disrupting application-based pricing using open platforms works as expected; it leads to a higher consumer surplus and a lower producer surplus.

We now explain the intuition behind these results. Open platforms favorably affect the consumer surplus in U1 and in parts of U2 because they let all consumers freely allocate the purchased traffic between calling and messaging. However, in U3 and in parts of U2, they significantly reduce the carrier’s incentive to serve the low-type consumers. Because the carrier is not able to restrict the low type to just calling in these regions, serving the low type requires ceding too large a surplus or information rent to the high type. On the other hand, closed platforms allow the carrier to restrict messaging only to the high type, creating opportunities to control the consumer surplus.

Consequently, with open platforms, the carrier serves the low type a quantity that is less than what it would serve with closed platforms. Such pullback on the part of the carrier lowers everyone’s surplus.

Traffic-based pricing or neutral pricing is analogous to a buffet system: consumers pay for traffic, which can be thought of as a buffet serving all applications. On the other hand, application-based or differential pricing works like an à la carte system that allows consumers to buy selectively; it allows them to pay for and use the application that they value more while skipping the one they value less. The disruption of application-based pricing is thus comparable to what we would see if all restaurants are forced to adopt a buffet system. Restaurants would respond by pricing their buffets in a way that would preclude many consumers who are not willing to pay for all items in the buffet from eating out. The prevalent view supporting open platforms is therefore not applicable in situations in which application-based pricing is essential in restricting certain applications to the high type only.

## Discussion

In this pa per, we f ocus on the f irst-ord er ef ect of open platforms on the way wireless services are currently priced. At present, not all digitized wireless traffic is treated the same; for example, traffic generated by voice calls, text messages, and wireless Web surfing are all priced differently. However, open platforms allow users to develop their own applications, which can make it difficult for a carrier to distinguish traffic generated by one application from that generated by another. We therefore argue that open platforms are likely to curb differential pricing across applications, facilitating a neutral pricing regime in which all digitized traffic is priced the same. To this extent, we also present a model to investigate how such a shift to neutral traffic-based pricing will impact consumer, producer, and social welfare. The common perception is that neutral traffic-based pricing works in favor of consumers as much as it does against carriers [12]. However, we find that there are circumstances in which this common perception does not hold. We prove that there are situations in which consumers prefer differential pricing just as there are some others in which carriers prefer neutral pricing. We also prove that the impact of neutral pricing on social welfare is often adverse.

To illustrate why the common perception can be misleading, we consider the pricing problem of a monopolist. The monopoly setting does not fully capture the realities of the U.S. wireless market, in which several major players compete. However, it is still useful. All major carriers in the United States possess substantial monopoly-like pricing power. For example, the price charged for carrying a text message is often several hundred times the marginal cost of carrying it. Clearly, the industry is not very competitive because, in a competitive market, we expect all prices to converge at the marginal cost. Such pricing power often results from the way the entire industry is organized. For example, the close association of a carrier with a leading handset manufacturer can substantially contribute to its pricing power. In short, examining the monopoly setting provides us with insights that are highly relevant, if not completely reflective.

Our model incorporates many critical facets of the market in the United States, such as heterogeneity in consumers’ valuations for wireless services. Specifically, we considered two consumer classes (see the second section). For the sake of simplicity, we also assumed that the monopolist offers only two services (applications), which we call calling, C, and messaging, M. As we demonstrated, this two-application setting is rich enough and it allows us to investigate interesting forms of consumer heterogeneity. In one possible form, which is discussed in the third section, one consumer class is the high type for both applications, and in the other possible form, which is discussed in the fourth section, different consumer classes are high types for different applications. Even though our model resembles existing models in the product differentiation literature [4], it is, in fact, very different from all of them: in our model, the applications are not substitutes and a utility-maximizing consumer may buy both applications.

In the third and fourth sections, we assumed that the carrier, while using a closed platform, uses application-based pricing—that is, it prices the applications separately. In these sections, we showed that, regardless of the form of consumer heterogeneity, a switch on the part of the carrier to open platforms and traffic-based pricing often lowers the consumer surplus. Specifically, we found that when the same consumer class is the high type for both applications, the perceived benefits of open platforms are realized only in situations that involve a few high-type consumers and a large number of lowtype consumers. In situations that involve a larger fraction of high-type consumers, the carrier, unable to prevent low-type consumers from using certain applications, starts underserving them. Forgoing the low-type consumers partly or fully leads to a lower surplus for the carrier. Further, when low-type consumers are not served, the carrier does not concede any information rent or surplus to the high type, since the consumer surplus is only required to keep the high type away from the low-type’s plan when both types are served. So, the consumer surplus also goes down when one type is not served or it is served minimal quantities.

The same phenomenon occurs even when different consumer classes are high types for different applications: under traffic-based pricing, one consumer type can get shut out, but under application-based pricing, no one gets shut out as each consumer at the very least uses the application for which he or she is the high type. This shutout of one group of consumers, which occurs despite their willingness to pay more for one of the two applications, lowers the consumer surplus in regions where it occurs. Again, this shutout is detrimental to the consumer surplus because the carrier concedes an information rent or consumer surplus only if it serves both types. The lesson, in summary, is that closed platforms allow a producer greater flexibility with regard to how it serves different market segments and that the flexibility is critical to avoiding shutouts and losses in consumer welfare.

This result regarding the impact on the consumer surplus is both counterintuitive and new. One way to interpret this result is to recognize that traffic-based pricing is analogous to selling a buffet consisting of both applications. In other words, with open platforms and traffic-based pricing, one class of consumers often pays for and uses both applications, or uses none, when they would have preferred to pay for and use only one application. This lack of flexibility causes them to forgo the use of wireless services partly or fully in certain situations, which in turn reduces the consumer surplus that the monopolist cedes to the other class. In this respect, our work is among the first to explain how consumers gain when different applications of the same information good (traffic) are priced differently.

Further, just as a buffet system can work in some cases to the advantage of the seller, we show that open platforms and traffic-based pricing, contrary to what many experts expect, can also be beneficial to the carrier. The intuition here is that buffets are like bundles, and bundles increase profits when consumer preferences are inversely related, that is, when different consumer classes are high types for different applications. Although similar, this result is not the same as the results found in the existing literature on bundling [15]. In the case of bundling, the seller decides on bundle compositions [1]. In the case of traffic-based pricing, it is the consumers who make that decision, that is, traffic-based pricing is best described as quasi-bundling [5]. We show that quasi-bundling is particularly effective from a producer’s viewpoint when different market segments have a similar willingness to pay for traffic. A major implication of this result is that the traditional view that deems open platforms to be unattractive from the carrier’s viewpoint is inaccurate in situations where certain consumers value one application more while the others value the other one more. We therefore also contribute to the economic theory on quasi-bundling by deriving the conditions under which quasi-bundling dominates individual selling.

In the fifth section, we considered a more complex setting. In this setting, the carrier can choose any pricing strategy while using a closed platform, that is, it is not restricted to application-based pricing alone. It can also choose bundling, traffic-based pricing (quasi-bundling), or a combination of several strategies. We also assume that the carrier is rational and it chooses the strategy that maximizes its profits. And we continue to assume that a switch to an open platform reduces its set of options to traffic-based pricing alone. We find that, even in this setting, the carrier’s best choice is pricing each application separately so long as the same consumer class is the high type for both applications. When the same consumer class is the high type for both applications, a switch from closed to open platforms is akin to a switch from application-based pricing to traffic-based pricing. In other words, in this complex setting as well, a switch to open platforms continues to be fraught with the risks of one consumer class getting shut out and the other class seeing its surpluses dwindle.

In the complex setting discussed in the fifth section, however, the carrier’s optimal strategy under closed platforms takes a more complex form if different consumer classes are high types for different applications. We also derived this complex strategy in order to explain why a switch to open platforms may still produce surprising effects. (See the proof of Theorem 5 in Appendix B.) We showed that if the relative sizes of the consumer classes are such that one consumer class gets shut out under traffic-based pricing, a switch to open platforms would lower the producer surplus without adding anything to the consumer surplus. This loss in social welfare is also explained by the fact that open platforms limit the carrier’s ability to act on the demand information, or the information that consumers are heterogeneous in their valuations. In this sense, our finding regarding the loss of social welfare is consistent with the existing theory on price discrimination that also argues that discrimination can improve social welfare [13].

While we unveiled an interesting effect of open platforms, we also have left out many others, including those related to how open platforms influence innovations with regard to the development of new applications. We also limited ourselves to a model that does not consider the usage uncertainty [9] on the part of the consumers and how this uncertainty influences their buying decisions. We considered two classes of consumers, while in reality, there may be a continuum of classes. We investigated a two-application setting, while in reality, there are more than two applications. Although these simplifications made our results less general than one might desire, they did not diminish the most salient finding that common perceptions about open platforms are not universally applicable. The implication of this work is that consumers need to be mindful about the negative consequences of being forced into a buffet system as much as the carrier needs to be aware of the benefits of adopting a buffet system. In our future research endeavors, we intend to generalize our results in order to make them applicable to other related debates, such as the debate on net neutrality. We expect that this research, despite its limitations, will provide us with the necessary foundation for further research in this area.
