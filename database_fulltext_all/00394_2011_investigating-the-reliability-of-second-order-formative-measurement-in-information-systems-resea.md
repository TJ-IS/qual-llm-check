# Investigating the reliability of second-order formative measurement in information systems research

Bongsik Shin<sup>1</sup> and Gimun Kim<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

Recently, a few studies empirically explored the stability of first-order formative measurement, and raised concerns with its estimation reliability. Interpretational confounding, the disparity in the nominal and empirical meaning of a formatively measured construct, is at the center stage of the concern. Our study examines the issue in the context of the higher-order abstraction, focusing on the formatively defined relationship between the second-order construct and its indicators (i.e., first-order latent variables). Although the second-order formative abstraction is a widely accepted practice in structural equation modeling, the estimation results have been given a blind faith with no attempt to evaluate their integrity. Our empirical test, therefore, constitutes an attempt to fill the void. This study observed moderations of the theoretical relationship between reflectively designed first-order constructs and formatively defined second-order constructs when there is a change of endogenous variables. For this, two different formatively defined second-order constructs (i.e., IT management capabilities and IT personnel expertise) are utilized for the empirical testing. The estimation reveals that, while there was a considerable moderation of weights between IT management capabilities and its first-order constructs, those between IT personnel expertise and its first-order constructs remained relatively stable. These results demonstrate that the formatively defined relationship between the first- and second-order constructs can be precarious depending on the choice of the dependent variables. The analysis, therefore, revealed a significant presence of interpretational confounding and a higher chance of Type 1 error in model estimation. This implies that it becomes difficult to retain the construct validity and external validity of a formatively defined second-order construct. Thus, researchers are encouraged to exercise caution in mobilizing the formatively defined second-order measurement.

## Introduction

Researchers have the choice of using formative or reflective models when they develop measures or reuse existing measures because most constructs are conceptually neither reflective nor formative without their measures in place (Borsboom et al., 2004; Wilcox et al., 2008). With the increasing maturity of Information System (IS) research, there is a prevalent usage of formative measurement. Formative measurement can offer notable benefits theoretically (e.g., modeling a construct from a set of potentially distinct but observable phenomenon) and nomologically (e.g., study of causality in a parsimonious setup) (Diamantopoulos et al., 2008; Cenfetelli and Bassellier, 2010). Recently, however, scholars began to raise concerns formative measurement might have to face in its conceptualization and estimation (e.g., Bagozzi, 2007; Howell et $a l . ,$ 2007; Wilcox et $a l .$ , 2008). Interpretational confounding, the disparity in the nominal and empirical meaning of a formatively measured construct, takes the center stage among the issues (e.g., Kim et al., 2010). Not surprisingly, this issue is closely related to the validity of formative indicators. Several recent studies (e.g., Hardin et $a l . ,$ 2008; Kim et $a l . ,$ 2010) raise concerns with the construct validity and external validity of a formative construct by empirically testing the estimation reliability of formative measurement.

Much of the theoretical argument of formative measurement is grounded on the fact that, unlike the reflectively defined measurement model (hereafter, reflective model) that can be estimated independently, the formatively defined measurement model (hereafter, formative model) relies on other dependent variables for the estimation of its measure weights. This inability to act independently of the others during model estimation is argued to subject the formative model to potential estimation corruption and subsequent sacrifice of external validity in empirical analysis. There are, however, countering views that the empirical demonstrations indicating such weakness of formative models are grounded on flawed methods (e.g., measurement model misspecification and dependence on simulated correlation matrices) and therefore biased (e.g., Bollen, 2007). With the debate at its nascent stage, Kim et al. (2010) suggested that more empirical efforts are necessary to settle the issue.

The extant studies that empirically explored the stability of formative measurement are based on the first-order construct and its indicators. For instance, Kim et al. (2010) conducted a comparative analysis on whether the alleged estimation instability of formative measurement is due to measurement model misspecification or simply its inherent shortcoming. For this, it framed a research design that cross-validates the two different viewpoints, and found vulnerability of formative measurement in interpretational confounding. Meanwhile, roughly half of IS studies with formative measurement adopted a higher-order abstraction for which the first-order latent variables act as its indicators. Despite the frequent appearance of second-order constructs, no attempt has been made to understand the estimation stability when they are formatively defined.

This research attempts to fill the gap. This study is not questioning the theoretical or nomological value of the second-order construct, but intends to test the reliability of the relationship between the second-order construct and its indicators (i.e., first-order latent variables) when their relationship is formatively framed. The findings of this work, therefore, complements Kim et al.’s (2010)

that focused on the first-order formative measurement. Although the early debates have covered different theoretical angles, our focus is on interpretational confounding, the most germane issue in assessing the estimation reliability of structural equation models. In the next sections, we introduce the theoretical descriptions of interpretational confounding, build the case of our test models with second-order constructs, and then conduct empirical tests based on an actual survey data set.

## Interpretational confounding

One of the frequented theoretical issues of construct measurement is interpretational confounding. It ‘occurs as the assignment of empirical meaning to an unobserved variable which is other than the meaning assigned to it by an individual a priori to estimating unknown parameters’ (Burt, 1976, p. 4). It represents the situation in which the empirically observed meaning between a latent variable and its measures differs from the nominal meaning expected under the original specification. Its presence becomes an indication that the tested measures of a latent variable are distorted or contaminated.

When there is a significant interpretational confounding between empirical and nominal meanings of a construct, construct validity cannot be warranted (Hardin et al., 2008) and statements of generalizability become problematic for formative constructs and their measures (Bagozzi, 2007; Cenfetelli and Bassellier, 2010). This also could hamper the reusability of latent variables limiting progressive knowledge accumulation in IS research. Howell et al. (2007) describe the negative bearing of interpretational confounding on cumulative production of research:

If a construct named ‘A’ in one study is substantively different from a construct named ‘A’ in another study, then accumulation of knowledge regarding a construct is rendered meaningless or impossible because the construct in one study is incommensurable with a different construct, but with the same name, in another study. (Howell et al., 2007, p. 209)

For formative measurement, interpretational confounding may be largely due to the fact that the construct weights (or gammas) cannot be estimated without referencing to other endogenous variables or constructs. When covariance among the measures of a formative construct is weak, weights can be affected by such extraneous forces as the number and nature of dependent constructs and their measures (Bagozzi, 2007). The estimation of a formative construct then becomes a function of both its indicators and other endogenous variables. This may make it difficult to maintain the interpretational consistency of the formative construct. When interpretational consistency varies depending on the dependent variables included or excluded, measurement becomes ambiguous (Edwards and Bagozzi, 2000: Bagozzi. 2007).

Whereas the coefficients of formative measures should depend on other variables<sup>1</sup> for identification, those of reflective measures can be estimated without relying on other variables. The reflective model becomes a ‘purely epistemic baseline measurement model’ (Howell et al., 2007). It should be noted that reflective measurements also engenders interpretational confounding when epistemic relationships are weaker than the parameters of a structural equation model. However, there exists a significant unitary (therefore replaceable) relationship among the measures of a reflective construct, and epistemic relationships are rarely weaker than structural parameters. This may make reflective measurement less vulnerable to interpretational confounding than formative measurement.

## IS studies with second-order constructs

The usage of second-order constructs is a widely accepted practice in modeling the relationship among studied variables. The second-order construct is multidimensional, and both first- and second-order constructs included in the structural modeling can be either reflective or formative (Diamantopoulos et al., 2008). This results in four alternative relationships among constructs: formative first- and second-order; formative first-order and reflective secondorder; reflective first- and second-order; and reflective firstorder and formative second-order ( Jarvis et al., 2003).

Recent empirical studies suggest that, among the four types of higher-order modeling, formative-reflective and formative-formative approaches in which the relationship between the first-order construct and its measures are formatively defined are subject to the estimation bias. In other words, it is a natural extension of empirical findings by Kim et al. (2010) and Howell et al. (2007) that pointed to the instability of formative first-order constructs against estimation bias. This line of reasoning, however, does not apply to the ‘reflective-formative’ model in which the first-order constructs are reflectively defined and therefore presumed to be stable in their parameter estimation. Nonetheless, no research effort has been made on understanding the estimation reliability of the ‘reflective-formative’ model.

In the meantime, a number of IS studies have been conducted utilizing higher-order constructs in structural equation modeling (SEM). Given the large volume of these studies, we summarized relevant publications appeared in the two representative IS journals (MISQ and ISR) since 2000. The summary shown in Table 1 is based on the crosstabulation of the relationships (reflective vs formative) of the first-order (between indicators and first-order) and the second-order (between first-order and second-order) constructs. The review uncovers that the majority of empirical studies grounded on the higher-order abstraction has adopted either ‘reflective-reflective’ or ‘reflective-formative’ modeling. Presuming the relative stability of the ‘reflectivereflective’ model in its estimation (Kim et al., 2010), we focus on examining the second-order reflective-formative model in terms of its estimation stability.

## Research method

## Data and study variables

For the estimation of test models, covariance matrices derived from the survey data set (Kim et al., 2010) were analyzed. Proponents (e.g., Bollen, 2007) who advocate for the formative modeling point out that early empirical studies (e.g., Howell et al., 2007) are flawed in assessing the reliability of formative measurement because of their dependence on simulated correlation matrices with no theoretical bearing. To avoid the potential bias, therefore, we utilize an actual data set that was originally gathered to examine how the competence of IT management and IT people affects firm-level IT processes and performance.

To examine the estimation reliability of reflective-formative models, this study utilizes seven first-order reflective constructs in four different test models. Three constructs (i.e., financial performance, IT performance, and business process performance) are endogenous variables and the remaining four (i.e., effectiveness of IT planning, effectiveness of IT control, technical knowledge and business knowledge) are exogenous constructs. The four exogenous constructs form two second-order formative constructs, IT management capabilities and IT personnel expertise. The measure items of the seven first-order reflective variables and their literature sources are summarized in Appendix 1. Refer to Kim et al. (2010) for the descriptions of the scale development procedure and the data collection method. The variables and their definitions are:

## Endogenous variables

\- Financial performance: A firm’s general financial perfor mance over the past 3 years.

\- IT performance: Contribution of ITs to the performance (e.g., productivity, sales, profitability, and competitive position) of a firm.

\- Business process performance: Operational efficiency of inter- and intra- organizational processes of a firm.

## Exogenous variables (first-order)

\- Effectiveness of IT planning: The level in which the planning of IT deployment and utilizations are structured according to formal and informal procedures.

\- Effectiveness of IT control: The level in which IT control activities (e.g., development, management, and operation) are structured according to formal and informal procedures.

\- Technical knowledge: IT staff’s understanding of an organization’s IT elements, including operating systems, programming languages, database, and networking.

\- Business knowledge: IT staff’s understanding of various business functions and business environment.

## Test models

Figure 1 illustrates test models, which include effectiveness of IT planning and effectiveness of IT control as the firstorder reflectively defined constructs, and IT management capability as the second-order formatively defined construct. The formative test models in Figures 1a and 1b are conveniently named RF-Model 1 and RF-Model 2. It can be seen that the only difference between the two is the choice of endogenous constructs: IT Performance and Business Process Performance for RF-Model 1, and IT Performance and Financial Performance for RF-Model 2.

The second-order formative construct, IT management capability, represents IT function’s ability to manage resources in order to transform them into business values at an organization. Judging from the general attributes of the formative perspective (Jarvis et al., 2003; Petter et al., 2007), there is a conceptually clear formative relationship between the second-order construct (IT management capability) and its sub-constructs (effectiveness of IT planning and effectiveness of IT control). First of all, effectiveness of IT planning and effectiveness of IT control constitute key theoretical facets of a firm’s IT management capability (e.g., Sabherwal, 1999; Van der Zee and De Jong, 1999), and therefore are conceptually divergent (not interchangeable). Second, the sub-constructs form the concept space of IT management capability and their addition or deletion moderates the construct’s conceptual domain. Third, the sub-constructs shape the degree of IT management capability, but not the reverse (direction of causality) (Coltman et al., 2008).

The test models in Figure 2 are identical in their structure and the choice of endogenous constructs to those in Figure 1. However, they include a different set of exogenous variables: Technical knowledge and Business knowledge as the first-order constructs, and IT personnel expertise as the second-order construct that represents the level of professional knowledge possessed by IT staff of an organization. The second-order construct (IT personnel expertise) and its sub-constructs (technical knowledge and business knowledge) also have a clear formative relationship. First of all, technical knowledge and business knowledge reflects the key theoretical facets of a firm’s IT personnel expertise, and therefore are conceptually divergent (not interchangeable). Second, the addition or deletion of the sub-constructs changes the construct’s conceptual domain. Third, the sub-constructs shape the degree of a firm’s IT personnel expertise, but not the reverse (direction of causality) (Coltman et al., 2008).

In the test models, the relationship between the first-order construct and its measures was designed to be reflective. With the reflective relationship, following four theoretical and empirical criteria apply to their relationship: the measures (refer to Appendix 1) share a common theme and therefore interchangeable; the addition or removal of an indicator has little effect on the content validity of the construct; variation in the four indicators is caused by variation in the construct but not the reverse; and, with the theoretical homogeneity among indicators, high internal consistency is expected.

The ranges of correlation coefficients among four indicators of each first-order construct (0.598 B 0.823 for IT planning; 0.645 B 0.778 for IT control; 0.604 B 0.797 for Technical knowledge; and 0.478 B 0.726 for Business knowledge) indicated significant internal consistency.

## Theoretical roots of test models

The general structure of test models has theoretical roots. Above all, the formative construct cannot be identified alone. Accordingly, Jarvis et al. (2003, p. 214) offered three approaches that enable the identification of the first-order formative construct: (1) a formative construct identified relying on two reflective dependent constructs; (2) a formative construct identified by having at least two reflective indicators; and (3) a formative construct identified by having at least one reflective indicator and one reflective dependent construct. This requirement extends to the identification of the second-order formative construct. Among the three choices, the first option is more reflective of academic research models and. therefore.

the general structure of our test models has one second-order construct and two reflective dependent constructs. As for the relationship between the firstand second-order constructs, we decided to include two first-order constructs to maintain the parsimony of the structural model as shown in Figures 1 and 2.

In addition, a second-order construct that has a formative relationship with the two first-order constructs needs at least one more dependent variable (actual measure) to identify itself. This is because the SEM tool (LISREL in our case) treats the second-order formative construct as an endogenous variable of the two first-order constructs, and therefore its identification needs at least one indicator. For the identification of the second-order formative constructs, therefore, a reflective indicator item (a connectivity measure) that represents IT infrastructure flexibility was additionally included in the test models as a non-latent, dependent (criterion) variable of IT management capability and IT personnel expertise (To avoid interpretive confusion, however, models in Figures 1 and 2 do not include the indicator). The survey item of the connectivity measure representing IT infrastructure flexibility reads ‘Our company’s IT infrastructure is better than competitors’ in linking to any other components inside and outside the organizational environment’.

## Empirical tests

In this section, we empirically examine whether the second-order formative constructs are exposed to the problems of interpretational confounding. LISREL is used for model estimation. Although many IS researchers adopt Partial Least Square (PLS) for the estimation of formative research models, it has fundamental limitations in conducing theoretical studies of this nature. First, PLS does not estimate overall model fit indices making it difficult to judge the suitability of research models (Kim et al., 2010). Second, with no estimation of model fit, PLS makes it difficult to judge the validity of formative indicators as a set (Diamantopoulos et al., 2008). Third, PLS assumes that the construct level error term remains zero (Hardin et al., 2010). This bears a theoretical weakness that, regardless of the reliability of chosen formative measures, they are presumed to become the perfect indicators of a formative construct.

Appendices 2 and 3 summarize the covariance matrices between measured variables in Figures 1 and 2. The degree of interpretational confounding for the secondorder construct can be observed by comparing the moderation of coefficients between the first- and the second-order constructs when their endogenous variables change. The estimation results based on test models in Figures 1 and 2 are summarized in Tables 2 and 3, respectively.

In all test models of Tables 2 and 3, the hypothesized relationships between the first-order constructs and their indicators are supported by the empirical testing, supporting the validity of the first-order indicators (Diamantopoulos et al., 2008). Also, the overall model fit indices of test models achieved their requirement levels, an indication of indicator validity as a set (Diamantopoulos et al., 2008). Finally, the beta parameters of all test models confirm the theoretical relationships between the second-order construct and endogenous variables, supporting the nomological validities (Jarvis et al., 2003).

IT management capability as second-order construct In both RF model 1 and RF model 2 of Table 2, the coefficients of reflectively defined first-order measurement models remain highly stable and consistent regardless of the change of a dependent variable. As for the standardized weights between the first- and the second-order constructs, reflective-formative models (RF-model 1 and RF-model 2) indicate significant fluctuations (e.g., 0.24 vs 0.16 for IT planning; 0.56 vs 0.68 for IT control) with the replacement of one endogenous variable. ## IT personnel expertise as second-order construct

The estimation results of test models in Figure 2 are summarized in Table 3. Similar to the results of test models in Figure 1, the standardized coefficients between the first-order constructs and their indicators maintain their stability regardless of the changes in endogenous variables. As for the relationship between the first- and the second-order constructs, the standardized weights (0.35 and 0.38 for Technical knowledge and 0.49 and 0.46 for Business knowledge) in RF models (RF-Model 1 and RF-Model 2) did not change considerably with the replacement of endogenous variables. This is a noticeable departure from the significant fluctuations of estimations occurred with the previous model.

In fact, in comparing the magnitude of changes in standardized coefficients between Tables 2 and 3, a question can be raised on what constitutes a significant difference in path weights. While the estimation gap was 0.08 for IT planning and 0.12 for IT control in Table 2, it reduces to 0.03 for both Technical knowledge and Business knowledge in Table 3. Testing the differences (i.e., 0.08 vs 0.03 and 0.12 vs 0.03) statistically is meaningless as the compared constructs have no conceptual relevancy. Nonetheless, it is also safe to say that the bigger weight corrections in Table 2 increase the risk of estimation errors in statistical testing and subsequent model fit. The path from IT Planning to IT Management Capacity that flip flops in its statistical significance due to the replacement of a dependent variable underscores the risk.

Overall, the discrepancy of estimation results suggests that the stability of weights between the formative second-order construct and its reflective sub-constructs can swing substantially depending on the particular choice or combination of endogenous variables. In other words, the theoretical meaning of a formative secondorder construct can partially rest on the endogenous variables included in the model. Some studies relying on IT management capability or IT personnel expertise as a formative second-order construct may conclude with construct validity, while others may not. A more serious problem is that researchers have little control or means to retain a study’s construct validity. This arbitrariness in estimation also makes it difficult to argue the external validity of an empirical test taking advantage of the formative construct (Hardin et al., 2008).

## Discussions and conclusion

Recently, scholars began raising concerns with the reliability that formative measurement might have to face in its conceptualization and estimation. A few empirical studies currently available reviewed the issue from the perspective of the formative construct in its first-order. This study examined the issue in the context of higher-order abstraction, focusing on the formatively defined relationship between the second-order construct and its measures (i.e., first-order latent variables). The key contribution point of this research is that, despite the wide acceptance and prevalent usage of the secondorder formative construct in SEM, there has been no empirical attempt to evaluate its integrity and subsequently the estimation results have been given a blind faith by researchers. Our empirical study, therefore, constitutes an attempt to fill the void. For this, our empirical test was performed to reveal the reliability of a prevalent type of second-order modeling: reflectiveformative modeling. To curtail the potential bias emanating from the use of simulated covariance matrices (Bollen, 2007), we took advantage of an actual survey data set.

From the empirical test, it is learned that the formatively defined relationship between the first-order and the second-order constructs can be precarious and unpredictable when the dependent variable(s) changes. The seemingly lack of consistency in the measure weights of the second-order construct could make it difficult to give confidence in the estimation integrity of the formative structural model. That the second-order formative construct is undefined without relying on other reflective variables or measures seems to make the overall model estimation less stable. In other words, just as the measure weights of the first-order formative construct are affected by the choice of endogenous variables (Howell et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2010), the weights of second-order formative constructs seem to become the function of the number and nature of endogenous constructs and their measures. The overall results of standardized weights and model fit seem to indicate that the weights of the second-order construct are resolved in a manner to optimize the path coefficients between exogenous and endogenous variables and the overall fit of the SEM model. This may result in the corruption of formative measure weights and the inflation of the overall model fit and path coefficients, leading to an increased chance of committing Type 1 error in model assessment (Kim et al., 2010). Consequently, securing the construct validity and external validity of a formatively defined construct may become problematic (Hardin et al., 2008).

Is there any solution that IS researchers can adopt to deal with the potential estimation corruptions of the second-order formative modeling? The answer is unfortunately no until a proven method that can undermine the risk is developed. This remains an important but challenging research subject. At the moment, we would like to suggest that researchers take a caution in exercising the reflective-formative modeling approach. For this. authors may consider the path in which the reflective-formative model is replaced with the first-order reflective model or with the reflective-reflective model to the extent possible. This suggestion is grounded on the theoretical position that the majority of formatively defined constructs can be transformed into reflective ones (Borsboom et al., 2004; Petter et al., 2007). Meanwhile, if the reflective-formative modeling is an absolute must, researchers may consider the pretest of the gathered data set based on two simplified, but different models as in Figure 1 to test the reliability of the formatively defined relationship (This will take 3 different dependent variables). If the pretest turns out to be relatively stable, then the researchers may proceed with the complete research model.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first attempt to examine the stability of formative structural models with a higher-order latent variable. The findings, therefore, are rather tentative and more empirical studies are warranted to reveal consistent patterns. Especially, we had like to highlight that the findings of this research are restricted to the context of reflectiveformative measurement model and cannot be extended to other types of high-order relationship (e.g., formativeformative).
