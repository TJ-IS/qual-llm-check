## a b s t r a c t

Internet tools used as knowledge retrieval mechanisms can be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for knowledge acquisition (KA). This study applies the concepts of decisional guidance and restrictiveness to three commonly used tools to predict perceived information overload, task quality, and task speed for tasks that differ in complexity. In an experimental setting we <sup>fi</sup>nd that less restrictive pull systems (i.e., web directories and Google search) increased quality in high-complexity tasks over more restrictive push systems (i.e., listservs) and lowered perceived information overload in both low- and high-complexity tasks. In low-complexity tasks, subjects using prede-<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance (i.e., listservs and web directories) performed better and faster than those using participative guidance (i.e., Google search). In high-complexity tasks, participative guidance provided lower perceived information overload and higher task quality. Implications for research and practice are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

While knowledge workers can select or re-use information that already exists in knowledge repositories, they may also need to acquire knowledge from external sources [19,34,57]. This knowledge acquisition process (KA) can result in knowledge being used immediately by the organization, or assimilated within an organization for subsequent use [30]. The Internet provides a large variety of knowledge retrieval tools that support KA. However, workers often have dif<sup>fi</sup>culties acquiring knowledge from external sources making some KA attempts unsuccessful [9,19]. This is disappointing given the large investment many companies have made on knowledge-related activities. Being able to effectively choose the right Internet tools is an essential step in developing organizational knowledge management capabilities [22,41]. Research that identi<sup>fi</sup>es and explains the conditions in which speci<sup>fi</sup>c Internet tools improve KA can inform this process.

Our research assesses three Internet tools that are commonly applied in the KA process: listservs, web directories, and Google search [18,29,57]. Overall use of these tools is widespread and frequent. Web directories are ubiquitous across the Internet, with large directories such as Yahoo! and the Open Directory Project maintaining millions of entries [70]. In July of 2011 the top 20 open-environment listserv hosts maintained over 190,000 active lists and delivered over 4.3 million messages daily [42]. Google handled 11.2 billion searches in the U.S. market in July of 2011 [11]. While decision support systems are commonly linked to decision performance (e.g., [35,47]), these Internet tools are more commonly linked to search performance (e.g., [33]) and information quality (e.g., [18]). Therefore, we examine their effects on KA task performance, which is an important outcome of knowledge management activities [1].

Our analysis derives from the theoretical implications of each tool's orientation toward facilitating or constraining user behavior (system restrictiveness) and the manner in which the tool guides users in the KA process (decisional guidance) [58–62]. Restrictiveness and guidance are theorized to in<sup>fl</sup>uence human performance in using interactive systems to accomplish a task [61]. Empirical tests suggest both factors are important to numerous types of information systems, including recommendation agents [68,69], forecasting decision support systems [52], audit support systems [15], and conceptual data modeling support systems [2]. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that performance of Internet tools for KA will be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by system restrictiveness and decisional guidance. To our knowledge, however, neither factor has been studied previously in the context of Internet tools for KA. This gap in the literature motivates our present research, which is directed toward improving performance of listservs, web directories, and Google search. Important bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be gained even if performance is improved only incrementally, due to the massive use each of these tools currently receives.

We analyze how restrictiveness and guidance affect task performance at two levels of task complexity. Task complexity plays a particularly in<sup>fl</sup>uential role in understanding information seeking and information retrieval system performance [12,19,32]. In addition, task complexity has been reported to interact with both restrictiveness and guidance in empirical tests [47,50]. Incorporating two levels of task complexity in our research design will allow us to detect interactive effects in the results.

The paper is organized as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst present the theoretical foundation of the research, followed by development and justi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the research hypotheses. We then present the research method and results. The concluding sections discuss research and practical implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 2. Theoretical background

We contend that the KA process is grounded in information seeking behaviors, that these behaviors are supported to a greater or lesser degree by knowledge retrieval tool features, and that the effectiveness of feature support is conditional upon characteristics of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c task. These ideas are based upon theories of information seeking, system restrictiveness, and decisional guidance, which we discuss in the following sections.

## 2.1. Information seeking

Information seeking is important for problem solving [69] and knowledge management [44]. It is critical for identifying, de<sup>fi</sup>ning, and addressing existing problems or opportunities [20,63]. Wilson's [71] information behavior model describes how information seeking behavior “arises as a consequence of a need perceived by an information user, who, in order to satisfy that need, makes demands upon formal or informal information sources or services, which result in success or failure to <sup>fi</sup>nd relevant information” ([72], p. 251). Success in <sup>fi</sup>nding relevant information leads to information use that either fully or partially satis<sup>fi</sup>es the need [71,72]. Other information behavior models including Kuhlthau's [37] stage process model, Leckie et al.'s [38] model of information seeking for professionals, Savolainen's [54] everyday life information seeking model, and Byström and Järvelin's [6] information seeking and retrieval model also describe similar elements of information seeking including an information need, information sources or channels, and information use to complete a task or solve a problem. Consistent with the general notion of information seeking from these models, our research model (see Fig. 1) contains these three basic elements: i) tasks (the need); ii) knowledge retrieval tools (the information source/service); iii) KA task performance (information use that satis<sup>fi</sup>es the need).

Implicit in our model is the idea that some knowledge retrieval tools will support a speci<sup>fi</sup>c information search better than others based upon their characteristics and the nature of the task. Informed KA requires the information search process to deliver an optimal amount of information. However individuals often <sup>fi</sup>nd too little or too much information, either of which can have adverse effects on

## 2.2. System restrictiveness

Information systems are more or less restrictive based on the degree to which the system's functionality limits the users' decisionmaking processes [59]. For example, restrictive systems can impose e-commerce decision strategies [69], enforce audit work processes [15], and prevent system designers from entering incorrect relationships [2]. Whether system restrictiveness improves or worsens task performance is conditional on the context in which it is being used. Although users can <sup>fi</sup>nd highly restrictive systems to be overly constraining, they may <sup>fi</sup>nd minimally restrictive systems to be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and more confusing to use [62].

Researchers suggest that systems are less restrictive when they provide users more control [69]. We study the perceived control associated with using certain Internet tools for locating information, and conceptualize tools with low restrictiveness as those that users perceive as affording them more control over locating information for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c task, and tools with high restrictiveness as those that users perceive as affording them less control.<sup>2</sup> We examine Internet tools that are designed to push information to users (e.g., through listservs) and that are designed for users to pull information (e.g., through web directories or Google search). Use of push versus pull delivery can strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ perceived control [45,46]. Push delivery automatically retrieves information from a repository and delivers it to the user based upon a user-created pro<sup>fi</sup>le [1,45]. For example, Tech-Republic is a popular listerserv where users can sign up for various topic-speci<sup>fi</sup>c newsletters that the listserv will automatically deliver to their email on a scheduled basis. Users perceive lower control over push delivery because the information is sent to them based on membership, rather than for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c task-related need. In pull delivery, users can actively retrieve information for speci<sup>fi</sup>c task-related needs [1,45]. Thus while push and pull delivery can provide the same informational content, push delivery is generally perceived to be more restrictive than pull delivery.

## 2.3. Decisional guidance

Information systems can support users' judgments by providing decisional guidance [60]. Decisional guidance is “the design features of an interactive computer-based system that have, or are intended to have, the effect of enlightening, swaying, or directing its users as those users exercise the discretion the system grants them to choose among and use its functional capabilities” ([61], p. 105). Guidance features can help users, for example, choose appropriate display formats [73], use appropriate statistical methods [52], compare trade-offs among products [68], and use a system development methodology [2]. When applied to the information seeking process, guidance can include navigation approaches that help users <sup>fi</sup>nd information more easily.

Decisional guidance can be predetermined (called prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance), or generated during system use based on user participation (called participative guidance) or system “learning” (called dynamic guidance) [60]. We focus on prede<sup>fi</sup>ned and participative navigation guidance because these modes characterize the Internet tools chosen in the present study. Navigation guidance is prede<sup>fi</sup>ned when navigation paths have been embedded into an information structure forming a directory or taxonomy [51] as is the case for listserv and web directory tools. Directory-based guidance is prede<sup>fi</sup>ned because the information sources connected to the words or terms in the directory structure are determined in advance.

Alternatively, navigation guidance is participative when users have the ability to search for relevant information by choosing search terms or keywords in a query-based search [51], as is the case for Google search. Participative guidance recognizes that users vary tremendously regarding the need for and use of available information. With query-based guidance every word in an information source is indexed or connected to the keyword search. The navigation paths are based solely on user's participation in entering keywords of their choosing.

While theory suggests that decisional guidance can improve decision making, guidance comes in many forms, and each form may have different bene<sup>fi</sup>ts depending on the task, the users, and the environment [61].

## 3. Hypotheses development

Our study focuses on three frequently implemented Internet tools that are used to support KA. Each tool presents a unique combination of restrictiveness and guidance characteristics (see Fig. 2).<sup>3</sup> Both listserv and web directory tools present a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned mode of guidance, but they differ in restrictiveness. Listservs deliver messages based on a preset pro<sup>fi</sup>le without regard for the user's immediate actions (more restrictive push delivery), whereas listings in web directories are delivered in response to immediate actions of the user (less restrictive pull delivery). In contrast, Google search presents a participative mode of guidance and less restrictive pull delivery.<sup>4</sup>

As illustrated in the research model, we propose that knowledge retrieval tool characteristics related to restrictiveness and guidance will interact with task complexity to in<sup>fl</sup>uence perceived information overload, task quality, and task speed.<sup>5</sup> Task complexity is a prominent characteristic of KA tasks that are relevant to both information processing and decision making [5,7,19]. We predict that task complexity will interact with knowledge retrieval tool characteristics for several reasons. First, increasing task complexity can diminish human performance by placing demands on individuals’ knowledge, skills, and resources [8,74]. Thus as complexity increases, individuals may not be able to utilize certain tool features. Second, empirical work shows that the information types and sources acquired during information seeking depend on level of task complexity [5]. This can affect task performance. Third, guidance and restrictiveness have been shown to produce different performance effects depending on task complexity [47,50].

Campbell [7] describes four task complexity dimensions: multiple paths (multiple ways to arrive at a solution), multiple unrelated outcomes (there is more than one solution, and some or all are unrelated), negative relationships among solutions (achieving one part of a solution con<sup>fl</sup>icts with achieving another part), and uncertain linkages between paths and solutions (uncertainty regarding how to solve the problem). Complexity depends on how many dimensions a task possesses (e.g., zero versus all four) as well as the degree to which the dimension exists (e.g., two versus six ways to arrive at a solution).

## 3.1. Restrictiveness and task complexity

The interactive effects of restrictiveness and task complexity are based on the idea that users perceive less control over information seeking when using more restrictiveness systems (and vice versa). Our <sup>fi</sup>rst two hypotheses predict that less restrictive pull systems (i.e., those systems engendering more perceived control) will result in lower perceived information overload as compared to push systems in both high and low-complexity tasks. Researchers suggest that when using email (i.e., a push system), perceived information overload refers to a lack of control over the information exchange because individuals are receiving unwanted information [66]. An empirical study <sup>fi</sup>nds that greater perceived control related to users' ability to make speci<sup>fi</sup>c information requests and use speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology features like search engines and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation schemes, decreases the perceived information overload associated with a new groupware system [56]. Further, regardless of task complexity, a lack of control may make the amount of conceptually irrelevant information that is delivered appear greater and result in greater perceived information overload [17]. Thus, while in general there may be lower perceived information overload in low-complexity tasks due to less information processing demands and higher perceived information overload in high-complexity tasks due to higher information processing demands, a lack of control will make processing appear more cumbersome in either low- or high-complexity tasks, resulting in higher perceived information overload. We therefore predict that pull systems where users have more control, will produce lower perceived information overload than push systems in either task type.

H1 (a). For low-complexity tasks, pull delivery will produce lower perceived information overload than push delivery.

H1 (b). For high-complexity tasks, pull delivery will produce lower perceived information overload than push delivery.

We anticipate that the less restrictive pull systems that lead to increased control perceptions will increase task quality in highcomplexity tasks, but there will be no difference in quality between systems in low-complexity tasks. High-complexity tasks tend to be ambiguous; thereby impairing the ability to determine what information is needed. Researchers studying personal control <sup>fi</sup>nd that individuals who perceive themselves as having more control over decision processes are better able to focus on the task's salient features in ambiguous (i.e., more complex) tasks [16]. Further, individuals with more control will use ingenuity and perseverance to complete tasks more successfully in dif<sup>fi</sup>cult environments than will individuals who perceive less control [6]. Thus using less restrictive pull systems that users will perceive affords them more control should result in better task solutions than using more restrictive push systems, in complex tasks that are more ambiguous about what information is needed. In low-complexity tasks control may have less effect on quality because there is more certainty regarding needed information. Empirical studies con<sup>fi</sup>rm that control increases quality in high-complexity tasks yet has no effect on quality in low-complexity tasks [14,65].

H2 (a). For low-complexity tasks, there will be no difference in task quality between pull delivery and push delivery.

H2 (b). For high-complexity tasks, pull delivery will produce higher task quality than push delivery.

Empirical studies show that control over one's work environment increases time needed to complete a low-complexity task while decreasing the time needed to complete a high-complexity task [65]. These results imply that users who perceive they have control may spend more time on a low-complexity task than is warranted trying to optimize or double-check the outcome. In a high-complexity task, lack of control obstructs obtaining appropriate information sources and identifying linkages among them. Thus, we anticipate users who perceive more control in high-complexity tasks will be able to complete the task in less time than those who perceive less control. These factors lead to the following hypotheses:

H3 (a). For low-complexity tasks, push delivery will produce faster task speed than pull delivery.

H3 (b). For high-complexity tasks, pull delivery will produce faster task speed than push delivery.

## 3.2. Guidance and task complexity

Information theory and related research <sup>fi</sup>nds that information structure helps individuals process information thereby decreasing perceived information overload [13,24,39,43]. However, this effect is likely to only occur in low-complexity tasks where there is more certainty about how to solve the problem. Prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance associated with directory structures (e.g., listservs and web directories) categorizes information in a manner that is consistent with users’ vocabularies [27]. This consistency will make it easier for users to <sup>fi</sup>nd directory paths that lead to the information they are searching for (that is known with greater certainty). When using participative guidance associated with queries (e.g., Google search), retrieved information based on a keyword match may not be conceptually relevant to the users’ own mental models [28]. Even when individuals choose an appropriate keyword, they are likely to receive a sizable portion of information that is irrelevant to their needs. The inconsistency between what they are searching for (that is known with greater certainty) and the search results will potentially lead to more processing and hence more perceived information overload.

H4 (a). For low-complexity tasks, prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance will produce lower perceived information overload than participative guidance.

In the case of high-complexity tasks, the structure provided by directories is less likely to help users <sup>fi</sup>nd relevant information because there is more uncertainty about how to solve the problem, and structuring can make it harder to identify and integrate essential information. Extensive exploration across unrelated resources may be required. Participative, query-based guidance provides a clearer route to explore disparate concepts [4], thereby reducing the perceived information overload.

H4 (b). For high-complexity tasks, participative guidance will produce lower perceived information overload than prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance.

Similar to its effects on perceived information overload, information structure can also increase task quality [39,43]. To predict how task complexity interacts with the level of structure, we look to prior research on Internet navigation mechanisms. This research compares user performance using more/less structured navigation systems when performing tasks that involve either <sup>fi</sup>nding information from one place in the structure (required in less-complex tasks) or relating items of information in the structure (required in more-complex tasks). General <sup>fi</sup>ndings are that for low-complexity tasks, users perform better with navigation mechanisms that are more de<sup>fi</sup>ned than with mechanisms that are less de<sup>fi</sup>ned [49,64]. This is because in low-complexity tasks a lack of structure may cause users to be distracted and disoriented by the diverse range of information provided and will adversely affect the quality of their solution [49,64]. Applied to our study, this means that prede<sup>fi</sup>ned, directory-based guidance that provides more structure will produce better quality task solutions in a low-complexity task than participative, query-based guidance that provides less structure.

H5 (a). For low-complexity tasks, prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance will produce higher task quality than participative guidance.

However, this same research <sup>fi</sup>nds that in higher-complexity tasks where various information sources may need to be integrated, more structure in navigation mechanisms may inhibit information comparison, thereby lowering performance [49,64]. Therefore, participative, query-based guidance may be preferred when analyzing and synthesizing concepts in complex tasks because keywords can be used in query-based guidance to tie disparate concepts together [4]. Also, using keyword searches makes it is easier to move from one topic area to the next, thereby broadening one's perspective on more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult problems [55]. This capability is harder for users to implement in prede<sup>fi</sup>ned, directory-based structures thereby hindering performance.

H5 (b). For high-complexity tasks, participative guidance will produce higher task quality than prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance.

Finally, we predict that there will be costs associated with participative guidance that extend the time taken to navigate and retrieve information. However, this effect will only be seen in the low-complexity task. For example, with participative guidance users must formulate keywords before they can navigate through the search results. While this may be relatively easy in a low-complexity task, it can still take more time than simply navigating through a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned directory structure. As task complexity increases, determining keywords will become increasingly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and take more time as the number of potential paths and solutions increase, as well as the uncertainty between the paths and the solutions. We anticipate these costs of participative guidance will counterbalance the costs of navigating through a prede-<sup>fi</sup>ned directory structure as task complexity increases. Therefore, while we make a prediction about the effects of guidance on task speed for low-complexity tasks, we do not make a prediction for guidance/speed effects in high-complexity tasks.

H6. For low-complexity tasks, prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance will produce faster task speed than participative guidance.

## 4. Research method

A laboratory experiment was conducted in which two treatment sets were analyzed, each crossing low-complexity and highcomplexity KA tasks as a within-subjects factor with two levels of a knowledge retrieval tool characteristic as a between-subjects factor. The <sup>fi</sup>rst treatment set assessed differences between push vs. pull restrictiveness (listservs vs. web directories and Google search). The second treatment set assessed differences between prede<sup>fi</sup>ned and participative guidance implemented by the knowledge retrieval tool (Google search vs. listservs and web directories).

## 4.1. Independent variables: task complexity

Each participant performed two tasks, each at two complexity levels. We developed tasks by collecting current events in information systems areas including customer relationship management, data mining, electronic payment systems, cloud computing, knowledge management systems, open source software, peer-to-peer networks, web services, and wireless computing. From these articles we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed tasks with varying complexity.

We operationalized complexity based on task complexity dimensions of multiple paths, multiple outcomes, and uncertain linkages. The multiple paths dimension was operationalized as multiple information sources that could be used to arrive at a solution. The low-complexity task required one or two information sources for the solution. The high-complexity task required three or more information sources. The multiple unrelated outcomes dimension was operationalized in the high-complexity task as three solutions, each pertaining to a different technology. The low-complexity task solution involved a single technology. The high-complexity task addressed the uncertain linkages dimension by requiring participants to assess which of several technologies had standards. The low-complexity task focused on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology, thereby providing more certainty regarding what information sources might provide the solution. The resulting task treatments represent those an information systems professional might be asked to perform (see Table 1).

## 4.2. Independent variables: knowledge retrieval tool

We operationalized the listserv treatment (push delivery/prede-<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance) using the Microsoft Outlook e-mail application. Email is commonly used to disseminate information to organizational or inter-organizational communities of practice or groups whose members share common interests [40,44]. To set up the inbox structure, we created email accounts for each participant to use during the experimental session. We sent the same 12 emails to each account. During the experiment, participants saw these 12 emails in their inbox, each with a different technology topic area in the subject line. By opening an email, the participant would see a consecutive listing of 8 information sources related to the particular topic area (e.g., 8 customer relationship articles). Thus, each email represented a different content area listserv that the user had previously subscribed. Because we used the content areas in the message subject lines, the inboxes served as directories structured by content area.

We operationalized the web directory treatment (pull delivery/ prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance) as a web page containing a directory of the 12 content areas. Participants used Internet Explorer to click on a topic area and thereby navigate to another web page with a listing of the 8 information sources related to that content area. From this they could navigate down one more level to another page containing the actual information source.

The listserv and web directory treatments contained identical information and information structuring, except for the application interface used to access the content (email application versus web browser). The application interfaces were used to invoke different control perceptions and thus restrictiveness. An email application is anticipated to invoke feelings of less control/more restrictiveness over the information seeking process because participants are accustomed to having little in<sup>fl</sup>uence over the information that is being delivered to them. A web browser application is anticipated to invoke feelings of more control/less restrictiveness as participants typically use browsers to actively retrieve needed information. A manipulation check was conducted to validate this distinction between the listserv and web directory treatments, and results from the check are reported later in the paper.

We operationalized the Google search treatment (pull delivery/ participative guidance) using the standard Google web interface, which supports searching throughout the web. Although this provides a larger knowledge domain than the other treatments, it is common for knowledge workers to utilize the World Web Wide during the KA process [44], and it would be unusual to limit use of Google search to only cover a speci<sup>fi</sup>c information repository. To ensure that the larger knowledge domain did not bias the experiment, we pretested possible keyword searches for the tasks to ensure the participants in the query treatment could <sup>fi</sup>nd similarly useful information in all treatment conditions.

## 4.3. Dependent variables

The dependent variables were perceived information overload, task quality, and task speed. We measured perceived information overload after participants completed each task using pre-existing scales modi<sup>fi</sup>ed slightly to coincide with the tasks [36,48] (see Table 2). The computer unobtrusively measured task speed in seconds beginning when task instructions were presented to the participant and ending when the participant submitted the task as completed. Task quality was assessed using a subjective coding template. Before the experimental sessions, the researchers determined the ideal solution or type of solution for each task and decided which components of each solution or solution type were needed to achieve a certain score. Scores ranged from 1 (terrible answer) to 5 (excellent answer). To receive a high score of 5, for example, a solution for the high-complexity task would need to include: (1) a de<sup>fi</sup>nition with components referring to a generally-accepted format or guideline that made interactions among system easier, and (2) identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of three technologies with at least one standard for each. Following administration of the experiment, two individuals coded the solutions based on this predetermined scoring method (one coder was one of the researchers and the other coder was a research assistant with subject area knowledge). Both coders were blind to treatments to which the participants were assigned. For any solution where the <sup>fi</sup>nal scores were different, a third party (the other researcher) reviewed the solution to resolve the difference. Overall coding consistency (interrater reliability) was approximately 90%.

## 4.4. Participants

Participants were 181 undergraduate business majors at a large Midwestern University. The participants were enrolled in an undergraduate Information Technology core course and received extra credit for participating in the experiment or for performing an equivalent alternative exercise. The experimental treatments utilize email and web technologies in which IT students have well-developed skills. Prior to beginning the experiment, the participants were asked to rate their knowledge of the task topic areas on a scale of (1) know nothing to (5) I am an expert. There was a 4.56 mean for the low-complexity task and a 3.34 mean response for the high-complexity task, indicating that participants felt themselves to be relatively knowledgeable in both domain areas. These observations suggest the participant sample constitutes an appropriate population for investigating the tasks and technologies used in this research. Participants were 33% female. Expertise and gender did not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly differ by treatment.

## 4.5. Procedure

Participants were randomly assigned to one of the three knowledge retrieval tool treatments upon arriving to the experimental sessions. Each session was held in a computer lab with room for up to 30 participants. All participants were given a packet containing a consent form and instructions. The instructions described a work setting in which the participants’ manager had asked them to seek information and perform tasks relating to speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology issues. Participants were also instructed they would receive extra credit based on their task quality and time. They were given the entire class period (50 minutes) to complete the two tasks. Average completion time for the two tasks was 17.8 minutes, and no participant took longer than 37 minutes to complete both tasks. The tasks were presented in two orders across each treatment to mitigate any fatigue and order effects. Approximately half the participants completed the low-complexity task <sup>fi</sup>rst and the other half completed the high-complexity task <sup>fi</sup>rst. A preliminary analysis including task order as an additional independent variable showed no main or interaction order effects. Therefore the order variable was omitted from the analysis. Participants could either type or cut-and-paste their answers in the text boxes provided for their response. After each task, participants rated perceived information overload, and at the end of the experiment they rated perceived behavioral control using measures shown in Table 2.

## 5.1. Manipulation check

We collected participant ratings of perceived behavioral control based on scales by [3,23], to understand if the restrictiveness manipulation induced differing control perceptions (see Table 3). A Scheffé multiple comparisons test following one-way ANOVA indicates the listserv treatment was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from both the web directory treatment (p=.001) and the Google search treatment (p= .001). No difference was found between the web directory and Google search treatments. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest restrictiveness was appropriately manipulated between more restrictive push delivery and less restrictive pull delivery treatments.

## 6. Discussion

This research highlights that system restrictiveness and decisional guidance are important for knowledge retrieval tool design and KA success. Results support the majority of our hypotheses, indicating that tool choice (website directory, Google search, or listserv) can impact perceived information overload, task quality, and task speed in both low-complexity and high-complexity tasks. In this section we present detailed research and practical implications.

## 6.1. Research implications

The <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings is showing how the restrictiveness of retrieval mechanisms can affect perceived information overload, task quality, and task speed. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the control afforded by pull systems is dependent on task and outcome variable. For example, while the pull system is generally bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in our high-complexity task, contrary to our predictions it did not result in a faster speed. This could indicate that perceived control does not alleviate the effort required in certain tasks. Future research could investigate if this <sup>fi</sup>nding generalizes to other tasks. In low complexity tasks, the control afforded by pull systems can be detrimental in some aspects. This could be because control distracts individuals in low complexity tasks, perhaps resulting in them over-analyzing and taking more time than might be needed to solve problems.

Comparing our <sup>fi</sup>ndings about restrictiveness and control with other research shows some inconsistencies where our research hypotheses are not supported. For example, contrary to our <sup>fi</sup>ndings some researchers do <sup>fi</sup>nd that control improves speed in highcomplexity tasks [65], and some do not <sup>fi</sup>nd detrimental effects of control on quality in low-complexity tasks [14,65]. These differences may be due to different contexts or task types, further suggesting that control effects are conditional. Future research should investigate these differences. There may also be future research opportunities to tease out these mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings by using three levels of task complexity. Future research should also investigate other factors that have been found to interact with control to affect performance including feedback [14].

The second contribution of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings is showing differences in the effectiveness of decisional guidance modes for different tasks. Prior research examining prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance does not include a task complexity moderator and therefore does not show these differences. For example, Parikh et al. [52] <sup>fi</sup>nd that prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance is not more effective than other guidance modes in task performance, which is similar to our <sup>fi</sup>nding in the high-complexity task, but not in the low-complexity task. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the importance of incorporating a measure of task complexity when studying the effects of guidance on task performance.

In high-complexity tasks, participative guidance may have been more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial because users could start the search over with new keywords. This may re<sup>fl</sup>ect a learning process that users undergo in more complex tasks, being able to browse through initially retrieved materials to gain a better understanding of the task requirements before retrieving additional materials. Users may not have been able to learn as well using prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance because of the structure imposed on them. While some research has examined learning as an outcome affected by guidance [52], future research should explore how knowledge retrieval restrictiveness and guidance affect learning. These results might also be explained by the limited ability of human editors to categorize a large content area in a meaningful way. Researchers are beginning to address this. For example, collaborative knowledge creation efforts aided by new collaborative technologies can overcome some of the problems associated with categorizing bodies of knowledge [25].

While we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd support for our hypothesis about guidance and perceived information overload in low complexity tasks, the means were in the correct direction (prede<sup>fi</sup>ned has lower mean perceived information overload than participative). This lack of result suggests to researchers that structure effects are not always straightforward and could be dependent on factors speci<sup>fi</sup>c to a study including task type and form of structure.

Another important contribution of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings relates to effortaccuracy trade-offs. Humans regularly trade-off quality to increase speed (i.e., reduce time), as a mechanism for reducing effort associated with decision making [53]. However, this trade-off is not universal, and decision strategies have been shown in some cases to produce a negative correlation between quality and time (i.e., lower quality-more time or higher quality-less time) [21]. Our study shows an effortaccuracy trade-off, but only for the high-complexity task where there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive correlation between task quality and speed (r=.20). Thus, subjects producing lower quality solutions spent less time and vice versa. In the low-complexity task, the results are just the opposite. We <sup>fi</sup>nd a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative correlation between task quality and speed $( \Gamma = - . 2 7 )$ ), indicating that subjects who took longer produced lower quality solutions, and vice versa. Research should investigate further the KA strategies using knowledge retrieval tools.

## 6.2. Practical implications

Our study also has several practical implications for organizations. First, we show that there is not a “one-size-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all” approach to knowledge delivery. Instead, task complexity, process, and outcomes should be considered when making knowledge retrieval tool design choices. For example, pull systems may be the most useful tool for high-complexity tasks because they produce the least perceived information overload and highest task quality, and are similar to push systems in task speed. However, for low-complexity tasks, pull systems are only advantageous for lowering perceived information overload, as they produce lower task quality and task speed than push systems. In this situation companies may have to trade-off perceived information overload to achieve better outcomes.

Our results also suggest that while companies can use both guidance modes to achieve KA bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, they should implement practice guidelines for when to use each retrieval tool, perhaps by categorizing tasks. One such guideline might be that high-complexity tasks might be made less complex by <sup>fi</sup>rst using a participative search tool like Google to eliminate some of the uncertainty in sources, paths, and linkages. This may even enhance employee learning.

Our results also support prior <sup>fi</sup>ndings that further improvements in decision quality for high complexity tasks could be made by training users how to write more targeted queries [55]. Training could include additional guidance. Silver [61] notes that “a given system may have many instances of decisional guidance—that is, many different features that enlighten, sway, or direct its users as they choose among and use its functional capabilities” (p. 105). Thus, a knowledge retrieval tool that provides both participative guidance to <sup>fi</sup>nd information and dynamic guidance to write better queries could produce higher quality outcomes for complex tasks.

## 6.3. Limitations

There are several limitations to this research. One limitation is that the high-complexity task while being more complex than the lowcomplexity task was relatively simple and did not involve signi<sup>fi</sup>cant data analysis, etc. While the high-complexity task did involve integrating information across disparate sources and content areas, our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings may only generalize to low computation tasks. Future research should investigate additional tasks.

Also our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are limited because the databases for the browsing tools were different from the database for the search tool (Google) in size and content. This may have confounded our results. However, to prevent detrimental affects to task quality, we ensured that similar information to solve the tasks could be found in both databases. We also ran a post-hoc analysis that shows Google was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from the web directory system in task quality or perceived information overload as would be expected because the Google database has more and different content (we excluded the listserv system from this analysis to avoid confounding push/ pull affects). Also, while Google did signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly differ from the web directory system in task speed, which would be expected due to its size, this was attributable to the difference in speed in the lowcomplexity task only. Because expectations regarding these differences are not consistently supported, it is unlikely that this affected our results.

Another limitation is that we used task speed as a dependent variable to measure performance. Task speed can be problematic because subjects may double-check their answers or give-up, thereby skewing the data. These behaviors may have been possible in the highcomplexity task where subjects traded effort for accuracy. However, such an effect has been found in other studies (e.g., [65]). Also, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the skewness statistics for speed and quality in both tasks are below the acceptable maximum level [31], and the mean task speeds seem reasonable. Further, to prevent subjects from taking excessive time in completing the tasks they were told they would be graded based on both accuracy and speed, and all subjects completed the tasks within the class time allotted.

Finally, we examined what might constitute a single community of practice. All subjects were taking an information technology class and information technology was the topic of the tasks. Different results may occur if users are from different communities of practice because communities of practice have their own unique and speci<sup>fi</sup>c vocabularies that facilitate communication within the community and may impede communication between them [27]. Therefore, prede<sup>fi</sup>ned guidance (listservs and web directories) may be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to implement and cause signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more perceived information overload than participative guidance (Google search).
