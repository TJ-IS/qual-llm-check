# LYING FOR VIEWERS: COMMINGLED PARTISAN FALSEHOODSINCREASE VIEWING AND SHARING OF NEWS MEDIA<sup>1</sup>

Seyoung Seol Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry, U.K. {seyoung.seol@wbs.ac.uk}

Jorge Mejia and Alan R. Dennis Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, IN, U.S.A. {jmmejia@iu.edu} {ardennis@indiana.edu}

Is there an economic incentive for celebrities and well-known media firms to commingle falsehoods into news stories? We conducted five experiments, plus a field validation using secondary data. When presented by celebrities and well-known media firms, a commingled partisan falsehood in an otherwise true news story significantly increased viewing and sharing intentions among politically aligned viewers. The effect was weaker but significant when we replaced the celebrity with an unknown speaker and disappeared when both the celebrity and the well-known firm were replaced by unknowns. This effect was explained by confirmation bias and the viewer’s belief that the falsehood was true. In contrast, a false news story focusing on the same falsehood increased viewing and sharing intentions only when presented by unknowns, with viewers’ belief playing a limited role. The field study found a significantly positive relationship between a commingled partisan falsehood in videos of well-known media firms and actual viewership. We conclude that commingled partisan falsehoods provide a significant viewership increase for celebrities and well-known media firms, creating an economic incentive for lying and posing complex challenges in the fake news era. We discuss the challenges and opportunities in this area for policymakers and media firms.

Keywords: Fake news, fact-checking, reputation, factitious informational blend, news video

## Introduction

Co-mingling of truth and fiction at the heart of most fake news is among the most problematic and least understood characteristics of the phenomenon. (George et al., 2021, p. 1070)

Truthfulness has long been important in news content because audience trust is driven by accurate and fair journalistic practices (Gallup & Knight Foundation, 2020). Media firms advertise their reputation for trustworthiness, with CNN claiming to be “the most trusted name in the news,” and Fox News claiming to be “fair and balanced.” Truthful information has been recognized as an essential element in attracting audiences (Newman et al., 2017). Yet over the last five years, the percentage of Americans with some trust in national news organizations has dropped from 76% to 58% (Gottfried & Liedke, 2021).

One recent trend that may have fueled this drop in trust is the increase in a more subjective, conversational, and argumentative news style, with more opinion segments such as partisan talks, debates, and interviews (Kavanagh et al., 2019) that may sacrifice truthfulness (Swift, 2017). For example, PolitiFact, an independent fact-checker, reported that a significant proportion of the statements by well-known media firms that they checked were false: 61% of Fox News and 44% of MSNBC (Sharockman, 2015). Over a quarter of the most viewed COVID-19 videos on YouTube found to contain misleading statements were from well-known media firms (Li et al., 2020). The top-performing stories on social media are usually from well-known media firms (Hutchinson, 2021), with Fox News and CNN having over a billion monthly views each (Flood, 2021). Media firms’ news videos via websites and television are also dominant in people’s daily news consumption (Barthel et al., 2020; Newman et al., 2020). Thus, commingled false news in mainstream news stories poses a significant concern (George et al., 2021).

Research on fake news has primarily focused on false stories from unknown sources (George et al., 2021) but has been relatively silent on falsehoods commingled with otherwise true news stories by well-known media firms. Such commingled falsehoods fundamentally differ from false stories—rather than the whole story being false, one or two false statements are mixed into otherwise true news stories (Li et al., 2020; Sharockman, 2015). Rojecki and Meraz (2016) note that a true story with a commingled falsehood (which they call a factitious informational blend) aims to be more appealing to viewers or to pass off a false claim as true. This includes situations where the speaker knows the statement is false (i.e., disinformation) or the speaker is misinformed and believes it to be true (i.e., misinformation). Factitious blends of falsehoods in an otherwise true news story have the potential to appeal to increasingly partisan news audiences (Silverman, 2016), making the story more likely to go viral (Vosoughi et al., 2018) and increasing viewership (and revenues) in the highly competitive news market (Anand, 2017; Walker & Forman-Katz, 2021).

This paper examines if a commingled attractive partisan falsehood in an otherwise true news story increases the viewership of political news videos (i.e., more viewing and sharing). We conducted five online experiments plus a field validation study. We found that a commingled partisan falsehood aligned with the political opinions of viewers increased viewing and sharing intentions when it was backed by the reputation of a well-known media firm with or without a celebrity speaker, but had no effect when it came from an unknown speaker and an unknown media firm. The field study found a significantly positive relationship between a commingled partisan falsehood in videos of wellknown media firms and actual viewership. Confirmation bias and the viewer’s belief in the truth of the falsehood explained the effects, suggesting that commingled partisan falsehoods have effects when they are believed, which the reputations of the speaker and media firm can help facilitate. The pattern is reversed for a false news story about the same falsehood: a false news story from an unknown speaker and an unknown media firm increased viewing and sharing intentions among politically aligned viewers but had no effect when it came from a well-known media firm. Confirmation bias explained the observed effect, but the viewer’s belief played no significant role.

Our work makes three important contributions. First, for “what,” or the type of fake news, our work focuses on commingled falsehoods in otherwise true news stories, whereas existing research focuses on false news stories (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019; Moravec et al., 2020; Pennycook et al., 2021; Turel & Osatuyi, 2021; Vosoughi et al., 2018). We found that commingled falsehoods increase viewing and sharing intentions for well-known sources with an effect size similar to that of false stories from unknown sources. Our field study shows this same pattern using actual viewership data. Our findings highlight the salience of the phenomenon.

Second, for “who,” or the source of fake news, our work studies falsehoods from well-known sources (e.g., Fox News and MSNBC) and the separate effects from the reputation of a celebrity speaker and a media firm, whereas existing research focuses on fake news from unknown sources (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019; Moravec et al., 2020; Pennycook et al., 2021; Turel & Osatuyi, 2021; Vosoughi et al., 2018). We found that the reputation effect of the celebrity speaker and the media firm is vital for commingled falsehoods but not for false stories, where we only identified effects with unknown sources. Our work calls attention to the economic incentive for well-known sources to commingle falsehoods as a critical element of the fake news phenomenon.

Third, for “why,” or the mechanisms of commingled falsehoods driving more viewing and sharing, our work shows that belief is important for commingled falsehoods to have effects; our research also contributes to the confirmation bias effect identified by prior research on false news stories (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019). Viewers’ belief in the truth of politically aligned commingled falsehoods plays a critical role in driving their effect on viewing and sharing, which is why the reputation of the speaker and the firm is important. In contrast, viewers’ belief played no significant role in false news stories. We discuss managerial and policy implications in the final section of this paper.

## Prior Theory and Research

The seminal definition of fake news (“news articles that are intentionally and verifiably false and could mislead readers,” Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017, p. 213) has been widely adopted in prior research (e.g., Horner et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2020; Ng et al., 2021; Turel & Osatuyi, 2021). The definition has four components. First, fake news is a news story (often political), not a product review or a personal story. Second, fake news is verifiably false—there is no ambiguity. Stories that cannot be verified lie outside this definition, as do “slanted” or biased stories—bias and hyperbole are not fake news (Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017; Holan, 2020). Third, fake news is created intentionally—not by mistake—and it says what the author intends (e.g., the author does not retract or correct it). This includes situations in which the author knows the information is false and is lying (i.e., disinformation) as well as those in which the author is misinformed and believes the information to be true (i.e., misinformation). Finally, fake news is likely to mislead readers into thinking that the false content is true (e.g., satire is not fake news).

Rojecki and Meraz (2016) introduced the concept of the factitious informational blend: “Facts mingle with half-truths and untruths to create factitious informational blends” (p. 25). A factitious informational blend is an otherwise true story that includes a commingled false, slanted, or unverifiable statement with the goal of passing off the statement as true or increasing the story’s appeal (Rojecki & Meraz, 2016). However, as our focus is on fake news, we do not consider commingled slanted statements or commingled unverifiable statements in this paper.

Building on the seminal definition of fake news by Allcott and Gentzkow (2017), we define a commingled falsehood as a statement in a news story that is verifiably false, spoken as intended (no errors), and capable of mislead its audience, where the rest of the content in the story is putatively true and normal journalism. Adding a falsehood to an otherwise true story does not make the story “half-true.” The falsehood is still false, and the story is still true. A “half-true” story contains no falsehoods but is biased or slanted because it attempts to mislead the audience by omitting important facts or taking facts out of context (Holan, 2020; Jacobson, 2022). Other mixes of truth and falsehood are possible, but they are qualitatively different and not our focus in this paper.

Commingling a falsehood into a story, especially an attractive partisan political statement, can increase the attractiveness of the story (DePaulo et al., 2004; Janezic & Gallego, 2020; Rojecki & Meraz, 2016) or can persuade the audience that the falsehood is true (Rojecki & Meraz, 2016). Different commingled falsehoods may have stronger or weaker effects depending on the nature of the falsehood, such as its noticeability, potential impact, or specific content. Our study focuses on potentially impactful commingled falsehoods in political news.

## Current Research on Fake News

Previous research has found that false news from unknown sources is prevalent, accounting for nearly 6% of all news consumption during the 2016 U.S. presidential election cycle (Grinberg et al., 2019). Online rumors spread faster and farther than true stories on social media (Vosoughi et al., 2018). False news stories from hyperpartisan blogs and hoax websites generated more user engagement than legitimate stories in the final months of the 2016 U.S. election (Silverman, 2016). Most past research has focused on false stories from unknown sources, typically web content designed to look like news stories (George et al., 2021). Falsehoods commingled in true news stories from well-known media firms have received little attention (George et al., 2021), despite the significant consumption of such stories on social media (Flood, 2021; Hutchinson, 2021).

Research on countermeasures to fake news has also focused on false stories from unknown sources, proposing flagging suspicious sources (Moravec et al., 2019; Ng et al., 2021), rating news sources (Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019), source identity verification (Wang et al., 2021), improving user information literacy (Pennycook & Rand, 2019), encouraging user reporting (Gimpel et al., 2021; Turel & Osatuyi, 2021), and automated detection (García Lozano et al., 2020; Zhang & Ghorbani, 2020). However, these measures may be challenged by commingled falsehoods in otherwise true news stories from well-known media firms. Well-known sources have valid identities, are highly rated, and are not flagged as fake news sources. In cases where a commingled falsehood represents only a small portion of a story, it is harder for users or algorithms to detect it. It is also hard to classify true news stories that contain a single commingled falsehood as “fake” without damaging the true story’s credibility and the rating organization’s credibility.

## Truthfulness and Reputation of Media Firms

Reputation is based on three dimensions: the objective, the social, and the subjective (Eisenegger & Imhof, 2008). In the objective world, agents are judged by their objective performance. In the context of news, this means reporting events truthfully and accurately. In the social world, reputation is measured based on whether an agent’s action is legitimate regarding social values such as ethics, legitimacy, and integrity. For news reporting, this means balanced and fair reporting. In the subjective world, the primary concern is emotional impact, or the extent to which an actor’s behavior matches the emotional desires of observers. For news reporting, this means appealing reporting that matches the audience’s tastes and political beliefs.

Achieving a balance between the objective and the social aspects of reputation has been the dominant goal of media firms for decades, with the subjective aspect taking a backseat. Most Americans (88%) believe that it is important for media to provide accurate and fair news (Gallup & Knight Foundation, 2020), and the loss of trust in truthful reporting causes people to avoid news media (Newman et al., 2017). However, increasing evidence shows that this balance may be changing: media firms may be giving more weight to the subjective dimension of reputation by providing more opinion and partisan reporting (Kavanagh et al., 2019) to the extent that more falsehoods are included in their news videos (Li et al., 2020; Sharockman, 2015).

## Viewership as a Revenue Driver

This shift toward more partisan reporting and the observation of falsehoods in news stories raises the question of whether media firms benefit from including partisan falsehoods in stories they report. The main economic driver of media firms is the viewership of their news videos, which provides an important basis for ad revenue (Anand, 2017; Walker & Forman-Katz, 2021)—ad impressions (the number of times an ad is displayed on a consumer’s screen) and ad views (ad impressions counted after a specific time, usually 10 seconds) are determined by viewership (Boehme et al., 2019; Goetzen, 2021; Graham, 2021b). Viewership is a function of the number of viewers and their viewing time (Polishchuk, 2021; Walker & Forman-Katz, 2021) and increases if more people watch a news video and/or if people watch it for a longer time. The competition around viewership is increasing; the news market is being flooded with new online competitors, and it is getting harder for traditional news to get through information clutter (Anand, 2017). Thus, media firms have begun tracking minute-by-minute viewing data to understand what content influences viewership and using that knowledge to design content to increase viewership (Coy, 2022).

## Commingled Falsehoods

A false claim can be made more persuasive if it is commingled with true information. Likewise, an otherwise true story can be made more appealing if an attractive falsehood is added to it (DePaulo et al., 2004; Janezic & Gallego, 2020; Rojecki & Meraz, 2016). With today’s increasingly partisan and digitally connected news environment (Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019; Silverman, 2016; Vosoughi et al., 2018), inserting an attractive partisan falsehood into a true story has the potential to increase the appeal and virality of the story without causing the story to be labeled as fake news, minimizing the risk of reputational damage.

Commingling a partisan falsehood that is aligned with viewers’ political opinions with true information helps the falsehood gain plausibility (Rojecki & Meraz, 2016). Falsehoods often attract greater attention and stronger emotional reactions because they are intentionally designed to be novel, surprising, eye-catching, or emotionally charged (Itti & Baldi, 2009; Meel & Vishwakarma, 2020; Vosoughi et al., 2018). Greater attention and stronger emotional reactions can promote higher engagement with news content (Godlewski & Perse, 2010) and lead to more sharing of news content on social media (Shestyuk et al., 2019). Higher engagement can better retain current viewers (i.e., increase viewing time), and more sharing can draw more new viewers to the story (i.e., increase the number of viewers), with both elements increasing viewership.

Factitious informational blends are used by “opinion entrepreneurs” and other elites because they can use their established reputations to increase the belief that a falsehood is true (Rojecki & Meraz, 2016). When information providers with a reputation for truth-telling choose to lie, audiences are often not cautious enough to detect the lie (Ettinger & Jehiel, 2021). Without an established reputation, it becomes more challenging for a news provider to gain acceptance of a falsehood since viewers will be more cautious.

For news stories, reputation can come from the speaker or the media firm (Kalogeropoulos et al., 2019; Loeb, 2015; Turner, 2014). The reputation of well-known media firms increases the credibility of their news content (Zloteanu et al., 2018), causing viewers to be less motivated to verify the content (Rojecki & Meraz, 2016). Viewers often develop parasocial relationships with celebrities, which creates a sense of trust and causes them to perceive them to be credible sources of information (Farivar et al., 2021; Reinikainen et al., 2020; Tukachinsky et al., 2020). Thus, the reputation of the person and/or firm presenting a commingled falsehood is central to viewers’ belief in the truth of the falsehood (Rojecki & Meraz, 2016).

It is still an open question whether a brief commingled falsehood in an otherwise true news story can affect viewership and whether the reputation of the speaker or media firm is important to this relationship. A commingled falsehood with reputational support may backfire: Since people care about accuracy and have some ability to detect falsehoods (Pennycook et al., 2021), a commingled falsehood may violate their quality expectations (Gallup & Knight Foundation, 2020; Newman et al., 2017) and thus reduce viewership. It is also an open question as to how much reputational support is needed for a commingled falsehood to be effective or backfire. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1: A commingled politically aligned falsehood increases news video viewership (i.e., more viewing and sharing) when it is presented by (a) a celebrity speaker and a well-known media firm (both reputations); (b) an unknown speaker and a wellknown media firm (firm reputation only); or (c) an unknown speaker and an unknown media firm (no reputation), with the impact being proportional to the level of reputational support.

## Potential Persuasion Mechanisms of Commingled Falsehoods

Past research has suggested that fake news draws attention via three theoretical mechanisms: confirmation bias (Moravec et al., 2019), the emotion of enjoyment, and the emotion of distress (Vosoughi et al., 2018). Thus, one or more of those potential mechanisms may be at play in commingled falsehoods. Confirmation bias is a fundamental property of our cognition (Kahneman, 2011) that automatically pushes us to focus on information aligned with our beliefs and ignore information that challenges them (Nickerson, 1998). Confirmation bias in political news drives users to ignore stories that challenge their beliefs (Moravec et al., 2019) and seek, view, and share stories that support them even when they are not sure the stories are true (Cinelli et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019). Thus, confirmation bias occurs regardless of news quality, and false news that supports audiences’ beliefs can create greater partisan appeal than true news. Whether a single commingled falsehood in a true story can provoke the same confirmation bias response as a false story is an open empirical question. Thus, we theorize that confirmation bias from politically aligned, appealing information is a theoretical mechanism by which a commingled partisan falsehood increases the viewing and sharing of news videos.

Emotions are less salient in true news stories (Vraga et al., 2012) since they compete with argument quality (Osatuyi & Hughes, 2018). However, false news often creates emotional reactions and drives greater viewer engagement than true news (Vosoughi et al., 2018). Thus, emotions may play a role in commingled falsehoods, especially with increasingly partisan news audiences (Silverman, 2016). Whether a commingled falsehood in a true news story can induce emotional reactions is an open empirical question. False news is more likely to be novel than true news (Vosoughi et al., 2018), and research shows that novelty increases enjoyment (Baek & Kim, 2016). Greater enjoyment often leads to more viewing and sharing of news videos (Godlewski & Perse, 2010; Shestyuk et al., 2019). Thus, we theorize that increased enjoyment is a theoretical mechanism by which a commingled falsehood increases viewing and sharing.

Conversely, false news stories are often designed to evoke disgust at others’ behaviors (Hasell & Weeks, 2016; Vosoughi et al., 2018). Stories of political opponents misbehaving, especially those in power, are common topics in false news stories (e.g., QAnon). These stories are not likely to invoke enjoyment, as they show leaders being immoral, but rather are intended to show the moral superiority of the in-group (e.g., QAnon supporters) over the out-group. Thus, distress is a potential motivator for viewing and sharing news videos because there is a strong emotional lure to sharing novel distressing content with in-group members (Martel et al., 2020; Scheufele & Krause, 2019). Thus, we theorize that unpleasant and distressing emotions are a theoretical mechanism by which a commingled falsehood can increase viewing and sharing. In summary, we hypothesize three potential theoretical mechanisms:

H2: (a) Confirmation bias, (b) enjoyment, and (c) distress mediate the positive effect of a commingled, politically aligned falsehood on news video viewership.

We conducted five controlled online experiments and one field validation study. Study 1 used two news video clips with celebrities and well-known media firms (Fox News and MSNBC), randomly including or omitting a falsehood that was independently fact-checked. Study 2 used the same basic design, but we replaced the celebrity speaker with an unknown speaker to understand the effect of the speaker’s reputation. Study 3 used the same basic design, but we further removed the well-known firm logos so that the videos had an unknown speaker and an unknown media firm (i.e., a source with no reputation). Studies 4 and 5 replicated Studies 2 and 3 with a false news story whose central message focused on the same falsehood rather than commingling with an otherwise true story. The field study assessed the effects of a commingled falsehood on actual news video viewership using four years of television viewership data.

## Study 1: Commingled Falsehoods with Celebrity Speakers and Well-Known Media Firms

## Method

Participants: We used Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) to recruit U.S. adult participants with approval ratings of at least 95% and at least 100 completed HITs (human intelligence tasks), following the guidelines to ensure high-quality data (Peer et al., 2014; Steelman et al., 2014). We removed participants who took more than 25 minutes to respond (i.e., more than three times the average completion time of 7.4 minutes); participants with unusually long response times often produce low-quality data because they perform other tasks simultaneously (Ahler et al., 2021; Read et al., 2022). There were 288 participants in the final sample: 58% were male, 70% were between the ages of 25-44, and 26% were between the ages of 45-64. About 73% were Caucasian, 5% were Hispanic or Latino, 4% were Black or African American, and 16% were Asian. About 48% had a college degree, and 14% had a graduate degree. About 59% self-identified as Democrats, and 41% self-identified as Republicans. We performed a power analysis using G\*Power to detect a small effect size (f = 0.10) (Faul et al., 2007) and confirmed that the power of our experimental designs was adequate. Study 1 had a power of 0.82, Study 2 of 0.94, Study 3 of 0.99, Study 4 of 0.89, and Study 5 of 0.88.

Task: Participants watched two news videos (each about 45 seconds) in random order and answered questions about them. One was from Hannity on Fox News discussing the U.S. travel ban against Muslim-majority countries. The other was from Morning Joe on MSNBC discussing Russian interference during the 2016 U.S. presidential election. We selected these videos from the videos in our field validation study that contained a falsehood fact-checked by an independent factchecker (PolitiFact). We chose these two videos because the issues discussed (i.e., immigration and election interference) were still ongoing at the time of our experiment. Figure 1 shows snapshots of the two videos. The scripts are provided in Appendix A.

Treatments: We used a 2×2 mixed factorial design. The first treatment was the presence or absence of a falsehood at the end of each video clip (randomly assigned<sup>2</sup>), which is a between-subjects factor. Participants either saw an entire video containing a falsehood or a 6-second shorter version that ended immediately before the falsehood. Prior research has often used video clips of slightly different lengths that add or remove the treatment (e.g., Kupor & Tormala, 2015; Mandel & Nowlis, 2008; Nelson et al., 2009). The falsehood in the Hannity video was: “The 9th Circuit, the most liberal court of appeals, the most overturned court in the country.” The falsehood in the Morning Joe video was: “I think that you can see that visits to Moscow were made during the campaign by Manafort and others.” PolitiFact determined that both commingled falsehoods were potentially impactful<sup>3</sup> and therefore fact-checked them (Holan, 2020). The two speakers who stated the commingled falsehoods, Sean Hannity and Maxine Waters, were the celebrities (Andreeva & Johnson, 2019; TIME, 2018), and Fox News and MSNBC were the well-known media outlets (Jurkowitz et al., 2020). We conducted a pilot study and confirmed that the two speakers were perceived as celebrities and that the two media firms were perceived as well-known (Appendix B).

The second treatment was political alignment between a news video and the participant, which is a within-subjects factor. All participants watched both a video aligned with their political opinions and a video that was not (in random order). Our participants self-reported the political party they voted for the last time (Republican or Democrat), and the two news videos were either right-leaning (Hannity on Fox News) or left-leaning (Morning Joe on MSNBC).

Measures: The dependent variable was viewing and sharing intentions, measured using four items, each on a 7-point scale (1 = not at all, 7 = extremely): continue viewing, or the participant’s likelihood of watching the remainder of the video; next episode viewing, or the participant’s likelihood of watching the next episode of the video; share, or the participant’s likelihood of sharing the video content or their thoughts on it with friends; and recommend, or the participant’s likelihood of recommending the video to friends. The first two measures focused on viewers’ further viewing intention (i.e., an increase in the viewing time of a video). The latter two measures focused on viewers’ sharing intention, which can draw more new viewers (i.e., an increase in the number of viewers of a video). The Cronbach’s alpha was adequate (?? = 0.94).

For the three potential persuasion mechanisms, first, confirmation bias was measured using items from prior research (Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019). We asked participants about their perceived importance of the issues before they watched the two videos using a 7-point scale (1 = not at all, 7 = extremely). After watching each video, we asked participants about their reaction to the speaker reporting the falsehood using a 7-point scale (-3 = strongly disagree, +3 = strongly agree). We multiplied the two items to make a scale from -21 to +21; this measured the participant’s position on what the speaker said, which was adjusted by their importance perception about the discussed matter. Second, enjoyment was measured with three items (7-point scale) from Auter and Davis (1991); their reliability was adequate (?? = 0.93). Last, distress was measured with three items (7-point scale) from Batson et al. (1987); their reliability was adequate (?? = 0.92).

Finally, we included the participant’s viewing frequency for each of the news shows from which the videos were taken (Hannity on Fox News and Morning Joe on MSNBC) to control for their existing tendency to view the two videos. The participant’s prior attitude regarding each show would likely affect their answers related to the video clip from the show. We included this control to improve estimation precision in our randomized experimental design because past viewing is highly predictive of future viewing (Kahan et al., 2014; Lee, 2016). ## Results

We performed a multilevel random coefficient analysis. Our data structure is hierarchical as two news video viewings (Level 1) were nested within each participant (Level 2). A multilevel random coefficient model is superior to a simple OLS since it accounts for the random variability of intercepts and slopes across participants (Nezlek, 2001). We used robust standard errors clustered by participant to account for potential error heteroskedasticity and within-cluster error correlation. Continuous independent variables were grandmean centered.

Table 2 presents the estimated interaction effect of a commingled falsehood (false) and political alignment between a news video and the participant (aligned) on viewership and the three potential persuasion mechanisms analysis results. The first column shows the effect on the aggregate viewing and sharing measure, and the other columns show the effect on each of the four individual measures. The interaction effect of false and aligned was positively significant for viewing and sharing intentions (p = 0.006), indicating that a commingled falsehood increases the participant’s viewing and sharing intentions when it is aligned with their political opinions. The estimates with the four individual measures showed the same pattern. The interaction effect of false and aligned was positively significant for continue viewing $( p = 0 . 0 4 2 )$ , next episode viewing (p = 0.019), sharing $( p = 0 . 0 2 1 )$ , and recommending $( p = 0 . 0 0 7 )$ Thus, H1a is supported. There was no significant difference between Republican and Democrat participants.

The mechanism analyses showed that the interaction effect of false and aligned was positively significant for confirmation bias $( p = 0 . 0 4 0 )$ but not enjoyment or distress, suggesting that a commingled falsehood induces a significant increase in confirmation bias but not enjoyment or distress. We followed Baron and Kenny (1986)<sup>4</sup> to test if confirmation bias mediates the interaction effect of false and aligned on viewing and sharing intentions. After controlling for confirmation bias, the significant interaction effects for H1a became insignificant for the aggregate measure and the four individual measures, suggesting that confirmation bias fully mediated the observed effects. Thus, H2a is supported, but H2b and H2c are not. Hence, one important mechanism is that people prefer to view and share politically aligned news videos that present a falsehood because it matches their existing political opinions.

## Discussion

Study 1 examines the case where a commingled falsehood is stated by a celebrity in a video from a well-known media firm. The results suggest that a commingled, appealing partisan falsehood significantly increases viewing and sharing intentions among politically aligned viewers; the effect estimate $( \beta \ : = \ : 0 . 6 0 5 )$ indicates a 21% increase in viewing and sharing intentions, compared to the mean viewing and sharing intentions score of 2.898. The opposite effect (significantly negative) was overall observed with viewers who were not politically aligned, as shown in the estimates of false in Table 2, highlighting the importance of political alignment between a news video and the participant in driving the observed effect. The persuasion mechanism analysis results suggest that confirmation bias is an important mechanism underlying the observed effect, as it fully mediates the effect. Therefore, we conclude that a commingled falsehood increases the viewership of wellknown media firms’ news videos presented by celebrities by appealing to the underlying partisanship (confirmation bias) of target (politically aligned) audiences.

## Study 2: Commingled Falsehoods with Unknown Speakers and Well-Known Media Firms

Study 1 examined the commingled falsehood effects when there are two sources of reputation (a celebrity and a wellknown media firm), which is a common occurrence. Study 2 examines what happens when we remove one source of reputation and rely solely on the reputation of the media firm. A falsehood may no longer be seen as believable if it is not backed up by the reputation of a celebrity speaker. In Study 2, we replaced the celebrity with an unknown speaker.

## Method

We again recruited U.S. adult participants from MTurk with approval ratings of at least 95% and at least 100 completed HITs. We removed participants who took more than 15 minutes to respond (more than three times the average completion time of 4.8 minutes) and those who gave a wrong answer on an attention check. There were 1,247 participants in the final sample. About 50% were male. About 62% were between the ages of 25-44, and 30% were between the ages of 45-64. About 72% had a college degree, and 18% had a graduate degree. About 68% self-identified as Democrats, and 32% self-identified as Republicans. About 80% were Caucasian, 4% were Hispanic or Latino, 8% were Black or African American, and 6% were Asian.

The treatments were the same as those of Study 1, but with a 2×2 between-subjects design. That is, the presence or absence of a falsehood at the end of the video (randomly assigned) and the political alignment between the video and the participant (either a right-leaning or a left-leaning video was randomly assigned). We performed manipulation checks at the end of the study (Hauser et al., 2018) and confirmed that the participants in the treatment with the falsehood were more likely to report hearing it. We created two news videos (each about 45 seconds long) similar to the news videos in Study 1, with stories adapted to the current news environment at the time of our experiment, i.e., post-2020 election. The rightleaning video discussed an appeals court decision on Trump’s challenge of the Pennsylvania race, and the left-leaning video discussed Russian interference during the 2020 U.S. presidential election. An unknown speaker presented the two videos; we hired a professional actor who is a member of the Screen Actors Guild and has extensive acting experience. We did not expect participants to be concerned about viewing an anchor they did not recognize because many Americans do not recognize all the top news anchors on major networks (Suls, 2014). Both videos used the same background image. The falsehoods were similar to those in Study 1 but updated to reflect current political events at the time of the study. The falsehood in the right-leaning video was: “The 3rd circuit, the most liberal court of appeals, the most overturned court in the country.” The falsehood in the left-leaning video was: “I think that you can see that visits to Moscow were made during the campaign by Giuliani and others.” Figure 2 presents snapshots of the two videos; the script is in Appendix A.

Participants watched one of the two news videos and answered questions; each participant watched only one video (rather than both videos as in Study 1) since the same unknown speaker could not appear in both Fox News and MSNBC videos. We verified that participants did not recognize the actor; the average recognition score was 1.7 on a 7-point scale (1 = not at all, 7 = extremely). All measures were the same as Study 1, except we removed the viewing frequency control because the videos from an unknown speaker were new to all participants.

## Results

We performed OLS regression with robust standard errors (see Table 3). The interaction effect of false and aligned was positively significant for the overall measure of viewing and sharing intentions $( p ~ = ~ 0 . 0 2 8 )$ . The results, once more, indicate the positive effect of a commingled falsehood on the participant’s viewing and sharing intentions when it is aligned with their political opinions. The coefficient size was significantly smaller than that of Study 1 (see Appendix C), suggesting that the reputation of a celebrity speaker is important. The estimates with the four individual measures showed the same pattern. Thus, H1b is also supported. There was no significant difference between Republican and Democrat participants.

The interaction effect of false and aligned was significant for confirmation bias (p = 0.050) and enjoyment (p = 0.019) but not distress. This suggests that including an appealing partisan falsehood induces a significant increase in confirmation bias and enjoyment among politically aligned participants. We performed a mediation analysis and found that both fully mediated the interaction effect of false and aligned on viewing and sharing intentions.

## Discussion

Study 2 results suggest that a commingled, appealing partisan falsehood by an unknown speaker in a news video produced by a well-known media firm increases viewing and sharing intentions; the effect estimate (?? = 0.386) indicates a 15% increase in viewing and sharing intentions, compared to the mean viewing and sharing intentions score of 2.642. The effects are significantly smaller than in Study 1 in which a celebrity stated the falsehood. Prior research suggests that viewers develop a parasocial relationship with celebrities and perceive them to be credible sources of information (Farivar et al., 2021; Reinikainen et al., 2020; Tukachinsky et al., 2020). Our results indicate that in the absence of a celebrity, the positive effect of a commingled falsehood on viewership diminishes but is still significant. The reputation of the media firm alone is sufficient to drive this effect but is not as powerful as having both sources of reputation.

The mechanism analysis found confirmation bias to be significant (matching Study 1), as well as enjoyment (which was not significant in Study 1). Study 1 had a celebrity speaker, whereas Study 2 did not. Viewers’ parasocial relationship with celebrities often leads to greater enjoyment (Farivar et al., 2021; Reinikainen et al., 2020; Tukachinsky et al., 2020), and we see this here—participants reported higher enjoyment with a story aligned with their political beliefs from a celebrity in Study 1 than the same aligned story from an unknown speaker in Study 2 (Study 1: ?? = 0.792 (95% CI: 0.518, 1.066), Study 2: ?? = 0.278 (95% CI: 0.044, 0.512)). We conjecture that both a celebrity speaker and an appealing partisan falsehood increase enjoyment, but the effect of a celebrity is so strong that an appealing partisan falsehood has a small effect relative to the celebrity’s other statements— i.e., viewers enjoy the celebrity regardless of the presence or absence of the falsehood. In contrast, viewers experience less enjoyment with an unknown speaker, so the falsehood can have a greater impact on enjoyment.

Study 3: Commingled Falsehoods with Unknown Speakers and Unknown Media Firms

In Study 3, we removed the well-known media firm logos from the news videos from Study 2. Thus, Study 3 examines the effects of a commingled falsehood presented by an unknown speaker in a news video from an unknown media firm. As such, it removes both sources of reputation.

## Method

We recruited U.S. adult participants from MTurk with approval ratings of at least 95% and at least 100 completed HITs. We screened out participants who took more than 25 minutes to respond (more than three times the average completion time of 7.2 minutes) and those who gave wrong answers on attention checks. There were 850 participants in the final sample. About 53% were male. About 52% were between the ages of 25-44, and 36% were between the ages of 45-64. About 74% had a college degree, and 18% had a graduate degree. About 67% self-identified as Democrats, and 33% self-identified as Republicans. About 80% were Caucasian, 4% were Hispanic or Latino, 6% were Black or African American, and 8% were Asian.

The treatments and measures were the same as Study 2, with a 2×2 mixed-factorial design: the presence or absence of a falsehood (randomly assigned) and political alignment (both right-leaning and left-leaning videos were viewed in random order). The news videos were the same as those of Study 2, with one modification. We replaced Fox News and MSNBC logos with an unknown logo and told participants the videos were from a local news station. Since the same unknown speaker could appear in both videos (without the politically conflicting media firm logos), we used the same repeated measures design as Study 1, where participants watched both videos. We confirmed that participants did not recognize the actor; the average recognition score was 1.4 on the same 7- point scale. Figure 3 presents snapshots of the videos.

## Results

We performed a multilevel random coefficient analysis with robust standard errors clustered by participant (see Table 4). The interaction effect of false and aligned was not significant for the aggregate measure and all four individual measures, suggesting no effect of a commingled falsehood on viewing and sharing intentions. Thus, H1c is not supported. Similarly, the interaction effects were not significant for confirmation bias, enjoyment, and distress.

Factitious informational blends are effective when presented by opinion leaders and other elites (Rojecki & Meraz, 2016). Study 3 removed the sources of reputation used in Studies 1 and 2 to signal opinion leadership (a celebrity and well-known media firm), and the effects on viewership in these two studies disappeared. Reputation is important to the effectiveness of commingled fake news because it provides an impetus to accept the falsehoods as true (Rojecki & Meraz, 2016; Zloteanu et al., 2018). We performed a post hoc analysis to better understand the effects of the participant’s belief in the truth of the falsehood. At the end of the experiment, we showed the two falsehoods and asked participants whether they believed each to be true (using a 7-point scale). We classified participants into three groups for each falsehood: those who believed it to be true (5-7), those who believed it to be false (1-3), and those who were uncertain (4). The results showed that the three-way interaction of the falsehood, political alignment, and the participant’s belief that the falsehood was true was significant for the aggregate viewing and sharing measure (p = 0.002) and all four individual measures (see Appendix D). The three-way interaction was also significant for confirmation bias and enjoyment, similar to the Study 2 mechanism analysis results, suggesting that viewers’ belief in the truth of the falsehood plays a vital role in driving the effects of a commingled partisan falsehood.

## Discussion

The Study 3 results suggest that an appealing partisan falsehood presented by an unknown speaker in a news video produced by an unknown media firm has no significant effect on viewing and sharing intentions unless the viewer believes the falsehood to be true. The earlier observed commingled falsehood effect in Study 1 (presented by celebrities and well-known media firms) diminished (Study 2) and disappeared (Study 3) as we removed the speaker and firm reputation components. The Study 3 post hoc analysis showed similar results to Study 2 with politically aligned viewers who believed the falsehood to be true, suggesting that a commingled falsehood has little effect unless it is believed to be true and that without the reputation effects of a celebrity speaker or well-known media firm, the falsehood is less likely to be believed.

It is noteworthy that the Study 3 results conflict with the results of prior research examining the large-scale cascades of false news versus true news posts on Twitter, which showed that false news posts, mostly posted by unverified users, spread faster and farther than true news posts (i.e., greater virality) (Vosoughi et al., 2018). A commingled falsehood from an unknown speaker and an unknown media firm, which is equivalent to an unverified user, does not affect viewing and sharing intentions. Thus, our findings suggest that the previously reported greater virality of false news stories with unknown sources does not generalize to commingled falsehoods.

## Study 4: False News Stories with Unknown Speakers and Well-Known Media Firms

In Studies 4 and 5, we replicated Studies 2 and 3 with a false news story whose central message focused on the same falsehood, rather than commingling it in a true news story, to understand whether commingled falsehoods work differently than false news stories. Study 4 examined the effects of a false news story under the same reputation effect as Study 2.

## Method

We recruited U.S. adult participants from MTurk with approval ratings of at least 95% and at least 100 completed HITs. We screened out participants who took more than 15 minutes to respond (more than three times the average completion time of 4.4 minutes), and those who gave a wrong answer to an attention check. There were 1,001 participants in the final sample. About 55% were male. About 64% were between the ages of 25-44, and 27% were between the ages of 45-64. About 74% had a college degree, and 15% had a graduate degree. About 66% self-identified as Democrats, and 34% self-identified as Republicans. About 80% were Caucasian, 4% were Hispanic or Latino, 9% were Black or African American, and 6% were Asian.

We used a 2×2 between-subjects design: a false news story (randomly assigned) and the political alignment (either a rightleaning or a left-leaning video was randomly assigned). All measures remained the same as in earlier studies. We updated the news stories of Studies 2 and 3 so that the earlier commingled falsehood in each story became the story’s central message; by adding further elaborations they thus became false news stories (King et al., 2021; Moravec et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021). We made news stories consistent to adequately compare the effect of a false news story to the effect of a commingled falsehood in the earlier studies. The discussion of the 2020 U.S. presidential election was still active, and the news story relevance affected all treatments equally. Figure 4 presents snapshots of the videos (see Appendix E for scripts). We confirmed that participants did not recognize the actor—the average recognition score was 1.6.

## Results

We performed OLS regression with robust standard errors (see Table 5). The interaction effect of false and aligned was not significant for the aggregate measure and all four individual measures. The interaction effect was significant for confirmation bias but not enjoyment and distress. Thus, a false news story triggered confirmation bias, but the effect on viewership was not significant.

## Discussion

The Study 4 results suggest that a false news story presented by an unknown speaker in a news video produced by a wellknown media firm has no significant effect on viewing and sharing intentions. Study 2 provided the same reputation effect but found a positive effect with a commingled falsehood. In contrast, the false news story did not show such an effect, although it triggered confirmation bias similarly. This is likely because there was enough suspicious content in the story to trigger thoughts about the political bias of the wellknown media firm. Political bias in the media is well-known (e.g., Sheridan, 2021). When a news source known for its strong right-leaning or left-leaning bias presents a false news story, people may be more hesitant to accept the veracity of the story because they are likely to believe that the political bias of the media firm may have shaped the story.

## Method

## Discussion

The Study 5 results suggest that a false news story presented by an unknown speaker in a news video produced by an unknown media firm increases viewing and sharing intentions. The results are consistent with prior research showing that false news posts on social media (Twitter), mostly posted by unverified users, spread faster and farther than true news posts (i.e., have greater virality) (Vosoughi et al., 2018); thus, our findings show that the higher performance of false news stories from unknown sources still holds with news videos. In contrast, Study 4 earlier found that the same false news stories had no effect when they were from well-known media firms. Although the political bias of well-known media firms is well-known (e.g., Sheridan, 2021), people nevertheless tend to assume that unknown firms are politically neutral, likely because they have no evidence to the contrary (Peterson & Allamong, 2021). Accordingly, viewers are less likely to consider political bias when a false news story is from an unknown media firm than when the story is from a well-known media firm. Thus, a false news story showed no effect when it was from a well-known media firm (Study 4) but had a positive effect when it was from an unknown media firm (Study 5).

The post hoc analysis results highlight that commingled falsehoods and false news stories work very differently. When a news story was presented by an unknown speaker and an unknown media firm, commingled falsehoods did not increase viewing and sharing intentions and did not trigger confirmation bias unless they were believed to be true (Study 3)—in other words, commingled falsehoods have effects when viewers believe that they are true, which the reputation effects of celebrity speakers and well-known media firms help facilitate (Studies 1 and 2). In contrast, false news stories increased viewing and sharing intentions and triggered confirmation bias with no reputational support. These effects were not significantly affected by the viewer’s belief regarding the truth of the falsehood—in other words, false news stories appeal to partisanship and drive the effects on viewing and sharing directly.

We compared the coefficients of commingled falsehoods from well-known sources in Studies 1 and 2 to that of false news stories from unknown sources (Study 5), which have been the focus of prior research and thus serve as a helpful benchmark. The Study 1 coefficient (0.605) is similar in size to the benchmark Study 5 coefficient (0.615), suggesting that both are similarly problematic. The Study 2 coefficient (0.386) is 63% of the benchmark.

## Field Validation Study

We conducted a field validation of our experimental findings. We collected independently fact-checked statements (by PolitiFact and FactCheck.org) for four years (2016 to 2019) in the news videos of well-known media firms (with celebrities). We identified 46 news shows from seven well-known firms containing such statements and collected the daily viewership of those shows on television<sup>5</sup> via Comscore TV Essentials. Political alignment between a show and its viewers is assumed due to the partisan selectivity of news sources (Grieco, 2020; Stroud, 2011). We created a binary variable (false statement) to indicate the presence of a commingled statement that the factcheckers determined to be false (n = 155) in an otherwise putatively true news video (a daily episode of a show). This is the focus of the field study and aligns with our experiments; an episode that includes a false statement is a factitious informational blend of a falsehood and an otherwise true story. We also included two other binary variables for episodes that contained statements that were fact-checked and determined to be entirely true (true statement, n = 57) and partially true (halftrue statement, n = 33, defined as being an accurate statement that omitted important details or was taken out of context). We do not theorize about the effects of fact-checked true or halftrue statements but include them for comparison.

First, we used all news videos without a fact-checked statement (non-fact-checked group) as a reference group to estimate the relationship between the three binary variables and viewership. As a viewership measure, we used the average number of viewers (Joyella, 2021; Sabnis & Grewal, 2015), which increases if current viewers view more of a news video and/or share it more, drawing more new viewers. We controlled for known viewership determinants like prior days’ viewership of the news show, time-varying newsworthiness of a day, timevarying attention to the news show (affected by a noteworthy segment, the host’s scandal, etc.), and regular or specia programming. We also added show and time (by week and by weekday) fixed effects.

Second, we conducted two analyses using non-fact-checked news videos matched to news videos containing a fact-checked statement as a reference group (thus, both groups were similar) to mitigate selection bias between the fact-checked and nonfact-checked videos using coarsened exact matching (Iacus et al., 2012). We further applied copula correction (Park & Gupta, 2012) to account for potential endogeneity between the three binary variables and the error term. Omitted variable bias is possible, as measuring and comparing all characteristics of news videos is not feasible, although important ones are included in our model. See Appendix F.

We found a significantly positive relationship between a commingled false statement and viewership for all three analyses (see Table 7). On average, the two matched data analyses showed a roughly 2% increase in viewership. In contrast, a commingled half-true statement did not show a significant relationship with viewership and neither did a commingled statement verified as true. This indicates that it is the appeal of a commingled false statement that affects viewership. Thus, our field data support our experimental findings. There was no significant difference between media firms with different political standings (see Appendix F).

## General Discussion

Our results show that a commingled appealing partisan falsehood in an otherwise true news story increases viewing and sharing intentions of political news videos among politically aligned viewers when it is presented by a wellknown media firm with or without a celebrity speaker (Studies 1 and 2); without a celebrity speaker, the effect is reduced but still significant. However, the same commingled falsehood presented by an unknown speaker and an unknown media firm had no effect unless the viewer believed the falsehood to be true (Study 3). The pattern was reversed for a false news video centered on the same falsehood: it had no effect when presented by a well-known media firm (Study 4) but increased viewing and sharing intentions when presented by an unknown media firm (Study 5). Our field study shows the same pattern as our experiments with commingled falsehoods, finding a significantly positive relationship between a commingled partisan falsehood in videos of well-known media firms and actual viewership. Figure 6 summarizes the results of our experimental studies.

We investigated three potential mediators and found that confirmation bias consistently explained the significant increase in viewing and sharing intentions from commingled falsehoods. The size of the induced confirmation bias decreased, as the two reputation sources were removed, matching the reduced effect of commingled falsehoods. The reputations of the speaker and the firm, as well as viewers belief in the truth of a falsehood, are important for commingled falsehoods but have no effect in the context of false news stories.

## Contributions to Research

Our work makes three important contributions to the fake news literature. First, for “what,” or the type of fake news, our work examines brief commingled falsehoods in otherwise true news stories, whereas existing research focuses on false news stories (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019; Moravec et al., 2020; Pennycook et al., 2021; Turel & Osatuyi, 2021; Vosoughi et al., 2018). Our controlled experiments show that viewing and sharing intentions increase when an appealing partisan falsehood is commingled in otherwise true news videos from well-known sources. Our field study shows this same pattern: actual viewership is greater for news videos that contain a commingled partisan falsehood than for news videos that lack them. These commingled falsehoods have the same size of effect on viewership as false news stories, highlighting the importance of understanding commingled falsehoods. This is a new direction for fake news research (George et al., 2021), which we hope spurs more work.

Second, for “who,” or the source of fake news, our work studies fake news (misinformation and disinformation) from well-known sources (e.g., Fox News and MSNBC), which are the top publishers of news videos on social media as well as on websites and television (Flood, 2021; Hutchinson, 2021; Kalogeropoulos et al., 2016; Newman et al., 2020), whereas existing research focuses on fake news from unknown sources on social media (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019; Moravec et al., 2020; Pennycook et al., 2021; Turel & Osatuyi, 2021; Vosoughi et al., 2018). Our results show that the reputation effect of the celebrity speaker and the media firm is essential for commingled falsehoods to affect viewership. The pattern of reputation effects is reversed for false news stories, which work with unknown sources but not with well-known sources. Our work calls attention to well-known sources having an economic incentive to commingle falsehoods as a critical element of the fake news phenomenon. Reaffirming the greater performance of false news over true news with unknown sources in news videos (as contrasted with text-based news posts) is helpful, given the growing popularity of news videos (Kalogeropoulos et al., 2016; Newman et al., 2020).

Third, for “why,” or the mechanisms through which commingled falsehoods drive more viewing and sharing, our work extends existing research (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019) by showing that confirmation bias is a primary mechanism (i.e., mediator) driving the effects of commingled falsehoods. Our research also reveals that viewers’ belief is important for commingled falsehoods. We find that viewers’ belief in the truth of politically aligned commingled falsehoods plays a vital role in driving their effect on viewing and sharing, which is why the reputation of the person and firm presenting the falsehoods is essential. When the viewer trusts the speaker and/or firm, they accept a commingled falsehood in a story as true, increasing their desire to view and share the story. When falsehoods cannot draw on the reputation of the speaker or firm, they only have effects if viewers believe them to be true. In contrast, we found that viewers’ belief plays no significant role in false news stories.

## Limitations and Future Research

In addition to the findings and contributions of our work, we need to consider its limitations. First, any controlled experiments use an artificial context. Our participants watched the news videos we selected, not the ones they selected. Second, there is an inherent concern about the generalizability of results from specific participants and materials used in any study. We believe that Amazon Mechanical Turk workers represent an appropriate population, as it is representative of the U.S. population, and we used actual news videos from media firms. Third, the experiments measured participants’ intentions rather than their actual behavior. It is common for experiments to study intentions because intentions and actions are theoretically linked and empirically correlated (Ajzen & Fishbein, 2000; Glasman & Albarracín, 2006). Our field validation study complements our work in the online lab and largely addresses these limitations by using actual viewership data of real news videos with over 100 factchecked commingled falsehoods.

Our research focuses on an underexplored aspect of the fake news phenomenon and invokes several interesting future research questions. First, future work could examine the extent to which the different characteristics of commingled falsehoods influence their effects, such as noticeability (e.g., saliency, novelty, and emotional appeal), potential impact, or the content being falsified (e.g., time, person, or location). Certain characteristics may lead to greater or lesser effects on user viewing and sharing. Understanding these relationships could help to identify the characteristics of commingled falsehoods that impose a greater risk and inform strategies to counter their effects.

Second, future research could define and assess other possible ways of mixing truth and falsehoods in a news story. For example, most false news stories contain a portion of truth (e.g., a story claiming that President Biden bribed Pope Francis contains the truth that Biden is the president and Francis is the pope). It may be possible to increase the amount of truth to increase the credibility of a false news story. Adding one or more familiar truths may create a cognitive bias and mislead people into thinking that a false news story may be true (Newman & Zhang, 2020). Future efforts to conceptualize and explore these possibilities are important to better understand emerging threats and tackle them accordingly.

Third, future research could examine AI techniques to detect commingled falsehoods from well-known sources. Current efforts that focus on detecting false news stories using granular cues such as affective information flows, discourse structures, and sentence relationships (e.g., Ghanem et al., 2021; Karimi & Tang, 2019; Kim & Ko, 2021) could be extended to detect commingled falsehoods. A sudden spike of emotion, a deviation from a speech structure, or a digressing sentence may help detect them. Future ideations and efforts for automated detection could help combat commingled falsehoods timely and efficiently.

## Managerial and Policy Implications

The practical essence of our findings is that well-known media firms should commingle politically aligned falsehoods in their news reporting to gain more views and shares. If media personalities and firms are economically rational, they will be inclined to add appealing partisan falsehoods to political news videos. Media firms have begun tracking minute-by-minute viewing data to understand what content influences viewership and are starting to use this knowledge to script content to increase viewership (Coy, 2022). As Coy notes: “It’s not just pride; it’s also money. Advertisers pay more for bigger audiences.”

Our work suggests potential agency issues within media firms. Commingling falsehoods has immediate benefits but may erode reputations over time if viewers discover the falsehoods. The gradual accumulation of such disappointments may be why the public’s trust in media firms is decreasing (Meek, 2021) and the avoidance of news is increasing (Newman et al., 2017). Both can harm media firms over the long run; a recent study shows that people care about news accuracy (Pennycook et al., 2021); thus, a pattern of commingled falsehoods has the potential for long-term harm. In the highly competitive news market (Anand, 2017), however, firms’ managers or news hosts may choose to commingle falsehoods to increase viewership in the short term with less consideration of the potential long-term effects.

For society, the intentional commingling of falsehoods in news videos by well-known media firms can harm our collective good because truthful information is of fundamental importance for political, business, and personal decisions (Anand, 2017; Barthel et al., 2016; Doherty, 2017). If economically rational actors are motivated to make decisions counter to the public good, is this a market failure? If so, a classic solution would be to consider market regulations (Krugman & Wells, 2006; Tirole, 2015).

Commingled falsehoods are information pollution (Meel & Vishwakarma, 2020), so one potential solution is to regulate them as we regulate other forms of pollution. For example, the Clean Air Act was introduced in the U.S. to shift manufacturing firms’ economic incentives to include pollution control (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2023). The regulation for commingled falsehoods may take the same approach. In the U.K., the government has implemented “due accuracy” rules to reduce fake news (Ofcom, 2021), and some U.S. news firms have been accused of breaching these rules (Horowitz, 2021). Even if these rules help reduce false news stories, it is unclear if commingling a single falsehood meets “due accuracy” rules since most of the content is accurate. We also need to protect free speech and the independence of news media (Nielsen, 2021), which makes this regulatory approach doubly challenging.

Another solution would be to take market-based approaches using the environmental, social, and governance (ESG) perspective, which considers the extent to which firms benefit or harm social welfare (Gillan et al., 2021; Governance & Accountability Institute, 2021). ESG involves investors and ESG scorers as necessary parties (Gillan et al., 2021). Thus, one approach is for ESG scorers to include the provision of commingled falsehoods (as measured by PolitiFact or other fact-checkers) as one element in ESG scores. A second approach would be to integrate the ESG viewpoint into viewership rating systems. For example, Comscore and Nielsen rating scorers could penalize news videos that contain commingled falsehoods as assessed by independent fact-checkers (e.g., by omitting or reducing viewership scores for shows that engage in such practices). Such a change to the viewership system would reduce the incentives for commingling falsehoods.

Our findings provide important insights into the problem of commingled falsehoods. Our work shows that well-known media firms have an economic incentive to commingle attractive partisan falsehoods into true news stories but have little incentive to produce false news stories. In contrast, the incentives are reversed for unknown media firms (they are incentivized to create false news stories but not to commingle falsehoods). Our findings on commingled falsehoods add difficult questions to the already complex regulatory discussions surrounding fake news and the need for new approaches to this societal problem. We hope our research sparks more discussion on what policy considerations are needed to address the conflicting incentives for commingling falsehoods in well-known firms’ news stories and supporting public welfare by providing truthful information.

Alan R. Dennis (ORCiD: 0000-0002-6439-6134) is a professor of information systems and holds the John T. Chambers Chair of Internet Systems in the Kelley School of Business at Indiana University. He also has written four books (two on data communications and networking, and two on systems analysis and design). In the model, ?? denotes each of the 46 news shows from 7 well-known media firms (ABC, CBS, CNBC, CNN, Fox Business, Fox News, MSNBC), and ?? denotes a day. As controls, we first accounted for the longitudinal nature of viewership that is dependent on previous days’ viewership. A show’s episode today often follows up stories of previous days, so viewers who watched previous episodes may watch the show again. Research suggests that two-day lags of viewership are the most appropriate (Sabnis & Grewal, 2015).

Second, we included newsworthiness to account for the time-varying newsworthiness (Sabnis & Grewal, 2015). We collected daily online search volume for the big three cable news firms (Fox News, CNN, and MSNBC) via Google Trends, theorizing that people would search those firms more to find content about a newsworthy event. We normalized each firm’s search volume. The z-scores were largely similar (average correlation of 0.87), suggesting that the scores capture the trend of daily newsworthiness. We used the average of z-scores.

Third, we included show attention to control for time-varying attention. For example, a noteworthy segment (e.g., a high-profile interview) or specific events related to a host or their show (e.g., a scandal or a past segment becoming viral) would lead more viewers to the show. We collected daily online search volume for the hosts of news shows via Google Trends and normalized individual search volumes (z-score). For multiple hosts of a news show, we used their average. We used the resulting z-scores (averaged if multiple hosts) by news show.

Last, we included the programming style of news videos (regular or special) and fixed effects. We coded special programming as 1 if a news video was a special and 0 otherwise. Also, three fixed effects were considered. We included the fixed effect of news shows of our news videos to account for time-invariant characteristics across news shows. We added week and weekday fixed effects to account for unobserved seasonal viewership trends.

## Matching Strategy and Implementation

We used five covariates (news show, week, special programming, newsworthiness, and show attention) for coarsened exact matching (CEM) (Iacus et al., 2012). Matching on the same week is helpful since it vastly reduces the unobserved obscurity of news stories across videos, i.e., news stories on the same week are likely similar. Thus, the two video groups from the same news show, the same week (hence, likely abou similar news stories), the same programming style, with similar newsworthiness and show attention, are believed to be reasonably comparable.

We followed a standard procedure for CEM using the MatchIt package in R. CEM performs exact matching for categorical variables (news show, week, special programming) and stratifies continuous variables for matching (newsworthiness and show attention) with Sturges’ rule (Scott, 2009) by default. All five covariates construct their own strata. Once all possible combinations across the strata (called bin signatures) are obtained, fact-checked videos and non-fact-checked videos are identified for each bin signature. If there are videos from both groups in a bin signature, they are matched. If not, unmatched observations are dropped. CEM then computes weights for observations by bin signatur to account for the imbalance in the number of matched fact-checked and non-fact-checked videos across bin signatures. Finally, the matched data with weights was used for estimation. CEM produced 898 matched observations: 217 observations containing a fact-checked statement and 681 without such a statement.

We checked to see if CEM produced a comparable non-fact-checked group: the viewership difference between the fact-checked and nonfact-checked groups should be small if they are truly comparable; the viewership of fact-checked group is still likely higher since the viewership already reflects more viewing and sharing from false statements. After CEM, the significant difference in viewership between the groups disappeared. Thus, the matching was satisfactory.

## Copula Correction

Copula correction is a popular method when an external instrumental variable is hard to obtain (e.g., Bachmann et al., 2021; Becker et al., 2022; Xing et al., 2021). A copula is a function to join multivariate distributions to one-dimensional marginal distribution. With the empirically driven functional form of the marginal distribution of an endogenous regressor and the assumption of the normally distributed error term, a copula (their correlation) can be obtained. The functional form of the copula can then be added as an additional term, like a correction term in a control function approach. It should be noted that estimates with copula correction may still be biased with binary endogenous regressors since their distributional information may be insufficient. Despite the bias, estimated coefficients will be nearer to the true value, mitigating endogeneity concerns (Park & Gupta, 2012). We considered the three focal variables endogenous, so three copula correction terms were added to the model.

## Robustness Checks

First, since media viewership can be serially correlated, we checked to see if autocorrelation is a concern in our data. With the before matching data, including lagged dependent variables largely reduced autocorrelation but not wholly; thus, we also estimated the Arellano-Bond estimator that is robust to autocorrelation (Arellano & Bond, 1991) and found consistent results (?? of False statement = 64582.445, p < 0.001). Autocorrelation was not an issue with the after-matching data.

Second, we performed a falsification test. We re-estimated our models with one change: the three binary variables were shifted backward by one day. Then, their coefficients should be insignificant since no effect is possible. This test helps ensure that our results are not driven by spurious correlation, inappropriate matching, model misspecification, and a pre-existing event (Bapna et al., 2018). False statement was not significant: our approach is valid and reliable.

## Limitations

First, our data analysis was constrained by the availability of fact-checks. A potential bias from endogenous sample selection is a concern as the fact-checkers’ selection process may not be random. While we could not directly control for this unobserved selection mechanism, we applied coarsened exact matching (CEM) to mitigate the concern. The satisfactory balance between the two groups after matching provides more confidence in our estimation results.

Another limitation is potential endogeneity in our models. We controlled for important viewership determinants, but omitted variable bias is still possible. We applied copula correction to mitigate this concern, although it is not a complete cure. The same conclusion adds more credibility to the positive relationship between a commingled false statement and news video viewership. Nonetheless, we need more research to overcome the above limitations.
