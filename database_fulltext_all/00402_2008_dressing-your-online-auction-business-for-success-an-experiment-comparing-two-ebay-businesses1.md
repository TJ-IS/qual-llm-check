## Abstract

Businesses can choose who they want to be online. Product and company attributes that are directly perceivable in the real world can be manipulated to make a favorable impression on online buyers. This study examines whether creating a more professional online e-image can signal consumers about unobservable product or company quality, and whether this signal influences their willingness to transact with the company, and ultimately the prices they are willing to pay for the company's goods and services. An empirical study is presented that examines two online auction businesses utilizing different company names and auction listing styles to sell items in parallel over the course of one year. The findings suggest that increasing the quality of an auction business's e-image does increase consumers' willingness to transact with the business, and increases prices received at auction. The study also demonstrates the ability to use eBay as an experimental laboratory for testing a variety of hypotheses about purchasing behavior online.

Keywords: Signaling theory, website quality, reputation, price premiums, online auctions, empirical research, e-image

## Introduction

Websites serve as an important (and frequently the only) point of contact for most e-businesses. They are used to convey information about the company, its products, and the level of service the consumer can expect to receive. Information signals provided within websites are used to influence consumers' overall perceptions of value, which can be a driving factor in e-business success (Lohse and Spiller 1998). These signals can include the presentation and quality of product information and background information provided about the business and its policies. Even the name can reflect on the appearance of professionalism of the business. Taken together, these factors create an online e-image for the business. $^{2}$ Businesses need to create positive e-images by providing the type of information and interaction necessary to satisfy website users and potential customers.

An e-image is the electronic image presented by a business or individual. Research suggests that the choice of an e-image is often consciously made to create a distinct online identity (Hesketh and Selwyn 1999; Sand 2007). E-image is composed of a variety of factors, which for online auction businesses include identity (username), website appearance and information content, reputation rank from the reputation systems, and any reputation feedback. This study examines how the selection of a username and improving website quality affects the success of online auction businesses.

Research suggests that the quality of a company's website may influence consumer impressions of the company (e.g., Barnes and Vidgen 2001a; Kim and Stoel 2004; McKnight et al. 2004; Shchiglik and Barnes 2004) and their willingness to transact with the company (e.g., Park et al. 2004; Ranganathan and Ganapathy 2002). However, this prior website quality research has assessed user's intention to buy without measuring actual purchasing behavior.

Past empirical research on the relationship between intentions and behavior (e.g., Adams 1974; Clawson 1971; Ferber and Piskie 1965; Granbois and Summers 1975; Jamieson and Bass 1989; Juster 1966; Mittal and Kamakura 2001; Morwitz and Schmittlein 1992; Spiekermann et al. 2001; Warshaw 1980) indicates that, although intentions are often used as a proxy variable for actual behavior, not everyone behaves as they claim they will. Studies seeking respondents' intentions of future behavior or how they would behave under certain hypothetical conditions can suffer from several different kinds of bias. These biases include common methods bias, a problem not eliminated by evidence of validity (Fiske 1982; Podsakoff et al. 2003); and distortion, either because respondents tend to maintain a consistent line in a series of answers (consistency motif) or because certain responses are more socially desirable (Podsakoff and Organ 1986). In addition, there can be error in measuring intentions caused by changes that occur between the time intent is measured and the purchase occasion (Young et al. 1998). Thus, measuring intentions may conceal important characteristics of the phenomenon under study, limiting the external validity of this type of research (West 1986).

The purpose of this study is to determine if creating a professional e-image can have a significant impact on consumers' willingness to transact with and the actual prices received by an online auction business. $^{3}$ This study uses a controlled experiment that measures the impact of e-image on actual purchases instead of using intentions as a proxy for purchase behavior. Online auction businesses were chosen for this experiment because they represent a large and growing sector of the online economy (Schonfeld 2005), auction businesses are relatively easy to establish, auction sites like eBay provide a built-in customer base for newly established businesses, and online auctions can easily be set up such that real customers are exposed to the listings for different experimental conditions in a real-world setting.

The experiment involved establishing and running two online auction businesses for one year. All aspects of the businesses were kept identical except for the e-images of the two businesses. Both businesses were new auction businesses with reputation scores less than 40 and 100 percent positive comments. One business was given a professional e-image by selecting a professional sounding user identity and maintaining a high quality website that provided clear return and shipping policies and a professional looking listing style. The second business's e-image appeared to be a more casual business with a less than professional user identity and a much lower quality website that provided only limited product information, no shipping or return policies, and a simple listing design. Actual sales of identical products were used to answer the following research questions:

(1) Does e-image affect willingness to transact with the online auction business?

(2) Does e-image affect prices received at online auctions?

(3) Are these e-image effects influenced by risks associated with different product types?

This study evaluates the tangible effects that e-image can have at online auctions. It shows that presenting a positive e-image increases willingness to transact and price premiums in a real-world setting in which consumers are actually charged for the items they purchase. This indicates that individual choices with respect to e-image can significantly affect the revenue generated by online businesses. This has not been investigated in prior literature, and represents a contribution to research on online auctions as well as a contribution to research on website quality.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: the next section reviews the current literature on information economics and website quality. The subsequent section discusses theory and includes our hypotheses. We then present a field experiment that explores the relationship between the online auction business's e-image and willingness to transact and price premiums. The paper concludes with a discussion of the contributions of this research.

## Literature Review

## Information Economics

The nature of online auction markets makes them especially useful for studying purchasing behavior where there is incomplete or asymmetric information. In most online auctions, the consumer is entirely dependent on the business for information related to the product, its quality, and its condition. This information asymmetry (Akerlof 1970; Mishra et al. 1998) makes it very difficult for consumers to assess product quality before purchase. In addition, online auction transactions usually occur between unknown parties (Resneck and Zeckhauser 2002), which leads to uncertainty about the service the business will provide, as well as whether the business will actually deliver the purchased product (Gregg and Scott 2006). In this setting, consumers would like information that allows them to distinguish the business that will provide high quality goods or a high level of service from the business that will provide lower quality goods or a lower level of service.

In environments with incomplete information, consumers must rely on signals provided by businesses to evaluate their ability to produce (and deliver) quality products (Shapiro 1982). Signaling theory (Spence 1973) states that in situations where there is information asymmetry (the level of quality is known by the business but not by the consumer), a signal is an action of the business that credibly relays information about unobservable product (or company) quality to the consumer (Rao et al. 1999). The more difficult it is for consumers to assess quality prior to purchase and the less they know about the business selling the product, the more likely they are to rely on signals to form expectations about quality (Kardes et al. 2004; Rindova et al. 2005; Spence 2002). E-image is one of the primary means e-commerce businesses have of signaling quality to consumers.

## Website Quality

Researchers studying online environments have found that the context in which a product was being sold is statistically a better predictor of customer attitude than the actual product being sold (Lu and Lin 2002). How the product is being offered for sale is important in acquiring customers online. This is supported by website quality research, which confirms that information content, design, security, and privacy are useful in discriminating between low and high purchase intention of shoppers (Park et al. 2004; Ranganathan and Ganapathy 2002).

Research on website quality can be classified into two different research streams: identifying the attributes that contribute to website quality, and quantifying the effect of website quality on consumer perceptions and intentions. The first stream has resulted in the development and testing of instruments to measure website quality (e.g., Aladwani and Palvia 2002; Barnes and Vidgen 2001a, 2001b; Loiacono et al. 2002). These instruments have been tested on a variety of selling and non-selling sites using experienced online shoppers, student web users, and web developers. Table 1 summarizes the primary website quality dimensions: information quality, ease-of-use, aesthetics, and service quality as found in 12 prior studies.

Researchers assessing perceptions about website quality have found website quality has a significant impact on perceptions of the abilities and trustworthiness of e-businesses (Barnes and Vidgen 2001a; Kim and Stoel 2004; McKnight et al. 2004; Park et al. 2004; Shchiglik and Barnes 2004). This, in turn, affects consumers' assessment of website credibility (Fogg et al. 2003), and their expectations about the quality of service that they will receive (Iwaarden et al. 2004). Interface issues such as information quality, usability, and attractiveness all have a significant influence on perceptions about a business and increase the percentage of those who intend to purchase from or use a website (Belanger et al. 2002; Fung and Lee 1999; Hassan and Li 2005; Kim and Moon 1998; Lin and Lu 2000; Nielsen and Norman 2000; Palmer 2002; Park et al. 2004; Ranganathan and Ganapathy 2002; Roy 2001; Shneiderman 2000; Venkatesh and Ramesh 2006).

Website quality at online auctions has received limited attention from researchers to date. Some secondary data studies have found that providing clear images of the auction items serves to reduce the information asymmetry and increase auction revenue (Kauffman and Wood 2006; Ottoway et al. 2003; Vishwanath 2004). Other studies assessing consumer impressions of online auction provider sites (such as eBay) found that the layout of information, the accuracy of standardized information, and website security influenced user impressions of website quality and their intention to bid (Barnes and Vidgen 2001b; Kwon et al. 2002). However, these studies provided no information on the importance of e-image quality to individual online auction businesses.

Researchers studying website quality have attempted to quantify its impact on purchasing behavior (Park et al. 2004; Ranganathan and Ganapathy 2002). However, they relied on self-reported usage and on the perceptions of subjects to measure purchase intention, which may not reflect actual purchase behavior (Young et al. 1998). This study adds to this literature stream by using a real-world experiment in which consumers are charged for the items they purchase. The real-world experiment will validate previous research, extend previous research to examine the influence of e-image on price premiums, and demonstrate the efficacy of performing real-world experiments at online auctions.

## Theory and Hypotheses

This study is concerned with assessing the impact of choices made by online auction businesses with respect to their e-image on consumers' willingness to transact and on price premiums—holding all other auction factors constant. The two e-image factors examined are user name/identity and website quality attributes that can be controlled by the online auction business. Website quality attributes examined include information quality attributes (e.g., accuracy and completeness of product data), attributes related to the presentation of the data (e.g., organization, usability, understandability), aesthetic attributes (colors, fonts, and layout), customer service policies, and availability of contact information. This study determines whether these e-image attributes can be used by online auction businesses to signal consumers that they possess appropriate business skills to successfully complete transactions.

## Willingness to Transact

Prior website quality research has found that the website quality does impact the purchase intentions of consumers (e.g., Park et al. 2004; Ranganathan and Ganapathy 2002).

This is consistent with studies related to information disclosure in accounting and auditing that have found improved information disclosure reduces the information risk to investors, providing a motivation to transact (e.g., Elliott and Jacobson 1994; Jahmani 2003). Exchange theory supports this, suggesting that individuals favor exchange relationships that are more likely to be successful (Thibaut and Kelley 1957). Since e-image can signal consumers about unobservable product or company quality (Rao et al. 1999), exchange theory suggests that improving e-image will increase a consumer's willingness to transact with the online businesses.

H1: There is a positive relationship between e-image and willingness to transact

## Price Premiums

The relationship between e-image and price premiums has not been investigated in prior research. Prior research has looked at the relationship between reputation scores (an attribute of e-image held constant in this study) and price premiums but did not account for business name or website quality. Economic theory suggests that price premiums are justified in situations where businesses provide a higher quality product or service (Klein and Leffler 1981). It also suggests that the price-quality correlation increases with information (Tellis and Wernerfelt 1987).

Price premiums help compensate preferred businesses for reducing transaction risks (Rao and Monroe 1996). Increasing e-image quality can improve the information available for consumer decision making and help to remove uncertainty surrounding the transaction and consequently reduce perceived risk. A positive e-image is likely to have a corresponding positive effect on prices consumers are willing to pay because it increases their confidence in the quality of the business's goods and services (Rindova et al. 2005). Thus, on the basis of extant theory,

H2: There is a positive relationship between e-image and price premiums.

## Product Risk

Perceived risk can be thought of as a consumer's belief regarding the probability of gains or losses associated with a particular transaction (Mayer and Davis 1995). In online transactions, consumers find it more difficult to assess the quality of certain types of products, creating a risk that the product delivered will not meet their quality expectations (Ba and Pavlou 2002; Gregg and Scott 2006). Theories from economics of information (Nelson 1970) have been used to identify product characteristics that increase online product risk. These characteristics include high product description complexity (De Figueiredo 2000; Jahng et al. 2000), and variability in functionality (Akerlof 1970; Bakos and Kemerer 1992; Resnick et al. 2000). For example, used products are believed to have higher transaction-specific risks because there is the increased possibility that the condition or functionality of the product being sold will not be captured completely in the product description (Scott and Gregg 2004).

Perceptions of risk can influence online transactions in two ways. First, prior research has found that increased perceptions of risk decrease consumers' willingness to transact (Jarvenpaa and Tractinsky 1999; Jarvenpaa et al. 2000; McKnight et al. 2002; Pavlou 2003). Second, perceptions of risk can increase consumers' price sensitivity. Businesses that reduce transaction-specific risks may receive price premiums (Rao and Monroe 1996), but these premiums are dependent on the perceived risk associated with the transaction (Ba and Pavlou 2002). Therefore, product risk will have a moderating effect between e-image and both consumer willingness to transact and price premiums.

H3a: The relationship between e-image and willingness to transact is stronger for high-risk products (e.g., used goods) than for low risk products (e.g., new goods).

H3b: The relationship between e-image and price premiums is stronger for high-risk products (e.g., used goods) than for low risk products (e.g., new goods).

## Research Methods

An experiment was conducted in which two online auction businesses using different e-images (one positive and one negative) were established and run in parallel for the period of one year. The experiment explores the relationship between e-image and both willingness to transact and actual prices received for two online auction businesses.

## E-Image Design

This experiment controls for two specific factors of e-image: professionalism of user identity and website quality, specifically through the listing quality of information content controlled by the businesses. The business user names were chosen to portray an e-image that was consistent with the listing quality attributes, so that the e-image would be uniformly positive or negative. The eBay username for the positive e-image business was selected as “CollegiateSales” to convey a professional image—consistent with that of an established business. The eBay business ID for the negative e-image seller was “NotAPro2003.” It was chosen to reflect the type of less professional name chosen by many sellers on eBay (e.g., junkrnot and new2unow, which were eBay seller identities at the time of the experiment).

The two listing styles were designed to mimic the two extremes of listing styles found at the actual eBay online auction site. A total of 100 actual online auction listings were reviewed and sorted by the researchers into better than average (28 listings), average (33 listings), and worse than average (39 listings) groups. The listings in the better than average group contained the information and style attributes that were consistent with high quality websites and the listings in the worse than average group were missing many key quality attributes identified in prior website quality research (e.g., Aladwani and Palvia 2002; Barnes and Vidgen 2001a, 2001b; Lin and Lu 2000; Liu and Arnet 2000; Loiacono et al. 2002; Muylle et.al. 2004; Ranganathan and Ganapathy 2002; Zhang and von Dran 2001). The common characteristics of the better than average listings were identified and used to create a high quality online auction listing for the positive e-image business. Actual product descriptions, terms of sale, and customer service policies were taken from several of the better listings identified during the sorting process. In addition, attributes related to the organization of the data and to the attractiveness of the listing mimicked attributes found in many of the better listings. Similarly, the information for the negative e-image business was taken from auction listings in the worse than average group. The negative e-image listing included minimal product information, no sales or service polices, and no aesthetic features. Since prior secondary data studies have already found that including a picture in the online auction listing does impact the bids received (Kauffman and Wood 2006; Ottoway et al. 2003; Vishwanath 2004), both the positive e-image auction listing and the negative e-image auction listing included product images (example listings are shown in Appendix A).

Even though eBay offers businesses the opportunity to use templates when creating their online auction listings, the low quality listing style is very common, especially for less popular products and new businesses. Following the experiment, 25 additional listings were examined to determine if low quality listings were still prevalent on eBay. Of the 25 listings examined in 2007, ten could be classified as low quality listings. Nine of the low quality listings were produced by businesses with eBay ratings between 2 and 37 (new businesses) and one of the low quality listings was produced by a business with a rating of 887 (an experienced business).

Before beginning the experiment, a survey was conducted to assess whether users perceived a noticeable difference between the online auctions using the positive e-image and the negative e-image. The survey was given to a group of 42 graduate and undergraduate students with prior experience buying or selling at online auctions. The survey asked the subjects to view the sample auctions with the two e-image styles (shown in Appendix A). $^{4}$ Subjects were then asked to indicate to what extent they agreed with 22 different statements about the e-images (primarily focusing on the website quality). The survey items were from validated website quality instruments (listed earlier) that pertained to the auction listing quality attributes that can be controlled by an individual business. Items from prior website quality literature that pertained to overall site quality (e.g., navigation, use of SSL, search features, etc.) were not included because these quality attributes are controlled by online auction service providers, not the individual auction business, and thus are not relevant when comparing businesses using the same auction provider. The questions used in the survey are presented in Appendix B.

Key descriptive statistics from the e-image survey validation are summarized in Appendix C. Appendix C includes results of a paired sample t-test, which found subjects perceived the positive e-image to have significantly higher quality (p < 0.001) for all of the questions except for the presence of contact information. This is likely because, although the positive e-image listing included the business's e-mail address and the negative e-image listing did not, all businesses can be contacted through eBay and this contact information is found on every auction listing. Overall, the subjects perceived a significant difference between the positive e-image style and the negative e-image style, indicating the two listing styles could be used to assess the impact of e-image on willingness to transact and price premiums.

## Experiment Design

The empirical study utilized data obtained from actual transactions conducted at the eBay auction site over the course of one year. The CollegiateSales online auction business sold items using the positive e-image listing style and the NotAPro2003 online auction business sold items using the negative e-image listing style, as shown in Appendix A. Both auction businesses were started on the same day at eBay. All attributes of the businesses and of the auctions were kept identical over the course of the experiment except for the e-image. This includes the starting price of the auction, the availability of other products for sale at the time of the auction (identical for matched pairs), the relative position of the listings in the search results (auctions were timed so that the listings would be next to each other but the position within the search results would alternate from trial to trail), the duration of the auctions (7 days), and the closing day and time of the auctions (kept the same for the matching pairs of products). Both businesses had reputation scores that started at 0 and increased to 39 by the end of the experiment. There was some slight variation in the user reputation scores for the two businesses during the study. This variation was due to timing of the feedback left by the actual buyers; however, throughout the study, the business ratings were less than 40 (indicative of a new/inexperienced business), both businesses had 100 percent positive feedback, and the feedback comments were very similar. A paired sample t-test performed for the feedback ratings shows that the variation between the ratings for the two businesses was not significant (p = 0.320).

The products sold as a part of the study ranged in price from \$1.99 to \$66.00. The products sold included new and used cinematic DVDs, new JumpDrives, and used Zip drives. Ninety auctions were conducted over the course of one year (45 per selling identity). Sales of identical item pairs were spaced at least 7 days apart so as not to reduce the price received by saturating the market. Starting prices for items sold by the two businesses were identical and were set based on the current market for the item. At the conclusion of each auction the closing price, number of bids received, number of bidders, timing of the first bid, and reputation score were recorded. Six of the auctions conducted did not result in a sale (three auctions each for both businesses). All of the auctions that did not result in a sale were for the same product (an “Austin Powers Goldmember” DVD). These no-sales appeared to be due to declining demand for this product (many copies of this DVD from other businesses also went unsold) as opposed to being due to consumer perceptions of the businesses. Following the unsuccessful auctions, the products were listed again and sold at a lower price.

One consequence of using actual transaction data is that it makes it impossible to directly measure the consumers' "intention to transact" with the different e-image businesses as had been done in prior survey-based studies (e.g., Ranganathan and Ganapathy 2002). Instead, online auction data was examined to determine if buyers demonstrated an increased willingness to transact based on their bidding behavior. Willingness to transact was inferred based on the time from the beginning of the auction to the receipt of the first bid, the total number of bidders participating in an auction, and the total number of bids received. The timing of the first bid shows increased willingness to transact since at this time both auctions have identical auction characteristics (except for the listing quality). The number of bidders and the number of bids provide an indication of how many bidders choose to transact with a business and how motivated they are to buy from them, also indicating they intend to transact. In research examining online auction reputation scores, price premiums have been measured as the difference between the price received by an individual seller and the average price received by multiple businesses (with different reputation scores) for a matching product (Ba and Pavlou 2002). For the purposes of this research, price premiums are the monetary amount a business with a positive e-image receives above a matching business with a negative e-image selling an identical product at the same time.

## Results

A repeated-measures ANOVA was used to determine if there were significant differences between the dependent measures both within and across the two different online auction e-images. $^{5}$ Even though the consumers bidding at both auctions were not necessarily identical, the repeated measures analysis of variance was appropriate because this research involved a matched sample measured across two conditions. In a repeated-measure ANOVA for a matched sample, measurements across treatment conditions are treated like repeated measures (Stevens 1996). Roy's largest root statistic was calculated to estimate the effects of e-image, new/used product group, and the interaction of e-image $\times$ new/used product group on closing price, first bid time, number of bidders, and number of bids. The test statistic shows significant main effects for e-image ( $p < 0.001$ ) and for new/used product group ( $p < 0.015$ ). In addition, it also indicates that there is significant interaction effect between e-image and new/used product group ( $p < 0.013$ ).

Evaluation of the individual dependent variables show that e-image had a significant effect on the timing of the first bid $p < 0.001$ with positive e-image businesses receiving their first bid 1.26 days sooner than negative e-image businesses (on average). Willingness to transact was also evaluated using the number of bidders and the quantity of bids received for each e-image type. These values, shown in Table 2, indicate that e-image had a significant impact on the number of bidders bidding at the auctions, with 21 percent more bidders bidding at the positive e-image business's auctions than at the negative e-image business's auctions ( $p = 0.006$ ). The business with a better e-image received 20 percent more bids than the negative e-image business (mostly for new products); however, this difference was not statistically significant ( $p = 0.113$ ). The fact that the positive e-image business receive first bids significantly sooner than the negative e-image business (when starting prices for both businesses are identical) and more bidders bid at the positive e-image auctions clearly indicate that consumers have an increased willingness to transact with businesses with positive e-images, supporting hypothesis 1.

E-image had a significant main effect on closing price for this analysis ( $p = 0.012$ ) with the positive e-image business receiving an average closing price of \$17.19 and the negative e-image business receiving an average closing price of \$14.62 (see Table 3). This produced an average price premium of 17.61 percent, supporting hypothesis 2.

It is interesting to note that the interaction between e-image and new/used product group was not significant with respect to the timing of the first bid $p = 0.894$ , the number of bidders bidding at the auction $p = 0.249$ , and the total number of bids received $p = 0.117$ . This indicates that consumers prefer purchasing from positive e-image businesses regardless of the perceived product risk, causing us to reject hypothesis 3a. However, there was a significant interaction effect between e-image and new/used product type with respect to closing prices $p = 0.007$ . Table 3 summarizes the mean prices received by the different e-image businesses by new versus used product type. It shows that when the product being sold had a higher risk of not working (used computer products and DVDs) the positive e-image businesses receive 38.69 percent more than the negative e-image businesses. However, for new products, where the risk of product malfunction was lower, the price premium for the positive e-image business was only 2.67 percent. This indicates that positive e-image businesses do receive significantly more when selling their products at online auctions but these price premiums are highly dependent on product risk, supporting hypothesis 3b.

While empirical evidence from real world practice has been gained to support three of the four research hypotheses, some limitations of the research design should be kept in mind. One limitation of the study is that the listings were constructed to be maximally different as opposed to providing the complete range of e-images currently seen on eBay.

A second limitation is that the experiment does not allow us to distinguish the effects attributable to the various elements of e-image: choice of business names versus auction listing quality. Previous research suggests that increased willingness to transact and price premiums could be due to the increased website quality (e.g., Park et al. 2004; Ranganathan and Ganapathy 2002). However, the choice of a reputable sounding business name may create a reputation effect that leads the buyer to believe that the listing is for an existing business that would already know how to conduct transactions effectively. Thus, any or all of the e-image attributes varied in this study could have been responsible for the signal(s) that generated the willingness to transact and price premiums. However, separating these effects would have required a more complex experiment with a larger number of listing attribute/business name combinations than was feasible in this initial study.

Finally, this study examined the impact of e-image for a few types of items, external drives and cinematic DVDs, which may all be considered commodity items. Research has indicated that bidder strategies differ between commodity and collectible online auction items (Kazumori and McMillan 2003; Strader and Ramaswami 2002) and consequently the needed information cues and expectation of professionalism may also differ. However, a review of collectible auction listings on eBay shows a wide range of e-images including differences in listing styles, business identity names, description information, and policy information. This suggests e-image and the corresponding willingness to transact and price premiums are likely to be issues for this type of auction item as well, representing an opportunity for future research.

## Discussion and Conclusions

The goal of this study is to better understand the impact e-image, specifically business user name choice and listing quality, has on willingness to transact and price premiums for online auction retailers. Our hypotheses are largely supported and suggest that e-image has a significant impact in the online auction domain. Consistent with signaling theory (Spence 1973), the results indicate that increasing the positive perception of a business's e-image contributes to an increased willingness to transact with these online auction businesses as inferred from the timing of the starting bid and the number of unique bidders bidding at the auctions. A positive e-image also results in price premiums especially for used products. This suggests that a business's e-image signals potential consumers that the business also has the other abilities necessary to be a successful online auction business. This has important implications for businesses in the online environment, which must have the specific abilities to complete transactions successfully (e.g., process payments, package the item appropriately, and ship in a timely fashion).

A second finding that product risk (e.g., new versus used products) does not moderate the relationship between e-image and willingness to transact demonstrates that consumers have a clear preference for purchasing from positive e-image businesses even when the product risk was very low (a new DVD or new USB drive). One potential reason for this is that there are other risks associated with purchasing from an unknown online auction business, including risks associated with the location, size, and quality of the businesses selling goods at auction. Online auction sites do nothing to screen new businesses, only requiring an e-mail address and a credit card to set up a business identity. The only information potential customers have about the business is the information provided in the auction listing and feedback posted by prior online auction buyers. Thus, the additional information provided by positive e-image businesses helps mitigate these business quality risks, creating an increased willingness to transact that did not diminish regardless of the product being sold.

While willingness to transact is not changed by product risk, the price premiums realized by positive e-image businesses is. The difference between the price premium for new products (2.67 percent) and for used products (38.69 percent) shows that, although buyers prefer transacting with the positive e-image business, they are not willing to pay significantly more if the risk associated with the product is very low. This is surprising since prior research has found that the greatest risk consumers face at online auctions is that the business will not deliver the product (36.2 percent of complaints) and not that the product will not meet their quality expectations (30.6 percent of complaints) (Gregg and Scott 2006). It is possible that consumers on auction sites have different risk profiles. Consumers willing to assume greater risks still bid on higher risk products but expect a low price in order to compensate for those risks. On the other hand, the lower risk consumers will only bid at high-quality sites. However, this is highly speculative and additional research needs to be conducted to see how variations in consumer risk profiles affect willingness to transact and price premiums.

## Implications and Future Research

A primary contribution of this research is that it demonstrates the ability to use eBay as an experimental laboratory for testing hypotheses about purchasing behavior online. Virtually all published studies on consumer purchase behavior have used purchase intention as a surrogate for actual purchase behavior. While this strategy is efficient because intention data are easier to collect, intentions have been shown to be a poor predictor of actual behavior (Young et al. 1998). As researchers attempt to make their research more relevant to practitioners, they need to focus on frames of reference that are meaningful to practitioners (Benbasat and Zmud 1999). Thus, it may not be enough to measure purchase intention; it may be necessary to measure actual purchase behavior. eBay and other online marketplaces provide researchers with platforms that can be used to demonstrate the real-world value in dollars of alternative e-image factors such as website designs, product attributes, and information content to practitioners. The ability to conduct controlled real-world experiments using the large pool of online consumers as subjects could potentially be a great asset to researchers wishing to avoid the potential biases of laboratory or survey research and the limitations of secondary data studies.

This research also contributes to our understanding of the e-image aspects of website quality and identity selection. It suggests that e-image can relay information about unobservable business quality in an environment where there is uncertainty about the products and services a business will provide. Interestingly, although eBay has a fee-based template service, as of spring 2007, many eBay businesses continue to use unprofessional sounding names and/or a very low quality listing style, $^{6}$ which is associated with negative e-images. The finding of this study suggests that it is important for online auction businesses to spend the time and resources necessary to present themselves and their products using positive e-images. All online auction businesses have the potential to improve their e-image (e.g., by developing high-quality auction listings) and reap the benefits of the increased quality signal it generates. However, additional research is needed to determine the reliability of these signals and to see if these signals continue to be effective in environments where more information is available about the business (e.g., the businesses have a high eBay reputation score or are established businesses).

Prior research has found contradictory information regarding the reliability of website auction listing quality as a signal of business quality. MacInnes et al. (2005) found that the amount of information an online auction listing contained about the product was not a significant predictor of whether or not there will be a transaction dispute. Future research could be undertaken to assess which specific e-image factors or combination of factors are the best predictors of online auction success and how these factors interact. These e-image factors should include improving the aesthetics of the auction listing, choosing a professional company name, providing detailed product descriptions or product benefits or warnings, providing product reviews, and including warranty information or a return/refund/exchange policy.

While only two maximally different e-images were evaluated in this experiment, actual online auction businesses have e-images spanning the entire continuum. Future research could also help determine how willingness to transact and price premiums are affected throughout this continuum by presenting mixtures of user names and different listing quality styles to produce multiple e-images from very positive through intermediate (slightly positive or slightly negative) to very negative. Additionally, reputation scores and comments in the presented research were consistent with those of a new business. Future experiments that allowed website quality and reputation to be varied simultaneously would help researchers answer which e-image factor is more likely to produce price premiums. Post-buying surveys could also be sent to consumers to determine which e-image factors they consider most important when making their buying decisions.

This research also has implications for those studying pricing at online auctions. There have been a number of studies investigating online auction purchasing behavior. Prior research has found that a variety of online auction characteristics do influence purchase prices at online auctions (Bapna et al. 2001, 2003; Hahn 2001; Kauffman and Wood 2006; Klemperer 2002). These characteristics include the availability of a specific item at competing auctions, the bid increment, and the inclusion of pictures in the auction listing. Previous research on online auctions and price premiums has also focused on the use of reputation systems for building trust in online auction businesses and to generate price premiums. While a business's online reputation from a reputation system is an aspect of their e-image, these studies have been inconclusive regarding price premiums associated with reputation, with some research indicating that reputation systems do affect price premiums (e.g., Ba and Pavlou 2002; Lee et al. 2000; Ottoway et al. 2003; Standifird 2001, 2002) and other research indicating they do not (e.g., Gilkeson and Reynolds 2003; Kauffman and Wood 2000; Resnick and Zeckhauser 2002). None of the prior studies examining pricing at online auctions controlled for the relative ranking of the business's e-image specifically through the quality of the auction listing and user name. One implication of our research is that studies examining pricing at online auctions will need to account for the e-image factors of website quality and user name selection.

Finally, while these results are specific to the online auction domain, it is possible that they can be extrapolated to more traditional e-commerce businesses. To date there has been limited research on which website design and information content attributes of an online business's e-image contribute the most to online profitability. One way to address this deficit would be to conduct experiments to determine which e-image attributes are the most important to all businesses and which attributes are important in specific domains (e.g., including information on ingredients might be very important to food businesses and emphasizing data security privacy might be important for an information aggregation service). The experimental methodology applied in this paper could be extended to other e-marketplaces (e.g., Amazon's zShops) to examine the relative importance of e-image on website types and determine whether websites with higher perceived quality can charge more than sites with lower quality.
