Online Information Quality and Consumer Satisfaction: The Moderating Roles of Contextual Factors - A Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

A meta-analysis involving 42 articles was carried out to study the existence of potential contextual moderators on the relationship between information quality (IQ) and consumer satisfaction in the online environment. A moderator analysis involving website type (retail or eservice), sample characteristics (respondent type, and respondent origin) as well as the IQ categories used (representational, and non-representational) in articles revealed that while website type and IQ categories moderate the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction, sample characteristics does not. Implications of the results for researchers and practitioners, as well as future directions for this research are presented.

Keywords: Online information quality; online satisfaction; meta-analysis; website type; sample characteristics; IQ categories

#

## 1. Introduction

Today, the Internet has become integrated into our lives as an important tool for information and communication [29,41]. The amount of online information has been rapidly increasing. For example, it rose from 1.2 zettabytes (1.3 trillion gigabytes) in 2010 to 1.8 zettabytes in 2011, growing by a factor of 9 in just five years [32]. The plethora of information available online raises concerns regarding its quality [81, 83, 108]. Many researchers [e.g., 83,108] have raised issues regarding the quality of online information as there are no general standards for posting online information, and as such, information may be easily plagiarized, altered, or created under inaccurate pretenses [82,83,108,79].

According to the Delone and McLean (D&M) Information Systems (IS) success model, information quality is one of the main factors impacting consumer satisfaction. Information Quality (IQ) in an online environment defined as “users’ perception of the quality of information presented on a Web site” by McKinney [80], has been investigated extensively in prior research [e.g., 9,63,4,5]. In the context of the online environment, high IQ is likely to help users to research and compare online products (e.g., books, music CDs, electronic goods) and services (e.g., health information, consumer banking and finance), and to consequently be able to make better online decisions. Thus, perceiving high online IQ increases consumers’ satisfaction and may help them to perceive websites as being more useful in accomplishing their tasks [22, 71, 94].

Some studies have argued for the significant moderating impacts of contextual factors on the relationships among constructs of the D&M IS success model [44]. Contextual moderators can make our understanding of the relationship between two variables more context sensitive [122]. Hong et al. [44] suggest that using the meta-analysis method helps to validate the relationships between variables and discover contextual factors that moderate those relationships across studies.

There are different contextual factors which could moderate the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction. For example, IQ may play a more important role in shaping online satisfaction for consumers who are searching for e-service information compared to those who are searching for product information [107]. This is due to the fact that in the case of eservices (e.g., health information), the consumer needs are the “information” itself, while in the case of products, the information is just a means for the consumer to obtain the right product, where the “product” is the main need of the consumer [33].

Consumer characteristics may also influence the relationship between perceived online IQ and user satisfaction. For example, as consumers from different countries have different values and cultures, they might exhibit differences in evaluating and percepting online IQ and its impacts [18]. In addition, in a meta-analysis conducted by Schepers and Wetzels [110] on the technology acceptance model, they found contradictory results between student samples as opposed to general public samples. Thus, sample characteristics, including respondent type (e.g., students vs. general public) and respondent origin (e.g., western vs. eastern) may affect the significance of the relation between perceived online IQ and user satisfaction.

#

Wang and Strong [121] developed a framework conceptualizing the underlying aspects of data quality (a concept similar to IQ [24]) that are important to consumers. The framework consists of four data quality categories, including intrinsic, contextual, representational, and accessibility data quality, and the underlying dimensions for each category. The importance of different IQ categories as predictors of consumer satisfaction may vary across different website types. For example, in the case of e-service websites (e.g., e-health), as consumer needs are the “information” itself [33], Stvilia et al. [117] found that intrinsic IQ dimensions such as accuracy and reliability of information are the most important IQ dimensions in increasing consumers’ online satisfaction. However, in the case of retail websites, as consumer needs are not the “information” itself [33], the representational dimensions such as clarity, consistency and conciseness of information may play a more critical role in increasing consumers’ online experience satisfaction in retail websites compared to e-service websites.

In their 2009 article, [93] conducted a meta-analysis to determine whether the IS success model had been validated by research studies in the IS literature. In their study, they evaluated the relationships in the D&M model including the relationship between IQ and satisfaction and found strong support for this positive relationship. However, this was not done for studies focusing specifically on the online environment and they did not explore the potential moderators between perceived IQ and satisfaction. There are differences in the nature of offline and online information. For example, compared to the offline information, there are relatively very large and quickly expanding online information [13]. In addition, due to the variety of information formats including video and audio in the online environment [38], information richness and interactivity is higher compared to the offline environment. Further, Seller Reputation Theory argues that in the online environment, to make additional profit, vendors are not willing to disclose bad product information unless the cost of sending false information is higher than the loss of reputation costs they will experience from providing misleading information [51,25]. Although it could be argued that this is also true in the offline environment, the concern is higher in the online environment due to the lack of direct physical interaction for customers with products. Thus, due to these differences in the concept and nature of offline and online information, there are higher potential concerns associated with online IQ [83]. In addition, in many instances, no one has to review the online information before it is made available to the general public, and individuals have to rely more on themselves in making judgments about the quality of online information [99]. Thus, the difficulty experienced by consumers in assessing online IQ is higher compared to the offline IQ. Further, as many studies have considered the relationship between online IQ and satisfaction, it is both important and timely to evaluate the possible contextual factors that moderate the relationship between these two important variables in an online context.

While the many studies that examined the relationship between online IQ and consumer satisfaction [e.g., 22,37,36,76] have consistently reported positive correlations between these two variables, a wide variance in the correlations reported is present across studies, ranging from as low as 0.13 [28] to as high as 0.82 [26]. Such inconsistency in research findings motivates, in part, this meta-analysis study to explore the potential influential moderators on the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction. Examining the role of such moderators is important to ascertain the role they may play in shaping this relationship.

#

In this study, we address the above research gaps by conducting a meta-analysis of previous studies that considered the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction while investigating the roles of different contextual factors including website type, sample characteristics, and IQ categories in moderating this relationship. A meta-analysis is appropriate for such an investigation because the many studies included in our data set have explored the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction while focusing on different types of websites, using different sample characteristics, and considering different IQ categories. Such a study also responds to Petter and Mclean’s [93] call for meta-analyses examining the important moderating factors of the relationship between perceived IQ and consumer satisfaction in the D&M IS success model. Finally, such an evaluation can help IS researchers identify possible research opportunities in this area, and conduct future studies to address them.

The objective of this paper is to address the following research questions: Among the empirical studies that have examined the influence of online IQ on customer satisfaction: (1) Does the website type moderate the above relationship? (2) Does the sample characteristics moderate their relationship?, and (3) Does the IQ categories considered in articles moderate their relationship? To answer these questions, this paper develops a theoretical model that integrates the influence of website type, sample characteristics, and IQ categories as moderators for the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction and tests the model through a combination of meta-analysis and weighted least squares regression procedures.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: First, the theoretical background is explained briefly. Then, a theoretical model is developed and hypotheses are presented. The methodology is then explained, followed by the analysis. Finally, the results of the study are presented, followed by a discussion of the results, implications for researchers and practitioners and a brief conclusion.

## 2. Theoretical Background

## 2.1. DeLone and McLean IS Success Model

In 1992, [20] identified the main variables of IS success in their well-known IS success model. Numerous extensions/modifications have been suggested to the D&M model since its publication. In response, in a 2003 article, DeLone and McLean reviewed the empirical studies related to their model and revised their original model. The updated D&M model has been broadly used by IS researchers in understanding the dimensions of IS success [92]. The model has six variables that contribute to IS success: information quality, service quality, system quality, satisfaction, net benefit, and intention to use. DeLone and McLean [22] argued that their updated IS success model could be adapted to the assessment challenges of the new e-Commerce environment. In D&M model, IQ has been found to be an important predictor of satisfaction [111, 22]. Petter et al. [94] suggest that generating high IQ is the foundation of good decision making and positive outcomes, and it is the main purpose of any IS.

## 2.2. Contextual Impact on the IQ-Satisfaction Relationship

John [52] suggests that contextualization helps researchers to better assess the applicability of their findings and eventually enhance the relevance of their research to practice. Whetten [122] defined context as “the set of factors surrounding a phenomenon that exert some direct or indirect influence on it—also characterized as explanatory factors associated with higher levels of analysis than those expressly under investigation” (p. 31). Bamberger [7] argues that context is a sensitizing device that helps us be more aware of the potential situational conditions to theories. According to Hong et al. [44], meta-analyses methods play an important role in validating the relationships between variables and discovering contextual factors that moderate those relationships across studies. Petter et al. [94] suggest that there are different contextual factors which could impact on the perceptions of IQ and moderate the relationships between this variable and other variables in the D&M model. However, they argue that only a few studies have examined the impact of contextual factors as moderators on the relationship between perceived IQ and other factors in the model. Petter et al. [94] outline different contextual factors that impact perceived IQ such as user characteristic (e.g., user expectations), organizational characteristics (e.g., IT infrastructure), and task characteristics (e.g., task difficulty, task compatibility). In this paper, we will focus on the moderating impact of website type as an organizational characteristic on the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction. Moreover, we will also evaluate the moderating role of sample characteristics, including respondent type (student vs. general public) as well as respondent origin (western vs. eastern) as user characteristics on the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction. Likewise, as the task characteristics in retail versus e-service websites are different [87;33], we will also focus on the IQ categories to understand if different types of websites need to focus on different IQ categories to increase consumer online satisfaction. We considered these characteristics as general classes of contextual factors that may have potential moderating impacts on the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction.

## 3.1. Online IQ and Consumer Satisfaction

Satisfaction is defined as “the sum of one’s feelings or attitudes [in a given situation] towards a variety of factors affecting that situation” [6]. Individual satisfaction is an established common measure of the success or effectiveness of IS in general [e.g., 20, 80, 96,109,94]. Within an online context, satisfaction is defined as an affective state indicating an emotional reaction to the user’s website experience [116, 90, 80, 74, 75]. Users visit a website if they perceive it will help them in accomplishing a task. However, their overall satisfaction with the website is the main factor that will make them transact with it and return to it in the future [54,56,55,68,53,61]. In the online environment, consumer satisfaction is important for establishing long-term customer relationships and plays a critical role in sustaining profitability and determining overall website success [91,128, 118, 12, 34].

IQ has been investigated extensively in prior research [5]. However, there is not a general definition of IQ [84]. Taylor [120] defined IQ as the value the information has in relation to the purposes at hand. Hilligoss and Rieh [43] underline the individuals’ needs, viewing IQ as the users’ “subjective judgment of goodness and usefulness of information” (p. 1469). Wang and Strong [121] defined IQ as “fitness for use,” where quality depends on the fitness of the information to a specific use or a judgment of value. Eppler [27] defined IQ as the degree to which the information meets the expectations of the user and the degree to which the information meets the requirements of the particular activity in which the user is engaged.

IQ is a major factor when investigating IS success and has been found to be an important predictor of satisfaction [111,112,113,21]. The importance of IQ has also been validated in ecommerce settings [80,22]. According to Aladwani and Palvia [2], Aladwani [3], and Lim and Ting [72], a website must contain clear, useful, current, and accurate information in order to create favorable consumers’ attitudes and intentions. In the context of the online environment, IQ refers to consumers’ assessment of the online products or e-services information dimensions such as accuracy, format, completeness, and whether it is up to date [89,60]. McKinney et al. [80] defined online IQ as ‘‘users’ perception of the quality of information presented on a Web site.’’ Likewise, Guo et al. [37] defined online IQ as “the usefulness of the available information about an attribute of a product in helping a decision maker evaluate the product.”

As there are no general standards for posting online information [79], many researchers [e.g., 83,108] argue that issues concerning the quality of online information are being raised. Thus, in the context of the online environment, perceiving high online IQ may significantly affect consumers’ satisfaction. Kim and Lennon [59] suggest that online IQ helps consumers evaluate

products, improve their satisfaction, and make more informed decisions with their online shopping experience. In addition, Bliemel and Hassanein [11] show that the quality of the contents of health websites positively impacts consumer satisfaction with IQ which is shown to in turn impact consumers’ overall satisfaction. Given these findings and the fact that the basic objective of websites is to act as a medium for the exchange of information, perceived online IQ is expected to significantly impact consumer satisfaction [109]. This relation has been validated extensively in the literature, and thus this article is focused on exploring the impact of different contextual factors as potential moderators on the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction, explained below.

## 3.2. The Moderating Role of Website Type on the IQ-Satisfaction Relationship

Researchers have called for studies to examine the moderating effect of website type in understanding the impact of website design features on customer satisfaction [87]. For example, Liang et al. [69] suggest that different relationships may exist in the business models of different website types. Therefore, they note that researchers must be careful when the results of their studies are generalized and applied to other domains. Likewise, Agarwal and Venkatesh [1] found that there may be an overall domain effect related to the quality of the website.

There are different classifications for websites. For example, Mithas et al. [87] classified websites into business domains on the basis of ownership (commercial or government), website offering (i.e., those that sell services versus goods), and the degree to which websites deliver information only or support commercial transactions (information versus transaction oriented sites). In another study, Kim et al. [57] classified websites in terms of distinct businesses, such as search portals, virtual malls, stock brokerages, and online games. They considered websites that belong to these domains to be different from one another and conducted a separate analysis for each domain. They suggest that the importance of design features may vary across different domains, which is consistent with Ivory and Sinha [50], who believe that important design features differ among different internet businesses.

As noted previously, in the context of the online environment, high IQ is likely to help consumers to assess and compare e-services (e.g., health information, consumer banking and finance) and online products (e.g., music CDs, electronic goods). Therefore, publications that focus on understanding the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer online experience satisfaction often focus on either retail or e-service websites. As such, in this paper, websites are classified based on what they offer (i.e., products or services).

Online retail and e-service websites differ on several parameters because of the nature of their varied purposes [85]. Moreover, the nature of consumer experiences also varies significantly across these two types of websites [87]. Research has shown that the consumer view of IQ differs across services and products [95,86,87]. Similarly, Zhang and Von Dran [129] indicate that in their study related to the consumer perceptions of website quality, consumers’ expectations across different websites types were significantly different. They report that IQ was the most important factor for users of financial and health service websites. In addition, prior research on consumer online satisfaction has found considerable differences between services and goods industries [31,86,87]. Schaupp et al. [109] suggest that different factors within the

#

D&M model impact consumer satisfaction differently across different website types. They suggest that researchers should be aware of the complex nature of website success in their investigations and if their study is focused on only one type of websites, they should clearly indicate the limitations of the generalizability of their results. They also note that researchers should take a broader perspective of what websites offer and conduct their investigations in various website contexts. They believe that prior research has missed some of the key paths to website success by not considering different website contexts.

As discussed earlier, IQ is an important influencer of consumer satisfaction in online environments for both retail [118,73,15,30] and e-service [11,101,14,102,64] websites. However, in the case of e-services like health information, the consumer needs are the “information” itself, while in the case of products, the information is just a means for the consumer to obtain the product, where the “product” is the main need of the consumer. Therefore, while perceived online IQ is still important for the satisfaction of consumers of retail websites, other factors such as on-time delivery, shipping and handling, and ease of ordering may also play a role in shaping their satisfaction [97]. Thus, online IQ may play a more important role for consumers who are searching for e-service information compared to those who are searching for product information [87,33]. Therefore, the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction may be stronger when consumers use e-services website than when they use retail websites. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H1: Website type moderates the relationship between perceived online information quality and consumer satisfaction such that it would be stronger for e-service websites than retail websites.

## 3.3. The Moderating Role of Sample Characteristics on the IQ-Satisfaction Relationship

As discussed above, sample characteristics (i.e., respondent type and respondent origin) is another contextual moderator which may affect the significance of the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction.

## Respondent Type & Respondent Origin

In a recent meta-analysis, Wu et al. [126] argued that, in the context of e-commerce, when a large number of studies use student samples in a research domain, researchers can perform a meta-analysis to compare studies with different respondent types to evaluate if there is a significant difference in their results. Although studies that use students to evaluate research hypotheses provide valuable testing, some researchers believe that students may have different concerns as well as different buying behaviors than general public [8]. For example, Kim and Stoel [58] argue that compared to the student samples who are not familiar or interested in rating shopping websites, nonstudent samples are considered as better respondents to evaluate the impact of perceived online IQ on their satisfaction. Thus, based on the above discussion, the relationship between perceived online IQ and satisfaction may be stronger in studies using general public samples as they may take the task more seriously and perceive online IQ as a more important factor in increasing their satisfaction while shopping online.

#

Cyr [18] argue that consumers from different countries may have different values, and cultures, which might result in differences in their evaluations of online IQ and its impacts. They conducted a study to compare user perception of the quality of online information in Canada, the United States, Japan, and Germany and found some differences between the United States, Germany, and Canada (western countries), but more significant differences between these countries and Japan (eastern country) on consumers’ perception of online IQ. Likewise, Cyr [19] argued that Chinese respondents were more concerned than Canadians with online IQ. Particularly, they found that Canadians received lower scores on Hofstede’s scale for uncertainty avoidance than Chinese. Thus, as Chinese consumers prefer to avoid risk when shopping on the Internet, they are more concerned about the quality of online IQ compared to Canadian customers.

Thus, we hypothesize that:

H2a: Respondent type moderates the effect of perceived online IQ on consumer satisfaction such that the effect would be significantly stronger for studies that use general public respondents compared to studies that use student respondents.

H2b: Respondent origin moderates the effect of perceived online IQ on consumer satisfaction such that the effect would be significantly stronger for studies that use eastern countries respondents compared to studies that use western countries respondents.

## 3.4 The Moderating Role of IQ Categories and Website Type on the IQ-Satisfaction Relationship

Prior research has investigated IQ extensively where it has been measured by different dimensions [121,66,100,43,5]. In this study, we used Wang and Strong’s [121] framework which uses a taxonomy of IQ dimensions grouped into four categories: intrinsic, contextual, representational, and accessibility to identify the online IQ categories used in the coded articles to evaluate online IQ. The dimensions and definitions of these categories are shown in Table 1 below.

As shown in Table 1, intrinsic IQ category denotes that information should have innate correctness regardless of the context in which it is being used [46]. The main dimensions of intrinsic IQ category are believability, accuracy, objectivity, and reputation [e.g., 121,117,46].

Contextual IQ category highlights that IQ must be considered within the context of the task at hand [117]. The main dimensions of the contextual IQ category are value-added, relevancy, timeliness, completeness, and appropriate amount of data [121,46].

Representational IQ includes aspects related to the format of the information [121]. The main dimensions of the representational IQ category are interpretability, ease of understanding, representational consistency, and concise representation [121,46].

Accessibility IQ refers to the ease with which the information sought is obtained [46]. The main dimensions of the accessibility IQ category are accessibility, and access security [117]. In the recent IS literature, only a few articles used the IQ accessibility category to assess perceived online IQ. This may be due to the fact that researchers may believe that the availability of online information is no longer a concern for people [8] and that other IQ categories may play a more critical role in forming individual perceptions of online IQ.

Consumers who are using different types of websites may place emphasis only on some of the IQ categories to evaluate the quality of online information. For instance, consumers who are searching through retail websites for product-related information may put emphasis on different dimensions of IQ compared to those who are looking at e-service websites focusing on finding service-related information. In the case of e-services, as discussed earlier, the consumer needs are the “information” itself, therefore, intrinsic and contextual IQ dimensions such as accuracy, completeness, and reliability of information may be more important for consumers than representational IQ dimensions which relate mainly to the formatting of the information [117,33]. Many researchers, such as Montoya-Weiss et al. [88], Koo et al. [64], and Hsu et al. [45] found that perceiving a high level of contextual and intrinsic IQ categories influences consumer satisfaction while using e-services websites.

In the case of retail websites, on the other hand, the “product” is the main need of the consumer and, as such, the information is just a means for the consumer to obtain the right product [33]. Thus, in the case of retail websites, not only do the intrinsic and contextual IQ dimensions impact consumer satisfaction, but the representational IQ dimensions and the way products and associated information are presented also play a significant role in increasing consumer satisfaction. Blanco et al. [10] suggested that the representational IQ category plays an important role in increasing consumers’ satisfaction when they are looking for product-related information (e.g., TV information). Studies such as Liu et al. [76] and Fang et al. [30] found that perceiving a high level of representational IQ dimensions along with other IQ categories significantly influence consumers’ satisfaction and their shopping decisions in the context of retail websites.

Therefore, for consumers looking for product-related information on retail websites, the relationship between online IQ and consumer satisfaction may become stronger when they perceive high representational IQ dimensions. On the other hand, for consumers looking for service-related information, the relationship between online IQ and consumer satisfaction is not

#

expected to change significantly when they perceive high representational IQ dimensions. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H3a: IQ categories and website type moderate the effect of perceived online IQ on consumer satisfaction, such that the effect would be significantly stronger for studies that focus on retail websites and consider the representational dimensions of IQ compared to studies that focus on retail websites but do not consider the representational dimensions of IQ.

H3b: IQ categories and website type do not moderate the effect of perceived online IQ on consumer satisfaction, such that the effect would not be significantly stronger for studies that focus on e-service websites and consider the representational dimensions of IQ compared to studies that focus on e-service websites but do not consider the representational dimensions of IQ.

## 4. Methodology

In this paper, a meta-analysis approach is used to combine previous studies in order to gather a clear explanation of the relationship between perception of online IQ and consumer online experience satisfaction by exploring the moderating effects of different contextual factors including website type, sample characteristics and IQ categories on the above relationship. Meta-analysis is a statistical method used to synthesize the literature and provides a means to combine the results of many empirical studies [35,47,62,93]. Previous studies suggest that metaanalysis is not only a useful tool for research synthesis [49,67,78], but also an effective instrument for testing research hypotheses [23,114,106,126]. According to Higgins & Green [42], meta-analyses that combine several subgroups and meta-analyses within subgroups are both valid approaches to test hypotheses.

## 4.1. Sample

In order to minimize the potential of biasing our data, the sample studies for this meta-analysis were selected from all possible sources, including journals and conference proceedings. Searches were conducted using databases such as EBSCO, Proquest, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar. Search terms included “online information”, “online/website information quality”, “online/website customer satisfaction”, “online/website content quality”, “online/website product information”, “e-service information”, and “DeLone & McLean model”. We also carefully considered the measurement of both online IQ and satisfaction in each paper considered. This was important because both of these constructs could be defined and measured differently in different studies [93]. Therefore, when coding a study, we used the list of possible measures for perceived IQ provided by DeLone and McLean [20] and Wang and Strong [121], and possible measures for satisfaction provided by DeLone and McLean [20]. Thus, even if a construct was not named online IQ or satisfaction in an article, it was still used in the meta-analysis when the measure captured the concept of online IQ and consumer satisfaction.

The bibliographies of studies were also reviewed to identify potential sources of data. In total, this extensive literature search yielded 112 potential manuscripts for examination. This comprehensive search strategy maximizes the number of studies and reduces source bias which

#

increases the strength of the meta-analysis [115]. We then used inclusion criteria to examine the studies to be included in our study. Wolfswinkel et al. [124] suggest that researchers evaluate the quality of the studies by establishing criteria for inclusion, using a multiple-rater technique, and evaluating inter-rater reliability. We considered four criteria for inclusion of studies in our analysis, including:

A study should have the relationship of online IQ and consumer satisfaction or any construct that could be justified as measuring the same concept.

• The sample size of the study should be reported.

• The reliability of constructs should be reported.

• The correlation between IQ and satisfaction should be reported.

One of the key assumptions in a meta-analysis is the independence of studies included in the analysis [78,125]. We compared studies by evaluating their statistical data with other included articles to ensure their uniqueness. If two or more studies were based on the same data set, they were considered as one study, and only one was selected.

As recommended by [89], to enhance the reliability of our coding, one of the authors and another IS academic (not involved with this study) separately assessed the selection of the studies. Based on the inclusion criteria, from the 112 relevant studies, only 42 were found to have enough data to be included in the meta-analysis. Disagreements between raters were resolved by adopting a consensus approach. The pool of studies selected based on the criteria explained above and the associated information required for performing this meta-analysis are shown in Appendix A (Table A1).

The studies were broken down into subsets based on the website type, sample characteristics, and IQ categories used. As discussed, these were the most likely moderators that could influence the IQ-Satisfaction relationship in the 42 studies included in this meta-analysis. The 42 articles identified for inclusion in our analysis were then coded based on their website type, sample characteristics, and IQ categories. First, each of the raters separately coded 20 of the 42 articles. We then computed Cohen’s kappa to assess the strength of agreement between the two raters [98]. Cohen’s Kappa provides the researcher with confidence in the ratings obtained, since it offers agreement beyond what could be due to chance [98]. The raters achieved a 0.94 inter-rater reliability on website categories, a 0.92 inter-rater reliability on sample characteristics, and 0.82 inter-rater reliability on IQ categories. As Cohen Kappa’s scores above 0.60 are considered to be representative of substantial agreement between raters [65], one of the raters coded the remaining 22 articles, requesting feedback from the other rater on articles that were difficult to classify.

## 4.2. Analysis

After determining the studies to be included in the meta-analysis and gathering data from each study, summarizing the findings is the next step in a meta-analysis [106,125]. To summarize the studies’ findings, the five-step Hunter and Schmidt’s [47] meta-analysis technique was performed. For this reason, the following five steps were taken. Table B1 presents the detailed results of these steps for the relationship between online IQ and consumer satisfaction.

1) The effect sizes (correlation weights) of the studies were corrected for unreliability using the following formula<sup>1</sup>:

$$
\mathrm{ES} _ {\mathrm{xy}} ^ {\prime} = \mathrm{r} _ {\mathrm{xy}} / \operatorname{sqrt} \left(\mathrm{r} _ {\mathrm{xx}} \mathrm{r} _ {\mathrm{yy}}\right)
$$

2) The resulting corrected effect sizes in the previous step were transformed to standard scores using the following formula<sup>2</sup>:

$$
\mathrm{ES} _ {\mathrm{Zr}} = 0. 5 \mathrm{x} \ln ((1 + \mathrm{r}) / (1 - \mathrm{r}))
$$

3) Based on Hedges and Olkin [40], the optimal weight for the effect sizes in the meta-analysis is the inverse of variance. This weight was transformed to (n-3) for standardized scores of effect sizes in which n is the sample size of the study from which the effect size was calculated. Similar to the procedure for effect sizes, the weights should also be corrected for unreliability using the following formula<sup>3</sup>:

$$
\mathrm {W^ {\prime} = W(r_ {xx})(r_ {yy})}
$$

After correction for unreliability, the weights were used to form the weighted z scores of the effect sizes.

4) The overall weighted mean effect size was calculated by dividing the sum of weighted effect sizes by the sum of the corrected weights, using the following formula<sup>4</sup>:

$$
\overline {{E S _ {z}}} = \frac {\sum (w \times E S _ {z})}{\sum w}
$$

5) Finally, the inverse Zr transformation was utilized to convert the overall weighted mean effect size to its unstandardized form, using the following formula<sup>5</sup>:

$$
\overline {{r}} = \left. (e ^ {2 E S _ {z}} - 1) \right/ (e ^ {2 E S _ {z}} + 1)
$$

We used funnel plots and fail-safe N to examine for publication bias in the samples. In 1984, Light and Pillemer [70] introduced funnel plot as a method of detecting publication bias. Funnel plots are scatter plots of the sample size (vertical axis) against the effect size (horizontal axis) [17]. To determine that there is no publication bias, the resulting figure should illustrate an inverted funnel shape with effect sizes from studies with small sample size scattering widely at

#

the bottom of the figure and the spread narrowing for larger sample size studies. Funnel plots should be considered in conjunction with other tests such as fail-safe N, because publication bias may not be the only reason for problematic funnel plots [106, 125]. Figure 2 shows a funnel plot for the correlation between IQ and satisfaction. The funnel plots did not demonstrate publication bias as a problem. Appendix C shows the funnel plots for the impact of website type, sample characteristics, and IQ categories on the relationship between online IQ and consumer satisfaction. As can be seen, the funnel plots did not demonstrate publication bias as a problem for these relations.

A fail-safe number represents the number of unpublished, nonsignificant studies that would need to be added to a meta-analysis to decrease an overall statistically significant observed result to nonsignificance at a pre-specified level $( \mathtt { p } \le 0 . 0 5$ in this study) [103]. We measured the fail-safe N for the correlation between IQ and satisfaction by using the value corrected for study artifacts<sup>6</sup>. Meta-analytic findings are robust if the fail-safe number exceeds the critical value obtained with Rosenthal’s [105] formula of $5 \ ( \mathrm { k } ) + \ 1 0 .$ , where k is the number of studies. As can be seen in Table 2, the fail-safe N is 280 which exceeds Rosenthal’s [105] critical value [5 (42) + 10 = 220], thus provides confidence in the robustness of this meta-analysis. This shows that at least 280 additional studies with non-significant results would be needed to make the average IQ/satisfaction correlation non-significant. Fail-safe N and the funnel plots show that publication bias is not a significant problem in this study.

The heterogeneity test is used to determine whether the observed effect size is more variable than would be expected from sampling error alone [104,39]. If it is, then there is a strong possibility for the existence of potential moderators in the findings [47]. To measure the heterogeneity, we conducted Q-test, which has a distribution similar to chi-square with k-1 degrees of freedom where k is the number of studies used in the meta-analysis [40,47,103]. The total number of studies in this meta-analysis that had measured the effect size is 42, leaving 41 degrees of freedom resulting in a chi-square value of 56.94 (df= 41 and p= .05). The Q-value of 2460.3 being much higher than the chi-square value indicates that the effect sizes are heterogeneous and that possible moderators exist.

In addition to the Q-test, we also conducted a weighted least squares regression (WLSR) analysis to check for the existence of potential moderators. Hedges and Olkin [40] introduced the WLSR technique to test research hypotheses in meta-analyses. WLSR can be also used to test assess the significance of hypothesized moderators. Unlike nonlinear/linear least squares regression, WLSR incorporates weights into the model-fitting criteria. Assigning a weight to each observation gives each data point its appropriate amount of influence on the final parameter estimates [125]. In this study, WLSR evaluated the slope in a regression model while considering the sample size as the weight, moderators as the independent variable, and the correlations between online IQ and satisfaction as the dependent variable. In the regression analyses, we use correlations corrected for measurement error.

## 6. Discussion

In this paper, a quantitative meta-analytic approach was used to analyze the relationship between online IQ and consumer satisfaction. Having presented the steps through which the effect size for the hypothesized relationship was calculated, we note that there was an abundant amount of heterogeneity among the selected studies. Based on the literature, contextual factors including website type, respondent type, and respondent origin, and IQ categories used in studies were expected to contribute to this heterogeneity.

After conducting a meta-analysis involving 42 research articles from the extant literature, the results of this study show that indeed perceived online IQ has a strong positive influence on consumer satisfaction, confirming the Petter and Mclean’s [93] study results that there is a strong

#

relationship between perceived IQ and consumer satisfaction. This study also concluded that this relation is moderated by the website type. Furthermore, the results of this study show that respondent type and respondent origin considered as sample characteristics used in studies do not have moderating roles on the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction. We believe that this could be due to the fact that as concerns about the quality of online information has significantly increased among individuals in general [108,79], student/general public and western/eastern consumers may all consider online IQ as being critical in increasing their satisfaction in using websites. In addition, the results show that although the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction is moderated by the IQ categories (i.e., representational, non-representational) considered in studies focusing on retail websites, it is not moderated by the IQ categories considered in studies focusing on eservice websites. More specifically, the representation of the information (i.e., representationa category of online IQ) plays a key role in forming online satisfaction for consumers searching through retail websites; while it does not play an important role in increasing satisfaction for consumers who search for information through e-service websites. This indicates that other dimensions such as accuracy, completeness and reliability of information play more critical roles to increase consumer satisfaction in e-service websites.

## 6.1. Contributions and Implications for Researchers and Practitioners

This paper represents the first attempt to examine the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction while taking into account the moderating roles of website type, sample characteristics, and IQ categories used in studies, which has valuable implications for both researchers and practitioners. For researchers, the findings of this study provide new insights on the inconsistent findings relating to the relationship between perceived online IQ and satisfaction. While all studies report positive correlations between IQ and satisfaction, a wide variance in the correlations is present across studies which motivated this meta-analysis and can be explained by the research model developed in this study.

This study also established a base level for researchers to report effect sizes for the relationship between online IQ and satisfaction (i.e., 0.64). Thus, the influence of perceived online IQ on consumer satisfaction has been proven to be strong. In addition, the moderator analyses of the contextual factors revealed important implications for researchers studying this relation. Caution should be taken when using different website types in research studies, due to the moderating effects of this variable on the relation between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction. Therefore, researchers must be careful when designing their studies, interpreting their results and applying them across different website domains. Moreover, the results of this study, interestingly, showed that respondent type as well as respondent origin does not have a moderating role on the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction. Thus, there is not much difference on individuals’ perception of online IQ and its impact on their satisfaction between student and general public samples as well as individuals who live in western and those who live in eastern countries. Hence, although Petter and Mclean [93] suggest that as studies use different respondent types and respondent origins to conduct their investigations, sample characteristics could be a potential moderator for the relationships in IS success model, no significant effect was found for these variables in moderating the relation between online IQ and consumer satisfaction. In addition, the results show that caution should

also be taken when using different website types as well as different IQ categories in research studies, due to the moderating effects of these variables on the relation between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction. This is in support of Petter and McLean [93] argument for using standardization for the measures used in assessing each of the D&M IS success model variables. Therefore, researchers must be careful when designing their studies, interpreting their results and applying them across different website domains.

For practitioners, this study’s results suggest development guidelines for website providers. First, online vendors and website developers should pay particular attention to the quality of information on their website as an important influencer of consumers’ online satisfaction. This appears to be particularly critical in the case of e-service websites compared to retail websites. One possible reason is that for consumers who search for product information, the information is just a means for them to obtain the product where the “product” is the main need for the consumers. However, when consumers search for e-services information, the information itself is the target of the consumer search. While IQ is still important for the satisfaction of customers of retail websites, other factors such as on-time delivery, shipping and handling, and ease of ordering may also play a role in shaping their satisfaction [97]. Second, online retailers and their website developers should pay particular attention to the IQ representational dimensions of online information (e.g., consistency, conciseness, interpretability) as it appears to play an important role in improving consumer satisfaction. On the other hand, e-service website developers should pay more attention to other IQ dimensions such as accuracy, completeness and reliability of information to increase online satisfaction for their consumers.

## 6.2. Limitations and Directions for Future Research

Although we have attempted to gather all the relevant studies that should be included to minimize potential bias, there are still some limitations to this study. First, the rule of thumb for conducting a comprehensive meta-analysis is to include more than 15 studies. This rule was observed in testing the first and second hypotheses. However, in testing the impact of IQ categories and website type together on the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction (the third hypothesis), this rule was not observed due to the number of relevant studies currently available. Thus, including more studies in future meta-analyses in this area would be desirable in order to further validate our third hypothesis. Second, meta-analyses can only be conducted on research that performs quantitative analysis. Thus, qualitative studies cannot be included in the meta-analysis, which decreases the number of studies that can be examined [93]. Third, each of the quantitative studies should report the statistics needed for a meta-analysis (e.g., correlation and sample size) to be included in a meta-analysis [93]. Studies that did not have these data were excluded from this meta-analysis which may affect the results. Finally, for testing the second moderator on the impact of respondent origin on the relationship between online IQ perception and consumer satisfaction, the fact that eastern and western countries may have different languages may have caused a location bias. However, it is important to note that such language differences also exist within eastern and western countries (e.g., French and German under western countries and China and India under eastern countries). The stress here should be placed rather on structural differences between eastern and western cultures (e.g., individualistic western culture versus collectivist eastern culture).

#

This study has shown that there are other potential moderators on the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction, which calls for further research into this area. For example, Yoon [127] argues that consumer online experience impacts the relationship between perceived online IQ and satisfaction. Moreover, Lu and Chiou [77] suggest that consumer demographics (e.g., gender) could impact this relationship. In our study, we did not examine the role of these potential moderators in our model, as we did not have enough articles to test for these moderators.

As the number of published articles covering the above areas increases, future meta-analysis could re-examine/examine the role of the above potential contextual moderating variables on the relationship between online IQ and consumer satisfaction. In addition, future research can also explore the role of potential contextual moderators in the relationship between other independent variables from the D&M IS success model such as perceived online service quality and perceived online system quality, and online consumer satisfaction.

## 7. Conclusion

The main purpose of this study was to analyze the relationship between online IQ and consumer satisfaction and to empirically examine the role of different contextual factors including website type, sample characteristics, and IQ categories as potential moderators on this relationship. Based on a meta-analysis of 42 empirical studies from the extant literature, results of this research confirm that there is indeed a strong and consistent positive relation between IQ and satisfaction in the context of the online environment. In addition, the results of this study show that while website type moderates the effects of perceived online IQ on consumer satisfaction, respondent type as well as respondent origin does not have a moderating role on the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction. The results also show that although the relationship between perceived online IQ and consumer satisfaction is moderated by the IQ categories considered in studies focusing on retail websites, it is not moderated by the IQ categories considered in studies focusing on e-services websites.

Considering the vast quantity of online information and the dependence of consumers on such information in making everyday decisions, understanding how IQ relates to consumer satisfaction and how this relation varies by context is of paramount importance for web designers and online vendors to attract and retain customers. Findings from this study represent important steps in that direction.
