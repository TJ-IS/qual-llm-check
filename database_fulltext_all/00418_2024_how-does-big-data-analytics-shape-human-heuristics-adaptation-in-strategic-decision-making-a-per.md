# How Does Big Data Analytics Shape Human Heuristics Adaptation in Strategic Decision-Making? A Perspective of Environmental Uncertainty Contingencies

<sup>1</sup>University of Nottingham Ningbo China, China, jin.chen@nottingham.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>National University of Singapore, Singapore, hengcs@comp.nus.edu.sg <sup>3</sup>ESSEC Business School, France, liyan@essec.edu

## Abstract

As big data analytics (BDA) has increasingly influenced strategic decision-making, researchers and practitioners are continuously debating the roles of humans versus machines in making decisions. Our multiple-case analysis examines how BDA shapes decision makers’ adaptation of heuristics in response to different dimensions of environmental uncertainty (i.e., complexity versus dynamism). Contrary to prior literature that suggests that BDA supplants human heuristics or impedes humans from adapting their heuristics, our findings underscore that BDA shapes the adaptation of heuristics through three distinct modes: alternative-reorienting, cue-patching, and relation-conditioning. Specifically, BDA shapes heuristics adaptation through the hybrid mode of cue-patching and relation-conditioning when environmental complexity is high and through the alternative-reorienting mode when environmental dynamism is high. However, when environmental complexity and dynamism are both high, the uncertainty in the environment may render BDA less effective, and substantial business acumen is required to adapt heuristics further. In addition, our findings reveal a pinning mechanism of BDA—that is, by keeping one component of human heuristics unchanged, a fixed point of comparison is created for evaluating the changes to other components of the heuristics. This study contributes to the literature by theorizing how BDA shapes heuristics adaptation and adds value to strategic decision-making in uncertain environments.

Keywords: Big Data Analytics, Human Heuristics, Strategic Decision-Making, Environmental Uncertainty, Environmental Complexity, Environmental Dynamism, Multiple-Case Analysis

## 1 Introduction

Recent information systems (IS) literature has suggested that big data analytics (BDA)—“the application of statistical, processing, and analytics techniques to big data for advancing business” (Grover et al., 2018, p. 390)—has become increasingly important in organizations’ strategic decision-making (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Chiang et al., 2018; McAfee & Brynjolfsson, 2012; Sharma et al., 2014). Strategic decision-making, which is characterized by a lack of structure and high uncertainty, can be even more difficult owing to decision makers’ limited information, time, and processing capacity (Bettis, 2017; Maitland & Sammartino, 2015). With an abundance of data and powerful computational capabilities that complement human cognitive capacity, BDA can quickly discover and supplement evidence-based insights (Dremel et al., 2017), enhance decision quality, and accelerate the decision-making process (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Ghasemaghaei, 2019; Grover et al., 2018).

In contrast, the strategy literature continues to highlight that human heuristics—cognitive shortcuts comprising three components: alternatives (i.e., available options of a decision), cues (i.e., relevant pieces of information), and relations (i.e., connections between cues and alternatives) (Shah & Oppenheimer, 2008)—are central to strategic decision-making (Bettis, 2017; Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2011; Maitland & Sammartino, 2015; Moldoveanu, 2009). Heuristics operate by repressing uncertainty awareness (e.g., altering decision makers’ perceptions of the situation to make it appear more certain) and by acting on a simplified model of reality. The primary advantage of heuristics is their simplicity, ease of use, and high adaptability, allowing for the capture of fleeting opportunities before the decision problems become irrelevant (Bettis, 2017; Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2011, 2014). Particularly, heuristics adaptation, defined as the process by which decision makers revise heuristics in response to newly considered information (Feduzi et al., 2022), greatly helps decision makers deal with uncertain environments effectively. For example, Bingham and Eisenhardt (2011) found that executives typically begin with a comprehensive portfolio of heuristics and intentionally trim it down to make decisions based on the most current business environments and conditions.

The strategy literature and the BDA literature highlight two seemingly contrasting approaches to strategic decision-making: relying on human heuristics versus relying on BDA (Davenport & Harris, 2007). This dichotomy has ignited an intensifying discourse on the roles of humans versus machines in this context. Some have argued that BDA outperforms human heuristics by discovering the “truth” from data and rectifying human biases (Dhar 2013; Lebovitz et al., 2021; McAfee, 2013; van den Broek et al., 2022), and that BDA should “substitute (not complement) for judgment” (Lyytinen & Grover, 2017, p. 215). Others, however, contended that human heuristics remain relevant not only because algorithms may prioritize short-term success (Luca et al., 2016) but also because business progress often outpaces the collection and analysis of data available (Bettis, 2017; Davenport, 2013; van den Broek et al., 2022). While it has also been suggested that decision-making jointly conducted by humans and machines may lead to superior outcomes (Raisch & Fomina, 2024; Sturm et al., 2021; van den Broek et al., 2022), the optimal strategies and critical boundaries of human-machine integration are still not well-understood (Golovianko et al., 2023).

Joining this debate, we highlight an overlooked possibility of the intricate interplay between BDA and human heuristics rather than a simplified case of one replacing the other. Given the increasing uncertainty in business environments, being adaptive is crucial. BDA can continually provide valuable, new information for strategic decision-making, while humans can adapt their heuristics by assimilating this new information. However, the dynamics of this interaction may not be straightforward, as how humans develop and modify their heuristics in the BDA era could differ remarkably from those in pre-BDA times. Before the advent of BDA, decision makers could apply and refine heuristics to reduce cognitive effort due to the limited scope of heuristics components (namely alternatives, cues, and relations) that could be brought to their attention (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2011). After incorporating BDA, the proliferation of data and analytical techniques has led to an overwhelming abundance of new alternatives and cues, alongside complex relations such as multidirectional causalities, which were not discernible before (Benbya et al., 2020). The combinatorial explosion in terms of alternatives, cues, and relations makes it increasingly challenging for humans to develop or refine their heuristics to navigate strategic decisions effectively. Moreover, after using BDA, it may “instigate a new round of digitally enabled interactions” between humans and data (Benbya et al., 2020, p. 3). For example, the interactions between humans and BDA are often characterized by “hyper-connections, hyper-speed, and hyper-turbulence” that can “create previously unprecedented levels of complexity and dynamism and pose fundamental challenges” to decision makers (Benbya et al., 2020, p. 13). In this light, previous findings on the adaptation of heuristics (and their constituent components) cannot be readily applied after incorporating BDA in strategic decision-making.

Thus, our research question is: How does BDA shape human heuristics adaptation in strategic decisionmaking under environmental uncertainty? Following prior research (Bakker & Knoben, 2015; Benbya et al., 2020; Thompson, 2003), we focused on two key dimensions of environmental uncertainty relevant to BDA effectiveness: environmental complexity and dynamism. By adopting an inductive, multiple-case approach (Eisenhardt, 1989), we compared four cases of strategic decisions that vary in their environmental complexity and dynamism. We found that BDA shapes heuristics adaptation in three distinct modes: alternative-reorienting, cue-patching, and relationconditioning (these three concepts emerged from our multiple-case analysis and will be elaborated on in our findings). We also found that different adaptation modes are associated with different dimensions of environmental uncertainty (namely, complexity and dynamism). We developed a new theoretical model to expound on the underlying theoretical mechanisms.

## 2 Literature Review and Theoretical Foundation

## 2.1 Heuristics Adaptation in Strategic Decision-Making

Strategic decision-making is characterized by a lack of structure, high uncertainty, and open-endedness (Bettis, 2017; Maitland & Sammartino, 2015). Confronted with limited information, time, and processing capacity, humans often find it effective to employ simple rules and subjective opinions as cognitive shortcuts to simplify their perception of such problems, which are referred to as heuristics (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2011). For example, in a global study of over 1,000 chief executive officers (CEOs) and chief financial officers (CFOs) focusing on capital allocation criteria, 48.2% of respondents rated heuristics as important or very important, and 60%-70% factored “divisional manager’s reputation/confidence” into their heuristics for project potential assessment and capital allocation (Graham et al., 2015). Simple to remember, easy to use, and quick to adapt, heuristics are particularly helpful in balancing efficiency and flexibility in uncertain environments (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2011; Eisenhardt et al., 2018; Moldoveanu, 2009).

Importantly, heuristics serve as “adaptive strategies” to suit complex and ever-changing circumstances (Gigerenzer, 1991, p. 101). When reusing past heuristics for future decisions, decision makers often need to modify these heuristics (or their constituent components) based on the feedback and lessons learned from experimentation and newly acquired information, which is referred to as heuristics adaptation (Feduzi et al., 2022). For example, Suarez and Montes (2019) suggested that new heuristics emerge when a mismatch exists between existing heuristics and the constraints imposed by a new context. Bingham and Eisenhardt (2011) found that executives begin with a set of initial heuristics, gradually add more, and purposefully replace superficial heuristics with higher-quality ones, resulting in a smaller set of heuristics over time. Decision makers may even combine heuristics to form more concise ones. For example, Bingham and Eisenhardt (2011, p. 1456) observed that “firms also chunked granular heuristics into abstract ones.” Similarly, Kazakova (2015, p. 105) noted “combinations of rules.” Gregory and Muntermann (2014) found that relevant pieces of information are selected and abstracted to arrive at a satisficing solution in a heuristic process.

It has been implicitly assumed that during heuristics adaptation, due to humans’ limited capacity for information search and processing, the scope of heuristics components that decision makers can attend to at one time needs to be restrained to be manageable (von Krogh, 2018). This limitation explains why decision makers tend to develop more condensed heuristics over time (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2011; Kazakova, 2015). However, prior studies have not highlighted the possibility of a dramatic increase in heuristics components due to the advent of BDA, which may render the traditional way of paying attention to heuristics components and successfully managing them impossible. As discussed above, heuristics comprise three distinct components: alternatives, cues, and relations (Shah & Oppenheimer, 2008). Alternatives refer to the available options of a decision which could be on an event, object, or person (Moldoveanu, 2009). Cues refer to the relevant pieces of information considered for each alternative (Shah & Oppenheimer, 2008). To sort alternatives based on a set of cues, the relations between cues and alternatives must be considered (e.g., functions or algorithms that integrate multiple cues to compute an alternative’s value) (Bettis, 2017; Moldoveanu, 2009; Shah & Oppenheimer, 2008). For example, let us consider the new market entry of a hypothetical US information technology (IT) firm. Before using BDA, alternatives regarding the first international market to enter might include Europe, Oceania, and Asia; cues used to evaluate those alternatives could be a few important market features (e.g., English-speaking countries, cheap labor, consumer preferences); and relations may be the linear relationships between market features and the anticipated success of a given market (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2011). After incorporating BDA, the granularity of alternatives may increase to enable a precise location at the area or city level instead of the country level; the range of cues may be expanded to include numerous digitally enabled factors that measure niche market characteristics; and the scope of relations could also be broadened to encompass nonlinear relationships between niche market features and the success of a niche market entry. Humans may have difficulty accurately discerning and managing these extensive and drastic changes and assessing their potential impact on the overall effectiveness of the heuristics. Whether human heuristics would be replaced or reshaped by BDA remains unclear.

## 2.2 Big Data Analytics and Strategic Decision-Making

The literature on BDA has suggested that the use of BDA in organizations’ strategic decision-making offers numerous benefits that can be categorized into Sharma et al.’s (2014) three-stage framework: data-toinsight, insight-to-decision, and decision-to-value. In the “data-to-insight” stage, BDA can spot novel patterns and trends to facilitate decision makers’ understanding of the causes behind those patterns by gathering more information, providing fast feedback, generating new decision rules, and developing adaptive, learning-based decision processes through experimentation (Lyytinen & Grover, 2017; Saar-Tsechansky, 2015; Sharma et al., 2014). In the “insight-to-decision” stage where decision makers transform BDA insights into decisions (Sharma et al., 2014), BDA can speed up decision-making and improve decision quality by invoking creativity and curiosity, cultivating a shared decision process, promoting microcoordination, and improving organizational culture (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Ghasemaghaei, 2019; Grover et al., 2018; Lyytinen & Grover, 2017; Williams et al., 2015). Finally, “decision-to-value” depicts the stage in which organizations implement their decisions to create value, and BDA can aid by improving the acceptance of good decisions, enhancing organizations’ dynamic capabilities, and transforming businesses (Baesens et al., 2016; Goes, 2014; Isık et al., 2013; Lehrer et al., 2018; Mikalef et al., 2019; Müller et al., 2018; Rai, 2016; Sharma et al., 2014). Among the three, the first stage is most relevant to the tension between BDA and human heuristics, as it focuses on identifying and interpreting data to inform strategic decisions.

The increasing prevalence of BDA has led to growing skepticism regarding the central role of human heuristics in the “data-to-insight” stage. In contrast to human heuristics that may represent biased and poor substitutes for computations, BDA is expected to generate fact-based insights to overcome the deficiencies associated with human heuristics (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Dhar, 2013; Lyytinen & Grover, 2017; McAfee, 2013). For example, with BDA’s greatly improved data, algorithms, and computational power, organizations can analyze entire populations rather than samples, uncover hidden correlations among variables, and develop more comprehensive and objective models to represent decision problems (Mayer-Schönberger & Cukier, 2013). Moreover, with the recent trend of integrating machine learning techniques in BDA, it has become more AI-driven, enabling efficient detection of patterns that experts may have overlooked (Rana et al., 2022; van den Broek et al., 2022). As a result, BDA is viewed as an innovative approach to generating novel solutions (Castro & Korte, 2013; Manyika et al., 2011). Not surprisingly, prior studies have mostly regarded human heuristics and BDA as two opposite ends of a continuum, with the former being subjective and biased and the latter being objective and useful in mitigating humans’ cognitive biases (Abbasi et al., 2016; Agarwal & Dhar, 2014) or substituting for the former (Lyytinen & Grover, 2017).

Despite the advantages of BDA over human heuristics, prior studies have cautioned against three limitations of BDA. First, because BDA algorithms are programmed on predefined goals and only execute the tasks they are given, they may be incapable of dealing with situations they are not designed to handle (Luca et al., 2016; van den Broek et al., 2022; von Krogh, 2018). Purely relying on BDA would be detrimental in uncertain environments where new information, tasks, and goals keep on emerging and forming unprecedented situations that cannot be understood by using historical data (Benbya et al., 2021; Duan et al., 2019). Second, BDA algorithms are often narrowly focused on the available data that may only reflect short-term success; however, there might be a conflict between short-term success and long-term goals, which humans can easily recognize but BDA algorithms cannot unless they are programmed (with difficulty) to do so (Luca et al., 2016). Third, how algorithms generate results is a black box that increases interpretation opacity (Lebovitz et al., 2022; Luca et al., 2016), especially given the recent adoption of AI-driven BDA (Rana et al., 2022). Similarly, Lebovitz et al. (2021) cautioned about the dangers of treating machines’ “know-what” too readily when humans’ underlying “know-how” is lacking. As strategic decision problems are often open to multiple interpretations, decision makers under resource constraints and time pressure may struggle to incorporate BDA results into their judgments.

As such, the discourse on the tension between BDA and human heuristics has sparked a debate on “data-driven decision-making” versus “intuition-based decisionmaking” (Abbasi et al., 2016; Dhar, 2013). Despite this debate, we posit that previous research has largely neglected the potential interplay between BDA (esteemed for its rapid experimental capabilities) and human heuristics (valued for their adaptability), which together enable decision makers to navigate uncertainty more effectively and efficiently. The dynamics of the interplay, however, remain underexplored.

## 2.3 Environmental Uncertainty, Heuristics Adaptation, and Big Data Analytics

Prior studies have suggested that environmental uncertainty, defined as the “inability to accurately assess the external environment of the organization or the future changes that might occur in that environment” (Bakker & Knoben, 2015, p. 259), is a critical boundary condition for BDA to be effective and thus might be key in explaining the interplay between human heuristics and BDA (Chen et al., 2021; Duan et al., 2019; Huang, 2018; Jarrahi, 2018; Lebovitz et al., 2022; Luoma & Martela, 2021). Specifically, two dimensions of environmental uncertainty are relevant to our study: environmental complexity and dynamism (Bakker & Knoben, 2015).

First, environmental complexity refers to the extent to which an environment consists of a large number of elements that have nonlinear interactions with one another (Cilliers, 2002). Nonlinearity is a precondition for complexity; otherwise, a large-scale system of linear elements can easily collapse into a small and simple system that can be analyzed accurately (Cilliers 2002, pp. 3-4). In contrast, with large-scale, nonlinear interdependency, a small change in one element would reverberate through many other elements and be subject to feedback loops, resulting in catastrophic chain reactions in the whole environment and demonstrating high causal intricacy (Bettis, 2017; Manson, 2001). Even if decision makers can successfully map all elements out, it is impossible for them to anticipate all possible nonlinear feedback loops and chain reactions; instead, only part of the intricate interdependency among elements can be analyzed at a given time (Cilliers, 2002). Thus, as complexity increases, the hidden, nonlinear interdependency among the elements in a system makes it challenging for humans to identify appropriate heuristics or refine them (Faulkner et al., 2017; Feduzi et al., 2022). For example, to choose which international market to enter, the hypothetical US IT firm referred to above (Section 2.2) could generate a list of market features (e.g., labor cost, culture distance, consumer preferences, government regulations, competitor strategies, new entrants, technology trajectories, product road maps, and national/regional differences) as cues for a heuristic. These cues would be interdependent to varying degrees, resulting in high complexity. Specifying values for each cue and determining how to integrate them to evaluate each alternative “is a nontrivial task since each [cue] cannot be specified independently of others” (Bettis, 2017, p. 2626); thus, decision makers often consider cues separately and assign weights in a linear fashion to aggregate the cues, at the cost of accuracy (Moldoveanu, 2009).

On the other hand, BDA is known for its advantages over humans in mitigating complexity, excelling particularly at discovering nonlinear relations and “the appropriate cause of action among many possibilities through causal loops” that are easily overlooked by humans (Jarrahi, 2018, p. 581; van den Broek et al., 2022). In this light, incorporating BDA could facilitate humans in adapting their heuristics to complex environments. Paradoxically, as Benbya et al. (2020) cautioned, incorporating BDA may unwittingly exacerbate complexity, as BDA introduces a multitude of new, digitally enabled alternatives and cues for heuristics that humans may not have the knowledge or cognitive capacity to interpret effectively. Furthermore, BDA can suggest patterns of multidirectional causalities (as relations for heuristics) that are often opaque for humans to understand (Benbya et al., 2020). If these new alternatives, cues, and relations conflict with decision makers’ prior knowledge and even lead to information overload, humans will tend not to internalize machine-generated knowledge (Bauer et al., 2023). For these reasons, whether BDA helps or hinders humans in improving their heuristics remains an intriguing question.

Second, environmental dynamism concerns the rate and unpredictability of change in an environment (Nadkarni & Chen, 2014). The rate of change (also referred to as volatility or velocity) captures the amount of change in an environment over a specified period, whereas unpredictability refers to the inability to predict when a change will take place and its outcome (Nadkarni & Chen, 2014). Together, the rate and unpredictability of change determine how dynamic an environment is. For example, the environmental dynamism of strategic decisions is high in nascent or disrupted markets where market structures, technologies, competitors, and customer needs change rapidly and unpredictably (Furr & Eisenhardt, 2021). Dynamism creates prediction and interpretation challenges that make it virtually impossible to know which data or values are still relevant and genuine for decision problems from time t to time t+1 (Feduzi et al., 2022; Furr & Eisenhardt, 2021). Thus, in dynamic environments, unexpected turbulence or the emergence of novel scenarios may lead to a situation where a heuristic that “the decision-maker (until now rightly) regards as possible is now rendered genuinely impossible” (Feduzi et al., 2022, p. 408). For example, because of the coronavirus outbreak, fundamental changes in economic, social, and political institutions would have made many of the aforementioned US IT firm’s previous assumptions about market opportunities obsolete.

While human heuristics can easily become obsolete in dynamic environments, BDA has been subject to similar criticisms. For example, BDA algorithms are less viable in hyperspeed, unpredictable environments because they are constrained by their preset goals and data and struggle when required by changing environments to operate “even a little outside a predefined domain” (Brynjolfsson and McAfee 2012, p. 15). In contrast, human heuristics “offer a holistic, intuitive approach” to dealing with dynamism (Golovianko et al., 2023, p. 881). In this light, it seems that humans have advantages over machines in managing environmental dynamism. But BDA is also valued for its ability to overcome the limitations of humans’ ineffective search routines in the face of dramatic change. For example, BDA may be able to conduct searches for novel solutions more efficiently (Haefner et al., 2021; Raisch & Fomina, 2024; Sturm et al., 2021; van den Broek et al., 2022). These inconclusive findings leave the question of whether BDA facilitates humans in adapting their heuristics to dynamic environments unanswered.

As environmental complexity and dynamism are two key factors that may determine the advantages or disadvantages of BDA over human heuristics, we adopted these two dimensions as our theoretical framework to uncover the nuances of the interplay between BDA and heuristics adaptation. Moreover, these two dimensions are orthogonal (Cilliers, 2002): a complex environment can be stabilized for a long time (i.e., low dynamism), and a dynamic environment can collapse into small parts that can be analyzed separately (i.e., low complexity). Grounded in these two dimensions, we followed prior research and developed a two-dimensional framework to depict environmental uncertainty (Clarysse et al., 2011), as shown in Figure 1. Quadrant I, titled “Simple & stable,” represents an environment with stable states and low interdependency among elements. Quadrant II, named “Complex but stable,” increases in complexity but remains low in dynamism; thus, the environment scarcely experiences turbulent states, but the synergy effect of any small change is unpredictable because of the heavy, nonlinear interdependency among the elements. Conversely, in Quadrant III “Simple but dynamic,” the environment is low in complexity but high in dynamism, which means that the environment experiences significant turbulence and discontinuity, but the direction of supervenient changes is deducible because the interdependency among elements is low and easy to understand. Finally, Quadrant IV, “Complex & dynamic,” describes an environment in which the slightest changes in one or a few elements often drastically shift the whole in unpredictable ways, and the whole can be very different from the sum of its parts. This framework guides our theoretical sampling (Section 3) and findings (Section 4).

## 3 Methodology

We adopted an inductive, multiple-case study because of its ability to compare and confirm the inferences from multiple cases based on a “replication logic” (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2011, p. 1440; Eisenhardt, 1989). To make sense of the data, we employed a synthetic strategy suggested by Langley (1999), which (1) is mostly applied to analyze the processes of decisions, (2) produces simple and moderately general theories, and (3) generates some initial constructs and measures from the detailed event data “to compare different processes and to identify regularities that will form the basis of a predictive theory relating holistic process characteristics to other variables (e.g., outcomes and contexts)” (p. 704).

We targeted strategic decisions related to new product development (NPD) for two theoretical reasons. First, the contrast between traditional data analysis and BDA should be sharp enough to allow for the isolation of BDA’s effect. Although traditional data analysis has been used in NPD decisions for decades (Castro & Korte, 2013; Manyika et al., 2011), NPD decisions with BDA demonstrate even shorter decision-making times (due to fleeting opportunities and competitors intensive use of BDA) and insufficient human processing capacity given the large volume and variety of data flushing (Cappa et al., 2021). Second, it is crucial for target decisions to demonstrate sufficient variation in terms of environmental uncertainty. NPD decisions vary greatly in their environmental uncertainty and thus fulfill this criterion (Atuahene-Gima & Li, 2004; Bstieler, 2005).

## 3.1 Selection of Sample Firms

Following the principle of “substantive significance,” or “theoretical relevance” (Sarker et al., 2013), we conducted theoretical sampling based on the conceptual framework shown in Figure 1. Specifically, we collected information for each case regarding its recent NPD decision to screen whether its task environment was representative of a quadrant shown in Figure 1. We used four NPD decisions from four different firms as our sample, each fitting into one quadrant. Details of the four firms are listed in Table 1.

Specifically, in Quadrant I, “Simple & stable,” we chose S-Social, a Singapore-based mobile social networking app provider with a major customer base in Saudi Arabia, and its decision to revamp the app to enhance the user experience. Motivated by a performance gap that “many functionalities are low in key performance indicators (KPIs),” this decision concerned which functionalities should be improved or dropped. It operated in an environment where dynamism was low (relatively stable) because S-Social had many loyal customers and did not face strong competitors in the Saudi Arabian market and the technology for social networking apps was steadily improving. In addition, the decision operated in an environment where complexity was low, given the interdependency among the elements in the system; that is, the targeted functionalities (as alternatives) were modular, the KPIs (as cues) were clear-cut, and the links (as relations) between functionalities and the KPIs were straightforward and easy to understand.

For Quadrant II, “Complex but stable,” we selected X-Payment, one of the top three online payment service providers in China. When being inundated with complaints from overseas sellers, X-Payment recognized a problem, “the chargeback rate of overseas payment is higher than the industry average.” They aimed to address this problem by developing an automated risk-control system. At that time, despite complaints, overseas sellers could not find a better platform to replace X-Payment for their overseas transactions in China. Other than X-Payment, competitors in China had yet to develop automated risk-control systems simply because most data went to X-Payment, owing to a winner-takes-all effect. As a result, environmental dynamism was low. Meanwhile, environmental complexity was high, due to the intricate nonlinear interdependency among the elements in their decision. For example, the weights of risk indexes (as relations) were largely dependent on the complex interactions among its payment system, overseas sellers, domestic buyers, and banks. A change in cues (e.g., risk indexes), alternatives (e.g., designs of risk-control systems), or relations (e.g., weights of indexes) can redefine how sellers and buyers communicate, thereby introducing chain reactions to other alternatives, cues, or relations.

For Quadrant III, “Simple but dynamic,” we found E-Navigator, one of the top three web-navigation providers in China, and its decision to develop customized recommendation tools. When confronted with the issue that “the value of the navigation webpage is far from being maximized,” the executives brainstormed and compared various distinct solutions (as alternatives) ranging from less noticeable functions (e.g., “Bookmark”) to more noticeable ones (e.g., “Guess what you like”). At that time, customers would have incurred few costs to switch to other webnavigation providers, and the information about customer preferences and needs was far from complete because of multihoming behaviors, resulting in high environmental dynamism. As evidenced, E-Navigator vacillated between various solutions (as alternatives) and various criteria (as cues) that sorted the alternatives. For example, they began with a formerly proven belief that “users are uncomfortable with changes,” but soon realized that this belief did not hold and instead hindered them from knowing more about the recent, fast-changing customer needs. While this case demonstrates high environmental dynamism, it was low in environmental complexity because the changes brought about by any element to other elements were predictable and unlikely to trigger massive chain reactions among their solutions, customers, and connected websites.

Finally, we deemed T-Video to be a good fit for Quadrant IV, “Complex & dynamic.” It was the first company to enter the market of video metasearch apps in China and was among the top three, with approximately 40 million users at the time of the interview. Prior to its video metasearch business, T-Video was a mobile gaming company. However, the CEO was not satisfied and remarked: “Users’ average length of stay in mobile gaming is about 10-20 minutes, not satisfying.” He was resolute on exiting the mobile gaming industry to venture into some “Blue Ocean” in a less contested manner. Which market to enter was a difficult decision, as the executives faced many distinct streams of technologies and competing types of multimedia (e.g., music versus video). Further, the market was too nascent to have a clear structure or clearly defined competitors and had quickly changing, vague customer needs. Thus, the external environment was rife with unexpected turbulence and unpredictable changes, characterized by high environmental dynamism. Moreover, any change to alternatives, cues, or relations would have attracted different sets of customers and defined distinct competitors, reverberating to influence other alternatives, cues, or relations in a chain reaction. Thus, this case also had high environmental complexity.

## 3.2 Data Collection

Our primary data source was semi-structured interviews based on interview protocols. To enhance the validity and rigor of our study, we conducted indepth interviews with two types of informants at each firm for better data triangulation: business specialists from business departments (e.g., CEOs, VPs, and project managers in charge of business) and BDA specialists from data-analytics (DA) departments (e.g., chief technology officers (CTOs), chief analytics officers (CAOs), and project managers in charge of DA). Following the “courtroom” procedure suggested by Eisenhardt (1989), we first asked informants for the timeline of their NPD decisions and then based our interviews on this to limit subject bias (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2011). With business specialists, we focused on the business aspects of decisions and background information, including firm history, strategy, market, customers, competitors, and firm structure. With BDA specialists, we focused on the use of BDA in decision-making and background information on data storage, data analytics technologies, and technology trajectories. From the business and BDA specialists, we gathered simple rules to make NPD decisions easier (i.e., potential heuristics). We also encouraged informants from the business and DA departments to comment on the other party’s contributions to decisions.

Each interview included three parts: (1) background information on the firm and its departments, (2) event chronology for specific NPD decisions, and (3) questions related to NPD decision-making (e.g., How was data used and inferred? Who was involved in the process of decision-making? What options did they have? What criteria were adopted? Were there any changes in options and criteria? Why and how? What would happen if simple rules held by different departments conflicted?). When building the event chronology, we used open-ended questions regarding the process of the NPD decision in detail (e.g., How many key actions were taken to make the NPD decision? Who oversaw each action?). We reviewed the event chronology with the informants to ensure that we covered all key actions. To prepare for the interviews, we searched for firm information on websites and mobile apps. The media coverage of these firms was also collected to identify significant strategic moves. To further enhance accuracy and completeness, we used direct on-site observations, follow-up emails, and archival documents to triangulate the interview data.

## 3.3 Data Analysis

Consistent with prior studies that have adopted multiple-case analysis (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2011; Eisenhardt, 1989), we conducted within-case analysis first, followed by cross-case analysis. In the within-case analysis, based on interview data, archival data, and case studies of companies, we created an event chronology of key decisions in each NPD project and how and why they were made. The analysis was refined through subsequent interviews and email exchanges, and the contextualized backdrop of each project was triangulated with our direct observation of the focal product (e.g., website and app) and press releases (e.g., reports on industry background, competitors, and customers) collected from external sources. Next, we coded the interview data of each NPD project to identify every heuristic and its components, following the criteria established in prior literature. According to Shah and Oppenheimer (2008), a heuristic should exhibit one or more principles of effort reduction in decision-making, and its components should include alternatives, cues, and relations. Figure 2 illustrates the coding scheme for the components of heuristics. Coding was derived from both theory in a deductive manner (e.g., following definitions from the literature to derive theoretical dimensions) and data in an inductive manner (e.g., identifying facts from case interviews, classifying them into categories, and linking categories to related concepts). Iterations between the theory and data were conducted until we had a good understanding of each case.

We use S-Social as an example to illustrate the deductive and inductive analyses used to identify heuristics. When S-Social planned to develop a new version of its app to enhance the user experience, it needed to choose which popular functionalities to improve and which poor ones to drop. The executives were quick to relate their industrial knowledge (e.g., WhatsApp’s experience) to the current decision problem without further hesitation. As one executive recalled: “Like WhatsApp, simply use 10 KPIs such as retention rate, number of active users, number of messages sent, etc.” This heuristic [Use 10 KPIs popularly adopted by the industry]<sup>1</sup> was mentioned by the informants, and we confirmed that it followed the definition of heuristics because it reduced decision makers’ effort by leveraging industry benchmarking practices to narrow the search scope under the assumption of significant similarity between WhatsApp and S-Social. In this heuristic, the functionalities targeted for enhancement or elimination were identified as the various alternatives to be considered. The 10 KPIs, serving as the metrics to evaluate the alternatives, were classified as cues. The postulated positive or linear relationships between the 10 KPIs and the popularity of the functionalities, indicative of the connections between cues and alternatives, were encoded as relations.

Second, in the cross-case analysis, we used charts and tables to identify the emergence of similar patterns across multiple cases (Eisenhardt, 1989). In particular, we looked for within-group similarities coupled with intergroup differences until a clear pattern was identified (Eisenhardt, 1989). Specifically, based on the alternatives, cues, and relations of all heuristics identified across cases, we focused on whether similar adaptation patterns/modes of these heuristics components existed over time and whether different environmental contingencies were associated with distinct adaptation modes of heuristics components. Owing to the relative lack of elaboration on heuristics adaptation modes in the literature, we adopted open coding of interview data to produce a grounded analysis of the modes of heuristics adaptation to derive first-order codes, mainly in an inductive manner (e.g., identifying facts from interview data and classifying them into first-order categories).

We then synthesized first-order codes to match the theory in an abductive manner (e.g., linking categories to their best concepts) to derive conceptual categories (second-order codes) with iterations between theory and data. For example, we observed that BDA is helpful for either adding a new cue to a heuristic or revising the set of existing cues (first-order codes), and we aggregated these two changing patterns to be an adaptation mode termed “cue-patching” (second-order codes). Here, we adapted a similar concept, “patching,” which was developed by Siggelkow (2002) and refers to the adoption of new organizational elements. In this way, we iterated between the literature and the data to arrive at our final coding scheme for the adaptation modes of heuristics components, as shown in Figure 3 above. We then refined them using replication logic, frequently revisiting the data to systematically compare and verify the occurrence of specific patterns across cases until our analysis reached theoretical saturation (Glaser & Strauss, 1967).

## 4 Findings

## 4.1 Heuristics Adaptation Shaped by BDA

We found that after involving BDA, heuristics adapt in three distinct modes: alternative-reorienting, cuepatching, and relation-conditioning. Appendices A-D elucidate the detailed adaptation processes of each case. Appendix E lists the definitions and sources of all the concepts. Across the four cases, we found that BDA triggers heuristics to undergo adaptation by introducing two types of new information: (1) the feedback of fast experimentation with BDA or (2) the requirements of BDA itself.

Alternative-reorienting: We term an adaptation mode of heuristics components “alternativereorienting” if BDA revises the set of alternatives in a heuristic by replacing invalid alternatives or enlarging the set of existing alternatives. Consequently, the target options of the decision are reoriented with changes in alternatives (e.g., changing the target industry from one industry to another).

First, heuristics can undergo alternative-reorienting because of BDA feedback. For example, the executive team of T-Video commented as follows:

Multimedia metasearch sounds promising, but which type of multimedia to focus on? Frankly speaking, we had no idea at that time. We started with the music industry because it resembles the gaming industry. We only proceed with a project if its performance is significantly better than the industry average. However, our music app called “Mobile Music Box” was unlikely to beat other similar apps as its retention rate was 10%-20%, rather similar to the industry average. ... Unexpectedly, upon analysis, we discovered that the market for video search integration and recommendation could be the “Blue Ocean.” The first week’s data revealed that users’ length of stay in our new app was 1-1.5 hours! In comparison to similar products on the PC version, which only held a user’s attention for an average of 0.5-1 hour, we were delighted with the results.

As quoted above, at the beginning, relying on an implicit assumption regarding the similarity between gaming and music industries, T-Video developed a heuristic [Enter the metasearch engine app market for music] to first target the music industry (as an alternative) with a beta version app of a music metasearch engine called “Mobile Music Box.” With BDA, it was discovered that the retention rate (as a cue) of this music metasearch engine was not greater than the industry average (as a cue). There was, however, another serendipitous discovery from BDA (segmentation analytics) showing that the market for a video metasearch engine could be the “Blue Ocean.” Without hesitation, T-Video developed a beta version of the app for a video metasearch engine (as a new alternative). In less than one week, about 5,000 users registered. BDA results revealed that the video metasearch engine demonstrated extraordinary performance in terms of users’ length of stay (as a cue) compared with similar products on the PC version. This high-quality feedback from fast experimentation with BDA (segmentation analytics) gave rise to a heuristic [Enter the metasearch engine app market for video because one week’s data shows that users’ length of stay was 1-1.5 hours]. To ensure that the result of the week was not an anomaly, T-Video also created another heuristic based on BDA (trend analysis) [Keep monitoring for a month to make sure that the week’s data trend was not accidental]. They recalled that in that month, the KPIs continued to climb. By shifting the alternative of their heuristic from “music industry” to “video industry,” they developed a new app and harvested a large user base within a short amount of time.

Second, the alternative-reorienting of heuristics can also be triggered by the requirements of BDA. Consider E-Navigator as an example. Regarding where to put recommendations, they started with a heuristic [Develop functionalities such as “Bookmark” to quietly recommend precise content to users]. However, they found that the data generated from “Bookmark” was too sparse, only capturing a limited sample that was not sufficient for data analytics. As suggested by BDA techniques, the results based on the entire population were more reliable, and recommendation models fitted from the entire population were more accurate. Boldly driven by this requirement of BDA to cover the population as much as possible, the alternative of the heuristic was reoriented from a lighthanded and less noticeable function (“Bookmark,” a small icon on the top right corner that users click to expand) to a more conspicuous one (“Guess what you like,” a block of recommendations placed in a prominent position below the search bar), resulting in an updated heuristic [Develop conspicuous functionalities such as “Guess what you like” to directly recommend precise content to users]. The outcome of this alternative-reorienting was remarkable, as commented on by a VP: “In ‘Guess what you like,’ the average earnings per click is 2 Yuan, as compared to 0.5 Yuan in the past.”

Cue-patching: In addition to alternative-reorienting, BDA may also revise the set of cues in a heuristic by replacing a false cue with a new one or by enlarging the set of existing cues. We term this adaptation mode “cue-patching,” owing to the interesting parallel between the concept in our study and that by Siggelkow (2002). Siggelkow (2002) defined patching as “adopting a new core element, and its subsequent reinforcement” to solve decision problems (p. 140). Unlike Siggelkow, who focused on the adoption of new elements, we included both adopting new and deleting false cues, as the findings suggest. As mentioned above, we found that BDA triggers heuristics to undergo cue-patching because of feedback from BDA or the requirements of BDA. To illustrate the first scenario, departing from a previously adopted heuristic [Use KPIs popularly adopted by the industry], S-Social derived a new heuristic [Use “whether synchronize address book” as a new KPI] by adding “whether synchronize address book” as a new KPI (as a cue) to the set of existing KPIs adopted from WhatsApp, as suggested by the results of BDA (correlation analysis). In another example, X-Payment generated a heuristic [To monitor payment security, one shall combine internal and external secondary data sources] to update a previous heuristic [To monitor payment security, one shall compose a risk score based on 30 indexes from internal data sources] by adding new cues (i.e., external data) to the existing ones (i.e., internal data), as the results of BDA (multisource data joint analysis) revealed.

Second, cue-patching occurs in heuristics when BDA techniques dictate some data collection requirements prior to analysis. For example, to develop recommendation tools, E-Navigator first generated a heuristic based on its experience and used user inertia as a cue to sort alternatives [Limit changes to the extent that users are indifferent to them]. Thus, E-Navigator piloted the project as “Bookmark” to minimize the changes perceived by users. However, the DA team found that many users did not notice the small change, and the data size could not inform the needs of the population if they wanted to conduct BDA (correlation analysis). Thus, bolder innovations were inevitable. E-Navigator adapted the heuristics to [Bring changes to the extent that all users can be aware of them], drastically replacing the existing cue (i.e., user inertia) with a new cue (i.e., user awareness).

Relation-conditioning: In addition, BDA revises the relations between cues and alternatives in a heuristic. We term this adaptation mode “relation-conditioning” because it changes the understanding of the interdependency between cues and alternatives and the conditions under which their interdependency applies. Our case analysis reveals that BDA triggers heuristics to undergo relation-conditioning, as suggested by BDA feedback or revealed by BDA requirements.

First, heuristics undergo relation-conditioning owing to BDA feedback, and one prominent advantage of BDA that we observed is its superiority in detecting nonlinear relations to update heuristics. For example, to sort functionalities, S-Social began contemplating how to best rank the popularity of functionalities. Faced with many possibilities, the business department, based on their business acumen, immediately chose a very simple but visualized approach [To reflect the relation between functionalities and KPIs, use scatter diagrams of functionalities and KPIs]. In this heuristic, the relations between KPIs (as cues) and functionalities (as alternatives) were regarded as bivariate; that is, one KPI is associated with only one functionality each time. Quickly, the DA department segmented data along multiple dimensions, obtained more nuanced insights, and adapted the heuristic [To reflect the relation between functionalities and KPIs, use segmentation analysis and rely on the combination of multiple dimensions]. The results of BDA (segmentation analysis) show that the relations between the KPIs (as cues) and functionalities (as alternatives) were multivariate and nonlinear; that is, one should rely on the synergy of multiple KPIs to understand the utility of functionality. Furthermore, the DA department found that some unpopular functionalities might be necessary for the entire app to function well and that they thus could not simply be ranked by the KPIs. Upon realizing this, the NPD team was compelled to rethink the extent to which the performance of a single functionality is equal to the performance of the overall app. This led them to enlarge the scope of existing relations by incorporating a new relation (“cue-alternative weights”) to assign different weights to the KPIs that assessed a single functionality versus those that assessed the entire app, resulting in a new heuristic [Differentiate the KPIs that assess an individual functionality from those that assess the overall app]. This helped them salvage some seemingly trivial but actually very important functionalities.

Second, heuristics undergo relation-conditioning when BDA requirements lead to the loosening of certain predefined conditions. For example, E-Navigator initially charged advertisers based on a single index [Charge advertisers according to the CPC, CPS, or ROI index, or monthly payment]. In this heuristic, indexes (as cues) were used exclusively—one choice negated the others— in generating a pricing model (as an alternative). Enabled by BDA (multisource data joint analysis) on advertiser performance, there is now greater flexibility such that advertisers can be charged using a combination of these indexes within one pricing model. In addition, the team leader observed a shift in the duration of advertisement validity owing to BDA (real-time analytics), stating:

When an advertiser comes to us, we can launch their advertisement instantly. If they wish to discontinue, we can take it down just as quickly, with the minimum duration being 10 minutes.

This statement highlights a revised pricing model and duration flexibility. As BDA does not require exclusivity among the indexes, E-Navigator adapted the original heuristic to a new one [Charge advertisers using a dynamic hybrid revenue model supported by data analytics]. In this case, the indexes (as cues) were no longer used in isolation but were integrated to create a pricing model (as an alternative) in real time, enabling nonlinear relations between cues and alternatives that were not available prior to BDA’s inclusive approach.

In summary, we identify three distinct modes through which decision makers adapt their heuristics in response to BDA feedback or requirements. These three adaptation modes are not mutually exclusive; rather, they can be combined and used in tandem. Thus, we present our first finding:

Finding 1: BDA shapes heuristics adaptation in three modes: alternative-reorienting, cue-patching, and relation-conditioning.

Environmental complexity: We found that BDA often reveals unexpected evidence, identifies previously neglected components of heuristics, and hints at their validity. However, triggered modes of heuristics adaptation vary across different levels of environmental complexity.

Specifically, when environmental complexity is high, we found that heuristics adaptation shaped by BDA is mostly realized through a hybrid mode of cue-patching and relation-conditioning (shown as “C&R” in Figure 4). As evidenced, X-Payment and T-Video (i.e., cases with high environmental complexity) experienced four and two instances of C&R hybrid adaptation, respectively, because of BDA. For example, one informant from X-Payment commented on the initial stage of the automated risk-control system development:

Business is always doable, but data are not always ready. We cannot afford to wait for data to be totally ready to analyze. We have to work without enough data.

As such, when assigning weights of risk indexes to develop the automated risk-control system, they decided on a heuristic [To assign weights of indexes, one shall rely on business sense because data are always not totally ready]. They relied on business sense to assign weights and combine them in a linear fashion. Nevertheless, based on BDA (outlier identification analysis), the DA department soon accumulated increasing insights into the behaviors that could be labeled as high risk, suggesting many real-time adjustments to the preassigned weights of the risk indexes. As the DA department illustrated:

Let’s say we have a UK customer who often pays in the morning with a small amount around £30 and using coupons. If they were found to use an American credit card to pay a much larger amount in a different time, this would be considered an outlier. Such highrisk payments are directly rejected. And that is on the PC end. If on mobile devices, we have more data such as mobile phone fingerprints, device numbers, geographical location, environmental information that can help adjust the weights and reduce the risk to be much lower than face-to-face transactions.

As quoted above, at this stage, BDA became an engine that enabled X-Payment to adapt their heuristic to a new one [Adjust weights of indexes by real-time data analytics] through a C&R hybrid adaptation—i.e., identifying critical, new cues (e.g., real-time data) and replacing the linear relation (e.g., a linear combination of existing risk indexes) with a nonlinear one (e.g., assigning greater weights to real-time data and combining them with existing risk indexes in a nonlinear fashion).

Similarly, in T-Video, when recommending videos to users, they started with a heuristic [Design personalized recommendations with an interface preferred by mobile users] using very general user preference in a linear fashion, simply regarding all mobile users as sharing common characteristics. The DA team, based on BDA (segmentation analysis), identified user characteristics, device features, and video features as critical cues and employed segmented dimensions and an integrative algorithm in a nonlinear fashion, resulting in a C&R hybrid adaptation that gave rise to a new heuristic [Develop personalized recommendations based on segmentation analysis along user characteristics, device features, and video features].

In contrast, when environmental complexity was low, we found no such C&R hybrid adaptation. As evidenced, neither S-Social nor E-Navigator (i.e., cases with low environmental complexity) employed a C&R hybrid adaptation; instead, cue-patching (if any) or relationconditioning (if any) was always employed separately. For example, S-Social employed BDA (correlation analysis) to unearth a previously unavailable KPI “whether synchronize address book” as a new cue. As they recalled,

## whether a user synchronizes their address book or not can help predict the initial number of the user’s friends on the app and finally their retention rate.

Remarkably, the advantage of this cue-patching lies in its early detection (i.e., good signal) of how active the user will be in the future before other KPIs take effect. More importantly, patching this new cue to the original heuristics does not require any change in the relations (i.e., no need for relation-conditioning) or the use of other cues. In another example, based on BDA (real-time data-driven revenue analytics), E-Navigator discovered a new relation (i.e., different indexes can be integrated to create an alternative), which was not available prior to their use of BDA, and updated their heuristic regarding the pricing model [Charge advertisers by a dynamic hybrid revenue model supported by data analytics]. Again, this relationconditioning was not combined with any cue-patching to realize its effect.

In summary, the evidence suggests that in simple environments, BDA enhances decision-making by identifying new cues or relations independently. However, in complex settings, BDA prompts a C&R hybrid mode of heuristics adaptation to reveal overlooked cues and relations together, resulting in an updated understanding of the nonlinear interdependency among critical cues. Thus, we present our second finding:

Finding 2: When environmental complexity is high, BDA shapes heuristics adaptation through a hybrid of cue-patching and relation-conditioning, instead of using them separately.

Environmental dynamism: We found that when environmental dynamism is high, unlike the aforementioned scenario for environmental complexity, BDA could help hint at the possibility that some previously appropriate heuristics components become incorrect over time. This is mostly realized through the alternative-reorienting mode (shown as “A” in Figure 4). As evidenced, E-Navigator and T-Video (i.e., cases with high environmental dynamism) experienced two and four instances of alternativereorienting because of BDA, respectively, resulting in an updated understanding of the validity of previously employed alternatives.

For example, a VP of E-Navigator mentioned a longstanding belief that had been historically validated (i.e., “Users are uncomfortable with changes to familiar webpages”). To trade-off between webpage changes and user inertia, E-Navigator piloted the project in a less noticeable manner (i.e., “Bookmark”), which was a small icon on the top right corner for users to click to expand [Develop functionalities such as “Bookmark” to quietly recommend precise content to users]. Unfortunately, the heuristic failed this time because customers became increasingly accustomed to drastic changes in other navigating websites. When E-Navigator’s DA team attempted to analyze the data right after releasing “Bookmark,” as a VP recalled, they found the data of “Bookmark” was too sparse to analyze, given that many users became too lazy to access and click the inconspicuous “Bookmark” button from the drop-down menu. E-Navigator’s original beliefs about alternatives were inapplicable, and they came to the critical realization that they were being bogged down by previously proven facts. The DA team leader realized that a promising opportunity should have a large effect size. For this reason, they shifted to developing a function of “Guess what you like,” with a block of recommendations in a prominent position below the search bar. They arrived at a new “Aha rule” from BDA [Develop conspicuous functionalities such as “Guess what you like” to directly recommend precise content to users], by overriding the obsolete alternative (i.e., inconspicuous places to put recommendations) and introducing a new and more promising one (i.e., conspicuous places to put recommendations).

Similarly, when T-Video designed its app, it initially emulated Google, Baidu, and other similar PC-end search engines to generate a heuristic [Design a search bar with an interface following PC-end industry leaders’ best practices] and developed a search bar for users to enter keywords to obtain a list of videos. However, they neglected that the shift from a website to an app was drastically turning a previously good alternative into an invalid one. As their BDA later clarified, unlike PC users, mobile users do not prefer to search. The CEO reflected:

Our beta version with search bar did not work. We compared user behavior on PCs and mobile phones, and found that such a traditional search bar is more suited to PC users ... This failure triggered us to skip the search function and directly recommend videos to users. So, users can open the app, immediately see what they want, and click the quick-play button.

As quoted above, upon BDA (segmentation analysis), T-Video arrived at the updated heuristic [Design personalized recommendations with an interface preferred by mobile users] by completely abandoning the obsolete alternative (i.e., search bar) and then shifting to a new alternative (i.e., personalized recommendations) that became relevant and valid with the shift from website to app.

In contrast, when environmental dynamism was low, we found no such alternative-reorienting. As evidenced, neither S-Social nor X-Payment (i.e., cases with low environmental dynamism) underwent an alternative-reorienting adaptation of their heuristics; instead, they relied on the other two adaptation modes.

In summary, the evidence suggests that as environmental dynamism increases, BDA phases out obsolete alternatives and introduces new, pertinent alternatives. This leads to our third finding:

Finding 3: When environmental dynamism is high, BDA shapes heuristics adaptation through alternative-reorienting.

Interaction between environmental complexity and dynamism. Furthermore, we found that when both complexity and dynamism are high, decision makers may realize that they must leverage substantial business acumen (in addition to what is offered by BDA feedback or BDA requirements) to further adapt heuristics through alternative-reorienting, cuepatching, and relation-conditioning.

For example, to underscore alternative-reorienting in complex and dynamic environments, relying solely on alternative-reorienting triggered by BDA may be inadequate. T-Video’s decision on the type of video is a good example. T-Video initially relied on an implicit association between the success of metasearch tools and users’ repetitive use to generate a heuristic [Start with long videos because they can retain users for a longer time]. This heuristic was confirmed by the positive market responses to the initial type of long video (e.g., TV dramas and shows). Subsequently, they added short videos (e.g., cartoons, entertainment, and sports programs) because their BDA (correlation analysis) suggested that short videos could promote users’ sharing with friends and expand the user base [Expand the business by including short videos]. Contrary to their expectations, the add-ins did not take off on a large scale. T-Video quickly reverted to its business acumen in the implicit association between the success of metasearch tools and users’ repetitive use. The DA manager explained this as follows:

Short videos, while easy to share to others, don’t enhance app engagement. In comparison, long videos, although not easy to share, can keep customers engaged longer.

In other words, the DA team realized that while BDA (correlation analysis) may be valid in revealing the cooccurrence between short videos and sharing behavior, sharing behavior does not predict users’ length of stay. Thus, the heuristic was reoriented by shifting the alternative from both long and short videos back to long videos only, arriving at a third version [Remove short videos and stick to long videos]. Despite appearing to backtrack, T-Video had a clearer rationale for reverting to their previous decision.

As another example, to underscore cue-patching and relation-conditioning in complex and dynamic environments, solely relying on those triggered by BDA may be inadequate. Again, T-Video offers a good illustration. When recommending videos to users, T-Video generated a heuristic based on the feedback of BDA (segmentation analysis) [Develop algorithms of personalized recommendations based on segmentation analysis along user characteristics, device features, and video features]. However, these algorithms, although accurate in predicting what users liked based on BDA (segmentation analysis), did not lead to a high click rate. Why do users not click on videos that they like? This is a scenario extending beyond what can be explained by BDA insights, and it also presents the limit of how far BDA can bring us. Substantial business acumen is required to interfere in such a scenario to decode the mystery that BDA cannot. As T-Video’s CEO recalled:

Our algorithms can generate very accurate recommendations, but users seldom click those videos because they have already watched the recommended videos before using our app. So, high accuracy recommendation rate does not mean high hit rate. We need to bring in our understanding of users’ true needs to adjust the algorithms and reduce the side effect of those recommendation algorithms.

Thus, based on substantial business acumen, T-Video quickly arrived at an updated heuristic [Mitigate side effect of recommendation algorithms by incorporating business sense of users’ needs] by adding “users’ needs” as a new cue and adding “positive and linear”

as a new relation. In this case, decision makers realized that the criteria for BDA techniques (e.g., high accuracy) are not always good proxies for business success (e.g., high hit rate). Overemphasizing the former may hinder the alignment between BDA techniques and business success. Consequently, T-Video synthesized its business acumen with BDA to adapt its heuristics further. Notably, because of the high levels of environmental dynamism, cue-patching and relation-conditioning appeared in the hybrids.

In summary, we found evidence of humans’ use of substantial business acumen to adapt heuristics to overcome BDA’s limitation in complex, dynamic environments through alternative-reorienting or a hybrid mode of cue-patching and relation-conditioning. We thus present our fourth finding:

Finding 4: When environmental complexity and dynamism are both high, BDA may reach its limit; in this case, substantial business acumen is required to interfere with BDA to further adapt heuristics through alternative-reorienting or a hybrid of cue-patching and relation-conditioning.

## 5 A New Theoretical Model

In this section, we develop a new theoretical model, as shown in Figure 5, to explain the underlying mechanisms as to (1) why and how BDA shapes heuristics adaptation through certain mode(s) when only a single dimension of environmental uncertainty is high (i.e., either high complexity or high dynamism) and (2) why and how humans must employ substantial business acumen to interfere with BDA in order to shape heuristics adaptation when environmental uncertainty is high in both dimensions (i.e., high complexity and dynamism).

## 5.1 Environmental Complexity and the Hybrid C&R Mode

First, we explain why and how BDA shapes heuristics adaptation through a hybrid mode of cue-patching and relation-conditioning (C&R) under high environmental complexity, as depicted in Panel A of Figure 5. When environmental complexity is low, the relations between cues and alternatives are mostly linear, and decision makers can easily decompose the whole system into separate parts. Accordingly, it is not difficult for decision makers to employ simplified cognitive representations (e.g., heuristics) as relatively good proxies of objective reality. In this circumstance, BDA helps uncover additional cues or relations that become available after using BDA. In essence, because of low interdependency, the additional cues and relations are often detected separately instead of being discovered as a hybrid.

In contrast, when environmental complexity is high, it is essential to identify the set of critical cues and reveal the underlying nonlinear mechanisms between cues and alternatives, as well as the conditions under which they may become decoupled. However, the nonlinear interactions between cues and alternatives and their contingencies are hardly apprehensible for decision makers who, in their simplified cognitive representations (e.g., heuristics), tend to rely on linear thinking and oversimplify objective reality (Bettis, 2017; Manson, 2001). In other words, it is challenging for decision makers to fully map the nonlinear interdependency between cues and alternatives. More often than not, decision makers simply (erroneously) assume a linear relation when it is quadratic or of a higher order relation, consider interacted cues as isolated, or neglect important relations between cues and alternatives. In these circumstances, decision makers would miss critical cues and relations and use wrong cues and false relations in their heuristics. By enabling a C&R hybrid mode of heuristics adaptation, BDA can help decision makers decompose the intricate, nonlinear relations between cues and alternatives and focus on the most critical cues as the intricate relations unfold. As such, BDA helps decision makers rectify wrong cues and false relations simultaneously.

Furthermore, when determining if decision quality has improved due to the changes of cues and relations in complex environments, BDA maintains the original set of alternatives as a constant baseline. This means that BDA evaluates the effectiveness of any refinements in cues and relations against the unmodified original alternatives. We refer to this steadfast reliance on an unchanged component of heuristics as the “pinning mechanism.” By keeping one component of heuristics unaltered (e.g., original alternatives), BDA creates a fixed point of comparison that is crucial for evaluating changes to other components (e.g., cues and relations). Without this pinning in place, BDA would not have a benchmark, making it challenging to assess progress or the extent of changes made to the cues and relations within the algorithm. The pinning mechanism is a novel concept in the literature on heuristics, distinguishing BDA from human decision makers. While BDA depends on pinning to hold certain elements constant for comparative purposes, humans typically find this challenging. When faced with the combinatorial explosion in heuristics components, humans lack the capacity to accurately discern or control changes to specific components and to understand the subsequent implications for the heuristics. In this light, the pinning mechanism explains how BDA and human approaches to refining heuristics diverge.

## 5.2 Environmental Dynamism and Alternative-Reorienting

Second, we explain why and how BDA shapes heuristics adaptation through an alternativereorienting mode under high environmental dynamism, as depicted in Panel B of Figure 5. In stable environments, where states remain steady, decision makers typically find it easy to anticipate future states when constructing their heuristics.

In contrast, increased environmental dynamism makes it more difficult for decision makers to determine whether the alternatives of a heuristic still hold true from time t to time t+1. With rapid shifts in such dynamic settings, the body of evidence upon which an alternative was raised at time t might have become completely irrelevant by time t+1; therefore, an alternative that was valid at time t could become invalid at time t+1 (Feduzi et al., 2022). The greater the dynamism, the higher the likelihood of abrupt discontinuities and turbulence in a decision problem, rendering existing alternatives invalid or irrelevant. New, unforeseen alternatives may suddenly arise, overturning the previous assumptions held by decision makers. BDA allows decision makers to swiftly recognize when existing alternatives have reached their limits and to redress them accordingly. Essentially, BDA helps decision makers discard invalid alternatives and discover new, more promising alternatives.

Furthermore, to assess whether decision-making quality improves as a result of updated alternatives when environmental dynamism is high, BDA employs the pinning mechanism, whereby it sticks to the initial cues and relations as a benchmark to compare the performance gap between the original and newly adopted alternatives. Without this pinning to existing cues or relations, BDA lacks a foundational reference point from which to gauge the extent and nature of changes made to the alternatives within the algorithm. Again, BDA’s heavy use of the pinning mechanism explains the differences between it and humans in shaping the adaptation of heuristics.

## 5.3 When Environmental Complexity Meets Environmental Dynamism

Third, we explain why and how BDA calls for substantial business acumen to adapt heuristics when environmental uncertainty is high in both dimensions, as shown in Panel C of Figure 5.

In complex and dynamic environments, decision makers are faced with the challenging task of discerning intricate, nonlinear relations among different cues and deciding on the relevance of various alternatives. It can lead to the adoption of heuristics that are susceptible to invalid alternatives, wrong cues, and false relations, all at the same time. Moreover, owing to high environmental complexity and dynamism, the pace of business progress can exceed the speed at which the available data are collected and analyzed. Alternatives may still take shape, cues may be scarce, and relations between cues and alternatives may not emerge. Consequently, BDA encounters significant obstacles when attempting to employ any sort of pinning mechanism. It hinders the ability of BDA to recommend changes that are coherent with the underlying business logic, regardless of whether these changes are based on BDA feedback or BDA requirements.

First, with respect to changes based on BDA feedback, our inductive analysis revealed that when there is a misalignment with the fundamental business logic, following BDA feedback can generate outcomes that deviate from expectations and are challenging to interpret. This misalignment requires a substantial level of business acumen to make sense of the underlying business logic and critically interpret BDA feedback. For example, reflecting upon the situation of T-Video, which operated in a nascent industry, T-Video’s BDA feedback recommended the inclusion of short videos because BDA found a positive correlation between short videos and an uptick in users’ sharing activities. Yet strategic decisions predicated on this BDA feedback proved ineffective because the detected correlation was merely superficial: A heightened sharing rate did not necessarily translate into prolonged user engagement—a more critical metric reflecting user retainment, which is the essential business logic.

Secondly, regarding changes based on BDA requirements, if the underlying link between BDA requirements and business logic is not thoroughly understood, adhering strictly to those BDA requirements may come at the expense of business success. Looking again at the case of T-Video, which adhered to the requirements of BDA algorithms to enhance the accuracy of video recommendations to the greatest extent possible, although the algorithms were successful in predicting the videos that would appeal to users, the recommendations often included videos that users had already watched. As a result, the accuracy of recommendations did not necessarily translate into increased click-through rates—a critical metric reflecting user interest, which is another essential business logic. This is similar to Luca et al.’s (2016, p. 124) observation that “the algorithms are optimizing for a goal that can be measured in the moment—whether a user clicks on a link—without regard to the longer-range and more important goal of keeping users satisfied with their experience on the site.”

Therefore, when environmental complexity and dynamism are high, decision makers are prompted to engage in deliberate reflection regarding their use of BDA tools. They must critically assess whether the BDA feedback or requirements are in harmony with the underlying business logic rather than depending exclusively on BDA or heuristics for strategic directions. In other words, “one recognizes and consciously attempts to go beyond one’s intuitive as well as purely algorithmic (systematic, but uncritical) responses to a situation” (Luoma & Martela, 2021, p. 7). By reassessing foundational assumptions and realigning BDA with business logic, decision makers can further adapt their heuristics to better inform their decision-making.

This leads to a noteworthy and thought-provoking question: In scenarios where the pinning mechanism of BDA is deemed unnecessary (e.g., in Panel C of Figure 5), how do human decision makers adapt their heuristics with the aid of BDA? Are the three modes of heuristics adaptation—alternative-reorienting, cue-updating, and relation-conditioning—still employed? Our findings substantiate the continued use of these modes by humans. In doing so, humans can effectively compare the changes made to their heuristics with those shaped by BDA, or they can easily incorporate changes from both sources into their heuristics. Take cue-patching as an example. Just as Artinger et al. (2015, pp. S46-S47) suggested, in inherently dynamic and complex environments, “models [BDA] are too consistent and, for instance, cannot pick up cues that might have high predictive validity but occur only rarely. Such cues can be readily identified by experts.” Through a cue-patching mode like BDA, decision makers can make the process of heuristics adaptation comparable between different sources (i.e., machines versus humans) and learn how to best synthesize multifaceted strands of knowledge. Therefore, when realigning BDA with business logic, humans can continue to adapt their heuristics through alternativereorienting, cue-updating, and relation-conditioning. This persistence, however, is not a result of the pinning mechanism; rather, it is driven by the need for comparison and integration of various sources of insights.

## 6 Discussion and Contributions

The BDA literature and the strategy literature have underscored the tension between strategic decision makers’ reliance on BDA and human heuristics (Davenport & Harris, 2007). Specifically, it remains unclear whether the involvement of BDA leads to changes in how humans use and update heuristics for strategic decision-making. Our multiple-case analysis addresses this important question and explores how BDA shapes the adaptation of heuristics in strategic decision-making processes, particularly under conditions of environmental uncertainty. Table 2 summarizes our findings, theoretical contributions, and managerial implications.

## 6.1 Theoretical Contributions

Our study makes three important contributions to prior literature. First, the literature on BDA has predominantly assumed that BDA aims to supplant human heuristics with “objective and measurable facts” (van den Broek et al., 2022, p. 1559) or impede the use of human heuristics by inundating them with a combinatorial explosion of heuristics components (namely alternatives, cues, and relations), which humans may struggle to manage. Our Finding 1 challenges these views by demonstrating that BDA does not replace or invalidate human heuristics; instead, it aids decision makers in refining their heuristics through alternative-reorienting, cue-patching, and relationconditioning. Furthermore, by introducing the concept of the “pinning mechanism” to the literature and highlighting BDA’s heavy use of pinning as central to the uniqueness of the BDA-shaped heuristics adaptation process, we clarify the differences between heuristics adaptation with and without BDA. In addition, our findings indicate that after using BDA, humans continue to follow BDA’s three modes of adaptation even when they are intentionally using business acumen to refine their heuristics. This suggests that BDA fundamentally reframes how humans learn and process information for decision-making. Therefore, our study reveals a significant and novel manner in which BDA modifies how human decision makers employ their heuristics.

Second, by revealing nuanced insights into how BDA shapes heuristics adaptation to uncertain environments, our study also contributes to the literature on IS and BDA. While prior IS studies have examined the various benefits of using BDA in strategic decision-making, whether BDA creates value in uncertain environments remains underexplored (Benbya et al., 2020; Golovianko et al. 2023; Lyytinen & Grover, 2017; Sharma et al., 2014; van den Broek et al., 2022). Adopting the lens of heuristics adaptation, we offer three important modes through which BDA enables organizations to refine their heuristics for more informed decision-making in uncertain environments (Finding 1). Specifically, our findings suggest that in complex environments, human heuristics may miss unseen cues and relations, both of which BDA can uncover simultaneously (Finding 2). This finding aligns with previous research asserting BDA’s superiority in clarifying nonlinear relations and pinpointing pertinent cues (van den Broek et al., 2022). However, it contradicts the view that humans resist integrating machine knowledge that disconfirms their prior knowledge (e.g., Bauer et al., 2023)—a discrepancy that may stem from context differences, such as theirs, which focuses on operational decisions, and ours, which focuses on strategic decisions. Moreover, our findings indicate that in dynamic environments, where suitable alternatives at time t may become outdated at time t+1, BDA enables decision makers to efficiently update

obsolete alternatives with new, relevant ones (Finding 3). This finding addresses the previously unclear role of BDA in dynamic environments (Golovianko et al., 2023; Sturm et al., 2021) and confirms that BDA creates strategic value by aiding organizations to adapt to such environments more effectively (Haefner et al., 2021; van den Broek et al., 2022). In addition, by differentiating the two dimensions of environmental uncertainty, we also enrich the understanding of the contingent value of BDA in strategic decision-making.

Third, our study contributes to the ongoing discourse on the roles of humans versus machines in decisionmaking in the IS literature (Abbasi et al., 2016; Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Dhar, 2013; Lyytinen & Grover, 2017). Prior studies have predominantly viewed human heuristics and BDA as two opposite ends of a spectrum, with the former being subjective and biased and the latter being objective and useful in mitigating cognitive bias (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014). Moreover, with the recent emergence of AI-driven BDA, some have even argued that machines, while not perfect, can be used to extend, augment, or assume human decision-making responsibilities (Ågerfalk et al., 2022; Demetis & Lee, 2018; Dietvorst, Simmons, & Massey, 2018; Fügener et al., 2021). While it has been increasingly suggested that human-machine joint decision-making may be able to achieve superior outcomes (Raisch & Fomina, 2024; Sturm et al., 2021; van den Broek et al., 2022), the effective approaches and critical boundaries for human-machine symbiosis remain underexplored (Golovianko et al., 2023). It is not clear, for example, “given that there is a natural tension between data and intuition (Davenport & Harris, 2007), what is the ideal balance between data and intuition? What kind of theory can inform this balancing act?” (Abbasi et al., $2 0 1 6 , \mathsf { p . x i } )$ . Answers to these questions can shed light on the situations in which decisions can or cannot be delegated to machines, as well as the underlying reasons for these limitations (Golovianko et al., 2023).

Our study contributes to these inquiries by demonstrating a method whereby machines can be used jointly with humans—specifically, through influencing humans’ adaptation of heuristics—to improve decision-making, and by highlighting environmental uncertainty as a key boundary condition that necessitates the synthesis of human heuristics and BDA. For example, our Findings 2-4 and the theoretical model (Figure 5) suggest that in complex (or dynamic) environments, BDA is valued for its ability to rectify human biases, implying that delegating strategic decisions to machines is possible. Nevertheless, if the environment is both complex and dynamic, close collaboration between humans and BDA becomes necessary, which raises concerns about the effectiveness of delegation to machines and automation. In such situations, our findings resonate with Tremblay et al.’s (2021) concept of “theories in flux”—described as “evidence-based inferences that emerge from analyzing large amounts of data” and with large amounts of “managerial sense-making” (p. 456). Consequently, our study delineates the circumstances under which BDAs might be more, or less, effective, contributing to the discourse on the interplay and integration of human and machine capabilities in decision-making within the IS literature.

## 6.2 Managerial Implications

Our study highlights critical implications for organizations seeking to harness BDA for strategic decision-making but unsure of its optimal use. Some organizations might overly rely on BDA’s seemingly objective insights, sidelining valuable human heuristics perceived as subjective. Others may undervalue BDA, treating it merely as an adjunct to business acumen. Our research clarifies this tension, underscoring that neither BDA nor human heuristics should be dismissed; in uncertain environments, BDA and human heuristics work intricately in tandem to inform strategic decisions.

Specifically, our findings suggest that if an organization operates in an environment rife with sudden turbulence (i.e., high dynamism), such as those with rapid technological, customer, or competitive shifts, BDA can help decision makers validate the relevance of the alternatives of their heuristics (e.g., which international market to enter, in the example of the hypothetical US IT firm referred to above). In contrast, if an organization operates in an environment with nonlinear interdependencies (i.e., high complexity), BDA can help update the set of cues (e.g., market characteristics) and relations (e.g., linear or nonlinear) employed in human heuristics. In addition, if an organization operates in an environment that is both dynamic and complex, such as a nascent market environment, BDA alone may not align with business logic, necessitating strong business acumen to supplement BDA’s limitations. As Sturm et al. (2021) commented, even though reliance on data evidence can help organizations in uncertain environments, “this requires significant investments in the initial setup of these systems as well as adequately coordinating them with humans” (p. 1581) to form “a deep and current problem understanding” (p. 1597). The merits of humans and machines should be integrated to create strategic value.

In addition, our findings underscore the importance of both BDA feedback and the requirements for adapting heuristics, although many organizations value the former but overlook the latter. It is crucial to engage BDA experts early in the decision-making process to understand the capabilities and limitations of BDA, rather than relying solely on retrospective data analysis, which is a common practice. To navigate big data’s challenges and opportunities, many organizations adopt a “hub and spokes” structure (Jackson, 2009) with an independent data department—the hub—led by the CAO and staffed with data analysts. Surrounding “spokes” represent business functions like sales, marketing, and production, which typically pull data (often as a postmortem query) from the hub to aid strategic decision-making. Instead, our findings strongly advocate for the involvement of big data analysts from the hub in the spokes at the initial stage of decisionmaking, rather than only for postmortem queries.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

We acknowledge some limitations that call for future research. First, while our findings suggest that BDA helps decision makers adapt their heuristics in three modes and reveal certain hybrid modes (e.g., a C&R hybrid), it also raises questions about how different adaptation modes interrelate. For example, does a hybrid mode arise in particular uncertainty scenarios due to a causal mechanism of the two modes (e.g., one serving as a prerequisite for the other) or their constitutive causality (e.g., the two modes complement each other, see Markus & Rowe, 2018)? Investigating these questions could illuminate how various BDAshaped adaptation modes interact and how they might be optimally employed in decision-making.

Second, our findings suggest that synthesizing human heuristics with BDA can achieve superior outcomes compared to employing either alone, acknowledging that both have inherent limitations. We have elucidated the underlying mechanisms necessitating such synthesis in complex and dynamic environments. Nevertheless, beyond environmental uncertainty, are there any other boundary conditions for this synthesized effect, such as decision makers’ background, organizational learning capabilities, types of BDA technologies, or BDA usage levels? Future research in these directions could significantly enrich our understanding of BDA’s contingent value in strategic decision-making.

## 7 Conclusion

BDA is increasingly regarded as valuable in guiding organizations’ strategic decision-making and improving business performance in uncertain environments (Grover et al., 2018). However, researchers and practitioners continue to debate the tension between humans and machines and between human heuristics and BDA (Davenport & Harris, 2007; Lebovitz et al., 2021; van den Broek et al., 2022). Challenging prior views that BDA replaces human heuristics or impedes humans’ use of heuristics, our study reveals that BDA changes how human decision makers use (particularly adapt) heuristics and offers theoretical accounts of why different BDA-shaped adaptation modes are useful for addressing the different types and varying levels of environmental uncertainty, making impactful contributions to the BDA literature. Moreover, by uncovering how BDA and human approaches differ in adapting heuristics, notably through BDA’s heavy use of the pinning mechanism, our study enriches the understanding of BDA’s role in strategic decision-making. In addition, our study also adds new insights to the debate between humans and machines by suggesting that BDA has limitations in complex and dynamic environments and requires substantial business acumen to complement it for heuristics adaptation. In sum, this study lays an important foundation for a theory explaining how BDA shapes heuristics adaptation for strategic decision-making in various contexts of environmental uncertainty. This paves the way for future research to further enhance the understanding of the dynamics between BDA, human heuristics, and strategic decision-making.

Figure A1. Heuristics Adaptation at S-Social <sup>a</sup>

S-Social’s NPD project, a new version of the app to enhance user experience, was triggered by a performance gap in which “many functionalities are low in KPIs.” They quickly developed S1.1 to select 10 KPIs from hundreds of indexes. BDA added a new KPI (S1.2) that could predict how active a user would be well before other KPIs took effect. When contemplating how to rank the popularity of functionalities, S-Social initially chose a simple but visual approach (S2.1), and BDA added further insight (S2.2). Realizing the difference between single functionality performance and overall app performance, they further developed S2.3. To decide which functionalities to drop, S-Social started with S3.1, which was later adapted by BDA to become S3.2. Regarding launch time, S-Social followed S4.1 because “In the past, Ramadan is a good time for new product releases in Saudi Arabia because users have a lot of time to play with our app.” However, BDA revealed new cues and adapted the heuristic to S4.2.

Figure B1. Heuristics Adaptation at X-Payment<sup>a</sup>

Realizing that “the chargeback rate of overseas payment by buyers is higher than the industry average,” X-Payment embarked on an NPD project to develop an automated risk-control system for overseas payments that involved domestic buyers, overseas sellers, and banks. Whose information to start with: buyers, overseas sellers, or banks? They developed X1.1 and started with seller information. Soon they realized that seller information could be used to collaborate with banks, resulting in X1.2. Among more than 200 indexes, they created X2.1 to rely on internal data to construct 30 risk indexes from more than 200 indexes. Soon after, using external data from banks, they were able to improve it to X2.2. When assigning weights of indexes, they underscored, “We cannot afford to wait for data to be totally ready and analyzed. We must work without enough data.” As such, they first decided on X3.1. Very soon, the quickly accumulated data enabled this heuristic to adapt to X3.2 and X3.3.
