# Designing an evaluation framework for IT service management

## A B S T R A C T

Applying IT Service Management (ITSM) is a key issue in the management of an organisation’s IT function. The IT Infrastructure Library (ITIL) is the most popular and influential framework for applying ITSM. With adoption growing globally it is important to understand the benefits that ITIL processes can bring to an organisation. To date the benefits of implementing and using ITIL processes have been predicted or assumed with very little research and minimal anecdotal evidence. We present a design of a holistic evaluation framework for ITSM improvement efforts with particular focus on ITIL.

## 1. Introduction

IT Service Management (ITSM) is a key issue today; the traditional function of IT management (hardware and software installation, network/systems, applications management, and the help desk) now includes business-oriented service support, in which IT services are planned and managed according to their contributions to required business processes.

The ITSM processes, as described in the Information Technology Infrastructure Library (ITIL) are: Incident Management, Problem Management, Release Management, Change Management, Configuration Management, Service Level Management, Financial Management of IT Services, Capacity Management, IT Service Continuity Management, and Availability Management [10].

Industry has been responsive to ITIL with a variety of companies now running training courses, providing certification, and consulting. As organisations push for better IT governance and more efficient IT functions, the ITIL framework is one solution. To date there are 50,000 ITIL certified professionals worldwide and 24.1% of IT managers in large organisations are familiar with the ITIL standards.

The key benefits that an organisation expects from an ITIL implementation are: alignment of IT services with current and probable future business needs [8], improved quality of IT services, and a reduction in the long term costs of service provision. These benefits, however, have not been demonstrated through practical research. To date, the benefits have been predicted or assumed [2,6], though basic surveys have been conducted by organisations. Despite the positive statements of organisations who have adopted ITIL, there is a lack of quantifiable data on the impact that ITIL will have on organisations who have adopted it.

Forrester Research reported in 2004 that widespread adoption of ITIL best practices by internal IT departments would soon be evident, with implementation occurring over the following decade. However, many organisations that have started implementing ITIL processes do not seem to be aware of the tangible or intangible benefits that this can bring [7]; according to one study, only a third of these organisations claimed to have a general methodology for IT benefits computation.

What is therefore needed is a holistic, prescriptive, multidimensional framework with both objective and subjective means of assessing ITIL benefits from a variety of perspectives. We therefore decided to work to provide such a methodology. Our goal is to develop a practical, holistic evaluation framework that is applicable to ITSM improvement efforts.

## 2. Overview of ITSM and ITIL

During the last two decades a number of ITSM-related management frameworks have been developed to help managers improve IT operations. The frameworks have mainly been proprietary in nature and company specific, e.g. Microsoft’s Operations Framework, the IBM Systems Management Solutions Lifecycle, and the HP IT Service Management Reference Model.

![](/api/attachments/7QMBS5SG/fulltext/images/b4cf1cdbcb36570fc30a8c02eec5c637fe33cf9add66b2a3a4f2011ca8026524.jpg)  
Fig. 1. The ITIL framework.

However, the Information Technology Infrastructure Library (ITIL) has become the most popular and influential. It is a collection of defined and published best practice processes for ITSM, developed in 1989 by the British government in their Central Computer Telecommunications Agency (now the Office of Government Commerce), in an effort to improve the management and efficiency of IT. The IT Service Management Forum, a non profit association, now owns and supports this framework, with chapters in many countries around the world; with no defined international standards for ITSM, it is likely to become the de facto industry standard. It is being predicted that ITIL will become an ISO standard by the end of the decade.

The framework of ITIL is shown in Fig. 1 with seven process modules situated between the business and the technology. The layout of the figure indicates the relationship among the seven processes, e.g. Application Management is viewed from the standpoint of both Business and Technology. The core of ITIL is Service Management, made up of Service Support and Service Delivery. These are the two most commonly implemented areas of the framework and each of them breaks down into a set of defined processes; they are shown in Fig. 2 for the Service Support module.

## 3. Related evaluation frameworks

There have been several evaluation frameworks proposed and used; they were intended to provide improvement of Service Quality, IT functional assessment, and evaluation of IT benefits. We evaluated their suitability as a tool for ITIL. A summary of our analysis is shown in Table 1. This left us with the questions:

\- Which evaluation framework is best?

\- Should IT managers select one based on their specific needs, or rather some combination of them?

\- Would it be best for an organisation to create its own methods and tools for evaluation, or contract consultants?

We decided that we should combine common elements from all; expand some elements to make them more prescriptive and specific to ITIL.

In addition, the existing evaluation frameworks were weak because they did not consider the degree to which ITIL best practices contributed to crucial business processes. For example, almost all business processes would include steps that depend on specific local IT resources or practices, and thus the mapping from the IT resource or practice to the business process and its monitoring were good indicators of the degree to which all business processes were supported. Our analysis showed that no evaluation framework provided this capability, though the SLA Comparisons method could be helpful. The problems with this method, however, were that the SLA was rather thin compared to ITIL best practices and its level of prescription was low.

## 4. The research methodology: design research

Our goal was to design a holistic evaluation framework for ITIL and then assess, test, and validate it to determine its usefulness. Thus our goal was not to design a framework to evaluate business outcomes, but to design a framework for evaluating the extent to which ITIL best practices are successful and support business processes. This implies that there are parameters that determine business outcomes as well as IT practices. A design research approach of was taken to create the framework and a contextual inquiry of industry experts was the method used to assess it.

Design research involves of a set of analytic techniques and perspectives for research in IS [3,5]. It involves the creation of an artefact, such as a software tool, algorithm, user interface, system development methodology, or conceptual framework. Once it has been designed, it is evaluated against its defined criteria or quality attributes to ensure that it complies or performs better than previous designs. Interpretivism views the world as possible multiple realities that have been constructed by design researchers considering the world to be multiple states that are sociotechnologically enabled; each state can be evaluated with respect to its validity and quality attributes. Such is the spirit of our development of the ITIL evaluation framework.

## 5. Development of the evaluation framework for ITIL

Our proposed evaluation framework for ITIL is shown in Fig. 4. It is intended for ITIL evaluation, but has general concepts and building blocks that make it applicable to other ITSM improvement efforts. It is a guide that is relatively easy to use. Quantifiable metrics were used as objective measures and surveys were intended to be issued within the organisation as a way to provide the subjective measures. In addition, the four perspectives of management, technology, IT users, and IT employees were considered to be the way to determine the benefits and value promised by ITIL.

![](/api/attachments/7QMBS5SG/fulltext/images/e17030677a7129df3bb4cef879e2928cffa9ec43229cc6943b8f8180a9396c34.jpg)  
Fig. 4. Development of the ITIL evaluation framework.

The framework is intended to be used by management when initiating ITIL improvement and change efforts. It should be used to evaluate the change, perform benefit realisation, conduct performance assessment, and direct future improvement. It consists of:

\- Four perspectives of evaluation—management, technology, IT users, and IT employees.

\- Two levels of abstraction: corporate and process levels.

\- The use of IS adapted SERVQUAL.

\- The use of an IS adapted Reverse SERVQUAL and comparison with IS adapted SERVQUAL.

\- A range of drafted metrics for each ITIL process, consisting of three types—Efficiency, Effectiveness, and Capability.

\- A range of drafted survey questions for each ITIL process and ITIL best practice.

\- A summarising scoring system which incorporates:

\* A methodology for scoring metrics.

\* A methodology for scoring survey questions.

\* Their application and comparison over time.

The four perspectives of evaluation were developed based on the predicted benefit of applying or improving ITSM within the organisation using ITIL. These four included:

\- Management Perspective: the highest level and or executive management in the organisation, included to ensure that areas such as financial impact, business impact, and the collective user experience are properly considered in the evaluation.

\- Technology Perspective: that of IT/IS management and the IT department as a whole. It ensures a positive effect on areas such as technological efficiency, IT personnel and resource efficiency, and specific IT related costs and budgets.

\- IT Users Perspective: that of the individuals who need the systems, technology, equipment, products and services of the IT department on a daily basis to support business processes. Users also interact with the IT department via means such as the IT Help or Service Desk. This is included to ensure that the effect on areas such as IT service quality, expectations, and perceptions are considered as it relates to their work.

\- IT Employees Perspective: that of the personnel within the IT department, especially operational staff affected by ITIL-related change. This may include first and second level support staff, network administrators, security personnel, database administrators, and application owners; they are the workers who directly deal with the implementation of new processes and changes. This perspective serves as a check on the validity of the IT user perspective and vice versa; these perspectives should be comparable.

These four perspectives of evaluation must be considered at: the generic corporate and a process specific levels. The first is intended to capture and evaluate the overall benefits from the ITu. improvement, e.g. in service quality, the culture of service provisioning, user satisfaction, and operations. Survey questions were created and distributed based on IS SERVQUAL in order to assess user perceptions of service quality and their satisfaction with the IT services. Survey questions were also given to assess the

IT Employee perspective through an IS Reverse SERVQUAL to look back internally at their perceptions on the quality of service provided by those giving the service to see whether ITIL was instilling the notion of a service culture into the organisation’s IT department and its employees.

The process level of the evaluation framework is more detailed and process specific, with additional metrics and survey questions that parallel those of the four perspectives in each area of the ITIL improvement effort or key process area. Its focus is on the benefits of each of the processes being implemented, changed, or improved. Metrics and survey questions were designed to elicit both tangible and intangible benefits. By having management evaluate and assess performance from the four perspectives at this lower process level, an integrated and holistic view of performance will be produced. The information should also be valuable by provoking continual improvement, directing resources into lagging areas, and for stimulating individual process owners.

Objectivity is needed when an organisation intends to adopt ITIL best practices. This means that those surveyed must express their opinions without distortion or personal bias and that comments must be based on observable phenomena. Metrics are specific indicators that are stated to show progress or achievement; they are based on simple descriptive statistics, such as percentages or means, etc. In the process level of the evaluation framework three types of metrics are:

\- Effectiveness: the performance of an attribute relative to a target or standard.

\- Capability: the shape distribution of performance of the attribute.

\- Efficiency: the cost of delivering a service, resource usage and time.

Useable metrics have been provided for each of the ITIL processes; they measure the benefits and outcomes of the overall process rather than acting as specific performance metrics used to monitor and control a process. The metrics in Table 2 illustrate those for the ITIL Release Management process. Others have been drafted. Organisations will differ in the information they can obtain and wish to track; therefore managers can pick and choose from these metrics. The document titled ‘‘The Evaluation Framework for ITIL: Metrics, Survey Questions and Sample Output’’ is available upon request.

In addition to objective means of assessment, subjective means help to balance and complete the picture. Our framework uses questionnaires to capture and quantify subjective opinions. Rating and summated scales are used and response values can be assessed allowing for numerical comparison over time.

Because the process level will be lengthy and detailed, it is important to be able to summarise the amount of information so that top managers can view it while retaining details for lower level mangers, key employees, and process owners. To achieve this, a system of self defined weights for each process and survey question scoring was used. Each perspective at the process level could then be represented by a single value for easy comparison over time.

The complete methodology for evaluating ITIL requires repetition of the evaluation over time to make it valuable for diagnosis, performance measurement, benefits realisation, and resource direction. Thus the framework should be applied before any change or implementation is made to determine a baseline. The same elements should then be applied in the middle and towards the end of the implementation to assess any changes to them; this should then be re-applied after an extended period (e.g. 6 months to a year) to ensure that continual improvement is occurring and that the newly implemented processes continue to bring value and benefit to the organisation. The same measures could be used on a more regular basis and tracked over time to assist in continual improvement, performance assessment, monitoring and control of new ITIL processes.

## 6. Assessing the framework by using contextual inquiry

Contextual inquiry is used in systems development to allow analysts and developers to gauge a better understanding of user requirements and work processes by observing and interviewing users performing their daily work. It allows discussion among designers and users to go beyond surface abstractions by using artefacts to uncover details. The approach combines the benefits of observational approaches and standard face-to-face interview. Researchers are now using this approach to conduct qualitative research and evaluate prototypes as an interactive exploration of the issues rather than normal interviews [11].

By using contextual inquiry, we can determine what problems might exist in a prototype; it could also be viewed as a pre-test or exploratory study aimed at capturing relevant items in the questionnaire and clarifying its wording. It is therefore appropriate to use for improving a new evaluation framework for ITIL by ascertaining whether it will be accurate, useful, and easy to use.

The contextual inquiry used four experts from industry: see Table 3 for their background. The interviews included the following steps.

1. Traditional Interview and Context Building: The session started by the member explaining his or her work environment. This was intended to establish trust; consent forms were signed. We then provided context for our evaluation framework (background on ITSM and ITIL, existing methodologies, and an explanation and demonstration of our new evaluation framework).

2. The Switch: Here we told the expert that he or she was the ‘master’ with us being ‘apprentices’. The expert was told that we were trying to learn from his or her knowledge and experience.

3. Observation: The expert now imparted knowledge through example and discussion. We merely maintained the focus of the session and asked questions as needed.

4. Summarisation: We summarised what we had learned from the inquiry and the expert gave feedback to ensure accuracy and that the message had been understood.

The discussions focused the experts on an agenda of questions relevant to ITSM, ITIL best practice, quality attributes of the framework, and modifications and directions needed. The discussion questions posed to the four experts were:

1. Discuss the need for evaluation and benefits realisation in relation to ITSM improvement efforts e.g. ITIL implementations. Feel free to reflect on your own experiences.

2. Does the evaluation framework presented fill this need?

3. Are all of the concepts in the evaluation framework understandable?

4. Discuss usefulness of the framework. (Consider the 4 perspectives, the first corporate level, the process level, the metrics and survey questions, the weighting system for the process level, and the appropriateness for ITIL.)

5. Discuss the ease of use of the framework. (Consider calculating the metrics, administering surveys, analysing results, presenting and using the results, summarising the scoring system for the process level, and establishing weights.)

6. Would you use this evaluation framework?

7. What can be changed or improved with the evaluation framework?

8. Discuss whether the framework would be useful for general ITSM improvement projects/initiatives or other ITSM frameworks.

9. Discuss weather the typical organisational scenario is relevant and accurate to the presentation and understanding of the framework.

10. What is your opinion on the validity of the metrics—both corporate level and process level?

11. What is your opinion on the survey questions—best practice for corporate level IT employee perspective and all process level questions?

Interviews were recorded and transcripts created. A thematic analysis of the interviews uncovered seven key topics for which there was a clear, though imperfect consensus.

A summary of the seven topics was:

There is a need for an evaluation framework for ITSM. All strongly agreed.

The evaluation framework would be useful. All but one agreed, but they were not 100% confident of its usefulness. Two gave it 8 out of 10, one gave it 7 out of 10, and one was non-committal.

The ease of use was undecided. Although this was a common topic, the responses were mixed. One expert gave it 6 out of 10. One simply said it was straightforward. One stated that the framework would be easier to use than ITIL itself. One argued that the framework would benefit from automation with a spreadsheet having built-in macros or a web-based automation to mask complexity; otherwise it would be hard to use.

The evaluation framework could be used outside ITIL. All agreed that it could be used for other ITSM evaluations, but differed on their reasons. One argued that only the corporate-level evaluation was reusable. Two argued that all evaluation aspects were reusable. One saw the framework as being useful for a prior needs assessment before making a decision on whether to implement ITIL.

The metrics and survey questions should serve as a guide rather than providing a prescriptive content. All agreed that the framework was at the right level and should be used as a guideline for organisations, but that specific metrics would be organisation-dependent.

There are some ambiguous concepts in the framework. Each expressed lack of clarity in some of the concepts, but there was no consensus. One found it hard to distinguish between the technology and the management perspectives. One found it hard to distinguish between the IT employee and technology perspectives. And one suggested that the IT employee perspective be renamed as the IT service perspective.

There is a need now to test the framework in real organisations. All agreed that the framework was sufficiently mature to test in a real environment. A comment by one expert was: ‘‘I’m willing to facilitate this at my organisation. I like the look of what you’ve done and would like to try it out.’’

## 7. Discussion and conclusion

Our use of contextual inquiry interviews resulted in a partial validation of the ideas and concepts of the proposed evaluation framework for ITIL. It is clear that the framework fills an existing need, can be used for ITIL evaluation, and be modified for use in broader change projects. The metrics and survey questions could be a useful guide for managers using the framework.

It can be used more broadly for evaluating general ITSM improvement efforts but will require some modification. It aids in performance assessment, benefits realisation, finding areas for IT service improvement, and directing resources.

The contribution of our work to academia is that our evaluation framework provides a good step towards developing an improved holistic evaluation framework for ITSM. Although further proof-ofconcept testing is needed, our work is a step in the process to developing better means of evaluation for ITIL and ITSM.

The contribution of our work to industry is that the framework as partially validated is useful to IT managers involved in ITIL implementations, ITSM improvement efforts, and IT services. It is useable in organisations; however, there are no guarantees or claims that can be made on how well it will perform as it has not yet been used. As one expert suggested, automating parts of the evaluation framework by building an Excel<sup>TM</sup> spreadsheet with built-in macros would make the framework easier to use as well as mask its complexity and size.

Lundy Lewis is Professor and Chair of the department of Computer Information Technology at Southern New Hampshire University and the Chief Technology Officer at Altusys Corp. He received the MS degree in Computer Science from Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute and the PhD in Philosophy from the University of Georgia, specializing in logic and artificial intelligence. Previously he held positions as Director of Research at Aprisma Management Technologies/Cabletron Systems and Technical Director at General Dynamics Electric Boat Division. He holds 25 US patents and has 14 patents pending in the areas of alarm correlation, policy management, information security, and situation man agement. He has published three books on network and service management and has authored over 70 technical publications.
