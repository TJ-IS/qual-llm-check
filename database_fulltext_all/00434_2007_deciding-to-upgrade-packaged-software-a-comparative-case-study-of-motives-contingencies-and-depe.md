# Deciding to upgrade packaged software: a comparative case study of motives, contingencies and dependencies

Huoy Min Khoo<sup>1</sup> and Daniel Robey<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

Packaged software is widely adopted and has become an integral part of most organizations’ IT portfolios. Once packaged software is adopted, upgrades to subsequent versions appear to be inevitable. To date, research on packaged software has not paid attention to the upgrade decision process. To explore this area, we studied cases of upgrades to two widely used packaged software products (SAP and Windows) in a multi-billion dollar division of a Fortune 500 company. From an analysis of the cases, we induced a theoretical model explaining the influences affecting the decisions to upgrade. Our model suggests that upgrade decisions are the outcome of interaction between motivating forces that can originate from internal and external environments, and contingency forces. Business need was found to be one of the key internal requirements, along with organizational risk mitigation policies. Organizational reliance on packaged software also creates dependence on vendors that gives them influence over the organization’s upgrade decision. The decision to upgrade is also contingent on the availability of internal resources. Although scarcity of internal resources can postpone an upgrade when the need to upgrade is deferrable, organizations will prioritize internal resources when the upgrade is deemed necessary. ## Introduction

Packaged software is widely understood to be ‘an application program developed for sale to the general public’ (Software Package, 2005). Although packaged software can be customized to some degree, organizations adopting packaged software do not have the same freedom to customize software packages as they do with proprietary software programs, which are ‘made-to-order systems and are typically built for specific users’ (Sawyer, 2000). Packaged software is typically developed and marketed as a solution to problems that software users experience in developing and using proprietary software (Light, 2005). Indeed, enterprise resource planning (ERP) software solutions became a way for many corporations to reengineer core business process during the 1990s while avoiding the time and expense needed to develop their own software. Packaged software has become a ubiquitous technology for personal users as well as large organizations. According to International Data

Corporation, the combined market for all packaged software was \$179 billion in 2004 (Anonymous, 2005). Despite this enormous economic impact, academic research has largely neglected packaged software, with the exception of ERP systems.

One of the most neglected issues related to packaged software is the decision to upgrade from one version to another. According to Microsoft’s support web page, ‘An upgrade is a software package that replaces an installed version of a product with a newer version of the same product. The upgrade process typically leaves existing customer data and preferences intact while replacing the existing software with the newer version’ (Upgrade, 2006). As software vendors enhance their products to respond to anticipated business needs, they release upgrades with more features and better performance. Although performance improvements are not necessarily anticipated in an upgrade, it is reasonable to expect organizations to upgrade software packages for the purpose of achieving benefits such as greater efficiency and improved functionality that the current software lacks (Paine, 2000; Beatty & Williams, 2006). To the user of software packages, deciding whether and when to upgrade from the current version requires an ongoing process of evaluation.

Although practical guidance to upgrade decisions has begun to appear in the literature (e.g., Beatty & Williams, 2006), most studies of packaged software have focused on initial adoption, and empirical research on packaged software upgrade decisions has been limited. In one of the rare studies of ERP maintenance and upgrade decisions, Ng (2001) found that maintenance and upgrades were adopted to realize increased business benefits and that cost was a major concern in those decisions. Although valuable, Ng’s study combined maintenance and upgrade decisions together making it difficult to understand the upgrade decision process independently.

Upgrades to packaged software appear to be inevitable for several reasons. First, packaged software vendors typically establish ‘sunset dates,’ after which vendors discontinue support for current versions. Thus, organizations that require vendor support are pressured to upgrade before the vendor’s sunset date. Although an organization may choose to operate unsupported software, packaged products eventually become incompatible with other software or require new functionality. Unless organizations develop their own software or find another source, their only option becomes upgrading to a new, vendor-supported version. However, organizations do not need to upgrade to every new version of software because vendors typically support multiple versions at the same time. Thus, choices must be made, making it important to study how organizations decide to migrate to the next version of software.

In this paper, we report a comparative case study of two upgrade decisions at a Fortune 500 company in the United States. The study was exploratory because there is not much understanding of the packaged software upgrade phenomenon. Our research questions are:

1. What factors influence the decision to upgrade packaged software?

2. How do the factors that influence upgrade decisions interact?

Using an inductive research strategy and a comparative analysis of the cases, we construct a theoretical model to explain the factors influencing the decision to upgrade packaged software. We found that the decision to upgrade is influenced by the interaction between motivating forces, which originated from both internal needs (business and IT needs) and external dependence on software vendor, and internal resource availability. To understand software upgrade decisions, these factors must be considered in relationship to each other.

The paper is organized as follows. We begin with a review of areas of the information systems (IS) literature related to decisions to acquire and maintain IS. Although not dealing with software upgrades in particular, this literature provides background for our investigation. We then describe the method used in the exploratory study. Our results are presented for two cases: one involving a large ERP system and the other involving an operating system. In the Discussion section, we present a model of the upgrade decision induced from the cross-case analysis. In the concluding section, we offer implications of our findings for research and practice.

## Related literature

There is a large research literature on the reasons why organizations adopt information technology, and its findings are well known. The three most common reasons for adoption are (1) competitive advantage (Harrison et al., 1997; Watson et al., 1997), (2) cost reduction (Hinton & Kaye, 1996), and (3) productivity improvement (Ryan & Harrison, 2000). To the extent that packaged software upgrades are investments in information technology, these primary motivating factors should also influence upgrade decisions. However, unlike the more general case of new technology adoption, upgrading software is a decision to continue using software that has already been adopted, implemented and used. As a result, factors that are considered crucial for new technology adoption may be less relevant to the decision to upgrade. Although firms may need to adopt packages to keep up with competition, it is unlikely that a newer version of packaged software can provide one firm with an advantage over another. Thus, it appears that understanding packaged software upgrade decisions is not advanced very far by considering the traditional factors affecting decisions to adopt technology.

The research literature that is most closely related to packaged software upgrade is software maintenance. Kim & Westin (1988) defined software maintenance as ‘the activities performed on software after the programs have been installed.’ A typical proprietary software system receives corrective, adaptive, and perfective maintenance throughout its useful life until it is eventually replaced (Swanson & Beath, 1989). Swanson & Beath (1989) found that the lifecycle for software systems averaged about 12 years before they were replaced.

It is somewhat more difficult to gauge the lifecycle of packaged software and to situate upgrades within the lifecycle. If we consider a packaged software product’s lifecycle to begin when it is adopted and end when it is replaced by a different product, the intervening upgrades to new versions of the software can all be seen as maintenance activities. This view assumes that packaged software has an overall software lifecycle and that version upgrades are improvements to the package. An alternative view would consider each version of a software package to have its own lifecycle. From this perspective, an upgrade would be a maintenance task performed at the end of the lifecycle of an existing version of the software. For example, Nah et al. (2001) considered upgrades to ERP packages as a form of perfective maintenance performed at the maturity and decline stages of the existing version of ERP package.

The main commonality between maintenance and package upgrade decisions is the importance of responding to users’ business needs. Just as user demand for enhancements and extensions is maintenance’s most important problem (Lientz & Swanson, 1978), users may also demand upgrades to obtain functionality that an existing version of a package lacks. Thus maintenance and upgrades serve a similar functional purpose. However, there are two major differences between packaged software upgrades and traditional software maintenance. First, packaged software is developed by a vendor whereas IS studied in the maintenance literature are either custom-designed by in-house IT personnel (Hirt & Swanson, 2001) or outsourced to a vendor (Natovich, 2003). Second, packaged software upgrades usually replace the previous version of software with a new version, whereas maintenance usually targets specific functions that need to be corrected or perfected (Swanson & Beath, 1989). These differences between packaged software upgrade and traditional software maintenance are likely to make the decision processes different. For instance, maintenance of proprietary software developed in-house usually does not involve outside software vendors, who are more likely to play a major role in decisions about packaged software upgrades (Beatty & Williams, 2006). Even in the case of proprietary software outsourced to a vendor, requests for maintenance usually reflect organizational needs. For this reason, findings from research on software maintenance may not directly apply to upgrade decisions on software packages.

Software upgrade decisions may also be influenced by an organization’s strategic orientation toward new technology. In a study of strategic orientations toward technology, Maier et al. (1997) identified three types of information technology adoption patterns: exploiters or innovators, competitors or early adopters, and effective/ efficient followers. On the one hand, a firm that prides itself as an exploiter or innovator of technology will probably adopt the latest upgrade as soon as the new software is released in the market. On the other hand, a firm that considers itself as a follower of technology will probably wait to upgrade until the migration is absolutely necessary and the new version has been thoroughly tested by earlier adopters.

In addition to a firm’s own strategic orientation toward technology, upgrade decisions may be influenced by the environment (Lederer & Mendelow, 1990). The main implication of including environmental factors such as vendors in upgrade decisions is the need to consider risk factors not under the organization’s control. The most obviously relevant environmental influence is software vendors, who stand to profit by selling or licensing packaged software. Vendors actively market new versions well before their availability, promising features and functions that have not yet been built or properly tested. Such pre-announcements can affect the timing of customers’ decisions to upgrade or purchase (Hoxmeier, 2000). When critical software is no longer supported by the vendor, an organization is forced to upgrade to the next version. For these reasons, vendor relationships have begun to be incorporated into explanations of ERP upgrades and maintenance (Hirt & Swanson, 2001; Nah et al., 2001).

Taking all of these issues under consideration, it seems clear that upgrade decisions are more complex than they first appear. Given the exploratory nature of this study, the literature reviewed above is useful in identifying expected influences over decisions to upgrade packaged software. These include users’ needs for improved software performance, an organization’s strategic orientation toward new technology, and vendor dependence. Unfortunately, related literature provides an inadequate basis for formulating a research model a priori. Thus, we take these issues as a starting point and adopt an exploratory research strategy that allows us to derive a theoretical model inductively from original data.

## Method

A qualitative case study was deemed appropriate for studying software upgrade decisions in their natural organizational setting (Yin, 1984). A single site, comparative case study was used to allow us to examine upgrade decisions for two different packaged software products: SAP 4.6 and Windows 2000. The comparative research design was devised to leverage the similarities and differences between the two cases in order to construct a theoretical model of the factors influencing upgrade decisions.

The philosophical assumptions underlying our inquiry align most closely with the realist approach advocated by Miles & Huberman (1994), which has recently become recognized within the IS research community under the name ‘critical realism’ (Mingers, 2004; Smith, 2006). This approach includes a realist ontology insofar as the phenomena being investigated are assumed to exist independently of human perception; that is, reality is not limited to socially constructed phenomena. This ontological assumption allows us to examine such issues as the materiality and functionality of packaged software, as well as economic and social realities surrounding its use. Critical realism also assumes an interpretive epistemology, which acknowledges the socially constructed nature of knowledge and the fallibility of the research process to describe reality. The aim of our realist approach is to identify theoretical mechanisms and relationships that influence upgrade decisions, but we do not treat such mechanisms as determinants of upgrade decisions. Rather, we assume that mechanisms explain tendencies, which are subject to a number of other mechanisms and causal influences that may also exist. Critical realism promotes a wide variety of both quantitative and qualitative research methods in hopes of supporting inferences about the mechanisms that generate events in the real world (Mingers, 2004).

Although employing inductive techniques, our approach differs from two other well-known inductive approaches. Specifically, we did not follow the inductive theory-building methodology described by Eisenhardt (1989) because our goal was not to generate hypotheses testable through positivist methods. Such methods are inconsistent with the epistemological assumptions of critical realism. Likewise, we did not employ grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Strauss & Corbin, 1990) because of its underlying ontological position that reality is socially constructed (Suddaby, 2006).

## Research site

A theoretical sampling technique was used to identify an appropriate site for the study. Theoretical sampling specifies that selected cases should clearly represent the phenomenon under study (Yin, 1984; Miles & Huberman, 1994; Creswell, 1997; Mason, 2002). Accordingly, we gained access to a multi-billion dollar business unit of a Fortune 500 company (given the pseudonym BIGCORP in this paper), which had recently upgraded to version 4.6C of SAP’s enterprise software and to the Windows 2000 operating system from Microsoft.

We chose to study the upgrade decisions for both SAP and Windows software packages because they represented different technologies. This choice of research design is another principle of theoretical sampling called nested, within-case sampling and is typically undertaken to observe different instances of a phenomenon so that more theoretical sense can be drawn from comparisons between cases (Miles & Huberman, 1994). Although both upgrade decisions occurred in the same corporate environment, they pertained to software products with different material and functional characteristics and involved different stakeholders. SAP is application software that runs business critical operations, and Windows 2000 is operating system software that is critical to IT operations because it serves as the platform for application software. By choosing software packages with different characteristics, we hoped to see the similarities and differences in decision making due to both organizational factors and technology characteristics. The software upgrade projects were also two of the most recently completed projects, an important consideration because our data collection was retrospective.

Both software packages can be considered de facto industry standards in common use in hundreds of companies. Both SAP and Microsoft periodically issue new versions of their packages, making upgrade decisions about them a regular occurrence in corporate settings. As such, findings regarding decisions for these products might be transferable (generalizable) to other organizations using these same packages. However, our sampling strategy was not meant to produce the type of generalizations from sample to population characteristics that is normally undertaken in positivist research using large samples.

## Data collection

We used multiple data collection methods in this study. The main approach was to conduct both unstructured and semi-structured interviews. Data were collected in two stages. One stage began in April 2002 and lasted 9 months. During that period, both first-time and followup interviews were conducted. A second data collection stage was conducted in May 2004 and lasted 2 days.

A snowball sampling technique was used to solicit interview subjects within BIGCORP. Through a contact in the company, one of the authors gained access to senior persons in the two separate IS departments involved in the software upgrades being studied. The department involved with the SAP upgrade is called SAP Support Department (SSD). The department involved with the Windows upgrade is called Technology Infrastructure Department (TID). The contact persons in these two departments referred the researcher to key persons in the respective upgrade projects. The solicitation of subjects continued as new relevant subjects were recommended and added to the interview schedule.

Over the course of the research, 57 subjects were interviewed generating a total of 67 interviews, each lasting between 60 and 90 min. Of those interviews, seven were telephone interviews with people at two remote manufacturing facilities. Twenty people were approached for interviews concerning the SAP 4.6C project, and 33 people were approached for the Windows 2000 project. Four interviews were conducted with IT personnel in the SAP-affiliated department for contextual information. Users who experienced both systems were asked about both systems. All interviews were tape recorded and transcribed for analysis.

In addition to interviews, documentation and observation were used to supplement the interview data.

## Data analysis

Three rounds of coding were conducted. Initial coding was performed to assign descriptive codes and interpretive codes to segments of text in the transcript (Miles & Huberman, 1994). For example, the phrases ‘do not want beta version’ and ‘company policy to stay on vendor’s support’ were interpretive codes assigned to text segments in the initial round of coding. In the second round of coding, segments of text with similar descriptive and interpretive codes were grouped to form categories representing common themes. For example, both ‘do not want beta version’ and ‘company policy to stay on vendor’s support’ were considered instances of the theme ‘general upgrade policy’ that applied to all IT projects within the organization. A third round of analysis was conducted to derive higher-level concepts that would comprise the theoretical constructs in a model of the influences on software upgrade decisions. For example, ‘general upgrade policy’ was conceptualized as a strategy that mitigated the risk of using packaged software and influenced the timing of an upgrade.

Based on the coding categories for each case, we assembled a within-case analysis that identified the factors contributing to upgrade decisions. We then compared categories across cases to identify the similarities and differences between cases. As analysis progressed, the researchers consulted additional literature in an effort to locate theoretical concepts relevant to the categories being derived from the data. For example, the theory of resource dependence (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978) was consulted for insight into dependence on external vendors. The interplay between data and theory is another established principle in qualitative research that allows theoretical relationships to be developed from data analysis (Mason, 2002).

## Results

We first present the two within-case analyses, beginning with the SAP upgrade and followed by the Windows 2000 upgrade. We then present a summary cross-case analysis that identifies the similarities and differences between the cases. The cross-case analysis is instrumental to the development of the theoretical model presented in the Discussion section.

## SAP 4.6C upgrade decision

The first case reported is the SAP upgrade from version 3.0F to 4.6C. The SAP system was managed by SAP Support Department (SSD). Led by the director, Angela, the department was responsible for the entire lifecycle of SAP systems, including mapping business requirements, configuring software, testing, implementing, and supporting business users. At the time of the study, SSD had more than 65 full-time employees and contractors. SAP was a critical application running key business processes at BIGCORP. Most of its users were employees in business departments like accounting, finance, and order management. At the time of the study, all business units at BIGCORP used SAP. The centrally managed system could be accessed by all 44 field offices and manufacturing facilities through remote login to servers located at corporate headquarters. The upgrade of SAP software took approximately 6 months to complete. Owing to user interface changes, business users reported their learning period to be approximately 3 months before they felt completely comfortable using the software.

Our research identified three main influences on the decision to upgrade from SAP 3.0F to 4.6C. These included business needs, corporate and IT policies to mitigate software upgrade risks, and internal resources. Each of these is presented below, along with supporting data.

Business needs A major influence on the SAP upgrade decision was business need. SSD’s major role was to support business units and, even though Angela made the decision to upgrade, she was strongly influenced by the needs of different business units. Not every business need prompted SSD to upgrade existing software. However, business needs usually led to a consideration of an upgrade. Angela explained that the current SAP version had become incapable of delivering some of the functionality that the business needed:

It was working okay for us, and then we started to acquire more businesses and we were growing. The flexibility that we want to have on our software was not there in the older release. The newer releases were so much better than the old one.

One of the functional team leaders recalled that functionality in 4.6C was one of the major reasons that 4.6C was adopted:

The bottom line is we need to upgrade because we needed a lot of functionality. We need a lot more functionality, a lot more flexibility with the system.

To meet these business needs, SSD preferred to receive software functionality from the software itself than to develop the functionality through additional programming. Besides, modifications to SAP’s core program were forbidden except in rare circumstances. A technical lead explained:

We prefer to not have to do a lot of custom coding. So if it’s something that SAP is going to provide then that’s a definite factor in making the decision to go ahead with the upgrade.

Thus, the availability of user requested functionalities in SAP 4.6C that were missing in 3.0F was a strong influence over the upgrade decision. Upgrading allowed SSD to meet users’ needs without any new development effort, and with SAP’s support.

Corporate and IT policies to mitigate software risks Several of the restrictions on software upgrades were codified into policies designed to mitigate the risks of using packaged software. One important policy affected all packaged software used at BIGCORP. As a multi-billion dollar company, adopting unstable software releases produced unacceptable business risks. To protect BIG-CORP from implementing unstable software, SSD had a policy prohibiting any upgrade of software that was earlier than release $' \mathrm { c } '$ in each version. As the director explained:

We never go before ‘c,’ because ‘a’ is the beta, ‘b’ is the fixes, and ‘c’ is the stable release. yParticularly, we don’t want a beta version.

Indeed, their preference was to wait for other companies of compatible size to perform the upgrade first.

We want to make sure [vendors] have it in production and that other customers are using it before we consider using it.

There were two reasons for this practice: to sort out any known problem in advance, and to learn from another company’s upgrade lesson.

Another corporate policy required all packaged software to be on vendor support to ensure continuous system operation and timely receipt of vendor support if a problem occurred. Vendor support was very important because SSD relied heavily on SAP’s Online Support System (OSS) to solve their technical problems. OSS was a database that contained SAP native code solutions that could be downloaded and applied to fix problems. When IT personnel encountered problems, OSS was the first place they looked for a solution.

In effect, this policy placed the vendor in an influential position regarding BIGCORP’s decision to upgrade to SAP 4.6C. According to the project manager, vendor support was the key reason why the upgrade was not truly a choice for SSD, but rather an ultimatum from the vendor. According to a functional team lead:

We were basically up on our contract with SAP. So, we were told we needed to upgrade.

Although SAP continued to provide technical support after the sunset date, it had to be purchased at an additional charge, and post-sunset customers were given lower priority than customers with a more recent version.

Resource availability One of the reasons why SSD waited so long to upgrade was that SSD personnel had been occupied with other SAP projects as a result of business changes. In the years leading up to the 4.6C upgrade, BIGCORP had acquired several new business units, and SSD personnel had been busy meeting their business needs, implementing one SAP project after another. When SAP was first implemented in 1996, it was installed in only one division. Immediately after the first upgrade, BIGCORP made the decision to implement SAP systems in a second division with very different business practices. The implementation was a massive undertaking, according to a functional team lead:

It was a much bigger division, and it was going to require a tremendous amount of effort to work with that division to understand business practices, business requirements, design, prototype development – literally a new implementation.

The second division’s implementation turned into a huge project for SSD, consuming 2 years of their time. Because SAP was designed to be an integrated system, SSD personnel had to add a great deal of new functionality for the second division and modify the existing system to accommodate both business divisions. Immediately following, they performed another implementation in an acquired business unit . Meanwhile, BIGCORP was in the process of acquiring NAC (Newly Acquired Company), a company twice the size of BIGCORP. During that time practically all of SSD’s attention was poured into meeting business needs. As a result, the routine SAP upgrade was postponed.

In summary, the decision to upgrade from SAP 3.0F to 4.6C was influenced by three main factors. One of these, BIGCORP’s policy to avoid using software not on vendor support, imposed a mandate to upgrade before SAP’s sunset date on version 3.0F. This required BIGCORP to allocate resources to the upgrade project that had previously been lower priority due to more pressing work by SSD to implement SAP projects in other business units within BIGCORP. Although business needs for newer SAP functionality were also influential, satisfying them was delayed until resources could be freed for the project.

## Windows 2000 upgrade

The second case involved an upgrade from Windows NT 4.0, Windows 95 and Windows 98 to Windows 2000. The implementation of Windows 2000 was carried out by the Technology Infrastructure Department (TID), which was led by a senior manager, Sonny. TID had over 60 employees, spread across four geographical locations that all reported directly to Sonny. TID was responsible for the desktops, laptops, servers, and network connectivity at the main headquarters location and three smaller regional headquarters in other parts of the country. It also had indirect, ‘dotted line’ authority over all manufacturing facilities and warehouses. Windows 2000 brought tremendous backend improvement to IT personnel with its Active Directory feature. However, users who previously used Windows NT 4.0 did not perceive many changes in the performance of their applications. By contrast, users who previously used Windows 95 found Windows 2000 to be a much more stable operating system. Overall, the Windows 2000 upgrade project took over 1 year to implement and affected approximately 125 locations and 10,000 desktop computers located throughout the U.S.

Our case results identified three influences on the decision to upgrade to Windows 2000: business needs to standardize applications related to acquisition, IT needs for Active Directory functionality, and availability of internal resources. As for the SAP case, we present descriptions of these influences with supporting evidence.

Business needs The decision to upgrade to Windows 2000 was motivated by a series of events beginning with the acquisition of NAC. The acquisition required the integration of two very different IT infrastructures. NAC used homegrown mainframe applications for order management and SAP for accounting, whereas BIGCORP used SAP for almost all business transactions. Following the acquisition, the decision was made to replace NAC’s mainframe applications with BIGCORP’s SAP system.

However, before the system integration project could begin, TID determined that it was first necessary to replace NAC’s hardware. Users at NAC mostly used terminal emulation programs to access the mainframe, and they had older microcomputers with limited memory that were not powerful enough to be SAP clients.

The decision to upgrade NAC’s hardware created the opportunity to standardize all operating systems within BIGCORP. Following the NAC acquisition, three different operating systems existed across the two companies: Windows 95 for NAC, and both Windows 98 and Windows NT 4.0 for BIGCORP. According to the project leader for Windows 2000, a company could not have standardized applications unless it also had standardized operating systems. Thus, the need to replace more than 3000 desktop computers for the system integration project opened the door for the Windows 2000 upgrade.

IT needs Although integrating the SAP applications and standardizing operating systems was the primary business need, the decision to upgrade to Windows 2000 was also strongly influenced by IT needs for a new service called Active Directory, included as part of Windows 2000. Active Directory was used to manage server and networking objects, which allowed the deployment of Windows 2000 to be controlled centrally. This was an extremely attractive feature to BIGCORP, which had over 25,000 desktop computers in 700 different locations. In the past, distributing software to users had been a nightmare, as a senior analyst explained:

Everybody had to go and visit each of the desktops to install the apps. What Active Directory allowed you to do is push applications and standards to the desktops remotely.

The capability to distribute software reliably to remote sites was enormously attractive to TID people who had become accustomed to visiting users with disks in hand to install new software applications. The senior manager explained:

So we wanted to move everything to Windows 2000 because ywe knew the value of deploying software, fixes, and software packages with the group policies, part of Active Directory. We knew the reduction of administrative cost with Active Directory.

Thus, the upgrade to Windows 2000 was directly influenced by TID’s desires for an operating system capable of supporting the management of distributed desktops.

Resource availability Although TID was aware of the tremendous improvements that Active Directory could bring to their operations, they did not perform the upgrade immediately because they could not justify spending the resources necessary based on Active Directory alone. Although TID could present a strong business case to justify Active Directory, that case had to be prioritized against even stronger business cases coming from other units. However, by linking TID interests with the general corporate objectives to standardize applications and infrastructure across the growing enterprise, TID was able to justify the upgrade. Without the need to replace hardware for NAC, BIGCORP would probably have waited longer before upgrading their operating systems. According to Sonny:

Right now, we are complete with the upgrade of Windows 2000. I think if we didn’t have [NAC], we wouldn’t be completed. y If you look at the two arms of the business case, one was we are replacing a bunch of hardware in [NAC]. Number two was there is awful lot of software rollout support benefit to go on Windows 2000.

Thus, the decision to upgrade to Windows 2000 was jointly influenced by the need to replace infrastructure for NAC and the operational benefits of Windows 2000.

Corporate and IT policies to mitigate software risks In contrast to the SAP upgrade, we did not find risk mitigation policies to influence the Windows 2000 upgrade. Like the SAP upgrade, the upgrade to Windows 2000 was subject to corporate policies designed to mitigate the risk of using packaged software. However, at the time of the Windows upgrade, Windows 2000 was considered a stable software package by the corporate standards committee at headquarters. There was also no time pressure to move more quickly because the sunset dates for existing operating systems were still a long way off. As the senior manager of the corporate standards committee explained:

It wasn’t necessarily a sunset date driving us toward [Windows 2000]. We didn’t sunset NT4 or Windows 95 or any of the other prior operating systems at that point. In fact we’d been doing Windows 2000 for a while.

For these reasons, risk mitigation policies did not play a direct role in the upgrade to Windows 2000. In contrast to the SAP upgrade, there was no immediate pressure from the vendor to move off previous versions of Windows. However, although the vendor’s sunset date did not influence the Windows 2000 upgrade, the risk mitigation policies applied equally to operating system upgrade decisions. According to a senior analyst, vendor support was critical to BIGCORP because they relied on vendor service packs for security or bug fixes. Thus, a vendor’s sunset date was very influential to IT planning. As he put it:

Really, the whole driver of it is around Microsoft sunset.

In the Windows 2000 case, however, important business needs arose well before the sunset date, thus changing IT’s usual preference to ‘ride it out as long as possible.

In summary, the decision to upgrade to Windows 2000 was initiated by a major business need to place newly acquired companies on a standard technical infrastructure coupled with IT’s needs for Windows 2000, which offered clear advantages with its Active Directory feature. Unlike the SAP case, however, Windows 2000 was implemented without the pressure to beat the vendor’s sunset date.

## Discussion

This study was motivated to fill a void in the literature regarding organizational decisions to upgrade packaged software. Just as the general topic of packaged software suffers relative neglect in the IS literature, so the understanding of decisions to upgrade packages demands greater attention. Research studies in related areas such as software maintenance, strategic orientation toward technology, and environmental influences on IS provide little guidance to researchers interested in packaged software. Given these shortcomings, we conducted a comparative case study of two packaged software upgrades in a large US corporation.

Based on an analysis of the cases and comparisons between them, we formulate the theoretical model presented in Figure 1. The decision to upgrade is shown to be influenced by factors classified into two main categories: motivating forces and contingency. The upgrade decision reflects the interaction between those two factors. Our explanation of the model begins with motivating forces, which can originate from both internal requirements and from the organization’s dependence on the software vendor.

## Motivating forces

Motivating forces are considered to be any event, or requirement that triggers the interest to adopt a newer version of packaged software. From the study, two types of motivating forces were found to influence both upgrade decisions: internal requirements and external dependence. Three specific internal requirements were identified as influences on the decision to upgrade: business needs, IT needs, and risk mitigation.

Business and IT needs One of the most important internal requirements identified in our study is business needs. Because information technology applications serve business users, it is understandable that demands from business users can motivate upgrade decisions. In this study, business needs prompted SSD to look into the new version of SAP, and the need to integrate business systems after BIGCORP’s acquisition of NAC prompted the upgrade to Windows 2000. Nevertheless, IT also has its own needs besides meeting the demand of business units. TID’s need for Active Directory features to help improve software management was an important influence in Windows 2000 upgrade decision.

Given the function of packaged software to support business objectives, it comes as no surprise that business needs are a primary influence on upgrade decisions. However, our findings help to clarify that not all business needs are equally influential. In the case of Windows 2000, the need for standardization can be considered a higher priority business need because it was related to the firm’s strategic direction of growth through acquisition. Compared to Windows 2000, SAP users’ needs for improved functionality were seen as needs that could be deferred in the face of more urgent business needs that SSD had to satisfy until the software came closer to its sunset date. Thus, we might think of differences in degree and kind when referring to business needs. All areas of business have an ongoing inventory of needs, but software upgrade decisions appear to be based on judgments about the importance of those needs. In this respect, our results are consistent with the software maintenance literature’s conclusion that users’ business needs, as indicated by their demands for enhancements and extensions, is one of the most important reasons to perform maintenance (Lientz & Swanson, 1978).

Risk mitigation In addition to business and IT needs, our model identifies risk mitigation as an important motivating force. In both cases, upgrade decisions were subject to policies designed to mitigate the risks of using packaged software. BIGCORP had a strict policy that required all packaged software to stay on vendor support at all times. Although the policy’s influence remained dormant when software was not close to the end of the vendor’s sunset date, it became a powerful influence when software approached the sunset date without being upgraded. Another company policy was ‘no beta version,’ which both SSD and TID observed. The policy prevented upgrades to untested or unstable versions of software packages. These policies mitigated the risks inherent in packaged software.

Risk mitigation policies affected SAP 3.0F, which approached its sunset date while other SAP projects were being pursued. Because further delays would have placed SAP 3.0F into the unacceptable status of nonsupport, vendor dependence became a salient motivating force for the SAP upgrade. Although covered by the same policies, the Windows 2000 upgrade was conducted well before the sunset date for the previous version and was not affected by the policy. However, the corporate policy would have applied to Windows also if it were getting close to sunset date. Thus, risk mitigation becomes a stronger motivating force over upgrade decisions as the risks associated with packaged software increase.

External dependence on software vendor When organizations adopt packaged software, they become dependent on the software vendor to provide them with software functionality and technical support. In essence, they become subject to ‘locked in’ relationships in which the external producers of software determine what their consumers must do. In the case of large producers such as SAP and Microsoft, this dependence becomes a primary influence over upgrade decisions for packaged software. Such vendors may use pricing and support strategies to exert pressure on their customers to upgrade earlier than they wish (Sawyer, 2000), while the vendors reduce their own responsibility for supporting multiple versions of software (Beatty & Williams, 2006). In brief, consumers of packaged software who depend on vendors for technical support and functionality do not have much choice but to comply. Thus, organizations in the environment surrounding a firm using packaged software exert influence over its software practices (Lederer & Mendelow, 1990).

The creation of internal risk mitigation policies by customers such as BIGCORP reflects the strong dependence on vendors, who have the power to remove support for earlier versions and thus force customers into upgrades even when business needs are not judged to be high priority. Although new versions offer greater functionality, which might meet business needs, the requirement to upgrade or lose vendor support becomes a pre-emptive criterion for organizations unwilling to accept the risks of unsupported software. Thus, vendors can prematurely end the life of a current software version by setting a sunset date even though customers may still deem current functionality to be adequate.

In addition to their dependence on vendors to provide support for packaged software, organizations also rely on vendors for software functionality. In both of our cases, the only way for SSD and TID to receive the software features that they wanted was to adopt the vendor’s upgrade. Despite large differences in the functionality of the respective software packages, we did not observe much difference in influences due to the software’s material characteristics. In both cases, the software was critical to the organization and was supplied by a powerful external vendor. Thus, because the consumption of packaged software is separated from its production by organizational boundaries, software characteristics may become practically irrelevant to the decision to upgrade. Only in cases where packaged software is not critical could a consuming company decide to abandon a package rather than upgrade it. Alternatively, a company could find another source, such as a different vendor or an open source application, for satisfying its software needs. However, neither of these situations was evident in our study. Thus, the exact nature of software functionality appears to play no special role in the decision to upgrade. Although different types of software functionality were needed, any functionality that is critical to the organization strengthens the dependence on vendors that supply those features. This dependence clearly influences the decision to upgrade unless the organization decides to develop new features in house.

These findings are consistent with the theory of resource dependence as developed by Pfeffer & Salancik (1978). According to the theory, an organization’s dependence on any important resource allows external organizations providing that resource to gain control over decision processes. According to Pfeffer and Salancik, ‘It is the fact of the organization’s dependence on the environment that makes the external constraint and control of organizational behavior both possible and almost inevitable’ (1978, p. 43). The extent to which the organization complies with external demands depends on the importance of resources, an external vendor’s discretion over resource allocation and use, and the availability of alternative resources (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978). In the case of the software packages that we studied, both Windows and SAP were extremely important to BIGCORP. Thus, the prospect of losing support and technical assistance was inconceivable. As a result, dependence on the vendor became one of the most important motivating forces in an upgrade decision, reflecting the customer’s dependence on external resources.

Vendor dependence is a long-term proposition. Once packaged software is adopted, future upgrades become almost inevitable unless the organization decides either to abandon its current software or to continue using the software without pursuing another upgrade. Prior research suggests that adopters of packaged software view migrations to new versions as an unavoidable part of the software lifecycle (Kremers & van Dissel, 2000). The choice is no longer between upgrading and not upgrading, but rather deciding when the upgrade should occur. For this and other reasons, vendor relationships have assumed greater importance in research on software packages, especially ERP (Hirt & Swanson, 2001; Nah et al., 2001).

## Contingency: internal resource availability

The availability of internal resources is an important contingency affecting upgrade decisions. As Mohr (1982) noted, ‘Motivations may determine behaviors, for example, but only if the relevant resources are adequate.’ Clearly, every organization faces resource constraints, so resources tend to be allocated where they are needed most. Although the availability of resources alone is unlikely to trigger the decision to upgrade, a lack of resources may mean that upgrade initiatives are postponed. The resource contingency played a definite role in the postponement of the SAP upgrade until the sunset date approached for the version in use. Also, despite the attractiveness of the Active Directory feature of Windows 2000, TID was not able to justify the expenditure of resources until other business needs increased the motivation. Thus, resource availability is contingent on the strength of the motivating forces. Although lack of resources can defer upgrade decisions when there is no immediate threat to business operations, resources can often be allocated when business needs to upgrade become more urgent.

## Interaction among motivating forces and resource availability

From our analysis, it is apparent that upgrade decisions for different software packages differ. In both cases, more than one motivating force was relevant, yet they exerted unequal amounts of force. This pattern bears resemblance to other dynamic organizational decision processes.

According to Mintzberg et al. (1976), stimuli for organizational decisions have different amplitudes, and a decision is evoked when the cumulative amplitude of stimuli reaches an action threshold. Decisions, therefore, are not determined by any single motivation and may vary from case to case. Even within a decision type, such as software upgrade decisions, forces may be unequally influential, making the general prediction of decisions difficult. The benefit of case studies of decision processes is greater explanation of the interaction among motivating forces and resource contingencies.

In the two cases of upgrades described in this study, amplitudes and action thresholds were not explicitly assessed as part of the decision process. The threshold level was a subjective reference point made by managers, and no formal cost/benefit analyses were conducted at BIGCORP. Nonetheless, it can be inferred from managers’ comments that the upgrade decisions for both projects were affected by multiple influences, as shown in the model. According to Bannister & Remenyi (2000), the ‘gut feel’ approach is in fact a quite common phenomenon in organization decision making. Similarly, Mintzberg et al. (1976) found that the favored mode chosen by individuals when evaluating alternative solutions was to make ‘a choice in his own mind with procedures that he does not, perhaps cannot, explain.’ In the two cases, the threshold can be inferred as a point at which managers perceived that the need to upgrade had become a necessity and could justify the expenditure of scarce resources.

Many organizational decisions, including upgrades to packaged software, involve commitments of resources that are in scarce supply. The condition of resource scarcity implies the need to prioritize projects in order of importance. As the two cases at BIGCORP demonstrate, projects shift in priority over time. In the case of Windows 2000, IT’s wish to upgrade was delayed until complementary business needs arose (the need to standardize new acquisitions and replace NAC’s hardware). In the case of SAP, the priority of the upgrade project increased as the vendor’s sunset date neared. Thus, resources are provided contingent upon other motivating factors and external dependencies.

In the study, motivating influences and internal resources interacted to influence decisions depending on their level of intensity and the availability of internal resources. In the normal situation where resources are scarce, the lack of resources dominates the interaction, particularly if the need to upgrade can be deferred. However, when the need to upgrade reaches the threshold so that it can no longer be deferred, it dominates the decision to make resources available. When the decision was made to upgrade SAP, the software was in danger of losing support and had to be upgraded immediately. In the Windows case, TID was only able to justify the division-wide upgrade to Windows 2000 when the need arose to replace hardware for NAC. The combination of stimuli provided the needed justification to conduct both upgrades.

While the above findings pertain to decision making under resource scarcity, it is likely that under conditions of sufficient resources, any legitimate motivation would evoke the upgrade decision. This claim is supported by anecdotal evidence about previous upgrade decisions not included in our study. According to the Director of SSD, when the SSD department had fewer competing projects, upgrades were performed according to the Director’s preference to upgrade to a new version of SAP every 18 months.

To recap, the model derived from our empirical data offers a relatively parsimonious theoretical view of the influences on corporate decisions to upgrade software packages. From our analysis, it should be clear that the relative strength of the influences on upgrade decisions is dependent on the internal requirements of the organization and the external influence of vendors. Thus, the model provides an empirically grounded basis for understanding how and why software upgrade decisions are made.

## Conclusion

The research reported in this paper is one of few empirical studies focused on packaged software upgrades. It offers an in-depth, qualitative view of decisions to upgrade two widely used software packages from major vendors. In relationship to the existing literature on packaged software, our focus is on activities near the end of a software package’s lifecycle. Given the growing importance of packaged software, and the apparent inevitability of upgrades (Light, 2005), it is increasingly necessary to understand the decision processes for packaged software upgrades.

Our contribution to this effort is a parsimonious theoretical model portraying the interactions among motivating influences (business needs and vendor dependence) and contingent resources. The model draws its inspiration from earlier literature on software maintenance and other related literatures. However, a software upgrade is a unique type of IS project, with characteristics that distinguish it from maintenance, traditional system development and initial adoption of a commercial system. In a packaged software upgrade, the vendor has substantial control over the development of packaged software, and the client organization becomes vulnerable to vendors’ actions. As our study has shown, sunset dates can have a dominating influence on an upgrade decision. The study thus draws attention to vendor influence over major IS projects.

Although our findings in some ways support the conclusions of earlier research on IT adoption and software maintenance, our model makes an original contribution by focusing on the interactions among motivating forces and contingent resources. Indeed, resource availability has been largely overlooked in IS research, while it plays an obviously important role in IT practice. Resources are directly implicated in sourcing decisions for information services, which recently seem to be affected by a normal condition of resource scarcity. To the extent that most IT decisions, including software upgrades and maintenance, require resources, we believe that resources should be theorized more directly. Following Mohr’s (1982) observation that reasons to act require resources to convert them to behavior, we have positioned internal resource availability as a contingent influence on upgrade decisions.

Our model also goes beyond the more obviously rational motivating forces for upgrades, namely business needs for functionality. Although business needs are always important, our model suggests that business need was not the only or always the most important factor motivating an upgrade decision. Rather, policies to mitigate the risks of dependence on packaged software placed vendors in powerful positions to influence upgrade decisions. Thus, even where business needs for new functionality were not overwhelming, upgrades occurred to avoid the loss of vendor support for software in its twilight years. This kind of influence is comparable to the structural exercise of power by IT professionals over users, identified by Markus & Bjørn-Andersen (1987) in the context of in-house development. Structural power by IT professionals involves ‘organizational structures and routine operating procedures that give them formal authority over users or foster user dependence on them for important resources’ (Markus & Bjørn-Andersen, 1987, p. 500). Organizations that adopt packaged software increasingly find themselves in similar situations with regard to vendors (Howcroft & Light, 2006). The importance of dependence on external resources suggests a more political than rational motive for an upgrade decision (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978). That is, dependence on packaged software enhances the vendor’s power to influence organizational decisions. By considering this influence directly, our model avoids the tendency to emphasize rational criteria in IT decision making.
