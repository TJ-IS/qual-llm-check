# Shadows and Screens: Exploring the Impact of Astroturfing as a Facet of Fake Reviews in the Movie Industry’s Social Media Dynamics

Xueyan Yin,<sup>1</sup> Ying Liu,<sup>2</sup> Pei-yu Chen,<sup>3</sup> Zhan Shi<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, xueyayin@cityu.edu.hk <sup>2</sup>University of Massachusetts Amherst, USA, yliu@isenberg.umass.edu <sup>3</sup>Arizona State University, USA, peiyu.chen@asu.edu <sup>4</sup>Arizona State University, USA, zmshi@asu.edu

## Abstract

Our research critically investigates the impact of social media astroturfing on movie sales, the circumstances under which it appears to have an effect, and the negative implications associated with this practice. Leveraging a distinct dataset from the Chinese movie industry, we utilized panel vector autoregression to dissect the dynamics between social media astroturfing, box office revenues, and the performance of media across digital and traditional platforms. We further applied a dynamic matching approach to address the identification issue. Our findings suggest that while social media astroturfing might temporarily boost box office revenues, its effectiveness is limited to the short term, and it harbors negative consequences for consumers. By systematically examining the effects of social media astroturfing, our study enriches both academic literature and practical understanding, cautioning against the reliance on such deceptive marketing tactics.

Keywords: Social Media Astroturfing, Panel Vector Autoregression Model, PVAR, Dynamic Matching

## 1 Introduction

Fake reviews are counterfeit endorsements that pose as authentic customer feedback designed to manipulate product or service perceptions. These fabricated reviews significantly influence consumer decisionmaking and brand reputation on digital platforms. Their impact stems from consumers’ heavy reliance on reviews to guide their purchasing choices, transforming the review landscape into a competitive arena.

The proliferation of fake reviews is linked to the potential benefits they offer in the digital market. It has been widely acknowledged that positive reviews can boost sales by improving the perceived quality and reliability of offerings and can therefore be decisive for consumers facing various options online. Digital platform operators may use manipulative practices to gain advantages, such as enhancing search rankings through false downloads, increasing revenues through the use of bot-generated advertisements, and inflating social media influence with counterfeit followers (Wilbur & Zhu, 2009; Gordon et al., 2021). Such tactics also include distorting auctions, misleading consumers with erroneous advertising (Rao & Wang, 2017; Chiou & Tucker, 2018), and inflating seller reputations with fake reviews (Mayzlin et al., 2014; Luca and Zervas, 2016).

One coordinated effort of the fake review phenomenon on social media platforms is “social media astroturfing.” This phenomenon has become a pervasive concern globally, manifesting under various aliases, including “click farms,” “sock puppets,” “troll farms,” and “internet water armies.” These operations often create the illusion of grassroots support or opposition, manipulate consumer behavior, and shape political discourse. In certain instances, individuals are paid to post comments utilizing either their genuine or fake profiles (referred to as sock puppets) (Chen et al., 2011). Alternatively, organizations may deploy automated entities, or social bots, that mimic real user interactions to disseminate astroturfing content (Ferrara, 2017; Kovic et al., 2018). Numerous brands have been caught up in scandals where influencers with purchased followers and engagement were used to market products, leading to a crackdown on inauthentic activity by platforms like Instagram. <sup>1</sup> Additionally, reports have disclosed that organizations deploy bots or organized user groups to artificially trend specific hashtags on Twitter (now X) and convey a misleading sense of popularity or significance for specific topics, products, or campaigns.<sup>2</sup>

Social media astroturfing is also a cultural phenomenon in China, leading to negative impacts. With over a billion internet users,<sup>3</sup> the online space is rife with fake content for different purposes despite its illegality. A Xinhua News survey found that 74% of respondents believed social media astroturfing to be prevalent on Chinese social media, <sup>4</sup> and it is often employed by public relations teams. A Weibo spokesperson noted that about 40% of the daily trending hashtags on the platform are generated by such groups. Social media astroturfing systematically produces and distributes fake content to sway online perceptions, aiming either to elevate a product’s status or tarnish a rival’s reputation. They deploy various deceptive methods, including creating sham social media profiles, publishing false comments and reviews, and utilizing bots to simulate engagement, thereby corrupting the integrity of online discourse. For instance, the TV show Eternal Love reported implausibly high viewership numbers on its premiere, likely inflated by social media astroturfing. Although such tactics can temporarily boost awareness, they cast doubt on authenticity, potentially eroding consumer trust and harming the platform’s credibility.

The real impact of social media astroturfing remains uncertain; while it can increase visibility, it also displaces genuine interactions and can damage a brand’s reputation if discovered. Our research delved into the mechanisms and market consequences of social media astroturfing, examining its effects on both digital and traditional media landscapes, as well as its implications for sales performance and consumer confidence. To do so, we cooperated with a major entertainment data analytics consulting company in China and collected data on movies released in 2017 and 2018. We constructed a daily panel dataset using a panel vector autoregression model, which allowed us to assess the nature of bidirectional relationships between all pairs of dependent variables as all dependent variables, box office sales, offline media activities, and online media activities were treated as jointly endogenous (Dewan & Ramprasad, 2014; Ahlfeldt et al., 2015). Furthermore, we were able to identify the lag effects and evaluate the dynamic relationships (Duan et al., 2008). Offline media activities capture traditional media activities, including newspaper articles and magazine articles, whereas online media activities reveal online media press activities, social media astroturfing posts, and organic social media posts. Our identification strategy depends on the engagement decisions of movie distributors: while some engage in social media astroturfing, others do not, and different movie distributors engage in social media astroturfing at different stages. We used an approach from the literature on treatment effects to measure the impact of engaging in social media astroturfing together with a dynamic matching strategy and investigated how movie ratings are affected in the long run.

Our research indicates that social media astroturfing can influence product sales by altering the balance of positive and negative social media fans. Initiating astroturfing activities one week before a movie’s release can increase visibility, attracting a broader spectrum of social media fans, including those with positive, negative, and neutral perspectives. However, such strategies may prove ineffective if implemented in the third week following a movie’s debut. Furthermore, our findings underscore the negative consequences of social media astroturfing on consumer perception. We found that movies associated with astroturfing campaigns tend to receive lower ratings over time, reflecting negatively on their perceived quality. This underscores the potential long-term drawbacks of engaging in deceptive marketing practices.

Our study yields several implications for both research and practice. We enhance the existing literature by exploring the economic and social ramifications of employing social media astroturfing. By investigating the economic influence of social media astroturfing on fake review literature, we deepen the understanding of market behaviors and the broader economic framework. We found that social media astroturfing activities can skew the market signals typically generated by authentic customer feedback. The uniqueness of our dataset provides a transparent view into the economic impact of social media astroturfing on both offline media engagement and the product’s financial performance. Further, our comprehensive dataset provided us with the unique capability to analyze the impact of fake accounts and fake posts separately. This distinction is crucial for understanding the nuanced ways in which astroturfing can influence consumer perceptions and behavior. Our dataset also enabled us to dissect both the immediate and enduring consequences of social media astroturfing interventions. Moreover, while previous research has predominantly concentrated on developing technical detection methods, we curated our innovative dataset to scrutinize the repercussions of astroturfing engagement on product financial returns and the adverse effects on consumer experiences. Our research offers valuable insights to practitioners, highlighting the necessity for social media platforms and consumers to address and regulate the use of social media astroturfing collaboratively. This collaboration is crucial for safeguarding consumer welfare and maintaining market integrity.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we review the related prior literature and propose our theoretical model. Next, we describe our data, followed by the development of our identification strategy. Finally, we present our concluding remarks.

## 2 Related Literature

## 2.1 Fake Reviews

Word-of-mouth (WOM) can be simply defined as the transmission of information from one individual to another on e-commerce and collaborative platforms. Research has demonstrated that online WOM significantly drives product sales (Lin & Wang, 2018; Jiang et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2021). However, prior studies investigating the use of fake reviews have revealed their negative impact on the reliability and usefulness of WOM (Hu et al., 2011) as well as their influence on the effectiveness of positive and abundant WOM in shaping purchasing behaviors (Zhao et al., 2013). In the context of new product introductions, research has found that booksellers may produce fake positive reviews to influence potential buyers (Hu et al., 2011). Similarly, restaurants may resort to creating positive online reviews when they experience a dip in their reputation, lack sufficient reviews, or are independently owned rather than part of a chain (Luca & Zervas, 2016). The propensity of hotels to encourage positive reviews has been linked to factors such as their chain affiliation and the size of their managing entities (Mayzlin et al., 2014). Businesses, including hotels, might also be the target of negative fake reviews from competitors, undermining their visibility (Lappas et al., 2016). Competition with platforms like Airbnb has been observed to influence hotels’ strategies regarding review manipulation, often leading to a decrease in such activities (Nie et al., 2022). Furthermore, Ananthakrishnan et al. (2020) discovered that platforms that flag fake reviews without removing them can bolster consumer trust.

## 2.2 Social Media Astroturfing

Social media astroturfing, a coordinated effort of posting fake reviews, disseminates fake perceptions through both identity-based and message-based strategies (Hancock, 2007). Astroturfing not only disguises the true identity of the individuals behind the fake reviews but also conveys information that is deceptive or misleading. The exploration of social media astroturfing spans sociology and political science, with Kovic et al. (2018) delineating a typology and suggesting counterstrategies for political astroturfing. The utilization of such practices in presidential campaigns has been documented, focusing on the identification and analysis of astroturfing accounts and their operations on platforms like Twitter (Keller et al., 2020; Golovchenko et al., 2020; Linvill & Warren, 2019; Xia et al., 2019). Research has also delved into the broader psychological impacts of astroturfing on individuals and society, highlighting its persuasive ability (Henrie & Gilde, 2019) and its detrimental effects on collective intelligence (Chan, 2022). However, the examination of online astroturfing remains relatively uncharted territory in information systems (IS) research, which has yet to thoroughly investigate this important topic. An exception is Zhang et al.’s (2013) study, which is among the first to describe the characteristics of online astroturfing from an IS perspective, proposing both mechanisms and theoretical frameworks for future investigation.

## 2.3 Research Gap

Table 1 provides a summary of recent IS research concerning the phenomenon of fake reviews and online astroturfing. While the majority of these studies have investigated the practices of corporate review manipulation, there exists a noticeable gap in the empirical evidence regarding the economic impact of fake accounts and fake posts. Moreover, the focus predominantly lies on the analysis of fake posts, with a lack of analysis directed towards the examination of fake accounts, which entails different dissemination strategies and detection risks. Purchasing social media astroturfing accounts leads to the widespread dissemination of messages, which may be more persuasive than repetitive posts by a single account. However, this pattern of dissemination is also easier for social media platforms to detect and can quickly be identified as manipulation. While Hunter and Zaman (2019) show that the number of posts created by bots is more influential in political arenas than the number of bots themselves, the relative effectiveness of post quantity versus account quantity remains to be validated by further empirical studies. Furthermore, the literature reveals a scarcity of investigations into the ramifications of fake reviews for consumers.

## 3 Data

In partnership with a prominent analytics organization in the Chinese entertainment sector, we investigated social media astroturfing for social media promotion. Our partner company deployed an algorithm to filter out fake fans and fake posts from authentic social engagement for business purposes. They monitor and identify social media astroturfing for influencers continuously as well as for specific movie and TV show releases during promotional periods.

Our comprehensive dataset encompasses 317 movies released in China during 2017 and 2018. We compiled a daily panel dataset that included box office revenue, media activity, and performance metrics across various channels—traditional media, organic social media, and the online press. We also tracked the volume of fake posts and accounts. Traditional media data included print media coverage, while online activities comprised press releases, social media astroturfing activities, and organic social postings. In our analysis, we distinguish between the total number of posts and the distinct number of accounts posting, acknowledging that multiple contributions may come from a single account.

Table 2 presents the variable descriptions and summary statistics from our dataset. An interesting finding is that the average screening duration of movies in Chinese cinemas is 44.5 days, which is marginally less than what is typical in the US market. This time frame encompasses both the pre-release period and the official theatrical release, constrained by the rigorous permit application process via the National Radio and Television Administration (NRTA). Initially, permits are granted for a 30-day showing in theaters, with any extension necessitating a new application. Due to the intense competition in the Chinese film market and the scarcity of available screens, many films are shown for shorter durations— often under 30 days and, in some cases, less than two weeks. Moreover, our dataset offers a broader scope of media performance metrics in comparison to box office data alone, tracking social media engagement well before a movie’s theatrical release. While traditional media promotions tend to be focused on the film’s launch due to their higher costs, social media campaigns often span the entire lifespan of the movie.

## 4 Method and Empirical Results

This study investigates the potential influence of social media astroturfing on box office revenue. Utilizing a panel vector autoregression (PVAR) model, we investigated the dynamics between box office revenue, the number of fake posts and accounts, and the activity across various media channels. Our analysis further categorized organic social media followers into positive, negative, and neutral groups to understand how different sentiments affect outcomes. To address potential endogeneity issues, we employed a treatment effect methodology augmented by dynamic matching to ascertain the true effects of social media astroturfing on revenue.

To begin, we conducted tests to ascertain the stationarity of our data. The specifications of our analysis presupposed that all endogenous variables are stationary. The PVAR estimator is known to be prone to weak instrument issues in cases where the modeled variable exhibits near-unit root behavior, rendering moment conditions irrelevant in situations where a unit root is present. To confirm the stationarity of our data, we employed Fisher-type tests (Choi, 2001) for panel data. The results of these tests are presented in Table 3 and indicate that all variables under consideration are stationary.

Our analysis, detailed in Tables 4 and 5, explores the impact of both the number of fake posts and the number of fake accounts on movie box office revenue, assessed at the post and account levels respectively. We first focus on movie box office revenue as the dependent variable in our regressions. The coefficient estimates for fake posts and accounts show a consistent pattern at both levels: There is a positive correlation between social media astroturfing activities and movie box office revenue, evidenced by positive and significant coefficients. Furthermore, the analysis indicates that genuine social media engagement and online media presence also positively correlate with box office revenue. Specifically, a 1% increase in inauthentic posts on a given day is associated with a 0.19% rise in box office revenue the following day.

Similarly, a 1% rise in the number of inauthentic accounts on a given day correlates with a 0.32% increase in box office revenue the next day. On the other hand, the quantity of traditional media posts has a slightly negative relationship with box office revenue, though this effect is minimal. The significance of traditional media coverage in relation to box office revenue is not statistically significant, suggesting no clear link between the two.

Our analysis extends to examining the interplay among social media astroturfing, organic social media engagement, online media presence, and traditional media coverage. Focusing on the influence of social media astroturfing, the data reveals consistent outcomes. At both the post and account levels, the impact of organic social media on astroturfing activities is not statistically significant, suggesting no clear link between the volume of genuine social media interactions and the prevalence of astroturfing activities. Conversely, a positive and significant relationship was observed with online media, indicating that an increase in online media presence is associated with heightened astroturfing activities. Traditional media, on the other hand, shows a negative correlation with astroturfing, with significant coefficients pointing to an inverse relationship between traditional media coverage and astroturfing engagement.

## 4.3 Addressing Endogeneity

In our prior analysis, we discovered a positive correlation between social media astroturfing and movie box office revenue, utilizing the PVAR method. However, this finding may be subject to limitations due to potential endogeneity issues. For example, the corporations’ decision to engage in social media astroturfing could be influenced by their expectations of a movie’s performance. Companies might engage in astroturfing for movies based on different performance indicators, such as current success, anticipated success, or recent positive trends. Conversely, the quality of the movie, which is not directly observable, could also play a significant role in these dynamics.

To provide evidence that social media astroturfing leads to increased box office revenue and to solve potential endogeneity issues, we adopted an empirical approach taken from the literature on treatment effects (Angrist & Pischke, 2008) combined with dynamic matching. For this approach, we categorized movies based on their social media astroturfing engagement during a given week. Movies engaged in social media astroturfing in a specific week were classified as the “treatment” group in that week. Given that the decision to employ social media astroturfing for a specific movie may vary across different stages of its release cycle, the composition of the treatment group changed dynamically. For instance, social media astroturfing might be employed for a movie in Week 1 but not in Week 2. We created “proper” control groups for movies that did not employ social media astroturfing in that week by using propensity score matching, matching not only on movie characteristics but also on prior activities and performance up to a focal week. Movie characteristics, such as movie genre, producing country, how many stars were used, star power index, how many stars were in different categories in terms of star power, etc., as well as the movie’s performance in the past two weeks and the movie’s social media astroturfing engagement behavior in the past two weeks, were used to match movies exhibiting similar patterns. We ensured that the control and treated groups were comparable in terms of movie features, previous performance, and previous astroturfing activities. Figure 1 shows the average box office revenue for movies with and without social media astroturfing engagements. Prior to release, the revenue figures for both groups were similar, indicating a balanced comparison. Following the release of the movies, the data indicates that the group of movies that engaged in social media astroturfing exhibited higher box office revenue compared to those that did not employ astroturfing.

## 4.4 Whether Astroturfing Hurts Consumers

In this section, we aim to assess the potential negative effects of social media astroturfing on consumers. We collected data on movie ratings from douban.com and conducted an analysis of movie ratings before and after the cessation of social media astroturfing activities. We continued to use the control and treatment groups established in the previous section. Due to the data availability, we focused on the treatment week of the second week following the release of the movie. During this week, we anticipated no significant differences in movie ratings between the treatment and control groups, as the groups were matched based on movie characteristics and previous performance. However, our analysis revealed that movies in the treatment group that employed social media astroturfing received significantly lower ratings compared to those in the control group after the treatment week. These findings suggest that social media astroturfing is employed to conceal inferior movie quality. Lower ratings of movies indicate that their actual quality falls short of what customers expect. These low ratings may either directly reflect the quality of the movies or represent a disconfirmation from the rating in the treatment week. Consumers are adversely affected because social media astroturfing may motivate them to view a subpar movie instead of a better alternative. This analysis is also critical from the perspective of the platform itself. Reduced ratings indicate negative consumer experiences, which undermines trust in the platform’s reputation system. Results are shown in Table 10.

To summarize the findings, our analysis reveals that the influence of social media astroturfing on sales is most pronounced prior to a movie’s release, a period characterized by significant information asymmetry. According to signaling theory (Spence 1973), consumers often rely on available signals to gauge a movie’s quality in the absence of direct information. Astroturfing acts as one such signal of quality. In situations where consumers are unable to directly evaluate the quality of a product due to a lack of information, they become more vulnerable to these signals, even when they are deceptive or manipulated. This vulnerability heightens the impact of astroturfing on sales. However, a few weeks post-release, as more information about the movie becomes available, consumers’ dependency on these manipulated signals decreases, leading to a diminished influence of astroturfing on sales.

Another possibility to consider is the growing awareness among consumers about the existence of astroturfing practices. As consumers become more skeptical about online information, especially in environments known for high information asymmetry, they might start to question the authenticity of signals that appear too coordinated or unnatural. Once consumers begin to question the authenticity of the signals, the signals lose their effectiveness (Herbig and Milewicz 1993). The awareness of astroturfing practices can lead to a deterioration of consumer trust not only in the astroturfing content but also in the brand or product. Over time, as consumers learn to identify and disregard astroturfed content, the effectiveness of these deceptive practices in influencing purchase decisions may diminish considerably. This shift underscores the delicate balance between the short-term gains of astroturfing and the long-term risks of eroding consumer trust, ultimately suggesting that the impact of astroturfing on sales is not only context-dependent but also transient, subject to the evolving awareness and responses of consumers. The erosion of trust may not only diminish the immediate impact of astroturfing but can also have long-term detrimental effects on brand and product perceptions.

## 5 Concluding Remarks

Companies engage in social media astroturfing to shape sales and reputations on social media platforms, which has become a widespread practice. This study delved into the social media astroturfing market, collaborating with a leading entertainment data consulting firm in China to collect movie data from 2017 and 2018. We constructed a daily panel dataset and applied a PVAR model and a difference-indifferences model to estimate the treatment effect, addressing the identification problem through dynamic matching techniques.

The analysis suggests that while social media astroturfing may initially stimulate product sales by stimulating engagement with emotionally engaged audiences, this strategy’s efficacy noticeably wanes three weeks after the product launch. For example, astroturfing activities carried out a week before a movie’s release might increase visibility and attract a diverse audience, encompassing both advocates and critics. However, the long-term impact on consumer perception can be detrimental, as evidenced by lower ratings for movies associated with such promotional tactics, indicating a potential erosion in quality perceptions.

This research makes several unique contributions to the literature. To begin with, empirical evidence on the use of social media astroturfing is scarce. Our study explores the economic ramifications of astroturfing, providing a comprehensive analysis of how these practices affect market performance. Furthermore, most existing literature on media performance relies on a posteriori quantity metrics, such as the number of Twitter posts, IMDB reviews, or radio plays. Our study differentiates between the number of accounts and posts, revealing distinct effects. Moreover, we take a holistic approach by examining how social media astroturfing affects both firms and consumers, investigating the dual perspective of the impact on firms’ marketing strategies as well as consumers’ reactions on social media platforms. Our research further examines the influence of astroturfing on organic social media posts, exploring how the presence of fake content can alter the dynamics of genuine user engagement and potentially crowd out authentic voices. Moreover, this study examines both the immediate (short-term) and enduring (long-term) effects of astroturfing campaigns, which improves the understanding of the immediate impact on sales and consumer behavior as well as the lasting repercussions on brand reputation and consumer trust.

Additionally, we consider the simultaneous impact of online media press, traditional media, and social media—a trio that has been largely overlooked in prior research. Prior studies have mainly focused on the interplay between traditional media and social media. However, as “online” is becoming increasingly ubiquitous, online media is quickly consuming audiences’ attention. Thus, our study is particularly valuable for policymakers and practitioners seeking to develop strategies for immediate response and longterm prevention.

This work has a number of other salient practical implications. Our aim is to provide insights to product marketing teams to ensure that they understand the potential consequences of deploying social media astroturfing. We show that while astroturfing may benefit product sales in the short term, it can damage a product’s reputation in the long term. Understanding the negative long-term effects of astroturfing on brand reputation can encourage businesses to adopt more ethical marketing strategies. Companies should focus on building genuine customer relationships and loyalty rather than resorting to manipulative tactics that could damage their reputation and trustworthiness. Secondly, we highlight how consumers can be negatively affected by social media astroturfing through exposure to low-quality content. Moreover, our study informs social media platforms about the importance of monitoring social media astroturfing. When the public realizes that social media platforms are largely occupied by social media astroturfing, they will reduce the amount of time they spend on them. For social media platforms, understanding astroturfing’s impact is vital for developing more effective content moderation policies and algorithms. By understanding the economics of social media astroturfing, stakeholders across various sectors can implement more effective practices to mitigate the impact of fake reviews, which can contribute to a healthier, more trustworthy, and less manipulated online economy.

This study does have some limitations. First, we only included one type of traditional media—print media, such as magazines and newspapers. Future research could explore other traditional media avenues, like TV shows, which are commonly used for movie promotions. Next, we focused on the volume of social media astroturfing rather than analyzing the content. Future studies could take this a step further and explore the content design of social media astroturfing posts. Overall, this work sheds new light on the economic impact of social media astroturfing activities and the potential harm to consumers. Our study provides key perspectives for product marketing groups, underscoring the essential need for a joint effort between social media platforms and users to manage and control social media astroturfing activities. This partnership is vital to protect consumers’ interests and uphold the marketplace’s reputation.
