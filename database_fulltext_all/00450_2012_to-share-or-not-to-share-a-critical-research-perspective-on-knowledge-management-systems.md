# To share or not to share: a critical research perspective on knowledge management systems

Mei-Lien Young<sup>1</sup>, Feng-Yang Kuo<sup>2</sup> and Michael D. Myers<sup>3</sup>

## Abstract

This paper looks at Knowledge Management Systems (KMS) from the perspective of critical research. Using the concepts of ‘gaze’ and ‘face’, we examine the development and use of a web-based KMS in Taiwan. The findings indicate that, although a web-based KMS was developed to enable knowledge sharing, the capabilities for surveillance inherent in the KMS severely limited sharing. This study raises broader questions about the relationship between cultural values and the production of knowledge in KMS. Keywords: knowledge management systems; critical research; Foucault; gaze; face

## Introduction

Knowledge management systems (KMS) are information systems (IS) that organizations employ to facilitate the creation, distribution, and utilization of knowledge for the purpose of enhancing organizational competitiveness (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Alavi et al, 2006; Kulkarni et al, 2007). There are many examples of the successful use of KMS, such as at Xerox (Hickins, 1999). However, up to 70% of KMS fail to meet organization objectives (Malhotra, 2005). Some studies have shown that cultural differences can lead to problems when KMS are deployed outside Western countries (Voelpel & Han, 2005; Ardichvili et al, 2006). Since KMS usually reflect the Western values of the designers (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995), such cultural issues are perhaps not surprising.

Whereas Western culture can be characterized as individualistic with a respect for individual rights in social interaction (Triandis, 1995), Chinese culture is usually described as collectivist where the self is defined, not by one’s inner qualities but by the sum of one’s social roles (Stover & Stover, 1976, pp. 202–203). In such a culture, knowledge creation and sharing has little to do with individual autonomy, but almost everything to do with face-work. Face is the ‘social esteem accorded by others’ (Yang, 1945, p. 167) and is ‘the respectability and/or deference which a person can claim for himself from others, by virtue of the relative position he occupies in his social network and the degree to which he is judged to have functioned adequately in that position’ (Ho, 1976, p. 883). Face is both personal (i.e., myself) and collective (i.e., my family, business, etc.). The personal face is what others have recognized and extended to a person (Ho, 1976), while the collective face represents the prestige and honor of the social group (Hwang, 1987). Face-work is the effort that a person makes to satisfy the face-wants of oneself or others, so that threats to the loss of face are minimized (Lim, 1994).

Our argument in this paper is that the concept of face is fundamental for understanding social interactions in Chinese culture and by implication, the development and use of KMS in such a culture. Participating in a social activity such as knowledge sharing involves acts that may enhance and/or threaten ‘my face’, ‘our face’, or ‘others’ face’. One’s concerns for face ‘exerts a mutually restrictive, even coercive, power upon each member of the social network’ (Ho, 1976, p. 873). This is especially critical in KMS where people might be constantly engaged in face-work to either sustain/enhance face or mitigate threats to face. For example, because being humble is considered an important virtue, to uphold one’s ‘humble face’ in knowledge sharing the knowledge contributor must both downplay the importance of his or her individual contribution and be careful not to be too critical. To avoid harming others’ face, one must use polite and indirect wording. Consequently, Chinese knowledge workers face immense pressure in creating and sharing knowledge when they are exposed to the public gaze of others in a KMS.

This paper, therefore, looks at KMS in light of the Chinese cultural concept of face (Hu, 1944; Yang, 1945; Goffman, 1955, 1967; Ho, 1976). We used in-depth case study research to examine knowledge production in the context of a web-based KMS in Taiwan. Our understanding of face in knowledge creation and sharing is informed by critical concepts from Foucault, especially his concept of gaze (Foucault, 1979). Our paper can thus be classified as an example of critical IS research (McGrath, 2005; Stahl & Brooke, 2008; Myers & Klein, 2011).

This paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we briefly review the approaches that have been adopted by IS researchers for studying web-based KMS. We then discuss the critical approach we have taken and our theoretical framework. The following two sections present the research method and our findings. The final sections are the discussion and conclusions.

## Approaches to studying KMS

In the IS research literature, various approaches have been taken to studying KMS. One treats KMS as a technical/ rational artifact, a repository system through which people can codify, store, retrieve, and distribute their knowledge (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). In this research, stream scholars are interested in how KMS system quality and information quality may affect individuals’ knowledge sharing behaviors. Mostly using DeLone and Mclean’s IS Success Model (Jennex & Olfman, 2006; Halawi et al, 2007–2008; Kulkarni et al, 2007), this stream of research perceives KMS to be a functional device for which success can be measured in terms of effectiveness and efficiency of knowledge sharing.

A related stream draws on social psychological theories such as Theory of Reasoned Action and Theory of Planned Behavior (Bock et al. 2005: Hansen & Avital.

2005; Kwon & Onwuegbuzie, 2005; Kuo & Young, 2008). The research findings show that in knowledge sharing practices, people do not always perform in a manner consistent with their espoused beliefs. This intention– action inconsistency is mostly explained by perceived self-efficacy.

A third stream looks at how organizational and national culture shapes knowledge sharing behaviors (Voelpel & Han, 2005; Alavi et al, 2006; Ardichvili et al, 2006; Lin & Kwok, 2006) and, conversely, how KMS can support sociocultural activities (Alavi et al, 2006; Ardichvili et al, 2006). For example, Hickins’ (1999) case study of knowledge creation and sharing practices in Xerox indicated that an organizational culture that values learning was the main factor explaining the success of their KMS.

A fourth stream relies on theories such as Social Capital Theory, Social Cognitive Theory, Social Network Theory, and Social Exchange Theory. This stream investigates how constructs like interpersonal trust and relationships, strong and weak ties of social networks, and boundary of belonging may influence practitioners’ behaviors through KMS (Bordia et al, 2006; Chiu et al, 2006; Ma¨kela¨, 2007). Overall, this stream of research perceives the role of KMS as a means for building and sustaining communities (Alavi & Leidner 2001; McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

All four streams of research have contributed various insights to the study of knowledge production behaviors in the context of web-based KMS. However, if we assume that the production of knowledge is culturally situated and power laden, as the French critical theorist Foucault (1979) claims, then it would seem that there is room for a fifth research stream. This stream can be called the ‘critical research stream’ into KMS. Hence, following the call of Myers & Klein (2011) for more critical research in IS, we draw on Foucault’s work to explore the development and use of KMS in Chinese culture.

## Theoretical framework

## Critical research in IS

Critical research in IS ‘is concerned with social issues such as freedom, power, social control and values with respect to the development, use and impact of information technology’ (Myers & Klein, 2011, p. 17). A critical stance is taken toward taken-for-granted assumptions about organizations and IS, where the aim is to critique the status quo ‘through the exposure of what are believed to be deep-seated, structural contradictions within social systems’ (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991, p. 6).

Although most critical IS research has been based on the critical social theory of Habermas (Myers & Klein, 2011), some IS research work has used concepts from Foucault (Willcocks, 2004). For example, Zuboff (1988) used Foucault’s concepts related to power/knowledge to examine information use and technology at work, while Doolin (2004) applied Foucault’s theory of disciplinary power to the implementation and use of a medical information system. Doolin showed how the surveillance process facilitated through the new information system increased the visibility of the resources used for patient care. Introna (2003), drawing on Foucault’s work, critically examined the status of IS as an academic discipline, while Introna & Whittaker (2004) used Foucault’s theory of disciplinary power to study what gets published in top IS journals. Avgerou & McGrath (2007), drawing on Foucault’s concepts of power/knowledge and the esthetics of existence, argued that the rational techniques of IS practice and the power dynamics of an organization and its social context are closely intertwined, requiring each other to be sustained.

The common thread in all these IS studies is that Foucault’s work is used to open up the topics of emancipation, surveillance, and power relations to critical inquiry (Brooke, 2002). Our approach builds on these earlier studies except that it is one of the first to apply some of Foucault’s concepts to KMS and, in particular, to the relationship between cultural values and the production of knowledge in KMS.

## Gaze

One of Foucault’s concepts is that of the gaze, which he developed during his empirical investigations into madness and medicine. The gaze is a technique of those in power to control those who are gazed upon (patients or inmates). ‘The gaze both gives power to the all-seeing so that they might cure the ills of the gazed upon and renders its subjects into self-policing docile bodies which behave in the “approved” manner’ (Hillier, 1996, p. 95). Although the concept of gaze is especially applicable to those incarcerated in prisons and in mental institutions, the concept is also relevant to the surveillance of people more generally.

Both Foucault and Lacan adopt a sociological approach in their focus on the Other’s gaze. In Lacanian theory, which Foucault built upon, gaze is that point from which the subject is seen, the point from which the subject is pictured. According to Lacan, ‘the gaze I encounter y is not a seen gaze, but a gaze imagined by me in the field of the Other’ (Seminar XI 84). The subject perceives the gaze as the Other’s object cause of desire, and that is why he claims: ‘Man’s desire is the desire of the Other’ (Seminar XI 115). This argument has several implications. First, one’s desire is essentially the desire of the Other, meaning that one becomes the Other’s object of desire. That is, a person turns into something that the Other wants because of his or her desire for recognition by the Other. Second, what turns a person into an object of desire is not the intrinsic character of that person, but the fact that the Other desires it. Finally, desire is always a desire for something else, since I cannot desire what I already have (Evans, 1996, pp. 37–38).

Thus, as long as the individual has the desire for recognition by the Other, he or she is inhibited by the gaze through an imagined relationship to the objects, as well as through an imagining of himself as object (Lacan, 1981). Although the subject is to some extent structured by the Other, the whole relationship between people is also structured by the discourse of the culture that we all inhabit.

In many KMS, the power of the gaze is amplified through surveillance technology that allows people to watch the activities of others without being seen. This vertical gaze via IT is associated with the hierarchical organizational structure that is normally exercised in the name of management or security (Ball & Wilson, 2000; Timmons, 2003). The horizontal gaze is from any member of the organization who wishes to serve as a gatekeeper of employee responsibility to organization as a whole (Zuboff, 1988). Hence, the vertical and horizontal gazes together can form an omnipresent gaze place where the individual is driven into self-surveillance (Zuboff, 1988; King, 2001; Engberg, 2003). In this situation, the individual is in a conscious and permanent state of being gazed upon. Under a perceived constant gaze from the Other, an individual (self-) disciplines his/her behavior. A permanent state of visibility thus assures the automatic functioning of power (Foucault, 1979; Zuboff, 1988; King, 2001).

## Face

A related concept to gaze, and one that is very relevant to Chinese culture, is that of face. According to Goffman (1967), face is one’s situated identity. To fail to have one’s identity ratified is to lose face in an encounter, whereas to have one’s identity ratified is to have face. Face, by definition, is not something that resides within an individual; rather, it emerges within the flow of events in an encounter (Goffman, 1955). Brown & Levinson (1987) argue that the desire for face is a universal phenomenon for social actors in their interaction with others in any culture. However, face takes different forms and meanings depending on the specific cultural environment (Chang & Holt, 1994).

In Chinese culture, ‘the concern for face is indicative of other-directedness, that is, having sensitivity to how one appears in the eyes of others, and attendance to act in ways which meet their approval y. A person’s face is assessed in terms of what others think of him; it includes what he thinks others think of him’ (Ho, 1976, pp. 875–876). This can be understood from Stover & Stover’s (1976) description of Chinese face:

For them [the Chinese], there is no psychological guesswork about sincerity because the self is defined by the sum of one’s social roles, not by an inner man who handles those social roles with fidelity to one’s true feeling y. One’s life is embedded in a social setting from which he derives his status and to which he owes the duty of interaction. Sincerity means discharging one’s formal duties to maintain the system in which he acts. Not to do so is to lose face, which is more than embarrassment or the loss of social poise, but the loss of one’s power position in a set of stratified relationships. (Stover & Stover, 1976, pp. 202–203)

In Chinese collectivistic culture, face-work is not only personal (i.e., my face), but also collective (our face). Chinese feel pride or disgrace from the successes and failures of members of their social group or families (Hu, 1944). Face is played out in the daily social interactions by following a set of rigid norms of varying degree of relationships and social ordering (Chang & Holt, 1994). One’s ability of playing out face and formulating the appropriate behavior to achieve an accord with others is considered one of life’s greatest accomplishments (Tu, 1985). Face-work in Chinese collectivistic culture therefore requires a high degree of self-reflection and selfmonitoring. If people fail to conform to group rules, they are viewed as immature and subsequently lose both personal and collective face (Yang, 1945; Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Triandis & Suh, 2002).

As a result, one might expect that face-work and fear of the gaze would regulate users’ conduct and discipline the creation and sharing of knowledge in KMS, which can potentially threaten both personal and collective face. For example, the knowledge shared has to be good enough to ‘earn’ face for both the individual and the group, while at the same time the individual has to be careful to appear ‘modest’ so as not to harm others’ face (Huang et al, 2011). KMS thus become a minefield for administering ‘my face’, ‘our face’, ‘other’s face’, and ‘other groups’ face’.

## Research site

## Teachers in a Chinese culture

The teacher community in Taiwan is commonly described as the ‘teacher family system’. This system is hierarchically structured in a rigid and tight manner. In this community, there exists a set of rigorous doctrines that are meant to be followed by all teachers: be kind, be honest, be reverential, be provident, and be modest. In practice, this means that teachers are expected to ‘respect seniority/authority’, ‘love colleagues’, ‘do not argue or fight against peers’, ‘do not show off’, and ‘be in conformity with professional conventions’. Moreover, the teacher community upholds the value of being ‘mistakefree’. This can be better understood if we consider that 28 September is designated as National Teachers’ Day. As the teaching profession carries a high degree of respect within the society, teachers are thanked on this day for embodying wisdom, excellence, knowledge, and perfection. Accordingly, being free of error becomes the belief of both the teacher community and society at large. These doctrines and values not only are internalized by the teachers, but also become shared habits ingrained in social interactions. Warnings are deployed to sanction teachers who violate the rules, rendering the teacher community a well-disciplined social system. For example, the injunction to ‘respect seniority/authority’ means that all teachers in Taiwan will not openly question or disagree with someone higher up. To do so would threaten the face of one's superior. Hence. a lack of questioning of authority is internalized by the teachers and becomes a shared habit.

## SCTNet and its historical context

During the early 1990s, in Taiwan, educational polices were dramatically changed and primary and secondary school teachers found themselves in a continuous struggle to grow in professional knowledge, to design teaching materials, and to develop flexible course assignments to evaluate students’ learning. A web-based KMS, named Smart Creative Teacher Network (SCTNet, http:// sctnet.edu.tw/), was developed as a response to this need in late 1999 along with the support from government funding, university professors’ leadership in systems development, and K-12 teachers’ endorsement.

The objectives of SCTNet are many: to provide a professional setting for teachers to share with and learn from others; to help teachers extend their social networks beyond their own school; to cultivate teachers into smart and creative professionals. With these objectives, SCTNet evolved through three stages. In the first stage, the SCTNet developers built the Resource Sharing features for teachers to share their class designs. However, after implementation, only a few teachers uploaded their designs (i.e., created knowledge) to SCTNet. In the next stage, the SCTNet developers implemented the Discussion Board that allowed teachers to discuss educational issues openly and resolved their problems via collective efforts. Again, to the disappointment of the SCTNet developers, SCTNet teachers either were quiet or raised only trivial issues in the Discussion Board. In the third stage, the Professional Workshop was added to SCTNet, aiming to cultivate smart and creative teachers. The Professional Workshop was a private space for members sharing the same special interest. Admissions to the workshop were granted only when existing members voted in favor. Other features were Coffee Shop, Chat Room, and electronic bulletin for teachers to chat, post, and share news about placement service and career planning.

Teachers have to be registered to become SCTNet members in order to post work-related issues, participate in discussions, critique postings from other professionals, and to upload and download instruction materials. The knowledge they contribute is verified and administered by knowledge managers who are selected for their in-depth knowledge of certain teaching domains.

From the time of its implementation, various stakeholders, such as central and local governments, university professors, and the tactical team formed by K-12 school teachers, have expended considerable effort in promoting SCTNet. These efforts include governmentbacked countrywide workshops, top-down formal policy reinforcements from education administrations, anniversary celebration meetings, and bottom-up informal verbal recommendations among teachers. By 2008, SCTNet had over 100,000 registered members and over 20,000 verified knowledge units were stored. However, despite this apparent success, the actual knowledge creation and sharing activities on SCTNet are rather meager. For example, according to the official log maintained by the system, only 2% of the total registered members access SCTNet each day, and 80% of the overall knowledge posted is contributed by less than 5% of those who access SCTNet.

## Research method

We used in-depth case study research to study SCTNet. The main architect of SCTNet was a university professor Klein and Myers (1999) who previously had been an elementary school teacher. Access to SCTNet was easy for us because this Professor and two of the authors were colleagues in the same academic department. More importantly, our interests in SCTNet converged as we observed that, despite most members having indicated their desire to create, store, distribute, and discuss their teaching knowledge. the actual usage of SCTNet was limited. After gaining formal consent from the SCTNet administration, our research team registered as SCTNet members, attended SCTNet meetings, collected documents, and had informal conversations with SCTNet members. Subsequently, the researchers, via their social network in SCTNet, identified both active and non-active members on the use of SCTNet to be interviewed. Most of the data were obtained over a 6-month period. A total of 49 participants, consisting of 21 males and 28 females, were interviewed in our study. Five of these were school principals, 18 were school deans, and 26 were teachers. The length of time informants had been using SCTNet ranged from 1 to 5 years. The profile of participants is shown in Table 1.

All the qualitative interviews were semi-structured and followed the recommendations suggested by Myers & Newman (2007). For example, we used the snowballing technique to identify further informants to be interviewed. All interviews were conducted in Mandarin. These interviews were subsequently translated into English by the two first authors (both are native speakers of Mandarin). The length of the interviews varied from 2 to 3 h for each participant. The interviews were recorded using a digital recorder and were transcribed verbatim. After the interviews, the transcripts were shared with the participants for their approval.

## Findings

In Taiwan, influenced by Confucian culture, the title of teacher embraces the notion of a ‘sacred role’ that is worthy of the respect. The consequences of losing face for any teacher would be losing the respect of peers/ colleagues. supervisors. students. parents. and society more generally. Therefore, this role demands a great deal of face-work especially in a setting like SCTNet, where a teacher’s behavior can be easily observed. Given the tight Taiwanese teacher community, everyone knows what other teachers do both at school and on SCTNet. Hence, Taiwanese teachers bring with them an acute sensitivity of face, which meant that our informants were ambivalent about using the KMS. On one hand, our informants recognized the potential benefits of SCTNet as a resource for helping them to broaden their horizons and a place where they could fulfill their sacred role of teacher. On the other, they saw the KMS as a gazed place, the ‘invisible eye’ that exercises surveillance without being seen. They now felt the need to act in a manner that was worthy of respect. ‘To share or not to share’ succinctly summarizes the ambivalent feelings of the majority of our informants’ concerning their experiences with SCTNet. We will now discuss the various themes that emerged.

## The gaze in KMS

An invisible eye In theory, a SCTNet ID provides access to SCTNet and shields a person’s real-world identity. Some KMS researchers have suggested that with virtual anonymity, individuals will be free to express their ideas in group discussion (DeSanctis & Gallupe, 1987; Jessup et al, 1990). Yet in reality, anonymity was easily lost in SCTNet due to the surveillance power of IT and the tight ‘familylike’ teacher community in Taiwan: As one teacher explained:

I have some concerns that if I put my viewpoints in detail, unknown others can easily recognize who I am via the traceable content and context that I depict. The teacher community is rigid and tight y. Even though on SCTNet (there is supposed to be) anonymity protection via information technology, it still is easy to recognize (who I am) through the teacher social network system y. Any clue can be traced to find out who I am.

The result is that teachers can easily find out the identity of other SCTNet members. Many teachers perceive SCTNet as a gazed place where their acts are gazed upon not only by close allies, but also powerful and unknown others:

Here [on SCTNet], people know my ID y using my ID is the same as using my name! In fact, I criticized the education policy of the Education Administrator on SCTNet once, and after that, I was told to shut up y

I use several IDs [on SCTNet] to secure my anonymity because I once had my ID revealed, and the opinions I posted on SCTNet were sent back to my school. This bothers me seriously y

It is easy to tell who posts messages as I read the tone of voice, the pattern of writing, and the content of the event description. The name doesn’t need to be written y. Actually, the teacher community is so tight that it is hard to retain anonymity y

Actually, from the text, it is easy to know who the author is y if you stay there [SCTNet] long enough. The teacher community is tight y. That’s why I need to put an extra note after I depict my opinion to state ‘this is only my personal perspective; it doesn’t reflect my school’s policy

The following tells the story of how one teacher discovered the gazed features of the KMS:

When I joined a discussion on SCTNet, I posted my opinions; yet, after I did that everyone stopped discussing y. Alarm bells started to ring – ‘Where was everybody?’ y. I got a big shock and realized that I was in trouble y

This teacher felt shunned by other teachers on SCTNet and feared that punitive sanctions might follow if her identity was discovered by management. This illustrates Foucault’s point that ‘He who is subjected to a field of visibility, and who knows it, assumes responsibility for the constraints of power y he inscribes in himself the power relation in which he simultaneously plays both roles; he becomes the principle of his own subjection’ (Foucault, 1979, pp. 202–203). Thus, the rigid and tight teacher community renders the supposed anonymity protection of SCTNet virtually useless.

Face-work rituals Rituals are the repeated observance of set rules and procedures of cultural communications and actions. In our case, the face-work rituals are performed by teachers who share a mutual belief in the validity of the interaction rules (and these rules are themselves grounded in the social norms of the teacher community). Some of these face-work rituals were repeatedly observed and performed in the KMS.

Given the teacher family system and the Panoptic power of IT (Zuboff, 1988; Ball & Wilson, 2000; Timmons, 2003), colleagues and peers in the teaching community can gaze upon one another’s activities on SCTNet. Teachers gaze upon others not only for the purpose of acquiring knowledge for self-improvement, but also to see how good or bad they are in comparison with other teachers:

I always gaze on others’ sharing to know where I am. I found there are plenty of grand masters on SCTNet; I cannot contribute much beyond them.

Being aware that other teachers can gaze on him or her, a teacher must act guardedly:

I am not only a teacher but also a member of K-12 education counselor of Education Board. In SCTNet, I always act cautiously to avoid criticizing the educational policies since they are made by the Education Board. I don’t want to threaten the face of Education Board and hurt the feelings of my boss y

Moreover, government administrators, school principals, and deans have the chance to conduct their vertical gaze on the behaviors of SCTNet members. As our informants explained:

I put a lot of efforts to encourage my colleagues to use SCTNet. Every night, I will check what my colleagues posted in the workshop and try to answer their inquiries. As a principal, SCTNet provides me an efficient way to communicate with my colleagues.

The Education Administration Department in our county requests every school that is under its administering to establish a workshop on SCTNet. The principal can go on his/her school workshop on SCTNet to see the content shared by the teachers.

Even those in authority must act carefully to ensure that their conduct on SCTNet does not deviate from the culture of face, as shown in the following quote:

I am a director. I have the duty to help my colleagues in absorbing new knowledge. Therefore, I hold a workshop in SCTNet and post a lot of resources on it. I always encourage colleagues to join my workshop as a member and read what I have shared. Luckily, my peers give me a big face that most of them join my workshop. I always urge them to check my posting in workshop as soon as I post a new message or new shared resource.

Respecting and following the ruler/subject relationship is a ritual of face-work, necessary for teachers, administrators, and principals. On one hand, administrators must be seen to be error-free; on the other, teachers must avoid offending administrators and principals in public. The consequences of not following this cultural norm on SCTNet can be seen as follows:

Our school workshop on SCTNet is like a ghost town so that no one is there now. This is because our principal found out a teacher posted a conflicting thought in the school workshop on SCTNet; he was angry and declared that he would find out who the teacher was. From then on, no one wanted to share in the school workshop on SCTNet.

Posting a ‘conflicting thought’ was thus seen as threatening the face of the principal, thus leading to the threat of punitive sanction. Not saying anything at all, however, can also lead to disciplinary consequences for teachers:

I have a bad impression of SCTNet because administrators forced us to use SCTNet y. I had the experience of being kicked out of SCTNet by the Education Administrator because I didn’t sign in on time to access what they posted

Clearly, the presence of an invisible eye from both vertical and horizontal gazes creates a certain amount of psychological stress for SCTNet members. Since being a teacher is a sacred role and a symbol of Confucian values, personal, collective, and others’ faces need to be carefully watched and guarded.

Reward and discipline The research literature suggests that KMS can improve efficiency, effectiveness, reliability, and accessibility of knowledge (Ofek & Sarvary, 2001). However, a KMS that is loaded with rules, policies, systems, structures, and processes can also be exploited by those in authority in the name of improving productivity. For example, some education officials implemented a policy to praise and reward teachers who accessed SCTNet frequently. An SCTNet function was set up to record the numbers of registered SCTNet members and the quantity of postings for each school district as the basis for assessing the effort put in by each district. These designs were considered by the district officers as important measurements of success and, accordingly, an indicator of gaining face. As expressed by one of the school principals:

In SCTNet, there is a forum for sharing educational resources. We encourage our teachers to share their resources with their peers on SCTNet. As a result of this, our school is top in our city for the amount of sharing. We always give support to teachers and encourage them to share on SCTNet.

At the same time, a teacher explained how the top ranking in her particular county was achieved:

Our supervisor requested all of us to submit course planning to SCTNet because when he was in the meeting of the Education Administration Department, the amount of sharing in our county was found to be very little. He was not happy with this, so he asked every school to upload their teaching plans onto SCTNet. As a result of this, our county was evaluated as the top county for participation.

As can be seen, this supervisor asked for greater participation from the teachers in his county so that his and everybody’s face can be saved. But it seems that knowledge creation and sharing was assessed by education administrators in terms of quantity rather than quality. Increasing the number of users of SCTNet became a way of gaining face.

I saw some workshop leaders posting a lot of news and information, proposing questions, and uploading resources to meet the quantity demand set by the Education

Administrator all the time y They don’t care too much about the quality of sharing y

SCTNet teachers’ pursuit of ‘quantity’ instead of ‘quality’ is thus a necessity for due care of face. Teachers must subjugate their own self to the disciplinary power of the KMS to ensure that they and their colleagues will gain or, at least, not lose face.

## The faces that are gazed upon

‘I am smart, but I am not free from error’ As mentioned earlier, the teaching profession in Taiwan carries a high degree of respect within society, and teachers are expected to embody wisdom, excellence, knowledge, and perfection. Teachers are also keenly aware that the intent of SCTNet is to provide a virtual shared forum for talented teachers from all over the country. They know that the first two letters of SCTNet stand for ‘Smart’ and ‘Creative’. Statements like ‘SCTNet is a professional forum’ frequently surface in official depictions of SCTNet.

Ironically, two opposing forces are at work on SCTNet. On one hand, a teacher who wishes to be recognized as a master (i.e., to earn a positive face) on SCTNet must contribute his or her ‘smart’ and ‘creative’ knowledge. On the other, any mistakes may cause him or her to lose face. A dilemma is created, as the following teacher explains:

Before logging into SCTNet, I will consider all possible situations that can happen and prepare well to deal with them. y Actually, I am afraid of failing as a professional teacher. Thus, while I share my opinion on SCTNet, I go to great lengths to prevent any problems occurring in advance

I am a teacher now, so I should think carefully before I post my thoughts or make responses to questions on SCTNet. ‘Does my posting inspire any one? Is it helpful? Am I professional enough?’ are always in my mind when I am sharing on SCTNet y because I am a teacher now y

The concern for face is especially critical for teachers in public settings such as SCTNet, where one’s identity might become known. In addition, the expectation of being ‘smart’ and ‘creative’ significantly raise teachers’ concern of face:

I am afraid y afraid to say something wrong, to be laughed at, not have enough insight, or to be perceived as having inadequate knowledge while I express my ideas on SCTNet y

Some of my teaching experiences are successful, yet some of them are failures. With regard to those failures in particular, I would rather share them with my closest friends in the teaching domain than with those strangers on SCTNet.

Subjected to the gaze of the Other, ‘remaining errorfree’ was recognized by our informants as the professional norm desired by the Other. Statements such as ‘I am afraid I am not good enough’ or ‘If my idea is not good enough, I feel embarrassed to present it’ were common in our informants’ narratives. Since losing face has the potential to inflict serious damage to the individual and the group, many teachers find it easier not to contribute to the KMS at all.

Keeping quiet to save face Supporting the findings of Detert & Edmondson (2007), our informants often chose to remain quiet in public interactions in order to minimize the probability of losing face. In terms of social distance, teachers in Taiwan consider those with whom they can share their intimate thoughts, beliefs, and feelings as the ‘in-group,’ and those whom they cannot as the ‘out-group’ (Huang et al, 2011). Participants in our research said that they discussed possible shortcomings and deficiencies only among their closest friends. They would keep silent with the ‘out-group’ to save themselves from embarrassment:

If I don’t know how unfamiliar people see me, I would rather choose to remain quiet in front of them y

I have had the experience of saying something wrong in front of strangers and receiving their criticism. I felt so stupid and lost face y. I should have kept quiet in front of them.

This pattern of remaining quiet to save face was also reproduced in SCTNet. Although SCTNet is supposed to be about knowledge creation and sharing, our informants perceived SCTNet as a transparent public social place where many others (some of them experts) gazed upon them. It was therefore considered very important to present oneself scrupulously in light of the fact that they were being watched, yet without being able to see who was watching. Informants discussed their anxieties about facing the Other’s gaze in two ways.

First, informants explained why in SCTNet invisible Others made them uncomfortable as they presented themselves. The main problem is that they had no way of knowing how well or how badly their faces were being evaluated under the gaze of Others:

While writing in the SCTNet, it makes me feel y as though I am naked and taking a shower over there y. In my mind, I know that many people are there, yet I don’t know what they think of my sharing. They just keep quiet.

Second, informants explained that thoughts expressed on SCTNet might expose them to danger and the possibility of losing face, particularly if their identity became known. One of our informants explained:

A lot of SCTNet members know my ID well. Recently, some teachers discussed their viewpoints about Habermas on SCTNet. However, their perspectives are completely different from my knowledge y. Gradually, I hesitated to share what I know about Habermas on SCTNet. I started to question myself ‘is my idea right?’ I am afraid to make a mistake. I choose to listen to others’ voices instead of expressing my viewpoint on SCTNet.

For this teacher, having a different perspective risked being stigmatized as being wrong. Hence, to avoid being seen as wrong and to save face, she prefers to remain silent.

The net result is that teachers in SCTNet, using their own self-governing capabilities, discipline themselves. They bring their own conduct into alignment with what society believes a teacher should be. They choose to keep quiet in SCTNet as an ethical principle and as a valid facework strategy in response to the Others’ gaze. As one teacher explained: ‘If I don’t know how the invisible other professionals perceive me, I will remain silent y’. Another commented: ‘I am afraid when speaking in front of invisible others y’. Here, the Others mentioned by our participants were teaching professionals and supervisors registered as SCTNet members.

Business as usual ‘The force of habit is so strong’ was often mentioned by our informants when they described their school life. Informants were used to working alone in settings secured from public scrutiny. These routines enabled them to conceal their deficiencies in the context of sharing practice, and ensured that their actions were consistent with those of others. They believed that as long as they followed these shared habitual routines, then they did not need to worry excessively about making mistakes and losing face. These culturally ingrained habits militated against the sharing of knowledge in a KMS.

First, informants indicated that they were trained to be good listeners rather than question-raisers in the Taiwanese education system. They were not used to going through the dialectical process required for effective knowledge creation and sharing:

When I was in teacher training, I don’t remember being trained to share y the training program didn’t mention how to discuss my knowledge with peers y

We didn’t have the opportunity to experience the dialectical process, since few discussions happened in the class. As a result, we never asked, ‘Why this but not that?’ y

Next, informants explained that in the rigid hierarchical structure of the teaching system, they were educated to respect seniority and to love their colleagues so as to maintain harmony in the school:

I am enthusiastic about helping others by providing my opinion, but it is always rejected. Other teachers advised me, as a novice, not to rush to express myself. I should do this and do that y, I think the suggestion made sense and so I followed this advice.

Our informants were aware that breaking the cultural routines valued by the professional society could create anxiety and stress for other teachers. An idea that was deemed ‘too good’ could be seen a threat to others’ faces and to the otherwise superficial harmony, especially if it had the potential to change the old ways of doing things. As some teachers stated:

I keep thinking that I should give my class design to others. I had the experience of people blaming me for doing too much. They said, ‘You did so much, then, what should we do?’

If a teacher’s proposition were accepted by the school principal, other teachers might need to increase their workload as a result. Then they would say, ‘It’s your entire fault; why are you so vociferous?

Accordingly, the old cultural routines persisted, and those who acted consistently with those routines were praised as model teachers. This was further strengthened by another recognized virtue of a good teacher – being humble. To be critical and creative could be seen as showing off, something that a good teacher should never do. In particular, teachers of a low rank are supposed to follow the routine of being good listeners when participating in knowledge creation and sharing activities, even though this might hinder his or her growth as a professional:

‘Is my statement right or will it arouses the anger of others?’ y Instead of worrying about it, I choose to be a listener y

I always answer the questions passively on SCTNet. I am afraid that if I respond to the question actively, people may think I am showing off my knowledge.

Meanwhile, seniority regulated informants’ knowledge creation and sharing behavior both in the school setting and on SCTNet. The practice of ‘saying what you can say according to your rank’ is a virtue that guided informants’ involvement in SCTNet, as illustrated by one junior teacher:

When I am on SCTNet, I can post nothing; simply sit in front of the computer reading the discourse y. In fact, I would like to participate, but I am a novice y. I am really concerned that my status is not high enough to warrant my making any comment. People may laugh at my opinions y it bothers me a lot y

Another informant explained that as a senior teacher, she should be able to provide qualified suggestions about knowledge creation and sharing practices. This was an ethical duty to honor both her position (being senior) as well as the teaching profession. Thus, when she gave feedback to questions on SCTNet, she asked herself:

‘Is my posting helpful to others or can it impart rudimentary knowledge to beginners?’ I have to think more before responding to the questions y. I am no longer a novice y

To be creative/smart teacher in knowledge creation and sharing practices often requires a teacher to be free from the restrictions and repression of the established order. They may question whether they should follow the shared habitual routines of being mistake free and face working, or if they should constitute themselves as moral subjects, becoming creative and smart in instructing and guiding students in innovative ways. However, in most cases, teachers under the Others’ gaze adjust themselves to ‘fit in’ with others and comply with the demand for face-saving.

## The ‘secured workshop’: freedom when faces are safe

Desire to own a private space for sharing In Taiwanese schools, teachers who wish to share do so in a private space in the school where they feel free to be themselves. In informants’ narratives, the most often mentioned place of free sharing was ‘the space for you and me’. This space, built by friends with kinship-like connections, allows individual teachers to freely show their true selves. This small space, with no surveillance function at all, was regarded as ‘the private in-group space’. As one teacher described:

When I have a new method of class management, I can’t wait to share what I have with my good friends y

Most teachers thought that it was acceptable to share professional knowledge in a private space only, since sharing it with others might be perceived as encroaching on their professional territory. Some of our informants commented:

In terms of professional skill, I would not dare to interfere with another’s expertise y unless he was my close friend. Otherwise others would ask, ‘Who do you think you are? What do you think you can teach me?’

If we were very familiar with each other, then I would share with you, otherwise I would be afraid that you may think I was stepping on your professional territory!

Given the anxiety aroused by the gaze of the Other, virtual closed-space workshops were established by the SCTNet developers as a private space for knowledge sharing. This ‘Professional Workshop’ feature enables teachers who have similar interests to get together to share their teaching experiences without any external moderation. The establishers of each Professional Workshop own the privileges of the space and determine who can be admitted. To be admitted, a teacher first has to apply and to be received by existing members.

For our informants, this meant that these private spaces were for in-group members only. Most were overwhelmingly positive about these private spaces.

I feel SCTNet is great! It provides a facility for us to decide whether our workshop will be open to the public or not. I can check and verify the credentials of whoever applies to become a member. I think this function is really important for us. Without it, others could easily read our discussions y this would make us feel very insecure y. Especially, from the gaze of administrators y

Everyone who wishes to join our workshop has to introduce her/himself to us. After that, all members of our workshop will vote to discuss her/his request. Once admitted, I will grant her/him permission to be one of us. This secure function is something to be thankful for.

With the closed-space workshops, teachers could now have debates with peers without the fear of encroaching upon others’ professional territory and losing face:

We discussed the method of constructivism of mathematics teaching for over thirty rounds in our workshop, despite the fact that we had conflicting perspectives y

We always have debates in our workshop on SCTNet y we expect that through our discourses, new ideas will emerge y

In addition, informants recognized that only in this private space could they reveal their shortcomings and mistakes without losing face:

In this group [a professional workshop on SCTNet], even if I say something wrong, they still encourage me y because we are a family and have the same aim – to be excellent teachers.

y I did something that was regrettable while I taught in school y. I explained this experience in our workshop [on SCTNet] and got a lot of support from my fellows.

As can be seen, the professional workshop is a private in-group space where face is protected. In this virtual private space, teachers exchange ideas, share intimate thoughts, and discuss government educational policies. In some ways, the workshops can be seen as a virtual equivalent of the offline ‘in-group’ of personal friends.

A new castle in the KM forum The creation of workshops on SCTNet was not without a price: while it created a secure, ‘family-like’ environment for teachers who were close to one another, the private workshop now became in effect a well-fortified castle. This meant that the knowledge creation and sharing on SCTNet was now compromised. Many of our informants were denied access to these castles and described their frustration as follows:

I wanted to join them and made an application twice, but they rejected me! I introduced myself in detail and tried to win their approval but I didn’t succeed. Any applicant who wants to join has to get two thirds of the group members approval to get in y

They are so closed-minded. They just share among their small group. This is not the essence of sharing y it should be open, unselfish, and fair y

These applicants, who were perceived as ‘outsiders’ by workshop members, resented being rejected. At the same time, workshop members were pleased that this IT design gave them legitimate authority to protect their castle:

In our castle, which we use as our own metaphor, only we can understand. We criticize school and education policies, talk about our daily life in the school, our ideology, and even our physical ailments. This is our language, which ensures that outsiders cannot know who we are and what we talk about.

It [the professional workshop] brings us together. We have been group members for many years. We own our shared understanding and we know each others’ stories. These experiences cannot be shared with outsiders y

The SCTNet professional workshops have thus become virtual castles. These workshops, free from the gaze of Others, enable teachers to protect and defend face. An unintended effect, however, is that this KMS feature now prevents knowledge sharing across the entire Taiwanese teaching community.

## Discussion

In this paper, we have examined the use of KMS in a Chinese collectivist culture. We found that cultural concerns about face constrain teachers’ knowledge sharing activities. ‘To share or not to share’ aptly summarizes the dilemma that the majority of our informants’ found themselves in. Many Taiwanese teachers would like to share their knowledge, but in the end decided that it was safer to use private professional workshops that serve as a ‘virtual castles’ or ‘safe enclaves’ (Hayes & Walsham, 2000; Newell et al, 2001). By using these private spaces, teachers saved face, but prevented the open sharing of knowledge. We now discuss our findings in light of some concepts from Foucault and, in particular, we explore in more depth the relationship between cultural values and the production of knowledge in KMS.

## Gaze and face in the KMS

In our study, we observed that SCTNet supervisors can legitimately conduct their vertical gaze on teachers’ knowledge sharing behavior with the authority granted them by the hierarchical structure. In addition, peers or unknown professionals with access to SCTNet can horizontally gaze upon others and see what reward/discipline supervisors have given to these participants. This vertical and horizontal gazing turns SCTNet into a technological panopticon, in which ‘ “the many” view themselves and each views “the other” ’ (Zuboff, 1988, p. 351). This has surpassed the original concept of Panopticon by Jeremy Bentham, whose proposal allowed only the supervisors to gaze on each individual from a central tower.

As we have seen, teachers in Taiwan have adapted to SCTNet’s intense surveillance. They have resisted the gaze of this KMS in order to retain a sense of self-control and to avoid face-threatening social interactions. The potential for one’s failure to become public drives teachers to either conform or find ways to thwart the power of the technological Panopticon. For example, they conform by adopting strategies such as ‘keeping quiet to save face’ and following the ‘rituals of face-work’, or they resist the gaze of the KMS by using the Professional Workshop facility to exclude others. These results were not the original intention of the SCTNet designers, who initially conceived of a KMS as an incubator for innovation and free sharing. Yet, the interaction of Chinese face culture with the power of surveillance technology inherent in the KMS has led to this unintended result.

## To be smart and creative while preserving face

In our study, SCTNet teachers felt obligated to act in accordance with various cultural virtues. For example, teachers are aware of their sacred role, which prescribes that they are to embody wisdom, excellence, knowledge, and perfection. Yet it also means that they had better not make public mistakes in their pursuit to fulfill this role.

In addition, they must follow the rigorous doctrines for the teacher profession: be kind, be honest, be reverential, be provident, and be modest. Departing from these virtues would be stigmatized as breaking with the social norms and cause them to lose face. As a result, SCTNet members are placed in a constant state of self-surveillance, disciplining themselves just in case someone, a peer or superior, is watching.

Furthermore, the establishment of private virtual spaces, the ‘secured’ workshops, on SCTNet became the way to deflect the gaze of others. In these workshops, teachers have the freedom to be themselves and to make mistakes, but the desire to become smart/creative teachers is practiced only in private.

## Gaze and knowledge ethics for knowledge creation

In Taiwan, where knowledge is perceived as a symbol of individual social position, a teacher has the privilege of making statements that are automatically asserted to be true. This is the power endowed on teachers by Confucian culture. But paradoxically, a teacher who desires to be recognized as ‘smart and creative’ becomes very cautious under the gaze of others’. This is because, if a teacher feels that s/he is not ‘smart and creative’ enough, he or she fears losing face. The net result is that most teachers in our study prefer to remain silent as a face-work strategy under the omnipresent surveillance of SCTNet. The surveillance capabilities inherent in this KMS restrict the production and sharing of knowledge.

This effect of the omnipresent gaze of SCTNet raises an important issue regarding knowledge ethics (Blain, 1994). Knowledge ethics refers to the freedom that one, as a subject and moral agent, can argue the truth of a problem and/or injustice in an active and convincing way (Blain, 1994; Foucault, 1983, 1984). Knowledge ethics is critically important to professionals in knowledge creation and sharing for knowledge production. The very concept of a professional in an educational setting is one who should always question whether their knowledge is making significant contributions to the field at large (Newbrough, 1993) and one who should pursue the core value of knowledge inquiry, even if this goes against the system (Foucault, 1984). All of these processes are vital to the pursuit of knowledge production.

Knowledge ethics is closely related to concepts of knowledge creation (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Conversely, if an organization discourages its employees from thinking for themselves, employees become objects during knowledge production activities (Foucault, 1979). For KM practices, the knowledge ethic fails if participants are expected to think only in terms of what management thinks. Unfortunately, this was the case with all the public spaces of SCTNet.

## The epistemic paradigm of KMS design

The technology design of KMS, as a virtual, shared public space where people can transform themselves from working alone to collaborating with their community, and as a means through which they can expand their experience from a limited to a broader horizon, is critically important. In our case, teachers recognized the ambiguity of Others in SCTNet as both an opportunity and an impediment to participating in knowledge production practices. On one hand, the Others provided the opportunity to help teachers transform and broaden their lived experience. This allowed teachers who wanted to retain the sacred role of a teacher to expand their network with others beyond the confines of the classroom, to strive to break habits and rules, and to pursue recognition and acceptance as a professional by means of knowledge production activities. On the other hand, the Others were the cause of fear of losing face as a consequence of making mistakes. With this understanding of the paradoxical nature of Others in the KMS, how can KMS technology be designed so that face is taken care of in knowledge production activities?

Here, IT managers and professionals need to consider a different epistemic paradigm of KMS, one that enables participants to emancipate themselves from unwarranted controls so that they can actively shape the way in which they conduct and evaluate themselves in knowledge production. Such KMS designs, without centralized control, are possible. For example, the recent emergence of social networking technologies such as Facebook or Twitter appear to be appropriate for this purpose. Without centralized control, social networking technology allows people who are interested in the same subject and are willing to share to form their own social network and knowledge repository (Accorsi & Costa, 2008; Yang & Ho, 2007). We suggest such a design could potentially support participants in their knowledge sharing and help them to cultivate their professional lives within the Taiwanese context, although the question of anonymity would still need to be resolved in some way.

## Principles of critical research

In order to evaluate the quality of our research, we summarize our paper with respect to Myers & Klein’s (2011) proposed a set of principles for the conduct of critical research in IS. ## Conclusions

KMS have the potential to improve the efficiency, effectiveness, reliability, and accessibility of knowledge (Ofek & Sarvary, 2001). On the basis of this potential, a web-based KMS, named ‘Smart Creative Teacher Network (SCTNet), was developed in Taiwan with the strong support of central and local governments. The espoused objectives for SCTNet were to enable the open, free sharing and creation of knowledge through the processes of interaction, collaboration, and social coordination. It was hoped that this particular KMS would enable all teachers in Taiwan to share with and learn from each other so that they would become ‘smart and creative professionals.

However, as we have seen, the objectives of SCTNet were not met. The actual knowledge creation and sharing activities on SCTNet ended up being rather meager, despite many valiant attempts to encourage greater use of the system. These attempts included three major software upgrades to introduce more features, government-backed countrywide workshops, top-down formal policy reinforcements from education administrations, anniversary celebration meetings, and bottom-up informal verbal recommendations among teachers. Unfortunately, all these efforts were to no avail.

One question that emerges is whether the undermining of knowledge sharing and the failure to achieve the objectives was a result of the KMS and its functionality, or rather, was it due to the prevalent cultural norms undermining the intent of KMS? Our answer is that it was not one or the other. Rather, it was the interaction between the KMS and cultural norms that undermined knowledge sharing. The functionality design of the KMS was such that it enabled teachers to create, distribute, discuss, and criticize knowledge openly. However, the pervasive face culture of the social system in Taiwan inhibited teachers’ willingness to speak out in public. The undermining of knowledge sharing within the KMS only emerged over time as a result of the interaction between the technology and people.

Hence, we conclude that KMS do not always translate well into different cultures. Chinese cultural norms, which can be characterized as being more hierarchical, conservative, conformist, and less open to criticism (at least in public), in effect prevented the sharing of knowledge on SCTNet. While face-work is not restricted to Chinese culture, in a Chinese cultural context saving face becomes all-important. Our study has revealed the culturally situated nature of knowledge sharing in KMS.

Additionally, we have seen how the capabilities for surveillance inherent in the KMS (anyone registered on SCTNet can read all posts, except those in the private workshops) can also restrict knowledge sharing – especially in a Chinese cultural context. In Chinese collectivistic culture, the cultural requirement to protect individual and group face constrains and disciplines knowledge sharing practices in KMS. This cultural requirement combined with the vertical and horizontal gazes of the KMS meant that individual teachers effectively disciplined themselves.

We have examined this self-disciplinary process of the teachers by using the critical theory of Foucault and, in particular, his concept of ‘gaze’ (1979). In Foucault’s theory, the ‘gaze’ is not necessarily the actual gaze of someone else, but can be a perceived or imagined gaze.

It is simply the possibility that someone else might be watching that changes someone’s behavior. In our study, we found that the perceived gaze of others was facilitated and magnified by the power of IT. It was also magnified by the Chinese cultural concern for face. As we said earlier, it was the interaction of both the technology and the culture that drove the teachers into a permanent state of self-surveillance. When these Chinese teachers were faced with a choice – to share or not to share – they considered that face-work was a higher priority than the creation and sharing of knowledge in the KMS.

One advantage of the in-depth case study research method we adopted is that we have seen knowledge management as a longitudinal, dynamic process – we have explored the interplay of technology and culture that shaped individuals’ knowledge sharing activities in this particular context. We have seen this KMS as a social place where individual’s knowledge sharing enactments involve social presentation. In this KMS, the teachers presented themselves to not only known, but also unknown, others. Of course, in a different context, fear of the gaze might diminish or might not be there at all. Hence, our study suggests that we should see KMS as an evolving socio-technical system that is constantly constituted by the interaction of its technical and cultural sub-systems.

One limitation of our research method is that, by definition, our study was limited to the in-depth examination of one KMS only. Given that our research was conducted in Taiwan, our findings may not be so applicable in other cultural settings. However, we believe that our findings may be of interest to all IS researchers doing work in KMS and in particular to those looking at the implementation of KMS in Chinese cultural settings.

In conclusion, by taking a critical research perspective, and in particular by using the concept of ‘gaze’ from Foucault (1979), we have seen that a web-based KMS developed to enable knowledge production among teachers in Taiwan ended up being largely a failure. The system ended up being used in way that was unintended by its developers. Our study is thus one of the first to apply some of Foucault’s concepts to KMS and in particular to the relationship between cultural values and the production of knowledge in KMS. We echo the call of Myers & Klein (2011) for more critical research in IS, and suggest that there is scope for further research of a critical nature into KMS and other related technologies such as social networking. For example, it would be interesting to look at how socio-technical systems such as KMS and social networking technologies continue to co-evolve along within changes in Chinese culture and society.
