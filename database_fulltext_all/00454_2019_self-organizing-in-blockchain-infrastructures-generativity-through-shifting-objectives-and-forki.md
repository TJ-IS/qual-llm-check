# Self-Organizing in Blockchain Infrastructures: Generativity Through Shifting Objectives and Forking

Jonas Valbjørn Andersen<sup>1</sup>, Claire Ingram Bogusz<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>IT University of Copenhagen, Denmark, jova@itu.dk <sup>2</sup>Stockholm School of Economics, Sweden, claire.ingram@hhs.se

## Abstract

Given the ubiquity of digital technologies, and increased use of autonomous algorithms, it is likely that many of today’s social and organizational processes will one day include autonomous elements. The Bitcoin blockchain is likely the first case of an increasingly generative and autonomous way of organizing, and the specific properties of blockchain infrastructures— distribution of control, openness to manipulation, and generativity of the underlying source code— make it an ideal case to study patterns of self-organizing. This paper investigates the phenomenon of self-organizing through a study of forking in the Bitcoin blockchain infrastructure between 2010 and 2016. It adds to the emerging body of research on digital infrastructures, and particularly blockchain infrastructures, by conceptualizing forking as a pattern of self-organizing in blockchain infrastructures that specifically involves the underlying infrastructure, the scale of code changes, individual objectives, and collective adoption, whether specific or general. Thus, this paper demonstrates how forking in blockchain infrastructures mediates between divergent organizing objectives and existing capabilities, on the one hand, and generates self-organizing on the other hand. In this paper, we further contextualize our findings in extant work on digital infrastructures, offer a guide for designers of blockchain infrastructures, and propose the concept of “generative mirroring” as a pattern through which blockchain infrastructures and organizing adaptively coevolve.

Keywords: Bitcoin, Blockchain, Digital Infrastructure, Self-Organizing, Forking, Generativity.

Professor Matti Rossi was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on September 19, 2018, and underwent two revisions.

## 1 Introduction

One of the most anticipated applications of blockchain technologies is that of the autonomous, self-governing and self-regulating infrastructure, for instance through a distributed autonomous organization (DAO)<sup>1</sup> (Elnaj, 2018; Beck, Czepluch, Lollike, & Malone, 2016; Chapron, 2017; Peters & Panayi, 2015; Wörner, von Bomhard, Schreier, & Bilgeri, 2016). Once fully realized, these DAOs, the archetypical instance of distributed organizing, are expected to be fluid and digital in nature—without offices, managers, contracts, policies or payrolls, and without centralized strategic agendas (Barrett et al., 2016). Instead, it is believed that reliable time stamps and automated, verifiable decision processes will allow not just for fully automated organizing processes at scale, but for more reliable organizing as transparent digital processes replace ad hoc social ones (e.g., Markus & Robey, 1983).

However, we are a long way from understanding what it takes to facilitate the creation of a robust and transparent DAO; the interplay between the autonomous and distributed capabilities of a digital infrastructure like blockchain, and the social objectives that can be realized through it, are still poorly understood. Moreover, early DAOs and firstgeneration blockchain infrastructures have been crippled by tensions between the capabilities of blockchain and the objectives of individuals and communities (e.g., Andersen & Ingram Bogusz, 2017; Avital et al., 2016). For instance, the theft of 150 million USD worth of tokens over an Ethereumbased DAO in May 2016 (see Leising, 2017) was enabled by flaws in the protocol that governed the DAO. The code itself had a loophole that could be exploited and the dynamics involving such exploitation were poorly understood. The theft represents the first of many instances of selforganizing in which the capabilities of blockchain infrastructures and heterogeneous objectives intersected and led to new patterns of organizing and to the pursuit of new objectives.<sup>2</sup> Our understanding of this notion of self-organizing and the dynamics that drive it are still under development: blockchain governance, decision rights, accountability, and incentives have all been highlighted as sources of potential friction (Beck et al., 2018) but have never, to our knowledge, been explored in the context of organizing. This lack of understanding surrounding the interplay between technology capabilities and organizing in the blockchain context may contribute to the observation that radical organizational transformations may take more time than expected (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2017) and may be related to the failure of some early instances of self-organizing mediated by blockchain technology.

In this paper, we focus specifically on organizing at the intersection of digital blockchain infrastructures and distributed social objectives—or, what we call “self-organizing.” Here, we define self-organizing as involving changes in structures, processes, and boundaries between independent actors guided by recursively applied, code-enshrined rules (Drazin & Sandelands, 1992; Holland, 1992; Pettigrew et al., 2001). We further consider the role of digital artifacts in these processes—in this case, blockchain as a digital infrastructure (Leonardi & Barley, 2008). We build on the observation that the fulfillment of organizing objectives through blockchain entails distributed organizing, leading to unclear chains of cause and effect (Beck et al., 2018). Indeed, the dynamics between a distributed blockchain infrastructure and distributed objectives, and how they lead to the fulfillment of these diverse objectives—more broadly thought of as organizing— remain to be explored.

Extant research demonstrates that organizing that “mirrors” the underlying digital artifact(s) on which it depends facilitates the efficient pursuit of organizing objectives (Burton & Galvin, 2018; Tiwana et al., 2010). While the objectives themselves are believed to evolve in response to exogenous environmental changes (Henfridsson & Bygstad 2013), artifacts are treated as controlled by the organization itself, with little influence over the resulting organizational objectives. Instead, hierarchical organizations have clear hierarchies and loci of control: aligning digital capabilities and organizing objectives through mirroring is therefore a question of top-down strategy and design rather than coordination (Lee & Berente, 2012). However, a digital infrastructure, defined as a “shared, unbounded, heterogeneous, open, and evolving sociotechnical system comprising an installed base of diverse information technology capabilities and their user, operations, and design communities” (Tilson et al., 2010, p. 748), has no clear hierarchy or locus of control. Instead, digital infrastructures are characterized by distributed control, fluid technological capabilities, and openness to manipulation by a large and heterogeneous community. Top-down governance is therefore not only unpopular, it is often impossible (O’Mahony & Ferraro, 2007). If there is going to be alignment between capabilities and the pursuit of objectives— and there is no guarantee that there will be—this will need to be emergent and cannot be controlled in the same way as it is in hierarchical organizations. Thus, the possibility of pursuing certain objectives, and thus organizing more generally, likely cannot be controlled ex ante.

Three characteristics specific to blockchain infrastructures affect whether and how diverging organizing objectives can be realized: (1) distributed control of decision rights through transaction verification (Beck et al., 2018), (2) openness to manipulation of infrastructure capabilities (Campbell-Verduyn, 2018), and (3) generativity of organizing objectives (Andersen & Ingram Bogusz, 2017; Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013). Algorithms called consensus mechanisms set the recursively applied rules that govern which node verifies transactions in the distributed ledger (Bano et al., 2017; Chiravuri et al., 2011). As a result, the addition of new transactions to the ledger is controlled by a network of distributed verifier nodes. The source code dictates how the infrastructure operates, including the rules whereby platforms, applications, and other modules connect to it (Nyman & Lindman, 2013). For most public blockchain infrastructures, this code is open to manipulation by an open source (OS) community (Howison & Crowston, 2014), meaning that some of what we know about OS communities may be relevant to understanding blockchain technologies. Finally, although code and algorithms play a key role, so too do the shared objectives of the members of its distributed community of users, developers, and miners (Beck et al., 2016).

We observe that these three characteristics of blockchain infrastructures, along with the related technological and social processes cannot be disentangled: organizing originates from neither the technological capabilities nor from the individuals involved. Instead, it emerges as “self-organizing” interactions between independent actors in a distributed system, governed by recursively applied (code-based) rules that generate stable social structures (Drazin & Sandelands, 1992; Holland, 1992). Thus, self-organizing entails patterns and regularity emerging without central control, whether individual, organizational, or digital (Anderson, 2012).

Research into the relationship between organizing objectives and the evolution of blockchain infrastructures falls short when it comes to understanding self-organizing in the face of the complex interdependencies described above. In this paper, we therefore ask the following question: How does self-organizing occur in blockchain infrastructures?

We address this research question through a multimethod, longitudinal case study of selforganizing in the Bitcoin blockchain infrastructure over the course of six years. The Bitcoin blockchain, while a digital infrastructure, does not have an organizational legacy (see, e.g., Star & Ruhleder, 1996). Instead, the infrastructure was created in isolation by an anonymous individual or group of individuals who later severed ties with the project. As such, Bitcoin does not have an ex ante organizing base (hierarchical or otherwise). Instead, selforganizing emerged from the infrastructure as it evolved. It can therefore be distinguished from both hierarchical and digital infrastructures more broadly, as discussed further below.

The case of the Bitcoin blockchain is instructive in providing insight into the larger phenomenon of selforganizing (Anderson, 2012), generativity associated with blockchain infrastructures (Siggelkow, 2007; Wörner et al., 2016), and ultimately organizing as a more autonomous process mediated by technological capabilities. Moreover, as it is generally considered the parent of subsequent generations of blockchain infrastructures (Avital et al., 2016); its ability to generate self-organizing is likely to help us understand the pursuit of diverging objectives in second (and third) generations of blockchain infrastructures (e.g., Ethereum, Hyperledger, etc.). However, for the sake of being able to draw clear empirical boundaries, we focus only on the Bitcoin blockchain infrastructure in this paper.

For simplicity, we will refer to the emergent social activities surrounding the Bitcoin infrastructure as the “community,” the heterogeneous objectives that emerge pursuant to organizing as “organizing objectives,” distributed ledger technology, in general, as “blockchain,” and to a specific record of transactions as the “distributed ledger” or the “ledger.”

This paper is structured as follows. First, we examine the existing literature on digital infrastructures to identify known organizing principles and outline the differences and similarities between digital and blockchain infrastructures. Based on our review of existing literature, we substantiate the notion of selforganizing in blockchain infrastructures before proposing and elaborating on how code changes, and code forking specifically, act as mechanisms of selforganizing. Thereafter we present the case background and research design, before showing how code forking in the blockchain infrastructure has mediated organizing shifts in the Bitcoin community. Finally, we discuss our research findings and their implications for self-organizing mediated by blockchain infrastructures.

## 2 Self-Organizing in Blockchain Infrastructures

In November 2018, the GitHub account for Bitcoin Core, the first generation blockchain, showed that its source code had been copied for reuse over 21,000 times, that 18,500 changes had been made to the original source code,<sup>3</sup> and that between June 2013 to November 2018 the number of people following its development had risen from 2,300 to over 35,000.<sup>4</sup>

This combination of high levels of activity, a growing user base, and divergent goals and objectives points to the idea that the blockchain is not only a project with a significant following, but also one that is constantly adapting, leading not only to improvements in the code, but also to new permutations with new commercial and noncommercial objectives. These shifts, in turn, lead to self-organizing in its community of users, developers, and miners.

In the following, we first review existing theoretical views on the interplay between infrastructures and organizing before highlighting the specific characteristics of blockchain infrastructures that enable self-organizing. Against this backdrop, we then conceptualize how self-organizing occurs in, and in perpetuation of, blockchain infrastructures.

## 2.1 Existing Views on Infrastructure Organizing

It has been argued that infrastructures possess an “overall capacity to produce unprompted change driven by large, varied, and uncoordinated audiences” (Zittrain 2006, p. 1980), or what is called generativity. The result is emergent (Edwards et al., 2007) and even “accidental” progress, or innovation (Austin et al., 2011) through slow and incremental changes. However, previous studies have not explored the patterns that explain how digital infrastructures, apart from those wholly controlled by single organizations (Ciborra, 2000; Eaton et al., 2015), generate this progression. This begs the question of whether infrastructures, in an age of increased automation and digital ubiquity, participate in their own evolution, and to what extent. Understanding how digital infrastructures perpetuate their own existence helps us unpack the relationship between digital infrastructures and social objectives during the process of (self-) organizing.

The literature on digital infrastructures presents at least four distinct views of how social practices, some of them hierarchical, give rise to digital infrastructure evolution. These views, which we label the adaptation, inscription, interaction, and managerial choice views, are rooted in different bodies of literature.

First, adaptation views see infrastructure evolution as a result of the efforts of distributed human actors to adapt to their environment and to other actors (Braa et al., 2007; Hanseth et al., 2006). Adaptation views build on developments of complexity theory (Holland

1995; Mol & Law, 2002). For example, Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) argue that digital infrastructure design can account for the adaptability of distributed actors, and Nan (2011) explores how the use of distributed digital technology emerges as adaptations between users, technology, and tasks.

Second, inscription views describe how infrastructures evolve as human actors translate and inscribe their interests into assemblages of technological components. Building on actor network theory (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1987), inscription views emphasize the relationship between human actors and technology in translating and inscribing behavior in structural terms. They see infrastructure evolution as changes to a set of relations between humans and technology that occur when human actors mobilize resources to support some organized action (Aanestad & Jensen, 2011; Hanseth & Monteiro, 1997; Yoo et al., 2005). For example, Eaton et al. (2015) describe how the tuning of boundary resources by a network of distributed human actors affects digital infrastructure evolution.

Third, interaction views argue that an infrastructure’s evolution entails continuous interaction between its users and stakeholders as they engage in sensemaking around an organized activity. Drawing on theories of collective learning and work practices (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 2000), interaction views see infrastructure evolution as a result of interactions within a community-of-practice resulting in the formation of sociotechnical relations (Pipek & Wulf, 2009; Star & Ruhleder, 1996; Vaast & Walsham, 2009). For example, situated learning by OS community participants leads to the ability to advise others, as well as the ability to improve the code (Fang & Neufeld, 2009).

Finally, managerial choice views emphasize the role of management decisions in facilitating infrastructure evolution. Infrastructure evolution is seen as a process whereby managers initiate and implement changes to information technology infrastructure in order to align strategic capabilities and strategic objectives (Beckert, 1999; Child, 1997). For instance, Broadbent and Weill (1997) explain how managers draw upon their understanding of the strategic context of their organization to determine the infrastructure capabilities they should implement in order to achieve their business objectives. The notion of “mirroring,” although rooted in a different intellectual tradition, is consistent with this view (Burton & Galvin, 2018).

The views described above and outlined in Table 1 all share three main assumptions about infrastructure organizing: first, that infrastructures are built upon, and follow from, preexisting organized practices (Star & Ruhleder, 1996). Second, in the course of infrastructure evolution, human behavior is inscribed onto the technological components of the infrastructure (Hanseth & Monteiro, 1997), and third that changes to the infrastructure require coordination among heterogeneous and distributed human actors (Ciborra, 2000; Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010). In other words, human objectives and practices, including coordination, determine digital infrastructure evolution.

However, the applicability of these assumptions to the Bitcoin blockchain is unclear, and this has not been tested in previous blockchain studies. In particular, the Bitcoin blockchain has few, if any, preexisting organized practices; the centrality of its recursive, code-based rules mean that it is not controlled exclusively by human objectives and practices, and changes to the infrastructure can occur even without coordinated activities.

Our aim is not to validate these assumptions here, but rather use what we already know about digital infrastructures as a departure point for developing an understanding of how the infrastructure itself, through code-based consensus mechanisms and adoption, leads to emergent self-organizing. In the following, we define and distinguish blockchain infrastructures before proposing and substantiating a specific organizing mechanism: the forking of infrastructure capabilities pursuant to organizing objectives.

## 2.2 Organizing in Blockchain Infrastructures

Blockchain has much in common with other infrastructures: control is not centralized, but rather dynamically negotiated (Weill & Broadbent, 1998), and it is comprised of a layer of code on which both platforms and applications are built and on which organizing practices rely (Tilson et al., 2010; Vaast & Walsham, 2009). As such, it is not just an OS project maintained by a decentralized community, but rather a specific subtype of digital infrastructure, which supports a set of unique and distributed relational practices through which it is organized (Star 1999). Implementations of public blockchain infrastructures (Beck et al., 2018), in particular, are best described as a new breed of digital infrastructure (Bygstad, 2010; Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010).

Although blockchain infrastructures belong to a class of digital infrastructure in that they are a “shared, unbounded, heterogeneous, open, and evolving sociotechnical system comprising an installed base of diverse information technology capabilities and their user, operations, and design communities” (Tilson et al., 2010, p. 748), the constraining properties of blockchains mean that they likely operate differently from infrastructures that have previously been studied (e.g., Iannacci 2010; Kuk and Janssen 2013). Extant research has examined how ex ante organizing structures (Henfridsson & Bygstad 2013; Star 1999) and boundary objects (Eaton et al., 2015) determine infrastructure organizing and evolution, while acknowledging that control is often dynamically negotiated and not necessarily fully centralized (Weill & Broadbent, 1998).

Further, there are at least three reasons why blockchain infrastructures behave in ways not previously discussed in the extant literature on digital infrastructure organizing. It is beyond the scope of this paper to explain all the technicalities of blockchain technology (but see, e.g., Böhme et al., 2015); however, three constraining properties are especially relevant in understanding selforganizing in blockchain infrastructures: distributed control (Beck et al., 2018), openness to manipulation of technological capabilities (Avital et al., 2016), and the generativity of organizing objectives (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010; Nærland et al., 2017).

First, blockchains are not only decentralized, but also distributed: they consist of multiple instances of a ledger that is shared and maintained among a disparate group of actors who are linked by a shared codebase and operate without any hierarchical social structure (Bano et al., 2017; Brenig et al., 2016; Guerini & Moneta, 2017). Also, blockchains are open, in the sense (among other things) that their technological capabilities are open to manipulation. In the case of public blockchain infrastructures such as Bitcoin, a community of distributed enthusiasts instigates potential manipulations (Beck et al., 2018; Campbell-Verduyn, 2018). Many of these individuals are also members of a larger OS community used to tweaking code at will without being subject to any legal or organizational impediment (Fang & Neufeld, 2009). This openness to manipulation leads to increasing heterogeneity in terms of both how the underlying infrastructure is used and the resulting organizing objectives (Ingram Bogusz & Morisse, 2018). Taken together, these characteristics mean that a new technological capability can be proposed and developed by anyone using code. Thus, the dynamics surrounding code development and adoption help us understand how dynamic self-organizing around an emergent organizing objective emerges.

However, community adoption plays a role in determining whether new (sets of) capabilities are discarded or adopted en masse. Further, the source code’s characteristics and the features of proposed changes to the code affect how important adoption is for the evolution of the infrastructure. Indeed, dynamics between the source code and proposed changes, mediated by adoption requirements, determine whether manipulations are incorporated into the infrastructure at all. As such, the capabilities of new as well as preexisting code have implications for how the infrastructure will operate in the future. These capabilities influence organizing activities mediated by the infrastructure, including self-organizing, by enabling and limiting what members of the community can do.

For instance, the distributed process whereby a new transaction is recorded ensures ledger immutability: one member of the community transmits a transaction to others in the network in order to verify that it is legitimate and consistent with previous entries (e.g., to ensure that it does not include already-spent tokens or come from a fake account) and to record it. The software is designed so that transactions can only be added onto the ledger after verification, and cannot be changed or removed once entered without changes to the entire blockchain infrastructure.<sup>5</sup> Should conflicting versions come to exist, the blockchain protocol stipulates that the version of the software, which includes the ledger, held by the majority of those performing the verification (“miners”) is the “real” blockchain (Nakamoto, 2008; Taylor, 2013). This version control system not only makes the ledger itself immutable, it makes the protocol that runs it subject to the same majority-rule requirement. Individual actors thus cannot change the ledger or the protocol. Instead, if one is unhappy with the capabilities of the current implementation, the OS nature of the code means that even fundamental infrastructure capabilities are open to manipulation (Nyman, 2015).

Distributed control and openness to manipulation of technological capabilities has another effect: for fundamental changes, known as forks, to be made to the blockchain, the majority of miners must adopt them. When this occurs, those miners running the version that is in the minority are seen to be running a de facto alternative. That is, they are no longer running a compatible version of the infrastructure: neither the source code that they run, nor the transactions entered into the minority-held alternative ledger, can be recognized by the original blockchain. This is, however, only true when the versions are inconsistent with one another; more subtle rules apply when minor updates of the code or consistent code additions are involved.

The generation and adoption of new technological capabilities are thus controlled both by the rules and the content of the preexisting code, and by a distributed community of miners, developers, and users (Catalini & Gans, 2016). Blockchain infrastructures thus differ substantially from both the hierarchical and digital infrastructures previously studied, as outlined above and summarized in Table 2. While our understanding of how they self-organize builds on the existing understanding of digital infrastructures, it also allows us to better understand next-generation applications of blockchain and related distributed and autonomous technologies. We now move on to explore self-organizing in a blockchain infrastructure and how it is actualized through code forking.

## 2.3 Forking as Self-Organizing in Blockchain Infrastructures

Given the fact that the evolution of blockchain infrastructure capabilities—and therefore organizing—is distributed and emergent, what happens when there is a disagreement about the future of the infrastructure, or what capabilities it should have or enable, from within the community? In a nondistributed or proprietary setting (“hierarchical infrastructure”), the disagreement would be solved with reference to the organization’s hierarchy (Burton & Galvin, 2018; Dahlander & Magnusson, 2005; Kartseva et al., 2010). However, among both digital and blockchain infrastructures, the answer is less simple. In general, members of the community try to come to a negotiated settlement (Nyman, 2015). Yet because blockchain infrastructures rely not just on the protocol at a set point, but also on the historical ledger generated over time, compatibility on a practical level is determined both based on what the protocol looks like at the time of inquiry and whether a proposed change is backward- or forward compatible with the existing blockchain infrastructure. This temporality implies that a disagreement so fundamental that it splits the community may lead to the emergence of two (or more) inconsistent versions of a blockchain infrastructure.

Previous research into hierarchical organizations has suggested that alignment between technologies’ capabilities and organizing objectives not only allows for the effective pursuit of changing organizing objectives (e.g., Burton & Galvin, 2018; Marwaha & Willmot, 2006; Tiwana et al., 2010), but that sustained effectiveness requires that technologies evolve in line with changing organizing objectives (Hirschheim & Sabherwal, 2001). Thus, within established organizations, researchers have examined not just the phenomenon of artifact-organization alignment, but also indicated the importance of adaptive evolution of this alignment in line with evolving infrastructure capabilities and organizational objectives. This research has suggested that (1) digital artifact modularity, or the decomposability of a system into components with limited interdependence (Simon, 1962), and (2) a loosely coupled organizational structure (Tiwana & Konsynski, 2010), facilitate this adaptive alignment. Indeed, modularity and loose coupling give an organization both the flexibility to add and subtract functionality on a technical level, and the agility to do so with limited intervention from the rest of the organization (e.g., Hagel, 2002, Tiwana & Konsynski, 2010).

The result is what has been referred to as “mirroring,” whereby the organizing objectives that govern an infrastructure reflect its underlying technology (Colfer 2007; Colfer & Baldwin, 2016). Such mirroring has been identified, for instance, in areas associated with organizational control (e.g., Weill & Ross, 2005; Tiwana & Konsynski 2010), as well as areas involved in product development and delivery (e.g., Colfer & Baldwin, 2016; MacDuffie, 2013). For example, arguing from the perspective of transaction cost economics, Burton and Gavin (2019) suggest that mirroring occurs for reasons involving effectiveness and discuss how hierarchies are superior to decentralized markets because they allow for more effective pursuit of goals (see also Geyskens et al.,

2006) and reduce technological uncertainty and information overload (Weber & Mayer, 2014, p. 345).

However, extant research has also pointed out that high levels of infrastructural complexity might make it more effective to “mist” the mirror: making alignment and adaptive alignment more limited (Burton & Galvin 2018). This observation has been echoed in studies of OS committal structures: when tasks are routine and risks are low, centralized control may be the most effective form of governance (AlMarzouq et al., 2015). However, centralization and hierarchies are may also have a chilling effect on innovation (Gawer & Cusumano, 2014), and be less effective forms of management when tasks are complex and high-risk (AlMarzouq et al., 2015). Thus, when an infrastructure is complex, emergent, and not centrally controlled, as with a blockchain infrastructure, not only is alignment with formal organizational objectives tricky, it may also be ineffective.

An increasing number of digital infrastructures, most notably those developed and governed by OS communities (O’Mahony & Ferraro, 2007) and those existing in innovation ecosystems (Wareham et al., 2014). are neither developed nor maintained by hierarchical organizations. Blockchain infrastructures are a relatively recent addition to this list. Although transaction cost economics would suggest that such distributed organizations are ineffective and characterized by high levels of uncertainty, research has shown that noncentralized organizations develop new modes of governance and control (O’Mahony & Ferraro, 2007) and deal with uncertainty and information overload differently (Jones et al., 2004). In the case of blockchain infrastructure, we propose that one way in which both control and effectiveness are maintained in the face of complexity and temporal dynamism is through the “generative mirroring” of infrastructure capabilities and organizing objectives— in this case, through blockchain forking.

Forking of infrastructure capabilities takes place when source code is copied and modified in spinouts known as “forks.” Forking occurs when “a part of a development community (or a third party not related to the project) starts a completely independent line of development based on the source code basis of the project” (Robles & González-Barahona, 2012, p. 137). Code forks are typically frowned upon because they negatively affect the reputation of the community and of individual developers (Nyman, 2015; Weber, 2004), and because multiple, incompatible versions of a software can discourage related future development (Meeker, 2008; Nyman, 2015). Nevertheless, both within and beyond existing communities, forks in the source code are a visible reflection of changes in organizing objectives. As such, the notion of code forking, in combination with the mirroring of infrastructure capabilities and organizing objectives, provides a theoretical lens through which digital infrastructure evolution can be studied.

We distinguish three kinds of forks: the pseudo-fork, which repurposes existing source code and therefore has no compatibility issues; development forks, which are forward incompatible insofar as they build capabilities that add to the code in the existing ledger; and the hard fork, which creates a fork that is either forward incompatible or both forward- and backward incompatible (Fang & Neufeld, 2009; Raymond, 1999). The first two types, pseudo-forks and development forks, involve the distribution of the original code along new channels. The resulting new distributions of the code are compatible with the old version (backward compatible) and benefit from future developments in the parent code (forward compatible) (Nyman, 2015). In the case of pseudo-forks, existing source code is repurposed, and the fork allows for the application of existing capabilities to achieve new organizing objectives. This allows for “variation” in organizing objectives without challenging or requiring changes to existing infrastructure capabilities. Development forks, by adding new capabilities, or new code, to the existing code base while maintaining the backward compatibility of the ledger, “adapt” infrastructure capabilities to serve new organizing objectives. In contrast, hard forks entail a fundamental change to the underlying code such that the new and the old versions of the blockchain are both forwardand backward incompatible. While other forks vary or adapt existing organizing objectives, hard forks support and reproduce radically new objectives that are incompatible with existing capabilities. The radical differences between them may extend to the creation of whole new communities, with new norms and objectives, or separate infrastructures within the same community. As a mode of self-organizing, we therefore refer to hard forking as leading to “speciation,” or “bifurcation” (Andersen & Ingram Bogusz, 2017).

In summary, both the forward and backward compatibility of changes to source code are potentially at issue when forks to a blockchain infrastructure occur. These determine the extent of the adoption required for a fork to actualize changes in organizing, together with both the capabilities of the original source code and the characteristics of any proposed fork. This is because blockchain infrastructures contain an immutable record with temporal reference points. What follows is a description of how we unpacked forking as self-organizing through a longitudinal multimethod research design.

## 3 Research Design

In order to answer the research question of how blockchain infrastructures evolve new forms of selforganizing, we conducted a longitudinal multimethod study (Venkatesh et al., 2013) of generativity in the Bitcoin blockchain infrastructure over a period of six years. In the following section, we first discuss our case selection and its background before detailing our data collection and analysis methods.

Our choice of case was driven by the need to meet four basic requirements: first, the infrastructure had to be predominantly self-organizing in the sense that the organization around it would have emerged in tandem with identifiable technological changes. Second, we had to be able to identify distinct instances of forking. Third, the case setting needed a history spanning over a longer period allowing us to study it both in detail and at scale. Finally, the infrastructure needed good records of both code forking and organizing practices to allow us to analyze the dynamics of code forking and self-organizing.

The Bitcoin blockchain is self-organizing, relatively long lived, and has good digital trace records, and therefore fulfills these requirements. Although the OS software has been reused to create new infrastructures (e.g., Ethereum, Ripple), these next-generation applications are still emergent and, as cases, are still nascent and therefore trickier to study (Yin, 2003).

## 3.1 Data Collection and Analysis

Our aim with this research was to identify instances of forking as self-organizing, as well as to tease out their antecedents and implications, in order to understand self-organizing in blockchain infrastructures more generally. To this end, we needed to establish a sequence of forking events and explicate both the patterns that generated them and their consequences. Our data collection and analysis therefore considered these objectives.

Overall, our methods were grounded in inductive reasoning and rested on the use of two sources of data: digital trace data from the Bitcoin online community and extensive documentation (see Table 3). These data both imbue our computational findings with context (Gaskin et al., 2014) and ensure the veracity of our findings.

Our primary data source was forum data, which we supplemented with documentary data. Although it is commonplace to study code repositories when examining code-level activities (e.g., Dabbish et al., 2012), a weakness of this approach is that it captures neither the context in which code-level activities (forks) occur nor their implications. In contrast, forum data provide good records about when forks occurred (or even potentially could have occurred) and the underlying social context (Gaskin et al., 2014). We therefore chose forum data over code repositories as our primary data source because our main focus was to analyze a specific phenomenon—namely, selforganizing in blockchain infrastructures (in line with Grisot et al. 2014)—rather than to follow an OS development project through a code repository (Howison & Crowston, 2014; Lindberg et al., 2016). Instead, the fine-grained semantic data contained in the forums allowed us to interrogate the motivations and context in which the code-level change occurred, as well as the corresponding changes in organizing objectives and practices. Additional documentary data sources were used to mitigate the risk of losing context when conducting computational analyses (Gaskin et al., 2014) and to distinguish proposed forking events from actual forking events in the Bitcoin community.

We collected the digital trace records by scraping community interactions from the forum bitcointalk.org (Hedman et al., 2013; Howison et al., 2011). In total, we collected 314,551 interactions posted between October 2010 and June 2016. Bitcointalk.org is a primarily English-language based forum dedicated to discussions about Bitcoin. It is among the most prominent forums used by Bitcoin enthusiasts. However, unlike forums like Reddit.com, it is often used specifically by Bitcoin professionals, meaning that interactions on Bitcointalk.org are more closely linked to the development of the Bitcoin code base. Furthermore, it contains sections that are both general and specific in nature, such as threads about the technicalities of blockchain and mining, as well as discussions of a more organizational nature.

We analyzed the forum data (from October 2010 to June 2016) as illustrated in Figure 1 and conducted open coding of the data using the computational natural language processing technique latent dirichlet allocation (LDA) implemented in the open source statistical software R (Blei et al., 2003). This process of applying computational techniques to digital trace data is not new to information systems research (see, e.g., Berente et al., 2018; Hedman et al., 2013; Howison et al., 2011).

LDA is a generative topic model that reveals patterns in a set of documents by extracting unobserved groupings (latent themes) based on semantic similarities between different parts of the data (Sievert & Shirley, 2014). LDA discovers latent themes within a collection of documents by sampling a topic for each word at every iteration of the algorithm and ranking words based on their relevance to each topic, which therefore has a unique distribution over words that can be compared using cosine similarity measures (Chuang et al., 2012).

## 4 Findings: Self-Organizing in the Bitcoin Community

Our longitudinal analysis of bitcointalk.org revealed three distinct patterns through which forking of the underlying infrastructure mediated the pursuit of new emerging organizing objectives and the resulting selforganization through adoption. Although selforganizing originated in shifts in objectives articulated at the level of code, adoption defined the ways and the extent to which changes in technological capabilities supported evolving organizing objectives.

It is interesting that forks occurred in response to shifting organizing objectives, and thus, indirectly, in response to changing environmental conditions. This observation is consistent with the existing understanding of infrastructure as including organizing practices and contexts as well as artifacts (Star & Ruhleder, 1996). Since this was a longitudinal study, we were able to examine not only when and how code forking in blockchain infrastructures supported new organizing objectives, but also when new ideas were incorporated into the existing infrastructure. Figure 2 presents the three distinct forms of code forking—i.e., pseudo-forks, development forks, and hard forks—their environmental conditions, and an overview of the sequence of forking events of importance to the online community.

As Figure 2 illustrates, forking in the Bitcoin blockchain infrastructure sparked significant divergence of both source code implementations and organizing objectives. Moreover, the occurrence of these patterns correlates with shifts in environmental conditions that encouraged heterogeneous groups to rally around different objectives. Of note is the fact that the underlying code in the blockchain only allowed some of these objectives to be actualized directly (here, pseudo-forks and development forks). Instead, a hard fork was needed if the objectives being pursued required a fundamental shift in the capabilities of the underlying infrastructure.

## 4.1 M1: Pseudo-Forks as Organizational Variation

One of the most compelling patterns of self-organizing that emerged from our longitudinal examination was the use of pseudo-forks to create organizational variation: the flexibility of the blockchain source code not only drove substantial changes in infrastructure capabilities, but also permitted variation in the fulfillment of organizing objectives when the environment called for them. These instances of variation are visible in forks $\mathrm { F } _ { 1 } , \mathrm { F } _ { 2 } , \mathrm { F } _ { 3 } ,$ and $\mathrm { F _ { 9 } }$ (see Appendix for a summary of forks) with the first three forks relating largely to the use of Bitcoin for illicit purposes, and the last related to the juxtaposition and relabeling of the existing blockchain as “Bitcoin Core” in response to a hard fork.<sup>6</sup>

Figure 3 illustrates how the relative importance of pseudo-forking events increased over time within the Bitcoin community. The pseudo-forks $\mathrm { F } _ { 1 } , \mathrm { F } _ { 2 } ,$ and $\mathrm { F } _ { 3 }$ show the same overall pattern: a gradual increase in interest over time, with no sharp breaks or sharp increases in interest over time (but see discussion of $\mathrm { F _ { 9 } }$ below).

Figure 3 further illustrates the significance of June 2011: At this point, the community started to grow significantly. The number of unique users in the community increased from 164 to 1,847 and community activity spiked from 2,413 interactions in April to 19,159 interactions by June 2011. Several key events occurred around this time. First, WikiLeaks and other prominent organizations began to accept the Bitcoin currency (aka BTC); second, the “2011 bubble” saw BTC prices soar to over 30 USD, only to burst in the following week when accounts at the prominent Mt.Gox Bitcoin exchange were compromised, resulting in the loss of 8,750,000 USD worth of BTC (Ingram Bogusz & Morisse, 2018). The combination of an increase in users and the publicity surrounding these events introduced heterogeneous and diverse objectives to the Bitcoin community. Moreover, this heterogeneity became visible as new users either built or created the market conditions for new capabilities in the blockchain infrastructure.

During the early stages of the evolution of Bitcoin in 2011 and 2012, increased heterogeneity in the blockchain community meant that users began to see the new blockchain infrastructure as ripe for conducting transactions outside the established financial system. As one user states “I saw many a lot of devious schemes to earn bitcoin. Just like HYIP, PONZI, Gambling, Bet etc. I am very afraid of RIBA. therefore, for guidance and assistance will be appreciated. Thanks” (March 03, 2012, 12:38:21 a.m.)<sup>7</sup>.

![](/api/attachments/844A6Z28/fulltext/images/970ed665a39108778bd08623f09191be7594366b8f24b91ea7159ac17cb6bf15.jpg)  
Figure 3. Discussions of Activity Relating to Pseudo-Forks

However, other users were quick to point out how some pseudo-forks, notably the ones identified above, broke with norms within the community and did not represent the objectives of the community as a whole. Nor should these forks be considered synonymous with the technology itself, even if it enables them: “Of course, Bitcoin is not the problem. People who misuse the bitcoin and abuse is are the ones who are the problem. If terrorist are using bitcoin, not really nice of them to shed Bitcoin in a bad light” (November 24, 2015, 02:36:19 p.m). While “unpopular” variations dominated, the contextualizing of the existing code in response to a split in the community (which also preceded a hard fork) also led to a specific kind of pseudo-fork, namely the defensive relabeling of existing code.

## 4.1.1 Defensive Pseudo-Fork

When changing social objectives were seen as “legitimate” by the community, and thus widely adopted, the result was not a hard fork but a pseudo-fork: the same set of code was infused with new social meaning (here $\mathrm { F _ { 9 } , }$ in May 2015). Our data showed one instance of this occurring in response to a hard fork (F<sub>10</sub>). It followed similar patterns to a hard fork, namely a sudden rise in interest (see Figure 3), but did not require a change at the level of code to be realized. Instead, defenders of the existing infrastructure labeled the existing infrastructure “Core,” highlighting how its capabilities, and degree of distribution in particular, emerged from a set of “core” organizing goals, notably concerning the democratic control of the infrastructure. Much like the variations in practice represented by the use of the infrastructure for illicit transactions, this kind of fork was an instance of practice-level variation to how the infrastructure was used:

The code which powers the Bitcoin network can be found here: https://github. com/bitcoin/ bitcoin. This code has evolved as long as Bitcoin has been around. But the debate over the block size limit and how to manage it has caused some of the best-known developers to set up another client for bitcoin, here: https://github.com/ bitcoinxt/bitcoinxt.... The former is now referred to as “Bitcoin Core,” and the latter “Bitcoin XT.” ... From the XT github README: “Bitcoin XT is more experimental than Bitcoin Core, and has a strong emphasis on supporting the needs of app developers and merchants. By running it you not only provide additional services to the network but help build confidence in the implementations, contributing towards consensus for inclusion in a future version of Bitcoin Core. (May 31, 2015, 05:28:36 p.m.)

Pseudo-forks, while seemingly something out of the ordinary, did not entail any underlying code change. They simply entailed variation in code use patterns that mediated the pursuit of new objectives, notably ones opposed by other members of the community. Indeed, this opposition, and the freedom to pseudo-fork despite the opposition, was commonplace and was enabled by the nature of the blockchain infrastructure itself. The qualities of distribution and semi-anonymity—seen as elements of building a new technical system based on trust in a system rather than individuals or institutions— paradoxically gave users the ability to repurpose the system for transactions that many in the community considered to be unethical, including involving drugs on the Darknet (F<sub>1</sub>) and Bitcoin-based scams (F<sub>3</sub>).

Pseudo-forks thus occurred when the existing code facilitated variation in organizing objectives. These variations on organizing objectives emerged from divergent goals and ideologies and manifested as forks on the infrastructure’s capabilities. Interpretation, narrative, and other events led to the emergence of these variations, while the preexisting code limited (and enabled) the possible interpretations. Moreover, pseudoforks attracted additional community members and influenced dynamics among existing members, thus affecting how the community interacted.

Having described how pseudo-forks enable and constrain self-organizing by enabling changes in organizing objectives to existing source code, we now look closer at how self-organizing manifests when the changes to infrastructure capabilities are more substantial.

## 4.2 M2: Development Forks as Organizational Adaptation

Variations in organizing objectives were not the only forms of self-organizing mediated by forking. Adaptation, or development that added to infrastructure capabilities through modular development of the underlying source code, was also an important and influential mediator of self-organizing. These development forks enabled new capabilities by building upon the existing source code, which led to adaptation in the community. Adaptation thus enabled additional organizing objectives through the creation of new infrastructure capabilities that built on the existing source code without introducing incompatible elements.

As shown in Figure 4, the events of June 2011 attracted not only those with criminal or malicious intent, but also entrepreneurs who anticipated the potential for Bitcoin to be extended to deliver a range of financial and other services—most notably, building credible merchant and retail solutions (F<sub>5</sub>) and building wallets for use by Bitcoin users and investors (F<sub>7</sub>).

![](/api/attachments/844A6Z28/fulltext/images/b63d4f298a1eb1d84acc75b9db2990f1af1097c1422b4c79351752e25ca8ddb7.jpg)  
Figure 4. Discussions of Activity Relating to Development Forks

Both extending credit (F<sub>4</sub>) and developing specific adaptations known as sidechains (F<sub>6</sub>) were dramatic changes to the blockchain widely discussed in the community in early 2014. However, while extending credit was incompatible with the infrastructure’s capabilities and thus could not be realized (without a hard fork), sidechains involved a mere addition to the source code, and, therefore, the specific organizing objective of transferring assets other than BTC through the blockchain could be realized.

As these developments built upon the existing source code, they enabled the pursuit of new objectives, i.e., enabling retail and credit services through creating start-ups that built complementary additions to the infrastructure. Among the ventures that started at this time are a number of Bitcoin exchanges<sup>8</sup> that are still successful in 2019, namely Bitstamp and Kraken, and the successful wallet Blockchain.info.<sup>9</sup> However, new adaptations were only accepted by the community under certain conditions, as one member of the community expressed:

Please understand that I have great respect for the work you’ve done. Your service is very well constructed and well-loved for good reason. While some of the things I’ve brought up might be improved with some tweaks here and there, much of it is simply the structural consequences of centralized services, trusted parties, web clients, etc.... I don’t think our community should take any actions which promote centralization or consolidation due to systemic risk if nothing else.... I don’t believe it should promote your wallet service either. (December 03, 2012, 03:02:53 p.m.)

But entrepreneurship did not just extend to services that catered to the existing community. Instead, some of the adaptations mediated by development forks allowed the infrastructure to interface with other infrastructures, enabling interorganizational linkages. One example is that of a point-of-sale (PoS) adaptation (F<sub>5</sub>), based on both the underlying infrastructure and on other adaptations to extend the usefulness of the infrastructure and include new users:

We have released an update of our Pos [point-of-sale] software witch includes a module to connect and process payments trough Bitcoin-Qt wallet. So with this update, more than 3,000 local busineses over the world who now are using Sysme Pos as point of sale software can have the tool to accept Bitcoins We hope that this will encourage them to accept Bitcoin as payment so this this project can make a step further. (June 05, 2013, 09:53:11 p.m.)

Such an addition was not only useful on the level of infrastructure capabilities, it also served to include more stakeholders in supporting and engaging with Bitcoin’s organizing objectives. Not only did the creation, through development forks, of entirely new adaptations on the level of code mediate the pursuit of new organizing objectives, such development forks also expanded the number of stakeholders interested in the blockchain. Indeed, development forks like the creation of wallets and merchant services created new interdependencies by integrating Bitcoin more effectively into the established financial system through creating services for merchants and points at which bitcoins could be exchanged for fiat currencies like USD, and also by linking Bitcoin and the blockchain to other related technologies through the creation of offline bitcoin wallets reliant on new pieces of hardware (e.g., Hurlburt and Bojanova 2014).

Adaptations that built on the infrastructure include those that indirectly allow the infrastructure to perform additional functions. While the blockchain’s original architecture was intended as a proof of concept for the transfer of a currency, one adaptation allowed for the transfer and maintenance of a centralized database holding assets other than that currency. In an adaptation known as a sidechain (F<sub>6</sub>), developers built on the blockchain to allow for an asset-agnostic transfer that interfaced with the blockchain:

The paper proposes two-way pegged sidechains as an extension mechanism for Bitcoin. The idea is that coins would be able to move from the Blockchain, to a sidechain, and then back again in a trustless way. This would allow sidechains to implement properties that are not feasible to implement on Bitcoin itself, while preserving the total number of Bitcoins. (September 10, 2015, 03:44:19 p.m.)

Though it represented a drastic development, this fork was still consistent with the underlying source code. This meant that, like in forks F<sub>5</sub> and F<sub>7</sub>, the new objectives facilitated by the adaptation also relied on elements of the infrastructure’s coded-in organizing practices in order to function. Thus, adaptation was enabled (and constrained) by source code through development forks. By attracting new users to the community and by making the infrastructure itself better able to support more things, these development forks added to existing organizing by changing its characteristics and capabilities on the code level.

## 4.2.1 Creative Adaptations

However, while some adaptations could be considered “plain sailing,” others were incompatible with the underlying infrastructure, and their pursuits were thus unable to be supported. One example of this concerned the notion of extending credit (F<sub>4</sub>):

I’m not being misleading, the services offered by your current credit union could be extended to Bitcoins. This is a real institution with full financial services we're talking about so everything is on the table for the steering committee to discuss. Mortgages, Credit Cards, LOC, metals, multi currency handling (fiat and BTC), insurance etc. (September 11, 2012, 04:28:10 p.m.)

Because the creation of new BTC was limited by the blockchain’s consensus mechanism, it was not possible to create more BTC for the sole purpose of supporting the leverage implicit in extending credit (Leland and Toft 1996). However, a creative creditlike adaptation was instead developed: namely, the granting of peer-to-peer loans:

I am familiar with BTCPop, BTCJam, and Bitlendingclub (BLC). I love the concept of peer to peer lending as a way of disrupting traditional banking, and I love bitcoin. The two ideas work beautifully together— although unfortunately I don’t think any company out there is doing it right yet. For example, loans are supposed to be tied to an interest rate, so that the sooner you repay the less interest you pay. This occurs with all traditional commercial debt, from mortgages to car loans to credit cards. All the bitcoin platforms, on the other hand, only calculate the debt over the term of the loan as a lump sum, so if you pay late or early it doesn’t matter—you pay the same interest. So, for just one example, it makes no sense to refinance any debt to a lower interest rate. I’m hoping they do away with that soon or a competitor pops up which does. Peer to peer lending thrives on markets and it needs those options to be there. (May 09, 2015, 04:31:21 p.m.)

In early 2013, the development of peer-to-peer loans was seen as both a merit of blockchain and a possible stumbling block to greater integration of the mainstream and Bitcoin financial systems. Altering the system so that it could have issued credit would have required a substantial change to the blockchain infrastructure. Unlike the Bitcoin XT change (F<sub>10</sub>) discussed below, the environmental pressure exerted in favor of extending credit was not (at the time) sufficient to drive the hard fork it would have taken to make it a reality. As such, it was a hypothetical hard fork that never reached the adoption threshold required to support a separate infrastructure with new capabilities. However, the environmental conditions that supported the idea of credit instead gave rise to another creative adaptation—the creation of unleveraged peer-to-peer loans.

## 4.3 M3: Hard Forks as Bifurcation and Speciation

The most dramatic pattern of self-organizing we observed was driven by a hard fork to the underlying code that created a new infrastructure that was both forward- and backward incompatible with the existing infrastructure.

![](/api/attachments/844A6Z28/fulltext/images/c74d0f5b5243aae2a13e19ca1b21232d451881fd9ef94934932fabf9c6506555.jpg)  
Figure 5. Discussions of Activity Relating to Hard Forks

If the community had adopted this “forked” version of the code en masse, it would have become the dominant infrastructure. In fact, one might even say that it would have become the infrastructure, while the versions of the code would have either been discarded or held by a less influential minority as a minority infrastructure. We refer to this pattern as “bifurcation,” because it resulted in two (or several) incompatible and competing infrastructures. The fact that a hard fork was necessary to pursue the creation of a new set of capabilities implied that, despite the substantial codelevel similarities they shared, at the level of code, they were fundamentally incompatible. This is also illustrated by the sudden increases in discussions around hard forks, shown in Figure 5.

A particularly illustrative example is that of the Bitcoin XT (“XT,” F<sub>10</sub>): it not only resulted in a new infrastructure, but also produced the defensive pseudofork Bitcoin Core (F<sub>9</sub>) described earlier. In late 2015, the infrastructure began to struggle to handle the number of Bitcoin transactions being initiated; there were delays and a backlog of transactions grew. Moreover, as demand was consistently increasing, the infrastructure needed better handling capacity and the infrastructure did not have a built-in way to scale. In other words, the infrastructure began to fail (Hearn, 2015) and prominent developers of the Bitcoin source code warned that the sustainability of the blockchain infrastructure was in jeopardy. An infrastructural shift, known as Bitcoin XT, was proposed as a solution. This proposed solution entailed increasing the size of each block in the blockchain from 1mb to 4mb, which would require a fundamental change in the underlying source code:

As Bitcoin has grown, so have the blocks Reasonable traffic projections indicate that as Bitcoin spreads via word of mouth, we will reach the limit of the current system [with a 1mb block size] sometime next year, or by 2017 at the absolute latest. And another bubble or press cycle could push us over the limit before even that. The result might not be pretty. So it is now time to raise the [block size] limit, or remove it entirely. (Mike Hearn, Aug 15, 2015)

Bitcoin Classic $( \mathrm { F } _ { 1 1 } ) ,$ like XT before it, also proposed increasing the size of each block in the blockchain. Both aimed to facilitate processing a larger number of transactions more quickly. However, the blockchain protocol needed to change fundamentally to allow for this. Moreover, increasing the block size would have reduced the number of miners able to run the software (due to processing power issues), as it increased the blockchain’s transaction handling capacity. The rules governing when changes to the operating protocol of the blockchain could occur were enshrined in the source code. Minor changes that created compatible versions of the software were easily dealt with, while major changes, like this hard fork, required active adoption beyond a certain threshold. Participants in the network, whether miners or entrepreneurs running platforms on the blockchain infrastructure, had to choose which version to run. Ultimately, the version that garnered the most support became the “real” blockchain.

Opponents of this change labeled the original version of the infrastructure Bitcoin Core and argued that, among other things, XT was untried and may not scale well (XT proponents disagreed). They also argued that the change would make the project more centralized, putting more power in the hands of fewer miners who could thus more easily make substantial changes in the future. OS communities often identify strongly with the underlying project (Ingram Bogusz & Morisse, 2018; Ren et al., 2012), so controversial attempts to change it are often taken personally:

Bitcoin’s own former lead dev, Gavin, and his henchman Hearn are in the process of sabotaging Bitcoin from the inside. You will hear about it when their XT Trojan horse deploys its payload, and attempts to force us all to join their altcoin at Bitcoin’s expense. (July 9, 2015, 03:42:23 p.m.)

This back-and-forth illustrates the effect of the political environment on the perception and framing of code changes, which themselves affected the community’s willingness to adopt new changes. In essence, the community agreed with the need to do something but argued that the technical shift to XT would change the social practices the infrastructure used to operate by centralizing control of the source code.

The blockchain source code ultimately bifurcated into two forward- and backward-incompatible versions of the blockchain within the same community. Since miners did not adopt the XT version of the code in sufficient numbers, it never became the dominant version of the infrastructure. Moreover, swaths of the Bitcoin community also boycotted entrepreneurs and users who switched over to XT. Unlike with a development fork, in which the new version of the infrastructure is compatible with those running the old version of the software, a hard fork means that those running pre-fork versions of the infrastructure cannot use the new version.

Another hard fork, labeled Ethereum (F<sub>8</sub>), revolved around alternative uses of blockchain-like infrastructures that did not involve Bitcoin, driven at least partly by the limitations that blockchain was seen to have at the time:

They can make an altcoin and voila, they’ll have a blockchain. But there’s no stronger blockchain than Bitcoin’s. And if that's advertised enough, organizations will come for the blockchain and will have to use bitcoin for that. For the moment, the bitcoin blockchain is the most “used” and it is the most secure but like I said “for the moment.” However it is normal that in the future someone will build something better than the actual bitcoin (this is obvious... technology). (July 15, 2015, 07:56:16 p.m.)

Users pointed both to the fact that Bitcoin transactions were slow (as we saw in the motivation for Bitcoin XT), and to the fact that the blockchain in its thencurrent incarnation did not easily allow for the storage of other kinds of information, assets, or the execution of smart contracts. Although the originating objective behind Ethereum was to facilitate the use of blockchain technology for other kinds of applications—for instance, smart contracts—the extent of necessary code-level changes and the threshold for adoption were sufficiently high that it resulted in a whole new species of blockchain with its own distinct community. The resulting Ethereum blockchain infrastructure, with its separate community and incompatible code base, was both operationally and ideologically distinct from the parent community.

Having discussed how the three different types of forks outlined in the literature lead to four distinct patterns of self-organizing, we now discuss how these findings inform emerging theory on blockchain infrastructures, specifically by relating them to generativity and selforganizing.

## 5 Discussion and Contributions

This paper makes the following main contributions to our understanding of forking as a pattern of selforganizing in blockchain infrastructures. First, it provides a vocabulary for forking as self-organizing in blockchain infrastructures that extends the emerging body of research on the role of blockchain in organizing (Avital et al., 2016; Beck et al., 2017, 2018; Catalini & Gans, 2016). More specifically, it complements the literature on infrastructure evolution (Andersen & Ingram Bogusz, 2017; Grisot et al., 2014; Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010; Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Tilson et al., 2010) by showing how forking in blockchain infrastructures leads to self-organizing. Second, it explains how the environmental conditions surrounding forking attempts—specifically community adoption, heterogeneity of organizing objectives, and code-level changes—enable the forking that mediates self-organizing in blockchain infrastructures. Finally, we discuss the implications of self-organizing for blockchain design, forking itself, and technology-organization alignment, known as “mirroring.”

Our findings describe the emergent consequences of the interplay between blockchain infrastructures and heterogeneous organizing objectives in the case the Bitcoin blockchain. In particular, we focus on forking, thus far seen as a governance phenomenon within the blockchain community (Beck et al., 2018) and OS communities more generally (Nyman & Lindman, 2013), as an important pattern that enables selforganizing facilitated by the medium of computer code. Understanding the role of forking is not just important as organizations and societies become more digitalized, it is also important in light of how blockchain infrastructures enable increasingly autonomous organizing, for instance through distributed autonomous organizations (DAOs, Beck et al., 2016, 2018). This manner of organizing is not only embedded in a distributed infrastructure, but challenges what we already know about how distributed and autonomous organizing occurs across technological, geographical, industry, and organizational boundaries.

While the ramifications of distributed self-organizing through blockchain infrastructures are potentially

radical and far-reaching (Wörner et al., 2016), this paper focuses on the more narrow question of how selforganizing has occurred in the past, specifically through a longitudinal study of the Bitcoin blockchain in its formative years, from October 2010 to June 2016. We now discuss the implications of these findings before concluding and proposing directions for future research.

## 5.1 Patterns of Forking as Self-Organizing

In the following, we discuss how the three forking mechanisms discussed above—pseudo-forks, development forks, and hard forks—led to different patterns of self-organizing by enabling or constraining the pursuit-organizing objectives. Inspired by research framing Bitcoin as an ecosystem (Andersen & Ingram, Bogusz 2017; Gandal et al., 2018; Wörner et al., 2016) and consistent with extant literature on digital infrastructure evolution (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010; Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013), we have labeled these patterns of self-organizing “variation,” “adaptation,” “bifurcation,” and “speciation” as outlined in Table 4 and Figure 6. We now discuss how these patterns of selforganizing emerge through forking, as well as how they link to the pursuit of organizing objectives and related adoption practices.

Variation refers to the process whereby existing infrastructure capabilities are employed in the pursuit of new organizing objectives. Variation in organizing objectives is thus facilitated by repurposing and reinterpreting existing technological capabilities through pseudo-forking. These variations connect unrelated organizing objectives to the blockchain infrastructure, as in the case of hacking (F<sub>2</sub>), scams (F<sub>3</sub>), and the relabeling of the Bitcoin blockchain as Bitcoin Core (F<sub>9</sub>). Variations on the purpose of existing capabilities do not require changes to the source code. Instead, the source code enables unexpected social practices in the form of new organizing objectives.

Adaptation refers to the process through which supplementary infrastructure capabilities are generated on top of the infrastructure’s existing capabilities, notably by adding code modules or creating supplementary but compatible capabilities. This process results in the infrastructure being used for new things in new domains, eventually resulting in an expansion of the blockchain user base, which leads to compatible supplementary organizing objectives operating within the existing infrastructure. Examples of this include the introduction of new entrepreneurial products like merchant (F<sub>5</sub>) or wallet (F<sub>7</sub>) applications. The added code allows not only for the pursuit of supplementary objectives, but also enables the creation of supplementary organizing and interactions reliant on the underlying blockchain infrastructure.

Bifurcation refers to the process through which diverging organizing objectives are generated in light of radical breaks with existing organizing objectives that are inconsistent with existing infrastructure capabilities. The organizing objectives that are pursued in this way require fundamental (and incompatible) changes to the capabilities of the underlying blockchain infrastructure. For bifurcation to occur, code changes must supplant existing infrastructure capabilities, generating a new infrastructure that is technically incompatible with the preexisting source code.

Moreover, the social conditions under which hard forks occur facilitate the creation of subcommunities with new organizing objectives within the larger community, rather than the creation of a whole new community. Two examples of such hard forks, one realized and one unrealized, are the BitcoinXT fork (F<sub>10</sub>) and the attempt to extend credit within the Bitcoin blockchain infrastructure (F<sub>4</sub>). Both of these hard fork initiatives divided the existing community because they introduced centralization to the blockchain infrastructure, a fundamental change to the underlying source code: Bitcoin XT did this by decreasing the number of miners who could run the blockchain protocol, while the initiative to extend credit would have required a third party capable of controlling a monetary supply. Bifurcating these blockchain infrastructures, thus, not only changes the capabilities of the infrastructure, but also who controls the infrastructure, constituting a departure from the distributed, nonhierarchical organization of the parent (“Core”; F<sub>9</sub>) infrastructure.

Speciation refers to cases of bifurcation in which changes in infrastructure capabilities and organizing objectives are so divisive that they spin off into completely separate blockchain infrastructures with their own sets of capabilities, objectives and, crucially, communities. An example of speciation in the Bitcoin infrastructure can be seen in the emergence of Ethereum (F<sub>8</sub>), which, despite being a fork of the Bitcoin blockchain, operates independently.

The impact of code forking, both as reflected in additions to the blockchain infrastructure and in how they are discussed, are relatively clear and distinct. Both variation and adaptation, generated by pseudoforks and development forks, respectively, result from compatible variations on or additions to existing code. In contrast, both bifurcation and speciation, generated by hard forks, result from incompatible additions to the existing source code. ## 5.2 Heterogeneous Objectives and Patterns of Adoption

Research elsewhere, both within OS communities more generally (Choi et al., 2015) and within the Bitcoin community specifically (Ingram Bogusz & Morisse, 2018), has shown that online communities are seldom comprised of groups of individuals with homogenous shared objectives and ideologies. Instead, these communities have multiple, heterogeneous, and potentially conflicting objectives. Indeed, as our analysis above shows, the larger the community, the more potential variation, and the more potential for conflict. While recent research on blockchain generativity has shown how “disagreements can be resolved in a decentralized manner if users initiate forks by copying existing code and developing it further according to their goals” (Beck et al., 2018, p. 27), the process through which this occurs, and the role of the code as infrastructure, have yet to be studied.

Our analysis of the Bitcoin blockchain shows that in the face of heterogeneity, it is the preexisting content of the code—what we have called infrastructure capabilities—that determines whether originating objectives give rise to the pursuit of compatible and complementary objectives (which mitigate dispute), or to diverging ones. Existing code that is either flexible or does not prevent a proposed objective, results in emerging objectives that are limited in scope, compatible with the existing infrastructure, and that may require specific adoption in order to have any effect at all (see Table 5). This is true whether the objectives pursued are accepted as legitimate by the community or not. Indeed, the resulting adaptation and variation cannot be severed from the originating infrastructure, and thus the code-level additions (adaptation), and new social employment of existing code (variation) cannot be prevented or sanctioned by the community. Opponents are, of course, free to avoid adoption, but this is a rather limited form of sanction (Feller et al., 2008).

However, the capabilities of the code may also generate divergent and heterogeneous paths within the same infrastructure (e.g., Bitcoin XT) or create distinct and incompatible blockchain infrastructures (e.g., Ethereum). The key differentiator for the realization of organizing objectives is the scope of adoption; adoption of a particular fork by a specific subgroup within the community or general adoption across the entire community influences the actual pattern of selforganizing. ## 5.2.1 Specific Adoption

The patterns of adoption that generate these changes— specifically, the extent of code adoption—also affect how self-organizing progresses and what its outcomes are. In the case of variation and adaptation, adoption is motivated to different patterns of organizing objective shifts—namely, heterogeneous objectives in the context of existing code (variation), and the creation of supplementary code in communities with relatively homogenous organizing objectives (adaptation). Moreover, specific adoption of a variation or adaptation is required, not general adoption of a new (blockchain) infrastructure, because there are no internal limitations in the infrastructure that prevent the realization of new capabilities and associated objectives. What transforms code-level capabilities into the realization of new objectives is the adoption of the specific reproduction or repurposing of code that occurs, not the adoption of a larger code base.

## 5.2.2 General Adoption

In contrast, the patterns of code replacement, diverging organizing objectives, and ultimate adoption in the face of hard forks differ substantially. Although all hard forks are prompted by environmental changes that motivate new objectives that are incompatible with the existing infrastructure, patterns associated with organizing objectives and adoption influence the degree of self-organizing and divergence that results.

When there is commitment to the existing capabilities of the infrastructure within the community, but a desire to create additional incompatible capabilities, such as faster processing power (as in Bitcoin XT), we see agreement (homogeneity) between those who would fork and those who remain behind. As such, the forward- and backward-incompatible code changes are necessary for the fork to occur within the fold of the existing community. For the emerging new fork to become the primary infrastructure, the bifurcation generated, in turn, requires a substantial general adoption within that community. In the absence of this general adoption, a hard fork could become an alternative infrastructure (with adoption by a minority), or a failed hard fork (without significant adoption).

In contrast, when the existing objectives of a community and a new set of objectives share little common ground—such as when the new objectives revolve around smart contracts more generally, not just currency transfers (as in Ethereum)—what results is the creation of new capabilities through both a separate species of infrastructure and a separate community. As with bifurcation, the resulting new infrastructure could eclipse its parent with sufficiently large adoption. However, given the heterogeneous objectives involved, what would be generated is either a whole new species of infrastructure with substantial adoption or a failed hard fork.

In essence, existing code supports and reinforces the status quo, and changes to it require significant resources and mobilization on the part of would-be forkers. Consequently, self-organizing through hard forks could pan out in one of four ways: (1) the hard fork could be absorbed by the existing infrastructure, which would require general adoption by a majority of users; (2) the hard fork could generate a whole new community; (3) the hard fork could generate a minority infrastructure; and (4) the hard fork could fail due to lack of general adoption. These four possible outcomes stem from individual and collective responses to an otherwise technical change: both the nature of the organizing objectives at play, and the threshold of adoption are relevant to what the generative consequences of a code forking are.

## 5.3 Implications

Overall, this paper conceptualizes how forking in blockchain infrastructures generates self-organizing. More specifically, it outlines the means by which forking facilitates the pursuit of organizing objectives, clarifies the role of the underlying infrastructure, and delineates the scale of code changes, individual objectives, and collective adoption, whether specific or general. By identifying four patterns of self-organizing and their antecedents, this paper outlines a framework for assessing the potential consequences of specific forking events. Moreover, our conceptualization of the antecedents of these patterns, homogenous and heterogeneous objectives, and thresholds of adoption needed for certain outcomes offer guidance to would-be blockchain infrastructure designers in terms of what to consider when developing a governance model. Unpacking these different mechanisms provides insight into how a blockchain infrastructure could be governed to facilitate—or prevent—the different outcomes described in Table 5.

## 5.3.1 Generativity and Blockchain Design

When it comes to blockchain design, blockchain infrastructures, unlike platforms and applications, do not have strict boundaries and cannot be defined through a specific set of functions or modules (Tilson et al., 2010). Instead, their boundaries are defined through use practices, evident in the pursuit of organizing objectives and adoption in the generation of new patterns of self-organizing as well as the maintenance of old ones. Our findings reinforce the idea that the existence and design of an infrastructure relies on continued use, maintenance and selforganizing by a distributed group of users (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013). Indeed, we build on these ideas to show how use and maintenance support the emergence of different patterns of self-organizing.

In particular, lowering the transaction costs of collaboration (Catalini & Gans, 2016) facilitates the emergence of self-organizing through a distributed process in which multiple users contribute to an underlying infrastructure (Yoo et al., 2012). This builds on findings related to committal structures in OS communities, where it was found that decentralization allowed for better performance in situations with high levels of uncertainty (AlMarzouq et al., 2015). Here, there were high levels of uncertainty, particularly in the case of hard forks: changes were often hotly contested at the level of adoption and use. Indeed, the Bitcoin community, like other OS communities, discouraged rogue changes through political ideology and community backlash (Dahlander & Magnusson, 2005; Ingram Bogusz & Morisse, 2018; Kirsch, 1997). The open nature of the code, however, meant that on a technical level, forking could not be prevented and that the resulting patterns self-organizing could also not be entirely prevented. Nevertheless, consensus mechanisms affect the degree to which a blockchain is open to generativity. It does this by determining the (quantitative or qualitative) conditions under which a fork generates variation and adaptation, or bifurcation and speciation. Therefore, for better or worse, fine tuning a consensus mechanism through design provides an avenue for enabling or constraining generativity (Yoo et al., 2012).

These findings thus have implications when it comes to designing for generativity in blockchain infrastructures. Extant research has suggested that the design of a digital infrastructure requires accounting for dynamic complexity (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010), but the very nature of such complexity makes anticipating possible generativity tricky. However, the conceptualization of the specific routes that generativity could take gives designers concrete areas for consideration when designing blockchain infrastructures. Some specific questions include the following. First, when it comes to what we call speciation and bifurcation, what are the code-level thresholds involved that will lead to changes to the fabric of the infrastructure through a hard fork, and do such changes require general adoption of a whole infrastructure? Second, when it comes to complementary areas of generativity—i.e., adaptation and variation—are there limits to where and how additions or variations can be added to existing objectives, or to what the specific patterns of adoption concerning the code for the new objectives are? These considerations, moreover, evolve as change is generated; thus, the changes to source code and code use outlined in Figure 6 are relational in nature and the maintenance of the community infrastructure necessitates constant interaction.

## 5.3.2 Forking

Although the aim of this paper is not to contribute to the forking literature but to analyze the role of forking in the context of blockchain, we have nevertheless built on literatures on forking in order to better understand blockchain as a new kind of digital infrastructure. While extant research has referred to forking as a governance phenomenon in its own right (e.g., Meeker, 2008; Nyman, 2015), we show how forking can be part of a larger process of selforganizing, given certain antecedents and in certain contexts. Moreover, a single kind of hard fork in the presence of different objectives and code capabilities can give rise to completely different self-organizing processes. In this case, a hard fork can lead either to bifurcation, when a new subcommunity with new objectives emerges within the existing community, or to speciation, which is the creation of a whole new blockchain infrastructure and community that does not compete directly with the parent infrastructure because it has distinct technological capabilities and organizing objectives. These contributions show not only that the code itself is pivotal in determining—at least in the case of blockchain infrastructures—what organizing processes result, but also that there is a complex dynamic between code capabilities and organizing objectives that could lead to outcomes previously not seen or theorized within the forking literatures.

## 5.3.3 Blockchain and Generative Mirroring

Ultimately, extant research sees changes in organizing as generating infrastructural evolution, rather than vice versa (e.g., Ciborra, 2000; Eaton et al., 2015). By showing how self-organizing is mediated by code forking in blockchains, we contribute a more nuanced understanding of the role of the digital in its own self-organizing: while intended objectives and adoption of the code are a vital part of organizing in this context, possibilities are also defined according to the code-level composition of an infrastructure, as seen in this specific case of the blockchain infrastructure.

This paper further begins to build a conceptualization of self-organization in digital infrastructures, including what we call “generative mirroring.” Extant studies of mirroring have shown how organizations can mirror the characteristics of technological artifacts on which they rely, in order to generate performance gains. However, this ex ante design reflects neither the dynamism between digital infrastructures and distributed users and developers nor how the relationship between the technology and organizing emerges through interaction (both social and code-based) and adoption. Instead, the distributed nature of control in blockchain infrastructures (i.e., through consensus mechanisms) leads not only to generativity, but also to generative mirroring, because the infrastructure and its community evolve in tandem. In this paper, we identify forking as one mechanism of generative mirroring, but there may well be others, especially as automation continues to proliferate. This finding adds to the emerging body of literature in generativity in digital infrastructures (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010; Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Koutsikouri et al., 2018; Tilson et al., 2010; Um et al., 2015).

The findings presented in this paper have several implications for understanding blockchain organizing (Beck et al., 2018; Böhme et al., 2015; Iansiti & Lakhani, 2017), especially in terms of public and permissionless blockchain infrastructures like Bitcoin that lack a priori governing structures. Existing research has suggested that organizing in the context of blockchain infrastructures is a topic in need of further attention (Lindman et al., 2017), and our findings contribute to this area. ## 6 Conclusion and Directions for Future Research

Digital infrastructures are gaining significance within both existing and emerging organizations (von Briel et al., 2018; Nambisan et al., 2018; Tilson et al., 2010) and with the rise of blockchain technology, autonomous organizing is less the stuff of science fiction, and increasingly a reality. Our examination of self-organizing in the Bitcoin infrastructure contributes to conceptualizing how blockchain infrastructures self-organize as a radical technological innovation that shapes, and is shaped by, organizing objectives. Understanding the patterns underlying selforganizing informs the growing body of knowledge on blockchain as an organizing phenomenon, not just a technological one (Beck et al., 2018; Catalini & Gans, 2016; Iansiti & Lakhani, 2017). This paper provides an examination of how self-organizing occurs in blockchain infrastructures, especially in terms of (1) the role of the underlying infrastructure, (2) the scale of the code changes, (3) individual objectives, and (4) collective adoption, whether specific or general. By identifying these four patterns of self-organizing and their antecedents, this paper outlines a framework for assessing the potential consequences of specific forking events. It builds an understanding of how the specific characteristics of blockchain technology lead to new patterns of self-organizing and illuminate the role of forking in these patterns.

By building an understanding of forking as a mode of self-organizing in blockchain infrastructures, this paper contributes to extant research on blockchain infrastructures in the following ways: First, it identifies and conceptualizes a new mode of organizing mediated by blockchain infrastructures in which forking is a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for the realization of new organizing objectives and practices. Second, it proposes, substantiates, and empirically identifies the concept of forking to show how and when code development practices combine into a pattern of self-organizing. In so doing, this paper provides a vocabulary for describing the different patterns of self-organization in blockchain infrastructures: namely, variation, adaptation, bifurcation, and speciation.

Blockchain infrastructures are, in many ways, the harbingers of infrastructures to come; distributed control, fluid technological capabilities, and openness to manipulation by a large and heterogeneous community are increasingly commonplace (Beck et al., 2018; Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010). This is partly due to technological advances, and also because community members (whether users or miners) themselves hold the skills and tools to support, or destroy, digital infrastructures through adoption and/or code forking (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Rullani & Haefliger, 2013). Indeed, since code-level changes and associated objectives are often hotly contested, both this paper and previous research in OS communities demonstrate that forking is sometimes unavoidable. Therefore, understanding the role of forking in the design and organization of digital infrastructures like blockchains is essential as such infrastructures become more common and, indeed, vital to modern societies. We therefore encourage future examination of the role of code—and code forking, in particular—in the contexts of organizational change and self-organizing. We further encourage research on patterns of selforganizing beyond forking, whether in blockchain infrastructures or in next generation distributed digital infrastructures. Finally, we encourage empirical studies that improve our understanding of generative mirroring.

## About the Authors

Jonas Valbjørn Andersen is an assistant professor at the IT University of Copenhagen and a researcher at the European Blockchain Center. He has a background in strategy and IT and holds a PhD in information systems and management from the Warwick Business School, UK. His research focuses on digitally distributed systems and processes related to organizational and algorithmic decision-making. He primarily employs digital trace data analysis as well as system modeling and simulation based on systems thinking in general and, more specifically, on a complex adaptive systems approach. His work aims to contribute with insights on strategy, management, and organizing related to distributed information systems such as blockchain, Internet of Things (IoT), algorithmic decision-making and digitally distributed organizations.

Claire Ingram Bogusz is a postdoctoral researcher at the Stockholm School of Economics’ House of Innovation, with a background in law, philosophy, and economics. Her research focuses on how code-based technologies (e.g., code, digital platforms, machine learning, and digital automation) change how we work, how organizing occurs, and what these things mean for us as individuals and as members of increasingly polarized and unequal societies. Her particular areas of competence are in understanding digital markets, digital entrepreneurship, and financial technologies. Among other things, she has taught digital strategy, fintech entrepreneurship, data-driven decision-making, cocreation, and digital ethics. Beyond her ongoing academic research, she has authored popular reports on crowdfunding (2013), fintech in Sweden (2015, 2016), the sharing economy (2015), blockchain standards (2017), digital trace data (2017, 2018), and digital identity.
