## a b s t r a c t

We propose an analytical model that evaluates the information completeness of object tracking systems based on their size, breadth and reading depth. We showcase that the larger size of a system’s configuration does not ensure better completeness; instead, the effect of reading depth is greater. The completeness of tracking information can characterize the visibility of the inspected objects the tracking systems offer. The model may be used for both ex-ante and ex-post evaluations of the visibility of tracking systems versus their deployment cost, considering that the emergence of real-time tracking and sensing technologies will boost the adoption of such systems.

## 1. Introduction

Nowadays, people, products or obiects continuously move and change locations. termed moving obiects (Civilis et al.. 2005), in a globalised environment. At the same time, recent advances in information and communication technologies, such as RFID (Radio Frequency Identification), wireless mesh networks, and smart sensors bring us closer to the ‘‘Internet of Things (IOT)’’, a vision of a global infrastructure of networked objects (Kortuem et al., 2009). Such a network can provide ubiquitous and real-time tracking information on movements of objects, giving firms the opportunity to achieve greater visibility on their supply chain entities; and, thus, streamline their supply chain operations.

Achieving end-to-end supply chain visibility requires enterprises to devise methods that extract the current location of products from data being collected on moving products from production to consumers. Object tracking systems represent an emerging class of information systems which aim to monitor products through the supply chain and register their on-going location (Jansen-Vullers et al., 2003). Such systems enhance the availability of product tracking information in critical supply chain processes, such as inventory management, production planning, and distribution management; and, consequently, influence managers’ relevant decisions and strategy formulation. Arguably, the effectiveness of these decisions will be directly associated with the quality of information that the decision maker has access to (Price and Shanks, 2005); namely the product location information offered by the object tracking system. Motivated by the above, researchers have recently started to investigate information quality in object tracking systems (Thiesse and Fleisch, 2008; Kelepouris and McFarlane, 2008; Kim et al., 2008; Sellitto et al., 2007; McFarlane, 2005).

An object tracking system is usually embedded in higher-level applications that require updated localization information of objects in the application environment. Apart from supply chain management, real world applications that depend on object location range from healthcare, livestock detection, food traceability and retail to natural disasters prevention. To name but a few, one can consider detection of patients and medical equipment in hospital settings, detection of explosives in a building or expired fresh food in retail stores. Yet, we are on the doorstep of a global wireless network of uniquely identifiable objects that can track and share the continuously changing location of moving objects, enabled by automatic identification and capturing technologies (especially RFID) (Welbourne et al., 2009). Such real life applications with object tracking capabilities are expected to boost and, further, appear in hitherto inaccessible contexts.

Considering the above, we define object tracking as the capability of a system to monitor the flow of objects through a number of locations; and capture their presence at preselected locations across their flow, which we define as capture points The system has a network of capture points that read, identify and register only objects with labels (e.g. barcode label, RFIDtag etc.) which contain their identity. We define as labeling level the object levels that we mark to be tracked by the system (e.g. a case-level object contains item-level objects). The selection of the system parameters, location of capture points and labeling level, offers alternative forms of the object tracking system, which we define as configurations.

This article proposes an analytical approach to model the configurations of object tracking systems, in regard to the two aforementioned system parameters. Based on the analytical expression of the system’s configuration, we devise a quantitative metric to assess objectively the information completeness of object tracking systems; and we demonstrate that completeness is influenced by the system configuration. The information completeness assessment of indicative, alternative configurations of real-world product tracking systems in the supply chain shows that more capture points do not ensure better completeness. Instead, the effect of labeling level appears to be greater. Also, apart from the number of the capture points and the labeling level, the location of each capture point influences the system’s deployment cost.

In essence, the information completeness of object tracking systems reflects the availability of tracking information (Kim and Lee, 2006; McFarlane, 2005), or in other terms, the visibility of the inspected objects. Hence, the suggested analytical metric of completeness can be utilized to assess the provided visibility of object tracking systems. Indeed, we can introduce a new system property, titled ‘‘visibility’’, which assesses quantitatively the ability of object tracking systems to monitor objects flowing through the inspected area. The visibility of an object tracking system is expressed by its degree of information completeness; and consequently, is affected by the adopted system configuration. In this spirit, our model may be used for both ex-ante and ex-post evaluations of the visibility of object tracking systems versus the required investment cost.

The next section discusses the rationale and background information of this research. Then, we present our model of the alternative configurations of object tracking systems. Moving on, we model the information completeness of object tracking systems and we demonstrate our thesis with different configurations (41 in total) of 10 real product tracking systems in the supply chain. Finally, we discuss the research and practice implications of this study and conclude with ideas for further research.

## 2. Background

Automatic identification and sensing technologies, such as RFID technology and wireless sensor networks are capable of providing ubiquitous, real-time identification and localization information of moving objects. Their emergence has fuelled the business and research attention to systems/applications that exploit these capabilities to track continuously moving objects between locations and register their position over time. To name but a few, the terms object detection, location-aware location sensing, networked-RFID, sensor-based positioning, wireless sensor networks or wireless positioning systems are used interchangeably in the majority of related literature. In essence, they are object tracking systems that transform the objects flow in location information flow in different monitored contexts, such as a humanitarian logistics center (Yang et al., 2010), a farm (Voulodimos et al., 2010), a sushi restaurant (Ngai et al., 2008a), a “smart" home (Biswas et al., 2006) or a hospital's blood transfusion chain (van der Togt et al., 2011).

Object tracking systems are essential for effective operations and strategy formulation because they influence the information quality of object positioning information necessary to make critical business decisions (Thiesse and Fleisch, 2007; Sellitto et al., 2007; Tellkamp et al., 2006; McFarlane, 2005). The information quality (IQ) stream of research investigates alternative ways to measure the ‘‘fitness-for-use’’ (Wang and Strong, 1996) or, more specifically, ‘‘fitness for intended use in operations, decision making and planning’’ (Redman, 2004) of information in a particular IS context.

It is widely agreed in the JO field that information quality is a multi-dimensional concept (e g. Ballou and Pazer, 1995: Batini et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2002; Wang and Strong, 1996). An overview and evaluation of the most elaborated and cited classification frameworks of IQ dimensions is offered by Eppler (2001), Price and Shanks (2005) and Fehrenbacher and Helfert (2008). Among the main IQ attributes in the existing literature, completeness is one of the most widely cited, well-documented and it can be measured in an obiective manner – measurement of quality that is intrinsic to the information itself regardless of the context in which it is used (Watts et al., 2009). Elaborating on the proposed completeness definitions in different contexts (e.g., Ballou and Pazer, 2003: Biswas et al, 2006: Even and Shankaranaravanan, 2007: Pipino et al. 2002; Scannapieco and Batini, 2004; Wand and Wang, 1996), we posit that completeness refers to an information/data set and adopt the following, context-independent, definition.

Information completeness of an information/data set is defined as the extent to which information describing the corresponding real world events is present in the set. Hence, completeness is quantitatively translated into the ratio between the number of values that are recorded in the information set and the number of values that could have been recorded (the recorded values correspond to real world events) (Ballou and Pazer, 2003). Our information completeness definition and assessment in the context of object tracking systems is compatible with the above objective definition and metric.

In the same spirit, information completeness has been assessed in a quantitative manner in different contexts, such as relational databases (Parssian et al., 2004; Scannapieco and Batini, 2004), peer data management systems (Roth, 2007), multichannel information systems (Cappiello et al., 2004), health content in user-edited online resources (e.g. wikipedia) (Clauson et al., 2008) and data streams (Biswas et al., 2006; Klein and Lehner, 2009; Sarma et al., 2006).

Considering IQ research in object tracking systems, the majority of scholars and practitioners have focused on tracking systems design and algorithms and techniques to improve the quality of the supplied location information in terms of accuracy and precision (Eisenman et al., 2009; Youssef and Agrawala, 2008; Coronel et al., 2008; Otsason et al., 2007; Civilis et al., 2005; Borriello et al., 2005). Gu et al. (2009), Liu et al. (2007a) and Hightower and Borriello (2001) provide elaborated overviews of the available systems and both the techniques utilized and their performance parameters. Generally, the quality of the captured data can be measured by a collection of attributes that includes accuracy, timeliness, completeness, throughput, coverage and cost to name but a few (Tolstikov et al., 2008; Bisdikian, 2007). There are a few recent efforts that evaluate such IQ dimensions as a function of the operational characteristics of the technological means utilized in the capture points, e.g. the reading rate and the signal strength of sensors (Klein and Lehner, 2009; Gillies et al., 2010; Bisdikian, 2007; Kim and Lee, 2006; Hossain et al., 2011, 2007). However, to the best of our knowledge, only Klein and Lehner (2009), Klein (2007) and Biswas et al. (2006) assess the completeness of sensor data provided by sensor-based object tracking applications based on sensor operation attributes, such as sampling and sensing rate.

Further, there is an increasing interest on the impact of object tracking systems on business decision making and performance through enhanced offered quality of resources localization information in the value chain. More specifically, many scholars investigate how accurate object location information affects the performance of critical supply chain processes, such as manufacturing and shipping (Thiesse and Fleisch, 2008; Kelepouris and McFarlane, 2008; Kim et al., 2008) through optimized decision making. These contributions reveal that alternative sets of selected capture points may provide different accuracy of location information: and consequently, business benefits, McFarlane (2005) states that data completeness mea: sures information availability to support effective decision making. In this spirit, many authors assume that the deployment of object tracking systems influences the availability of captured positioning information (e.g., Wang et al., 2010; Sarma et al., 2006; McFarlane, 2005). More specifically, it is implied that more reading points offer better information availability (e.g., Kelepouris and McFarlane, 2008; Fleisch and Thiesse, 2007; Rochel and Joyce, 2006). Also, some studies discuss that the level at which tags are attached or granularity level (i.e. our labeling level) (Brintrup et al., 2008; Tellkamp et al., 2006) influences the availability of tracking information. Nevertheless, a formal approach that associates the information completeness of object tracking systems with the high-level architecture of their configuration in terms of the distributed capture points in the monitored area and the object levels that we mark to be tracked is still missing.

Our work develops an analytical model of information completeness of object tracking systems as a function of their configuration parameters (locations of reading points and object levels at which we attach labels to be tracked). Our research goal is to demonstrate, in an objective manner, how the system configuration affects the provided information completeness. As such, in the following paragraphs, we first define and model the system configuration as a function of the systems’ configuration parameters. Then, we devise an objective, analytical metric of information completeness that considers the configuration parameters. To validate and demonstrate the value of our research, we assess the completeness of 41 configurations (in total) of 10 different product tracking systems in real-world supply chains.

## 3. Defining the configuration of object tracking systems

An object tracking system aims to capture the presence and identify objects at different locations they move through, in order to provide on-going objects’ location information. As a result, these locations are transformed into capture points that read objects moving through or standing at the points. The objects are identified by their label that contains their identity.

We define an object tracking system as a network of capture points that read, identify and register labeled objects flowing through or standing at the points.

When labeled objects flow through or stand at the capture points, the object tracking system is registering object movement tracks that we define as follows.

Anobject movement (O-M) Track is a data record of the form (object\_identity, timestamp, capture point\_identity) that reports when a labeled object associated with this identity was scanned at a capture point associated with this identity; and, then, stored in the system’s database.

The data field capture point\_identity refers to the capture point’s location. For example, in retail stores, barcode scanners at the point-of-sales/cashiers, i.e. the capture points’ locations, read products’ barcodes (namely, the objects’ identity) and register the consumers’ purchases.

Observing the objects’ flows, we identify the individual locations that objects may occupy at discrete time moments. Thus, we infer all the potential points where we can capture objects. But, at the capture points we read only labeled objects. Consequently, to build an object tracking system we need to select where we capture objects and which object levels we label in order to be tracked and registered by the system. For example, RFID-readers on retail stores' shelves scan products labeled at item-level; but, at the backroom entrance, staff scans products labeled at case-level and pallet-level to verify the shipments.

Hence, we define a configuration of an object tracking system as the selection of: (i) the location of capture points; and (ii) the object levels we attach label on (referred to as labeling level) in order to be tracked at the capture points and be registered by the system. So, alternative configurations of object tracking systems are offered depending on the values of two configuration parameters: the location of capture points and the labeling level.

## 4. Modeling the alternative configurations of object tracking systems

Let N be the number of distinct locations $l _ { i } , ( 1 \leqslant i \leqslant N )$ the objects transit between in the tracking system’s monitored area. Further, each pair $( l _ { i } , l _ { j } ) , 1 \leqslant i , j \leqslant N$ represents the objects’ transition from location l to location $l _ { j } .$ Composing the pairs of locations the obiects move between, we realize the obiects' transition patterns in the inspected environment and can depict them in an object flow graph.

An object flow graph is a directed simple graph $G = ( V , E )$ consisting of a set V of nodes together with a set E of edges, corresponding to directed connections between nodes. Each node represents a distinct location that the objects visit. Respectively, edges correspond to the objects’ transitions between locations.

The object flow graph is associated with the adjacency matrix L reporting the objects’ transitions between locations. L is a square $N \times N$ matrix (Table 1) and each element of $L , l _ { i j }$ is binary; $l _ { i j } = 1$ , if the corresponding locations $l _ { i }$ and $l _ { j }$ are endpoints of an edge, meaning the obiects transit between locations $l _ { i }$ and $l _ { j } ;$ else $l _ { i j } = 0 . \mathrm { \ A l s o , \ i f \ } i = j , l _ { i j } = 0 ,$ , since there are no loops.

## 5. Modeling the information completeness of object tracking systems

The object flow graph incorporates all the objects’ transition patterns in the inspected area and guides us to the composition of object flow paths.

An Object Flow Path expresses an objects’ transition pattern. It is a set of distinct locations $\{ l _ { 1 } , l _ { 2 } , . . . , l _ { w } , l _ { x } \}$ , such that each pair $( l _ { i } , l _ { j } )$ represents the objects transition from location l to location $l _ { j }$ .

While labeled objects traverse a path, the tracking system is recording a stream of captured object movement $\left( 0 \mathrm { - m } \right)$ tracks of the form (object\_identity, timestamp, capture point\_location) that report when labeled objects moved through or were standing at capture points located on the path. Still, information completeness involves an information set. Thus, we assess the information completeness of an object tracking system per stream of o-m tracks collected from the capture points that monitor labeled objects’ transitions on a path. As such, we adjust the ‘‘absolute standard for completeness that serves as a benchmark’’ (Shankaranarayanan and $\mathbf { C a i } ,$ 2006) to devise the following definition.

The information completeness of an object tracking system per object flow path (PC) reports the completeness of the collection of o-m tracks aggregated from the capture points on that path. It is defined as the extent to which the o-m tracks describing the corresponding real object movements are present in the collection. Namely, the degree to which the collection of registered o-m tracks includes all the real object movements between the path locations. It is measured by the ratio between the number of recorded o-m tracks and the number of tracks that correspond to all real object movement events on the path (i.e. the number of o-m tracks recorded in case all path locations were capture points and all objects traversing the path were labeled). It takes a measure between 1 (perfectly complete) and 0 (most incomplete).

Thus, a stream of captured o-m tracks on a path is complete only if we have captured every object instance moving through or standing at all path locations. Namely, an object tracking system produces a complete stream of captured o-m tracks providing that every path location is a capture point and all object instances that move along the path carry a label, independently of their object level. So, the completeness of an object tracking system per object flow path depends on the system configuration; and consequently on the two configuration variables, location of capture points and labeling level. Based on the predefined mathematical expression of the system configuration, we model quantitatively the completeness per path as a function of the two parameters, based on the following two assumptions.

First, there are no erroneous readings and no time delay is introduced between the real objects’ flow and the capturing. Hence, a stream of captured o-m tracks on a path is accurate and timely meaning that each o-m track carries error-free, up-todate object location information. Secondly, to simplify the formulas of the completeness per path PC (Table 4), only one movement track is captured whether an object moves through or stands at a capture point. For example, when a shelf in a retail store is a capture point, we consider the tracks generated for each product instance to be aggregated in one.

Let $l o _ { i }$ object instances move through location $l _ { i } , ( 1 \leqslant i \leqslant p l )$ of a path with pl locations. The number of object instances $l o _ { i }$ includes all ith-level obiect instances contained in (i + 1)th-level parent obiects, where $j \ni \left\{ 1 , 2 , . . . , k \right\}$ and the top obiect level is K. For example, when one beer case (2nd-level) loaded with 15 cans (1st-level) moves through the backroom entrance $( l _ { 1 } ) _ { \uparrow }$ then $l o _ { 1 } = 1 + 1 5 = 1 6$ object instances enter the backroom. But, when store employees transfer the beer case out of the backroom through the sales floor entrance, they remove the 15 cans from the case and replenish the shelf without the case. Thus, generally speaking, it is possible that (j + 1)th-level parent objects may not follow along the path the jth-level object instances they contain. Respectively, jth-level object instances may be placed in (j + 1)th-level parent objects while they move along the path. For example, when 10 shampoos (1st-level) reach the end of the production line (l ), they are stocked in one case and the capture point at the warehouse shelf reads $l o _ { 2 } = 1 + 1 0 = 1 1$ object instances

If the object tracking system’s configuration sets capture points at all path locations and attaches label on all object levels traversing the path, each lo object instances that flow through location $l _ { i } , ( 1 \leqslant i \leqslant p l )$ of a path with pl locations would generate $\sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { p l } l o _ { i }$ movement tracks in total. Still, this quantity of o-m tracks corresponds to all real object movement events on the path. Thus, according to the information completeness per path metric, it occupies the denominator of the fraction PC. In the beer example above, if store staff moves one beer case out of the backroom, through the sales floor entrance (1st path location) and replenishes the shelf (2nd path location), then $\textstyle \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { p l } l o _ { i } = ( 1 + 1 5 ) + 1 5 = 3 1$ object movement events can be captured.

Nevertheless, whenever a jth-level object moves through or stands at a location l of a path, one o-m track is registered only if the system configuration includes a capture point at location $l _ { i } ;$ and jth-level objects are labeled. But, the ability or not of a system configuration C to track a labeled jth-level object at path location $l _ { i }$ has been modeled through the product $c _ { i j } = c _ { - }$ $p _ { i 1 } \times l l _ { 1 j } ,$ where the binary elements $c p _ { i 1 }$ and $l l _ { 1 j }$ of vectors CP and LL report whether there is a capture point at location l $( c p _ { i 1 } = 1 )$ and jth-level objects are labeled $( l l _ { 1 j } = 1 )$ , respectively. So, when $l o _ { i }$ object instances move through location $l _ { i } , ( 1 \leqslant i \leqslant p l )$ , the captured movement tracks equal $c p _ { i 1 } \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { l o _ { i } } l l _ { 1 j }$ , where each object instance has its own j-level $( j \ni \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , k \} )$ . Hence, considering all the pl path locations, the enumerator of the fraction PC takes all the captured movement tracks $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { p l } c p _ { i 1 } \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { l o _ { i } } l l _ { 1 j } } \end{array}$ . In the beer example above, if the system configuration sets a capture point only at the sales floor entrance and only the cases are labeled $( c p _ { 1 1 } = 1 , c p _ { 2 1 } = 0 , l l _ { 1 2 } = 1 , l l _ { 1 1 } = 0 ) ,$ then when the beer case with 15 cans is transferred for shelf replenishment, $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { p l } c p _ { i 1 } \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { l o _ { i } } l l _ { 1 j } = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { 2 } c p _ { i 1 } \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { l o _ { i } } l l _ { 1 j } = 1 \times \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { 1 + 1 5 } l l _ { 1 j } + 0 \times \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { 1 5 } l l _ { 1 j } = 1 \times \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { 1 } l l _ { 1 2 } } \end{array}$ $\begin{array} { r } { + 1 \times \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { 1 5 } l l _ { 1 1 } + 0 \times \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { 1 5 } l l _ { 1 1 } = ( 1 \times 1 ) + \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { 1 5 } ( 1 \times 0 ) + \sum _ { y = 1 } ^ { 1 5 } ( 0 \times 0 ) = 1 } \end{array}$ movement tracks are collected in total.

Ultimately, the mathematical expression of completeness in Table 4 confirms that the completeness of an object tracking system per object flow path depends on the system configuration, namely on the location of capture points and the labeling level.

## 6. Assessing the information completeness of product tracking systems in the supply chain

This section applies the proposed evaluation model of information completeness on indicative, real cases of object tracking systems that have been described in the literature. The ultimate purpose is to validate the mathematical model and pro vide insights for practitioners and researchers on the impact of each configuration parameter on information completeness.

On the one hand, organizations wish to achieve greater visibility on their supply chain entities in order to streamline their supply chain operations. On the other hand, the real-time identification and sensing technologies can track objects’ movements (e.g. products, assets, etc.) throughout the supply chain at any point in time. RFID has been reported to give unprecedented visibility to the supply chain (Bi and Lin, 2009; Lee and Ozer, 2007). As a result, the business and academic research into RFID applications in supply chain management has proliferated significantly over the last few years (Ngai et al., 2008b). On this basis, we assess the information completeness of real product tracking systems that utilize the tracking capabilities of RFID technology and operate in the context of a supply chain.

To select the cases, we started from recent special issues on RFID technology and its applications in various journals (Communications of the ACM, 2005; International Journal of Production Economics, 2008; Production and Operations Management, 2007; Electronic Markets, 2009; European Journal of Information Systems, 2009; IEEE Internet Computing, 2009; IEEE Pervasive Computing, 2006). Then, we conducted a literature search in the most prominent information systems journals and conferences using the Science Direct (Elsevier), ACM, IEEE, Emerald and AISnet electronic databases. The literature examination was as exhaustive as possible, based on the descriptors “RFID” and “sensor" for the period 2000–2011. Furthermore, we reviewed the relevant citations found in the respective papers. Due to the increased business interest on technology-enhanced tracking systems, we also looked for application trials or full roll-outs of such systems published in practitioners’ journals, e.g. RFID journal (www.rfidjournal.com). 3a. The deployment cost of an object tracking system is also influenced by the location of the capture points, apart from the number of capture points and the labeling level.

3b. A higher deployment cost of an object tracking system does not necessarily result in better information completeness per path.

For example, configurations 3c and 3d of a tracking system in a retail supply chain share a common number of capture points and labeling level. Still, configuration 3c sets a capture point on the sales floor door, while configuration 3d on the cashier. Considering that a medium-sized retail store usually has 10 cashiers, configuration 3d sets 9 more capture points than 3c. Similarly. configuration 6e sets a capture point on the distribution center's receiving door, while configuration 6d on the shelf. But, a medium-sized retail store usually trades 10000 different products, namely configuration 6d sets (10000  1) = 9999 more capture points than 6e. Hence, configurations 3d and 6d carry extra deployment cost than 3c and 6e respectively. In case of applying real-time tracking technologies (e.g. sensors, RFID readers) at the capture locations, the extra cost is far from negligible. On the contrary, configurations 3d and 6d have marginally less information completeness than that of 3c and 6e, respectively. Specifically, configuration 3d has (86.67–85.33)% = 1.34% and configuration 6d has (58.72– 57.25)% = 1.47% less information completeness per path.

## 7. Discussion and conclusions

This research showcased that the abstract definition of information completeness dictates that the nearer the value of completeness is 1, the more the available information exists (Kim and Lee, 2006; McFarlane, 2005). Indeed, many researchers (e.g., Fleisch and Thiesse, 2007; Rochel and Joyce, 2006; Lee and Ozer, 2007; Bi and Lin, 2009) acknowledge that the incorporation of automatic identification and capturing technologies in a supply chain (mainly in the form of RFID-based tracking systems) increases the availability of tracked information and gives complete, unprecedented visibility of product flows in the supply chain. Generalizing in other contexts, the real value of object tracking systems is enhanced visibility of objects in the monitored area.

Based on the findings of this research, the term ‘completeness’ in object tracking systems characterizes the information availability that is associated with information visibility. Hence, the proposed information completeness assessment metric can be used to characterize the provided visibility of object tracking systems. We call this system property ‘‘visibility’’ of an object tracking system. It evaluates quantitatively the ability of the system to monitor all the objects movements in the monitored area. We demonstrate that this is differentiated according to the system’s configuration. Lee and Ozer (2007) argue that there is a huge credibility gap of the visibility value of real-time tracking technology-based systems and that it must be filled with solid model analysis. In this spirit, our research efforts present a first step to fill this gap by formulating an analytical model to assess the visibility of object tracking systems. Moreover, enhanced visibility of business resources is associated with improved decision making and, consequently, business performance. Thus, our evaluation model will give organizations the opportunity to consider the degree of offered visibility versus the required investment cost of an object tracking system.

Summarizing this research, we have presented an analytical, systematic approach to model and assess objectively the completeness of the total information provided by the network of capture points of object tracking systems. We devised alternative forms (i.e. configurations) of object tracking systems that differ in size (i.e. the number of capture points), breadth (namely, the locations of capture points); and reading depth that conveys their capability of capturing different object levels with attached labels. According to the aforementioned findings, the completeness of object tracking systems depends, to a different degree, on the above configuration properties. Many authors (e.g., Kelepouris and McFarlane, 2008; Fleisch and Thiesse, 2007; Rochel and Joyce, 2006) have assumed that the more reading points, the better the availability of supplied tracking information. But, they have underestimated the role of the reading depth. We empirically demonstrated that a change of the system’s reading depth is accompanied by a greater change in the completeness than that in case of altering the system’s size. Further, we showed that the locations at which we set capture points also influence the cost of object tracking systems. A bigger investment in a wider network of capture points does not ensure higher completeness. On this ground, we were prompted to utilize an object flow graph as a means to include in our model the notion of the location of a capture point and, more importantly, their connections and the respective object transitions between them.

We consider that this research has both theoretical and practical contribution, which is summarized in the two following subsections.

## 7.1. Theoretical contribution

First, opposed to the majority of IQ assessment studies, we adopt an analytical (operations research-OR) approach for the modeling and evaluation of information completeness on object tracking systems. The proposed model employs a formal notation, based on graphs, to map the locations and transition states of individual objects within a given network of capture points. So, this study promotes interdisciplinary nature research applying analytical approaches on the IS field.

Previous research efforts focus on quality of information at the micro-level (Thiesse and Fleisch. 2008: Kelepouris and McFarlane, 2008; Kim et al., 2008), namely per-location. They study the impact of accurate location information on business performance through enhanced decision making. Still, nowadays, information systems have been migrating from a hierarchical/ monolithic to a networked-based structure, where the set of potential data sources has dramatically increased in size and scope (Batini et al., 2009). Indeed, the combination of the Internet with emerging technologies (e.g. RFID, near-field communication and embedded sensors) transforms everyday day objects in networked data sources that communicate their information. Thus, the issue of the overall quality of the information flowing in networked systems has become more complex. Object tracking systems have networked-based structure where the set of capture points (i.e. the data sources) contribute to the overall information flowing in them. In this spirit, our research compliments the literature with a model that evaluates information quality on a macro-level. Indeed, we assess the information completeness of the aggregated information collected by the network of capture points.

Moreover, there is a recent, emerging research stream investigating the relationship between information quality and the physics of sensors used to track the objects in the monitored area (Klein and Lehner, 2009; Gillies et al., 2010; Bisdikian, 2007; Kim and Lee, 2006; Hossain et al., 2011, 2007). Specifically, Klein and Lehner (2009), Klein (2007) and Biswas et al. (2006) model the completeness of tracking information provided by sensor-based obiect tracking applications based on sensor operation attributes, such as sampling and sensing rate. However, these research efforts consider only low-level, tech nical characteristics of obiect tracking systems, namely how the capture points function on technical criteria On the contrary, we devise a higher-level view of obiect tracking systems that considers a higher-level architecture of the system. Specifically, the systems are differentiated based on how the capture points are spread in the inspected area and which object levels are visible while moving between them. Since there is a vast amount of on-going research towards the solution of technical defi ciencies of real-time tracking technologies, we trust that, in the long term, all the capture points will operate without reading losses. As a result, our assessment model reflects if there are capture points at critical locations to read the objects movements, not if and how they function. Our model is independent of the technology used at the capture points. It assesses com pleteness of information even for capture points that don’t use some tracking technology, e.g. store staff may record manually the products transferred out of the backroom for shelf replenishment.

## 7.2. Managerial implications

The proposed model may be applied to perform two distinct types of information completeness assessment of object tracking systems. During an a priori (or ex ante) evaluation, the model may be employed to assess the information completeness requirements of a given monitored context and guide pertinent design decisions. But, technologies for seamless tracking and real-time querying of moving objects’ location have high and, of unknown structure, deployment cost and unclear future payoff (Bose et al., 2009; Kapoor et al., 2009). Thus, this model is particularly valuable for managers who are risk averse and may use the model to evaluate alternative configurations and deployment strategies of object tracking systems based on the offered information completeness (in other words the visibility of the inspected context). This study proposes a toolkit that bridges estimates of deployment cost with information availability (IA)-return on investment (IA-ROI) to support decision making.

During an a posteriori (or ex post) assessment, the model may be applied to assess the information completeness of object tracking systems and identify areas of improvement. In this context, designers may evaluate the design choices of the system in question in terms of deployment preferences (such as number of locations equipped with Auto-ID technologies and object levels to be monitored) and determine improvements based on design best practices of other similar system instantiations.

For example, configuration 3b (see Table 5) of a tracking system in the printing industry supply chain sets 6 capture points, including shelf; and it offers 80% information completeness per path. Let us consider an alternative system configuration that excludes the capture point on the shelf and replaces it with one on the sales floor door. Based on the proposed approach, both configurations provide the same degree of information completeness per path. Still, a bookstore trades many books that are placed on shelves. So, configuration 3b sets more capture points and, consequently, carries extra deployment cost than our proposed configuration. Therefore, if the system designers had applied the proposed model, they would have acknowledged that there are other configurations with more moderate deployment cost and, most importantly, not in expense of the offered completeness.

## 7.3. Further research

We acknowledge that there is ample ground for extending our theoretical model. Although different system configurations may lead to different information completeness values, there is no evidence whether these differences are statistically significant. Hence, one avenue for further research is to develop a set of qualifiers that distinguish classes of system configurations leading to common values, in statistical terms, of information completeness. We are currently developing a simulation model of an RFID-based product tracking system in a retail store that reports information completeness per alternative system configuration. The end results will be analyzed with SPSS to identify the limits over which information completeness values begin to have statistically significant differences.
