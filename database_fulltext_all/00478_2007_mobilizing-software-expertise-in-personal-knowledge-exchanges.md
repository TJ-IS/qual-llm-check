# Mobilizing software expertise in personal knowledge exchanges <sup>q</sup>

Sanjay Gosain

Capital Group Companies, Inc., 15271 Laguna Canyon Blvd., Irvine, CA 92618, USA

Accepted 23 February 2007 Available online 12 April 2007

## Abstract

Personal knowledge exchanges (PKEs) are Web-based markets that match seekers and providers of knowledge and facilitate the pricing and transfer of knowledge assets. They show significant potential to function as infrastructure for the ‘‘elance-economy.’’ This study examines transactions for software expertimse in a personal knowledge exchange. It evaluates the mobilization of knowledge in terms of the speed and the number of knowledge providers that are matched to a knowledge request. Hypotheses are proposed based on transaction costs imposed by the characteristics of knowledge. We also study the impact of safeguarding and coordination mechanisms that may help overcome challenges to PKE use. We find that knowledge mobilization in the PKE is adversely impacted by knowledge transfer costs due to the tacitness, situatedness, and complexity of knowledge that is sought. To a lesser extent, knowledge mobilization is adversely afected by the likelihood of opportunistic behavior as indicated by the reputation ratings of the individual requesting the knowledge. The study enables a better understanding of the factors impacting the efectiveness of personal knowledge exchanges and provides important managerial implications for shaping their development.

## 1. Introduction

Over recent years, Internet-based exchanges have emerged that have begun to support knowledge transfer among individuals using market-based pricing and matching mecha nisms – we label these as personal knowledge exchanges (PKEs). The emergence of PKEs represents an example of the general trend towards business activity disaggregation and market-based coordination by free agents as Internet connectivity reduces coordination costs (Malone and Laubacher, 1998). However, it remains unclear if efective market per formance can be achieved for knowledge, which may be sticky and involved, without the benefit of close ties or common organizational afiliation. Further, PKEs require individuals to share this knowledge through relatively lean technology platforms with low inter activity and limited access to contextual details. Exchange of an involved, highly specific asset with strangers through lean channels raises opportunism or behavioral uncertainty issues (Jones et al., 1997) as well as knowledge creation, transfer, and assimilation challenges. This study is among the first to examine the potential of technology for transforming knowledge work towards an ‘‘e-lance’’ economy where tasks are not assigned and controlled through a stable chain of management but rather are carried out autonomously by independent contractors. These electronically connected freelancers, or e-lancers, join together into fluid and temporary networks to produce and sell goods and services (Malone and Laubacher, 1998).

The allure of PKEs lies in their potential to overcome some of the challenges plaguing the more traditional knowledge management infrastructure such as document repositories and Information Technology (IT)-enabled communities (Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Alavi and Leidner, 1999; Pan and Leidner, 2002; Gallivan et al., 2003). These are hindered by a lack of appropriate motivation mechanisms to sustain knowledge exploration and exploitation processes (Wasko and Faraj, 2003). In addition, knowledge sharing tends to sufer from ineficiencies caused by incomplete information about the sources of knowledge and satisficing behavior that manifests itself through reliance on local or close sources (Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Eschenfelder et al., 1998).

Personal knowledge exchanges are only in their infancy and the mechanisms used in these exchanges are still being tweaked. However, given their significant potential to transform knowledge work, PKEs need to be studied and factors afecting knowledge mobili zation need to be understood. Industry reports (cf. DataMonitor, 2000) analyzing the PKE business space were initially overly optimistic and projected revenues as high as \$6.7 billion for the year 2005. While there was considerable enthusiasm about these new institutions, a number of recent failures have clouded the future scenario for these exchanges. For instance, Soapbox.com, an ofshoot of the financial information site Mot ley Fool, aimed to be a marketplace for the exchange of knowledge related to financial investment analysis – but failed to gain traction and was shut down. On the other hand, Google has only recently introduced a new service (‘‘Google Answers’’) to match its users with providers of knowledge. Given the mixed level of success, an empirical investigation of these emerging institutions is warranted.

Alternative approaches to knowledge management conceptualize knowledge in diferent ways and focus on diferent aspects of how knowledge should be created and shared. In this study we focus on knowledge mobilization, which is defined as the extent to which people needing knowledge for specific tasks can be eficiently matched with counter-parties possessing that knowledge. This perspective treats knowledge as being made resourceful by being competently marshaled and utilized (Kreiner, 2002).

PKEs face significant barriers to widespread adoption because knowledge is often situated in a context, often too tacit to be articulated, and often embedded and socially constructed, to be easily exchanged (Blackler, 1995). Further, recent research has shown that introduction and use of network technologies designed to deliver service through impersonal interactions is problematic for embedded relationships (Schultze and Orlikowski, 2004). In addition, the usual challenges of electronic markets such as behavioral uncertainty tend to be exacerbated because it is more dificult to make an ex-ante evaluation of quality and how useful knowledge acquired in the exchange would be in attaining a desired capability. Information asymmetries and ‘‘non-contractibles’’ are likely to increase with knowledge exchanges between individuals with little past interaction. Knowledge assets are associated with greater asset specificity, and therefore can lead to opportunistic behavior (Teece, 1981), while there is limited ability to utilize contractual practices such as the use of outcome-contingent incentives which may reduce risk. Such opportunism may, however, be countered through safeguard mechanisms such as reputation management systems that provide signals of trust and quality (Avery et al., 1999; Resnick et al., 2000). Similarly, some of the challenges posed by the leanness of the technology-enabled communication can be addressed by explicit coordination-oriented information sharing to set mutual expectations.

In this study, we seek to understand these challenges to knowledge exchange and designed mechanisms to deal with issues in a technology-enabled market setting and pose the following research questions:

How do characteristics of knowledge sought in a personal knowledge exchange afect knowledge mobilization? How does use of safeguard mechanisms and coordination mechanisms afect knowledge mobilization?

This study examines two aspects of knowledge mobilization – the number of counterparties that choose to respond to an individual seeking knowledge and the speed of response. These outcomes are important to gauge the performance of a knowledge exchange since attracting a critical mass of participants and motivating them to share knowledge is critical to the success of knowledge management eforts (Gallivan et al., 2003). The unit of analysis in this study is a transaction, involving software-related knowledge exchanged for monetary compensation, triggered by a request for knowledge made by the recipient. The study contributes two important types of insights. Firstly, it examines the potential of PKEs for diferent types of knowledge, and secondly, it generates insights to help adapt and evolve these infrastructures to meet knowledge management needs in an economy with disaggregated value creation.

The study is conducted in the context of a PKE focused on the software industry and primarily supporting the exchange of intermediate knowledge inputs to the software development process. Software expertise constitutes one of the most active types of services transacted through PKEs (Snir and Hitt, 2003). There is a significant opportunity for restructuring value creation activities in this industry as software development is a highly knowledge intensive endeavor having multiple paths to a solution, conflicting interdependence among solution paths, multiple solutions, and uncertainty of paths and outcomes. Also, there are wide variations across countries in the quality for factor inputs for software development (‘‘quality of supply’’) in terms of resource availability, process maturity level, and cultural fit (Amoribieta et al., 2001), which such exchanges can target to broker. Thus, a better understanding of the factors afecting the performance of these exchanges will help to significantly impact software development practices.

This study provides implications for the information technology infrastructure supporting PKEs. The findings suggest that opportunism-related factors may be less of an imped iment to the success of PKEs compared to knowledge asset characteristics. We point to IT features such as shared screen interfaces, communal ontologies, and shared collaborative spaces that can help facilitate socialization to enable transfer of complex, situated, and tacit knowledge. We also find that support for coordination mechanisms helps to increase knowledge mobilization.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, the theoretical model addressing the above research questions is developed and specific hypotheses are proposed. This is followed up with a description of the research methodology and the context. Section four presents the findings based on the examination of transactions for software expertise in a knowledge exchange. The paper then discusses the findings and draws out implications for theory and practice.

## 2. Theoretical development

In this study, we primarily draw upon arguments underlying the transaction costs economics (TCE) perspective as well as the literature on electronic markets and knowledge management to theorize about transaction-specific costs impacting knowledge mobiliza tion. The transaction costs economics perspective (Williamson, 1985) suggests that firms exist in response to the threat of opportunism posed under conditions of high levels of uncertainty and transaction-specific investments in market exchanges. Post contractual hold-up determines the ex-ante investment decisions; bringing a transaction from the market into firm boundaries and within purview of authoritative monitoring, mitigates the hazards due to opportunistic behavior. While TCE is a theory of the firm it has clear implications for transactional performance at the underlying micro-level that is the focus of this study. TCE suggests that the specificity of assets involved in an exchange trigger a threat of opportunistic behavior that requires costly contractual safeguards to deter. In PKEs the possibility of opportunistic behavior can be reduced through reputation management systems that augment the payofs from a history of good behavior. Thus, the reputation of counter-parties is linked to the possibility of opportunistic behavior and market signals of the recipient’s reputation would impact the motivational disposition of the source and ultimately afect knowledge mobilization (Gupta and Govindarajan, 2000).

A further source of transaction costs in a PKE facilitated transaction is the cognitive and material costs incurred in the transfer of knowledge and these depend upon the type of knowledge being transferred (Inkpen and Dinur, 1998). The literature on knowledge management suggests that knowledge is not readily or easily transferred from the originator to the user of a technology, such transfer faces significant barriers (Attewell, 1992). This view is also supported by other work suggesting that knowledge is not as mobile as it has often been assumed (Kogut and Zander, 1992; Simonin, 1999).

The arguments related to dificulty in knowledge transfer also underlie the knowledge based view of the firm, a relatively newer perspective suggesting that firms exist and derive their unique advantage based on the ability to integrate the knowledge of individuals embedded in value-creation processes (Ghoshal and Moran, 1996; Grant, 1996). This advantage is derived from shared languages, knowledge, and routines that enhance the eficiency of coordination. Conner and Prahalad (1996) suggest that limited cognitive abilities on the part of individuals (bounded rationality) lead to knowledge-based transaction costs that are independent of the opportunistic considerations underlying transaction cost economics (e.g., Alchian and Demsetz, 1972; Williamson, 1985; Hart and Moore, 1990). In developing their theoretical arguments seeking independent force for knowledge resourcebased theory, Conner and Prahalad ‘‘assume that opportunistic behavior will not occur’’ (p. 477).

Both transaction cost and knowledge-based theories of the firm are essentially theories of market failure, arguing that firms possess inherent characteristics that make them suitable for situations of opportunism or for situations complicating knowledge transfer. This would suggest that the performance of markets would worsen as such characteristics arise, and in the extreme case we would expect to see markets that fail to arrange transactions within a reasonable timeframe. The unit of analysis in this study is the transaction – failure to match counter-parties in a reasonable period of time is an indicator for market performance, and in the extreme, of market failure.

The arguments underlying theories of the firm point to the need for a careful assessment of knowledge that is required to be transferred, as certain types of knowledge will be substantially more dificult to transfer in markets without the benefit of socialization conditions facilitated by firm organization or the possibility of rich interactions. Next, we propose characteristics of knowledge that provide a typology to assess the suitability of knowledge for transfer in a market.

We adapt the typology of knowledge proposed by Garud and Nayyar (1994): simple versus complex, explicit versus tacit, and independent versus systemic. Kogut and Zander (1992) propose a similar typology suggesting distinctions based on codifiability and complexity of knowledge. Garud and Nayyar (1994) propose that the position of knowledge on the above three dimensions afects the amount of information required to describe it and the amount of efort needed to transfer it. Therefore we expect that if the sought knowledge rates high on these dimensions, then a lesser number of counter-parties would be expected to be able to provide the required knowledge. Also, it is expected that more time would be needed to provide the knowledge. Our hypotheses development is guided by this rationale.

## 2.1. Hypotheses development

## 2.1.1. Knowledge asset characteristics

The first set of hypotheses proposed in this study is supported by literature on knowledge management which suggests that dificulties in the coordination and transfer of knowledge are a key cost to be borne for knowledge transactions (Conner and Prahalad, 1996). Information asymmetries must be bridged for knowledge to be conveyed to others (Cof, 2003). Given the absence of common language and cognitions among individuals without common firm afiliations (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990), it is expected that the probability of market failure would increase with increase in the factors underlying such asymmetry. Next, we develop testable hypotheses related to the characteristics of knowledge.

Complexity. Complexity dimension refers to the type of knowledge that evokes more causal uncertainties and requires more information to be transferred – it refers to a greater number of interdependent technologies, routines, individuals, and resources linked to a particular knowledge or asset (Simonin, 1999). A key reason for knowledge complexity is the need for integration across diferentiated domains of knowledge types, overcoming the problem of ‘‘incommensurability’’ and drawing the pieces into a single, coherent, and rigorously structured framework (Spender, 1998).

Complexity is expected to afect the comprehension of the totality of a knowledge asset and to impair its transferability (Simonin, 1999). A greater complexity of knowledge sought by a recipient would increase the efort for the provider and also create greater sources of uncertainty for the provider. Complexity entails a need to integrate diferent information sources and knowledge bases. Interaction among diferent knowledge components can further complicate this integration – it may become dificult to predict how new knowledge will interact with existing information on the issue under consideration (Grover and Davenport, 2001). Hence, we propose:

## Higher complexity of knowledge sought by a recipient in an exchange would be associated with less efective knowledge mobilization.

Tacitness. The tacitness dimension refers to how easy or dificult it is to codify and articulate the information that needs to be transferred for specific knowledge. Tacitness results from an implict accumulation of knowledge that results from knowing by doing (Reed and DeFillippi, 1990). The concept is closely associated with Polanyi (1958) who suggested ‘‘we know more than we can tell’’. Also, knowledge may be causally ambiguous (Szulanski, 1996) or embedded within an individual’s cognitive processes such that it is dificult to precisely communicate. Tacit knowledge is subconsciously understood and applied, dificult to articulate, developed from direct experience, and usually shared through highly interactive conversation, storytelling, and shared experience (Zack, 1999). There are conflicting views on the relationship between explicit and tacit knowledge (Stenmark, 2002). Nonaka has propounded a view where transitions between explicit and tacit knowledge are critical for transfer of knowledge. Transfer requires externalization – making tacit knowledge explicit, combination – consolidating multiple sources of explicit knowledge, internalization – deconstructing explicit knowledge into tacit knowledge and socialization – creation of tacit knowledge through shared experience (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995). On the other hand, Cook and Brown (1999) argue that explicit and tacit are two distinct forms of knowledge and neither is a variant of the other, and that one form cannot be made out of or changed into the other. Tsoukas (1996) also claims that tacit and explicit knowledge are mutually constituted and should not be viewed as two separate types of knowledge. In our concep tualization, we view articulated knowledge as being based on an unarticulated and tacitly accepted background of embodied knowledge and social practices that are internalized.

Increased tacitness of knowledge means that fewer providers would be able to obtain a clear idea of what is likely to constitute a good response to a request for knowledge. Also, there is a possibility of greater conflict as the recipient and provider could have very different interpretations of the conditions of the transaction. Thus, it would be expected that fewer counter-parties would respond to a request for knowledge or the response may be delayed on account of the need to expend greater efort in encoding knowledge into symbols. Hence,

Higher tacitness of knowledge sought by a recipient in an exchange would be associated with less efective knowledge mobilization.

Situatedness. The situatedness or embeddedness perspective on cognition and learning (Suchman, 1987) emphasizes that individual minds usually operate within environments that structure, direct, and support cognitive processes. Context may take shape as physical or task-based including artifacts and external representations of information, environmental or ecological (such as workplace and marketplace), and social or interactional. The social context, may also support and influence cognition through the presence of other minds to influence, assist, mislead, demonstrate, question, and ofer diferent perspectives (Jordan and Henderson, 1995). Knowledge that is situated in a specific context to a higher degree needs to be described in relation to an existing body of knowledge relevant to that context. It may also require greater information exchange for the interacting parties to learn about the context in which knowledge is situated or in which the new knowledge is expected to be used. Situatedness from a KBV perspective is diferent from asset specificity in the TCE literature. Situatedness refers to the cognitive costs of crafting a knowledge response that applies to the requestor’s context. Asset specificity refers to the opportunism that may be triggered by exchange-specific assets.

Increased situatedness of a request for knowledge would imply that it may be necessary for providers of knowledge to first obtain a better understanding of the context of the recipient. It may also require a length process of apprenticeship (Lave and Wenger, 1991) that may be dificult to structure and support in marketplaces. Also, generic knowledge may need to specially customized to fit that context, resulting in additional efort. Increased situatedness also increases information asymmetries. While the seller of knowledge has most information on how knowledge was generated and how it is codified, the buyer might have more information on how the knowledge would need to be applied within the context of the issue being addressed (Grover and Davenport, 2001). Past research has also found that recipients in knowledge markets tend to be concerned that knowledge may not ‘‘travel well’’ from a diferent context (Bell et al., 2002). Hence, we propose that:

Higher situatedness of knowledge sought by a recipient in an exchange would be associated with less efective knowledge mobilization.

## 2.1.2. Safeguard mechanisms

The transactions cost economics perspective argues and empirically finds that specificity of assets in an exchange trigger the threat of opportunistic behavior, requiring contractual safeguards. In a knowledge exchange, the knowledge asset is custom produced in response to a unique problem, and thereby the asset is transaction specific. The second set of hypotheses is supported by arguments related to safeguarding against opportunism. The extent to which opportunism can be controlled in a market setting would reduce transaction costs, incurred in writing more complete contracts to reduce risks associated with future contingencies and enhance the efectiveness of market exchange.

Online transactions create higher levels of vulnerability for consumers as they may be matched up with counter-parties that they never see face-to-face or purchase goods that they cannot examine before hand or create receivables that get settled over an extended period of time. Consumers of knowledge may also not be able to evaluate the competence of potential counter-parties in advance, while producers of knowledge face the risk of incurring sunk costs in creating a customized knowledge asset. In the research context of this study, risk is mainly borne by the provider of the knowledge asset because the transaction-specific knowledge is provided first and payment is made later after the reques tor has completely assessed the knowledge.

In order to deal with the higher level of vulnerability, digital repositories have been created by trusted third parties to enable consumers to ex-ante evaluate the trustworthiness of counter-parties they are entering into transactions with or other risk elements in the transaction such as the quality of the product being transacted. The introduction of an appropriate feedback mechanism makes sure that each agent’s behavior has reputational consequences.

Reputational consequences serve as safeguards for the exchange of customized assets in transactions by relaying the detection of deception, and thus efectively deterring opportunistic behavior (Jones et al., 1997). A number of studies have begun to examine the impact of reputation as managed by a reputation management system on prices in auction settings (see Resnick et al., 2004 for a review). The evidence so far indicates that seller ratings have a statistically significant efect on auction prices, especially for riskier transactions (Dewan and Hsu, 2001; Lucking-Reiley et al., 2000). Ba and Pavlou (2002) show that appropriate feedback mechanisms can induce calculus-based credibility trust without repeated interactions between two transacting parties. Trust can mitigate information asymmetry by reducing transaction-specific risks, therefore generating price premiums for reputable sellers. It is therefore expected that counter-parties would be more willing to transact with individuals with higher reputation ratings.

## Higher recipient rating will be associated with more efective knowledge mobilization

It is expected that the assessment of an individual’s reputation would depend on the number of ratings received. Thus a person’s level of reliance on another individual’s overall reputation rating is likely to be higher if that rating is based on a greater number of evaluations. The ability to build a reputation based on ratings received from a variety of counter-parties over a long period of time, engages long-run participants into ‘‘commitment’’ strategies (Kreps et al., 1982). Such strategies imply that the rated members are less likely to cheat and other parties are expected to use their past history as an indication of future behavior (Dellarocas, 2003). Hence,

The impact of recipient rating on knowledge mobilization will be moderated by the number of ratings.

It has been found that negative ratings have a stronger negative impact on trust than positive ones (Ba and Pavlou, 2002). It is expected that individuals should place a greater value on a long and unblemished rating profile. Counter-parties will calculate that a more reputable person is less likely to destroy his or her longstanding reputation for a single transaction (Scott and Derlaga, 1983). They will assume that recipients who have accumulated a good reputation would incur a high cost from cheating behaviors, and thus would be less likely to act opportunistically. Cabral and Hortacsu (2004) show theoretically through a model based on adverse selection and moral hazard, and empirically through data from eBay, that the first incidence of negative feedback is associated with lower subsequent efort and increased likelihood of subsequent negative feedback. Further, this results in strategic responses from both buyers and sellers. Thus, when a buyer has received negative feedback there is likely to be an expectation of diminished valuation of his or her reputation. Hence, we propose that:

Negative past feedback for the individuals seeking knowledge will be associated with less efective knowledge mobilization.

## 2.1.3. Coordination mechanisms

The third set of hypotheses is guided by the view of process support to overcome the leanness of the technology platform as a coordination mechanism. The seeker and potential provider of knowledge can set out their expectations related to the transaction in two ways. Qualification refers to the (optional) process facilitated by the PKE whereby the requestor can specify constraints that must be met by recipient in terms of their expertise or criteria that must be met by the knowledge that is provided. Clarification refers to the (optional) process of mutual interaction between a requestor and provider that is intended to clarify the knowledge request to provide better understanding to the provider.

Qualification. Qualification refers to seekers of knowledge providing criteria that must be met by the providers of knowledge or by the response to their request.

In information-rich contexts that are characterized by a large supply of information available in electronic forms, the scarce resource is typically not information, but the amount of attention that individuals can allocate to searching for, sorting through, and interpreting the available information (Ocasio, 1997). Hansen and Haas (2001) show that knowledge suppliers in internal markets in a consulting firm were able to develop a reputation for quality and focus – paradoxically, the less information they ofered the more they tended to be used. This suggests that knowledge mobilization can be more efective if recipients are able to signal their information needs and desired preferences so that the providers best able to satisfy those needs match up with them. For the providers, it also adds value as they can reduce the uncertainty about meeting the buyer needs. Hence,

Apriori qualification of knowledge sources by the individuals seeking knowledge will be associated with more efective knowledge mobilization

Clarification. Clarification refers to exchange of information between the seeker of knowledge and potential providers prior to the actual provision of knowledge. This is used by knowledge providers to make sure that they understand the request as intended by the seeker.

It has been proposed that in order for an individual to understand the knowledge of another individual there must be some overlap in their underlying knowledge bases – a shared knowledge space (Ivari and Linger, 1999; Tuomi, 1999). Interaction between recipient and potential providers of knowledge is expected to provide an occasion for sharing of context and allow a shared understanding to emerge. In the context of PKEs, the clarification process enables this shared understanding of the knowledge domain to occur.

There is also some evidence that interaction between the buyer and seller signals commitment on the part of the buyer (Radkevitch et al., 2006). An opportunistic buyer is not primarily interested in executing a contract through the online marketplace, but may be trying to use the marketplace for other purposes, such as obtaining insights into pricing to have a stronger position in negotiations with outside suppliers (Smeltzer and Carr, 2003). However, by engaging in interactions to clarify their requirements, the seeker of knowledge is signaling commitment.

Hence,

Clarification of knowledge requirements through interaction between seekers and potential providers of knowledge will be associated with more efective knowledge mobilization

## 2.1.4. Control variable: ofer price

Given costly knowledge gathering, it is expected that knowledge mobilization would depend on the price ofered by the person seeking knowledge (Snir and Hitt, 2003). However, the ofer price has to be judged relative to the complexity of the knowledge request being made. A high price ofered for relatively trivial knowledge is expected to lead to a higher knowledge mobilization as compared to a high price for more complex knowledge. Therefore, if we control for the characteristics of knowledge sought, a higher ofer price would lead to better mobilization on the margin. The ofer price is ex-ante decided by the seeker of knowledge and the market demand adjusts in response to the ofer price. Ofer price is, therefore, treated as a control and not an outcome.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Research context

This study is based on transactions on a PKE website (www.hotdispatch.com) that focuses on software development expertise. The site was chosen because it met criteria related to a critical mass of registered developers, focused knowledge specialty, and availability of archival transactional data at the time of data collection. The transactions represent participation in this intermediate market for the organization of the software development process rather than transacting for the outputs of the process (Mang, 1998).

The site has over 60,000 registered members who are globally distributed with a large number drawn from the US, India, Russia, and South America. The site is a buyer-driven marketplace – it allows software developers to post their requirements for help with issues encountered in their projects along with how much they are willing to pay for the knowl edge. Participating expertise providers compete to fulfill the submitted requests and compete with each other for the reward set by the Requester. The knowledge provided can range from snippets of software code, pointers to solutions, references to other knowledge sources, or simply claims of expertise. The individual seeking the knowledge can also qualify the kind of knowledge that they are looking for. In some cases, follow-up questions may be asked of the recipient and clarification provided on the original request. The recipient then pays the providers of the knowledge with the ofer price often split up among multiple providers. There is a bidirectional rating system such that both providers and recipient of knowledge are rated by counter-parties. The ratings of recipients are based on their fairness and timeliness in paying the providers of knowledge but other criteria may be used by providers at their own discretion. Appendix B provide examples of a typical request, clarification, and response and Fig. 2 illustrates the overall time-line for a transaction.

## 3.2. Data collection

The data for this study is based on a random sample of 100 complete transactions conducted at the site over a period of 3 months. By focusing on a relatively short time frame in which the level of participation in the exchange was reasonably stable, variations on account of changing competence available among the exchange members are minimized. For each of the transactions, the actual request for knowledge was extracted and information about the recipient and other attributes of the transaction available from the website were recorded.

A panel of 16 software developers was recruited to evaluate the nature of knowledge sought in the request. The recruitment was done through postings at software-interest newsgroups advertising the need for help with research study involving software problem-solving tasks, in return for financial remuneration. A software expertise profile was initially obtained and it was ensured that the respondents had a software-related college degree and at least three years of work experience. The developers, on average, had 7.3 years of experience in software development and represent a wide-ranging set of skills with diferent programming languages, operating systems and application categories. The developers were emailed a document to evaluate 25 randomly assigned transactions. The document had the exact text of the knowledge request for each transaction. Each transaction was evaluated by four developers to rate its tacitness, complexity, and situatedness based on provided scales (Appendices 1 and 2). The measures were then averaged across the four developers and the indicators for a construct. The use of multiple developers to evaluate each transaction as well as multiple transactions being evaluated by each devel oper, helped to ensure consistent evaluation of the knowledge characteristics for each request. Inter-rater reliability was assessed by computing the intra-class correlation coeficient – this indicated consensus among the raters. The measure estimates the average correlation among all possible orderings of pairs of observations for the same case. The intra-class correlation coeficient was assessed at 0.68 for tacitness, 0.73 for situatedness, and 0.84 for complexity.

## 3.3. Operationalization of constructs

Table 1 provides the details of operationalization of the constructs for this study. The PKE used in this study does not use a traditional auction format. It is based on consumers of knowledge providing an ofer price along with the knowledge request. In essence, therefore, prices are fixed while the participation by counter-parties is variable and essentially serves as a feedback mechanism to signal market supply at that price. A qualified response refers to the action on the part of the provider of the knowledge in making available either the solution to the request or a pointer to a feasible solution, with indicated acceptance on the part of the consumer.

The operationalization of knowledge characteristics was based on the scales (Appendix A) as assessed by the panel of developers. These scales were adapted from past work. An initial list of items based on past work was first developed. These items were then refined using a card-sorting exercise with five doctoral students. In the first round the students, who were unfamiliar with the study, were asked to sort the items into separate categories, based on the similarities and diferences among the items, and then to label the underlying constructs represented by each category. In the second round, the students were asked to classify the items into predetermined categories. The final scales exhibited adequate reli ability as assessed through Cronbach’s alpha (Tacitness – 0.78, Situatedness – 0.83, Complexity – 0.76). Discriminant validity for the three dimensions was assessed through a factor analysis (principal components). One item from the complexity scale was dropped due to high cross-loading. The remaining items all exhibited loadings greater than 0.7 on their own factor and less than 0.4 on the other two factors.

## 5. Discussion

Researchers have pointed to the need to study the role of IT in knowledge management and to focus enquiry on how IT can support knowledge management processes (Alavi and Leidner, 1999). While electronically mediated communication for connecting ‘‘weakly tied’’ people has been used in the past for sharing technical advice (Constant et al., 1996), PKEs ofer an alluring model to motivate knowledge sharing using market-driven mechanisms. This study has presented some of the key factors related to the costs imposed by the characteristics of the knowledge, as well as safeguard and coordination mechanisms that afect knowledge mobilization in these new settings.

The lack of significant results for some of the hypothesized relationships requires careful examination. The tacitness of knowledge sought led to significantly increased time to first qualified response, but did not significantly reduce the number of qualified providers. This may be because tacitness of knowledge sought may constrain providers in requiring them to devise novel ways of encoding information into symbols that may be transmitted. But ultimately, with time, most providers may succeed in overcoming the tacitness hurdle as they devise workarounds such as use of analogies or examples rather than direct answers, whereas situatedness and complexity may be more dificult to overcome.

Reputational ratings significantly impact the time to first qualified response but we fail to uncover a significant negative impact on the number of sources matched to a knowledge request. While research with larger sample of transactions is needed to better understand this result, it appears that the evaluation of opportunism may be more critical for the early providers, who would tend to stay away from transacting with higher risk recipients.

An interesting pattern is observed for clarification of requests and qualification of providers. Clarification tends to increase the number of sources but not significantly impact time to first acceptable response. A reverse efect is seen for qualification – it increases time to respond but does not significantly reduce number of sources. The findings suggest that clarification may be used to deepen the market to bring in more eligible counter-parties who need help interpreting and understanding the knowledge request. In addition to improving coordination, qualification and clarification may also serve as signals of the knowledge seekers commitment to the market and to consummating the transaction.

The overall pattern of results suggests that opportunism-related costs are not as signif icant as knowledge asset characteristics in determining how knowledge mobilization occurs in a PKE. This pattern needs to be interpreted carefully as this study was limited to relatively lower value transactions and may also reflect higher risk-taking propensity of part of the knowledge providers. Past research in physical product auction settings sug gests that the impact of reputational feedback on probability of sale is relatively higher for riskier transactions and more expensive products (cf. Dellarocas, 2003). Thus, the impact of reputation ratings may be greater for larger transactions.

In interpreting the findings, a number of limitations to this study need to be under stood. The study has evaluated the characteristics of knowledge that is sought based on the request being evaluated by a panel of software developers. This evaluation is subjective and may be biased due to the profile of the panel difering from the profile of participants in the exchange. The analytical methodology has not accounted for the possibility of measurement error. Structural equation modeling methods could be used to explicitly model for measurement errors.

It is also worthwhile pointing out directions for future research. This study has focused on knowledge mobilization aspects of knowledge exchanges but the process of transfer and utilization is deserving of attention as well. It would also be fruitful to examine factors related to the recipient such as recipient absorptive capacity and stickiness of knowledge (Szulanski, 1996) that would afect how this knowledge is assimilated and used. In terms of operationalization, future research can investigate alternative measures of knowledge mobilization such as quality of aggregate responses, diversity of responses, mean time of responses etc. The results of the study should be confirmed by investigation in other contexts and by examining a larger volume of transactions.

## 6. Implications

The results of this study suggest a need to draw upon innovative technology solutions to overcome challenges and embed PKEs in social practices. Exchange infrastructures that focus on facilitating isolated transactions with lean interfaces will be unable to generate the commonality of language and contextual understanding needed for transferring more embodied or encultured knowledge. Interface designs that force reflection and articulation of tacit assumptions, that encourage perspective-making and perspective-taking through rich technology-mediated conversations are more likely to enable shared understandings between knowledge sources and recipients to emerge. Also shared electronic spaces, that foster socialization and support individuals in sharing experiences, may also help knowledge exchange participants share a base of unarticulated beliefs that can then enrich the communications that occur in the exchange, as these can refer back to the shared base. Support for informal interaction can also help create trust in other individuals and help to better ground the perceptions of competence available in the exchange. Externalization of tacit knowledge into explicit concepts may also be supported through exchange infrastructure that supports the use of metaphors, analogies, concepts, or models (Nonaka et al., 1998). The ability to share screen interfaces, walk-through code repositories, link to past problems and solutions, and collaboratively edit documents may be some of the features that could help in this process. Shared communal resources in the form of stories, tools, and artifacts as well as support for structured repertoires of behaviors can help a community of practice to coalesce around these sites where the market institution can be embedded in a social container (Wenger, 2000).

A recent development on the Web is the emergence and the growing popularity of conversational genre such as Weblogs (blogs) or Wikis. These technologies can help to create a non-volatile and collaborative record of expressed knowledge (Wagner and Bolloju, 2005). These technologies can be used in conjunction with PKEs to deepen individual profiles and thus help to reduce knowledge transfer and opportunism related costs.

The study has found that complexity of knowledge sought is a serious impediment to knowledge mobilization. Exchanges will need to introduce more sophisticated processes that engage social collectivities to enable such knowledge needs to be supported. As an example, dynamic collaboration structures could be supported by the exchange infrastructure that permit a group of experts with complementary competencies to come together and provide their solution for intricate problems that span multiple domains.

Information-rich contexts enabled by new information and communication technologies have created a wealth of information but a poverty of attention (Simon, 1997). This has important implications for PKEs. Due to the constrained cognitive resources available to consumers, knowledge sources need to be qualified, be able to signal their quality, and provide high-quality and targeted information. This study found evidence to show if consumers that take upon themselves to signal their specific knowledge preferences they may end up delaying knowledge mobilization. Exchange platforms may be designed to better support qualification through better pre-screening that enables exchange participants to economize on their attention resources.

For the research community, the study provides initial insights and direction for the examination of PKEs – emerging institutions with considerable promise. There is a rich body of literature that examines eficient models of governance with the premise that boundary decisions are based on a comparison of the performance of alternative governance institutions – firms or markets (e.g., Poppo and Zenger, 1998). This study has applied the arguments underlying theories of the firm in a novel way. It separates out opportunism based explanations and knowledge-based explanations to explain knowledge mobilization. Such an empirical comparison of alternate sources of transaction costs has been lacking in the literature (Sampson, 2003) and past studies have tended to mainly examine firm organizing decisions (e.g. Afuah, 2001) rather than directly assessing trans actional performance.

It also points to research opportunities using archival transactional data from such sites. The study has gone beyond the available data to characterize the knowledge exchanged in transactions, using a pool of developers for qualitative assessment. This methodological innovation opens up new avenues for empirical investigation.
