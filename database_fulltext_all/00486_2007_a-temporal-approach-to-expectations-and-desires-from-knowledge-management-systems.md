# A temporal approach to expectations and desires from knowledge management systems

Dorit Nevo <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Yolande E. Chan <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Schulich School of Business, York University, Toronto, ON, Canada M3J1P3 Queen’s School of Business, Queen’s University, Kingston, ON, Canada K7L3N6

Received 18 May 2006; received in revised form 3 April 2007; accepted 25 April 2007 Available online 3 May 2007

## Abstract

This paper studies the formation of users’ expectations and desires from knowledge management systems, and their impacts on satisfaction with these systems. Building on a foundation of expectations confirmation theory and interviews with top managers, three important insights are obtained: (1) expectations and desires differ in their formation and content; (2) the conversion of abstract level desires into concrete product attributes is challenging to top managers; and (3) expectations and desires lie along a time continuum, with expectations playing an important role in shaping perceptions of the knowledge management systems in the short run, and desires being more oriented towards determining satisfaction in the long run. Furthermore, the existence of desires can mitigate the impact of expectations on satisfaction as users look forward to the positive benefits of their desires’ future realization.

## 1. Introduction

Knowledge management systems (KMS) are technologies employed by organizations to better retain and utilize organizational knowledge, as well as support knowledge sharing within and between organizations [2,29]. KMS are strongly tied to the organizational practice of knowledge management (KM), which is expected to lead organizations to positive outcomes such as better decision making, improved productivity, and enhanced competitiveness. While much has been studied on the overall practice of KM and the application of KMS (see the special issues of MIS Quarterly, March/June 2005), some aspects of these systems require further study. In particular, there is still mixed evidence with respect to the success of such systems in organizations [15,44]. Managing information systems projects is a complex task. For example, the Chaos report, published by the Standish group, indicates that in 2004 29% of IS projects failed, and a further 53% were ‘challenged’.<sup>1</sup> A major cause of these challenges is said to be rooted in the poor understanding of users and their needs, expectations, and desires from the information systems.

This paper thus focuses on one aspect of KMS that can contribute to studies on KMS success: the formation and realization of expectations and desires related to these systems. The particular path from expectations and desires to KMS success goes through satisfaction. The expectation-confirmation theory (ECT; [28,32]), a prominent theory from the field of marketing, places expectations and their confirmation as an important antecedent of satisfaction. IS researchers have demonstrated the applicability of this theory to IS products and the role of expectations in forming IS satisfaction in various settings (e.g., [3,22,23,26,36]). Satisfaction, in turn, has been widely investigated in the IS field in various contexts (see reviews by Khalifa and Liu [22], Sedera and Tan [31], Zviran and Erlich [46], and a meta-analysis by Mahmood et al. [25]). For example, IS researchers have examined links between satisfaction and IS evaluation [9,11,18], IS success [1,31], behavioral intention to use the IS [16], organizational effectiveness and performance [18,46], decision making and efficiency [3], and continued adoption [4,5,6]. Thus, a better understanding of expectations and desires from KMS, and their impact on satisfaction, is important in realizing a wide set of potential benefits.

The theoretical lens of the ECT suggests that people form expectations and desires concerning a specific product which they then compare to the perceived performance of that product. If expectations and desires are met or exceeded, satisfaction results. Otherwise the consumer is likely to become dissatisfied. Unfortunately, understanding expectations and desires of various stakeholders<sup>2</sup> is a challenging task in the context of IS products [22,27]. The novelty of many IS products, limited product knowledge and experience, and the dynamic nature of technology are but a few of the challenges involved in forming accurate and realistic expectations and desires [23]. These challenges may intensify with the complexity of the IS product, for example if the IS supports different business areas, promises a diverse set of benefits, or is functionally difficult to understand [27]. Such is the case of KMS.

KMS are most commonly defined as a group of technologies intended to support organizational knowledge management activities, with these activities being knowledge generation, codification, transfer, and application [2]. Indeed, an examination of a practitioners KM website<sup>3</sup> reveals a long list of technologies that are viewed as KMS, including business intelligence tools, decision support tools, collaboration tools, content management and document management tools, enterprise search engines and portals, intellectual property management tools, and records management tools. Moreover, although these technologies have been around for several years, KMS are still new to many stakeholders who have limited experience with KM in general, and even more limited experience with particular technologies.

As a result, we foresee three main challenges in forming and evaluating expectations, desires, and ultimately satisfaction from KMS. First, the limited product knowledge and poor understanding of KMS may reduce the accuracy of the expectations and desires generated, leading to unrealistic perceptions of what the system should deliver to its stakeholders. Second, the obscurity of the technology may make it difficult for users to link expectations and/or desires at the product attributes level (e.g., I expect/would like to have strong business intelligence capabilities) with expectations and/or desires at the organizational outcomes level (e.g., I would like to gain market share). It makes it difficult for users to understand and/or justify how the technology is actually providing the expected and desired benefits. And third, there may be a time discrepancy between the purchase and implementation of the KMS and the confirmation of the expectations and desires from it. For example, it is likely that there will be a delay before the realization of the desire for increased market share can be evaluated reasonably. This time difference is crucial to understanding satisfaction; it may enable KMS users who are aware of the benefit lag to be more tolerant and forward looking in forming their assessments of the KMS.

In this paper, we explore the three challenges highlighted above in greater detail in order to see whether ECT can extend to contexts similar to those of KMS. Specific research questions studied in this paper are: how are expectations and desires formed? How – and if – are they translated from an abstract level to the product attributes level? And how are they realized over time? The motivation for studying these questions is both theoretical and applied. In terms of theory, ECT applications in IS have thus far focused on simple IS products and services; the theory’s extension to more complex contexts is not straightforward. Thus an examination of the nature of KMS expectations and desires constitutes an important contribution to ECT applications within the IS field. In terms of practice, if systems analysts are able to pinpoint the difficulties that stakeholders have in forming accurate expectations and desires, they can devise better strategies for communicating the capabilities and potential benefits of the systems to stakeholders. Either way, as demonstrated above, better understanding of expectations and desires is expected to lead to improved satisfaction from the system.

Because of the limited knowledge on KMS expectations and desires, this paper takes an exploratory approach, gaining insights through interviews with top managers involved with the purchase decision of their organization’s KMS. Insights from these interviews can be used in the design of KMS, in the software sale process, in the implementation phase to improve satisfaction and KM project success, and even to support post-purchase adoption (e.g., [4,26]). The paper begins with a review of the literature, setting the background for our research objectives. We then describe the interviews conducted and the insights obtained. We conclude by highlighting the contributions of our study.

## 2. Review of the literature

## 2.1. Expectations and desires

An expectation is a belief about the probabilities associated with a future state of affairs [17]. The literature on expectations often defines them as a hierarchy of outcomes ranging from the ideal to the worst imaginable [30]. This hierarchy is mainly concerned with how the outcome is perceived. For example, ideal expectations represent beliefs about what “can be”, while predictive expectations represent beliefs about what “will be”, and minimum tolerable expectations represent beliefs about what “must be” [19].

Expectations have been linked to satisfaction by expectation-confirmation theory (ECT, [28]). In ECT, expectations serve as a comparison standard upon which the performance of a product is evaluated. If the product exceeds expectations, positive disconfirmation exists and is hypothesized to lead to higher levels of satisfaction. The opposite is the case for a product that has not met expectations (negative disconfirmation). Negative disconfirmation is expected to result in low levels of product satisfaction. This link between expectations and satisfaction has been studied in various contexts and products in the marketing as well as the IS literature.

In the initial ECT model, predictive expectations were used as the comparison standard. An example of predictive expectations in the IS research context can be the technical service guarantee of a vendor [36] or the likelihood of a website to provide information that is reliable or useful [26]. However, critics of the use of predictive expectations claimed that the confirmation of negative predictive expectations (e.g., “I expect the implementation to fail”) was not likely to lead to satisfaction. To overcome this problem, other comparison standards are now proposed such as desires, ideals, equity, or past product and brand experience (see reviews [19,45] and related analysis [39]. Also see [32,43]). While most of these standards are proposed as substitutes to predictive expectations, desires are proposed to work simultaneously with predictive expectations to affect product satisfaction [22,32]. In such a model, the perceived performance of a product is measured against two values — what was expected from the product and what was desired of it. Both comparisons result in some level of positive or negative disconfirmation and together affect satisfaction. This model is depicted in Fig. 1.

Unlike predictive expectations, which simply state what the individual thinks will happen (i.e., the probability of specific product attributes), desires represent what the individual would like to happen (i.e., the desired product attributes). Spreng et al. [32] provide examples such as the desire to protect one’s family from harm, or – in the context of a camera – the desire to obtain high quality pictures.<sup>4</sup> Desires are thus generated as abstract higher level values, such as the desire of an organization to become more cost efficient, which are then translated into levels of attributes that a consumer believes will lead to (or are associated with) these higher-level values [32]. For example, the desire to obtain more cost efficient production can be translated into the implementation of a specific information system designed to do so, one that will feature an inventory optimization tool, for example. Thus, while predictive expectations are evaluations of what the consumer expects to find in the product, desires are evaluations of what should be in the product in order for the consumer to obtain important higher level values. As highlighted by Spreng et al. [32]:

“Thus we believe that it is possible to distinguish clearly between desires and predictive expectations at the conceptual level. Expectations are beliefs about the likelihood that a product is associated with certain attributes, benefits, or outcomes, whereas desires are evaluations of the extent to which those attributes, benefits, or outcomes lead to the attainment of a person’s values. Expectations are futureoriented and relatively malleable, whereas desires are present-oriented and relatively stable.” (p. 17)

Spreng et al. [32] refer to desires concerning higher level values as abstract desires, and to those concerning product attributes as concrete desires.

## 2.2. Expectations<sup>5</sup> and desires in the IS literature

The ECT model has been applied in various contexts in the IS literature. For example, expectations confirmation was studied in the context of adoption and continuance intentions for online systems [4,5,21,24], computer-based training [6], and data warehouses [42]; in assessing perceived net benefits of a system [33]; and in assessing satisfaction from various aspects of systems [26,37,38] and services [13,36]. Desires were studied to a much lesser extent, for example in studies looking at the effect of the confirmation of both expectations and desires on end user computing success [35] and satisfaction [7,23]. Linking expectations and desires to variables such as satisfaction, success, and perceived benefits from IS reiterates their relevance and importance to IS research and practice.

While expectations have been generally accepted as a comparison standard in the IS literature, the use of desires in the IS context was initially advocated by Suh et al. [35] to study the success of end user computing. More recently, Chin and Lee [7] also highlighted the importance of desires in addition to expectations in the ECT model. According to their model, expectations and desires affect overall end user satisfaction from the product, showing both a direct effect and an interaction effect. Similarly, Khalifa and Liu [22] claim that desires are especially relevant in the IS context since expectations are difficult to formulate given the frequently limited (or non-existent) prior experience with the technology.

In addition to the incorporation of desires in IS studies, researchers have also shown that expectations and desires are likely to vary over time [36]. Khalifa and Liu [23] conducted a longitudinal study to assess the effect of both desires and expectations on satisfaction over time. They show that expectations and desires are adapted between the pre- and postadoption stages, as individuals gain more knowledge about the IS. A longitudinal approach was also taken by Bhattacherjee and Premkumar [6] who examined changes in beliefs and attitudes towards two technology products — computer-based training software and a rapid application development tool. Again, results show that beliefs about the IS tend to fluctuate over time.

In order to understand the value of expectations confirmation theory to IS research, we further examine the theory within the context of related conceptual approaches, particularly those that evaluate IS success, use, and satisfaction. Wixom and Todd [42] identify technology acceptance and user satisfaction as the two dominant approaches to investigating the factors and processes lying between IS investments and the realization of economic value from these investments. Thus, we build our discussion around these two themes.

Within the technology acceptance literature, the technology acceptance model (TAM, [8]) and its derivatives (e.g., the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology, UTAUT, [40]) are intended to explain and predict IT use behavior. Overall, the technology acceptance literature does not fully intersect with the ECT. Satisfaction is not a dependent variable in most acceptance models, and expectations confirmation is generally not considered as an antecedent. The primary objective of acceptance theories is to predict whether or not a system is likely to be adopted and used. ECT assumes that a system has been adopted and is concerned with whether or not users are satisfied. Thus, the focus of ECT is less on initial use, and more on postadoption satisfaction and continued use (e.g., [4]).

The stream of research on IS user satisfaction is more closely related to ECT than the literature on technology acceptance. In fact, as we have outlined above, IS scholars are turning to ECT to explain and predict satisfaction with organizational IS. However, most research on user satisfaction to date has not drawn on the expectations paradigm. Much of this literature explores the link between satisfaction and IS success [10,11,17], with satisfaction viewed as an important contributor to success [1,31]. Other outcome variables studied in the satisfaction literature include organizational effectiveness, decision making, behavioral intention, and performance [3,16,18,46]. The main difference between the satisfaction literature and the ECT is that while the IS user satisfaction literature has mostly focused on the outcomes of satisfaction, the ECT is more concerned with the antecedents of satisfaction.

In summary, the ECT has the potential to fill an important gap in the KMS literature, that is not currently studied in the related acceptance or satisfaction literature. We identify two specific gaps that need to be addressed. First, the technologies studied thus far are mainly commodity type IS, generally focusing on a single stakeholder (often the end user) and limited dimensionality of the product or service. The extension of ECT to products such as KMS, which are often more complex, involving multiple stakeholders and multi-dimensional benefits, is not straightforward [27]. Thus, an investigation of the applicability of ECT in those cases is warranted. Second, while studies have taken the longitudinal approach to investigate expectations and desires from IS, an important limitation still exists in that those studies assume that expectations and desires are formulated and disconfirmed at the same period in time. As we argue in this paper, this is not always the case. Indeed, some desires concerning KMS – such as the desire for better customer retention – may only be realized and confirmed in the long run. We explain these gaps in more detail below.

## 2.3. Expectations and desires from knowledge management systems

We have thus far discussed the relevance of ECT to IS products and the need to further study expectations and desires, especially in the context of KMS. We have also, in the Introduction section, highlighted the challenges that we foresee in forming expectations and desires from KMS. Specifically we noted that (1) stakeholders may find it difficult to formulate accurate and realistic expectations; (2) stakeholders may have a difficult time translating abstract desires to concrete product attributes; and (3) there may be a time gap between the realization of expectations and the realization of desires. We elaborate on these challenges below.

First, we note that KMS are not well defined. A review of the KM literature reveals that the most commonly used definition for KMS is that offered by Alavi and Leidner [2] which defines KMS by the KM area they are intended to support (e.g., knowledge generation tools and knowledge codification tools). Thus it is as much a classification as a definition. Moreover, even within these sub-groups, definitions are rarely found. As a result, many technologies are often loosely viewed as KMS. We highlighted a long list of sample KM technologies in the Introduction section. Another example comes from the Gartner group which publishes yearly technology “hype cycles”,<sup>6</sup> reviewing the state of practice in various technology areas. In 2005, more than ten different hype cycles were published under the KMS title, including hype cycles for CRM technologies; intelligence and data warehousing; collaboration and communication; content management; and e-Learning. Within each cycle, numerous technologies and products were listed.

In addition to the lack of clear understanding in practice regarding what constitutes KMS, some KMS are also not fully matured. Referring back to Gartner’s hype cycles for content management (as an example), while technologies such as web content management are very close to maturity, others – such as content integration tools and smart enterprise suites – are still some distance away. These various stages of maturities, coupled with the limited definitions of KMS, present difficulties in forming accurate expectations from KMS. Without a sound understanding of the product, stakeholders can find it challenging to form realistic expectations. As studies have shown, the formation of unrealistic expectations may impact satisfaction formation [37].

Second, challenges in forming expectations and desires may also arise due to unclear boundaries between the technology (KMS) and the business practice (KM). Specifically, the KM field entails much more than just technology [20,29] and there is a great deal of attention given to the organizational practice of knowledge management. As a result, there are quite a few expected (and desired) benefits from these practices. For example, Fedor et al. [14] studied expectations concerning the organizational impact of KM projects, and Wiig [41] identified a set of strategic, tactical, and operational benefits that organizations can expect to obtain from managing knowledge. For example, organizations can expect increased ability to produce products and services with higher knowledge content, better organizational and individual learning and reduced loss of knowledge, and better access to, and application of, knowledge when needed. It is possible that some of the expectations and desires pertaining to KM in general are used as the basis to form expectations and desires from the KMS. A risk involved with doing so is that some KM benefits may be due to managerial practices as opposed to the technology itself, and as a result individuals may dismiss the KMS because it fails to automatically provide these benefits. (Earl [12] made this point with respect to having too elaborate KM expectations but it is also applicable to KMS.) Combining the above arguments, with limited product knowledge and unclear understanding of what exactly the product is able to deliver, stakeholders may find it challenging to translate high level desires to concrete product attributes, as is required by the ECT model.

Finally, higher level desires such as better productivity, reduced loss of knowledge, or better organizational and individual learning may take time to realize. Conversely, expectations such as the existence of a graphical user interface, fit with other organizational IT, and timely implementation can be realized in the short term. The existence of such a gap between the realization of desires and expectations<sup>7</sup> can create a situation in which expectations disconfirmation may not lead to reduced satisfaction, if the stakeholders are aware of potential benefits to come, once their desires are confirmed. Thus, the applicability of the current ECT model is questionable in such cases.

## 2.4. Summary

This paper argues that studying and understanding expectations and desires from KMS are important. Because these systems are poorly understood, and because the boundary between the business practice and the technology is often vague, forming accurate and realistic expectations and desires might be difficult. Moreover, translating abstract desires into concrete product attributes may be challenging, limiting the applicability of the ECT model to KMS. Following the above review of the literature, and the gaps identified, the research questions studied in this paper are: how are KMS expectations and desires formed? How – and if – are they translated from an abstract level to the product level? And how are they realized over time? By answering these research questions we hope to extend the ECT model, and to offer useful propositions for future research.

## 3. Method

The above sections provide the motivation for this study and identify the gaps in the literature that we seek to address. Empirically, our goal is thus to explore the applicability of a well-established theory in a new context and to modify it as needed. We are especially concerned with the suitability of the independent variables of the theory – namely expectations and desires – in the new context. Thus, quantitative approaches to theory testing may not provide sufficient insights and understanding of these variables. Consequently, the selected approach to data collection involves the use of semi-structured interviews.

The level of analysis of the ECT model is the individual. In particular, the ECT mostly focuses on the consumer. Indeed, with simple consumption products, such as cars, clothes, and DVDs, the consumer takes on all possible roles — being the one who selects, buys, and uses the product. However, in the context of organizational IT products, such as KMS, these roles are often assumed by different stakeholders. In particular, while daily use is often a role assumed by end users (who are the focus of many IS papers), purchase decisions and product evaluations are more often made at the management level. Accordingly, the respondents in this study were members of the top management team, those with decision making authority, who select and evaluate the KMS.

## 3.1. Data collection

Data were collected for this study as part of a larger study focusing on the design and application of KMS in organizations. Specifically, the research involved a Delphi study investigating the most desired KMS capabilities, and follow-up interviews focused on exploring expectations and desires from the KMS. In this paper, we build on insights obtained during the interviews only.

Participants were carefully selected for this study from organizations in a large North American city. In selecting participants our criteria were top managers, involved with the organization’s KM initiative, and highly involved with the KMS purchase decision. This was to ensure that respondents had sufficient knowledge of both the business and the technology aspects of the project. We did not limit participation based on the timing of the KMS project. Thus some participants responded based on past projects and some responded based on ongoing projects. Overall 7 ongoing implementations and 13 post implementation projects were discussed. Because we are not as much interested in specific expectations and desires but rather in the process of formation, confirmation, and resulting perceptions, we do not believe that recall limitations substantially hurt the findings of this study. However, we briefly discuss the issue of recall as a potential limitation of this study in the final section.

Overall, 20 managers participated in the study, from different organizations varying in size from 200 to over 20,000 employees. Fig. 2a provides a breakdown of the organizations by industry, and Fig. 2b provides a breakdown of panel members by position in the organization. To ensure that a sufficient number of interviews were conducted we followed Strauss and Corbin [34] and assessed the level of saturation in responses. We felt that sufficient convergence was reached in the 20 interviews conducted with the Delphi panel, and therefore did not contact additional interviewees.

The extent of knowledge-intensive activities varied among firms. These activities included decision support, R&D, production, customer service, forecasting, business performance evaluation, employee management, and the documentation and improvement of business processes. The majority of participating organizations currently have technology support for KM in place, either developed in-house, by an external vendor, or purchased from vendors such as Microsoft, Oracle, or Siebel. These systems include knowledge repositories, professional support systems (with embedded business logic and rules), computer aided design (CAD) tools, customer relationship management (CRM) systems and communications tools. One respondent noted that she was just in the midst of purchasing a KMS for her organization.

Interviews were conducted over the telephone, recorded, and then transcribed. All interviews were conducted by the first author, following the interview protocol provided in Appendix A. After each interview, notes were reviewed to identify potential challenges or problems. Only in one case was it necessary to contact the respondent again for purposes of clarification.

## 3.2. Data analysis

Analysis of interview content was conducted using NVivo software. Building on the theoretical foundation of ECT, two themes were developed a priori — namely, expectations and desires. In coding the interviews, we distinguished between predictive expectations (or simply “expectations”) and desires. As outlined in the Introduction section, predictive expectations are specific beliefs about what will be provided by (or included in) the KMS. Desires, on the other hand, are often referred to as should be expectations [30]. Moreover, desires can be separated into abstract desires, higher level values (e.g., the desire to empower sales people), or concrete desires, focusing on attributes believed to lead to the attainment of these higher level values (e.g., the tool will provide real time market analysis) [32].

The two authors and an independent coder reviewed all transcripts. In addition to the concepts of expectations and desires, additional themes were added as they emerged from the data, following open and axial coding methods [34]. Emerging themes were then compared and discussed among the three coders until full agreement was reached. These new themes included a more refined categorization of expectations and desires in term of their subject matter, the formation of expectations and desires, and the notion of optimism in assessing the success of the KMS. Key insights from the interviews are summarized in the section below.

## 4. Research findings

Recalling the research questions presented earlier, our goal was to answer the following: how are expectations and desires formed? How – and if – are they translated from an abstract level to the product level? And how are they realized over time? This section provides insights from the interviews with respect to these three questions.

## 4.1. Formation of expectations

An important insight from the interviews concerns the formation of expectations and desires. We found that expectations are often rooted in past experiences with organizational information systems, or are based on promises made by vendors. Interviewees formed expectations based on past experiences with KMS, on past experiences with other information systems, or on knowledge of other organizations they either worked for or were familiar with. Vendors’ promises also formed a strong basis for expectations.

We can go to other insurance companies and compare packages that are standard to the industry. (President, Financial)

I went out and took a look at a number of [vendors], had meetings with them, called references on them, called people who had been using them. (COO, Service)

When we get the programmers involved you’d think if it’s [COMPANY] related, they all think “oh it’s not as good”, even though we’re a [COMPANY ] shop and stuff, they’re skeptical of anything [COMPANY], some of them. (Vice President of Business Development, Manufacturing/Software)

Expectations were commonly expressed in all interviews, focusing on tangibles such as the attributes of the software product or the process of implementing it in the organization. Specifically, interviewees expressed expectations in terms of the implementation of the product, its features and capabilities, its ease of use and training, and the service provided by the vendor. Some examples are given in Table 1. Negative expectations were also expressed such as expected resistance from employees or expected complex implementation.

## 4.2. Formation of desires

Unlike expectations, desires seem to be more focused on the business concept behind the technology and reflect how knowledge management is perceived in the organization, as well as the needs of the organization in terms of knowledge support. Desires were formed based on interviewees’ perceptions on what KM is and how it would benefit their organization. Desires most often reflected the business need that led to the purchase of the KMS.

You need to look at the vision from the benefit that it can provide and the culture change it can provide. You can’t get hung up on the complexity of it and you can’t get hung up on the cost of it. You need to approach it from not a system implementation perspective — you need to approach it [with] a “we are going to facilitate change within the culture to get things correct and have the right information available for people [perspective]”. Desires were strongly tied to what respondents knew of KM as a practice. For example, some respondents only saw the system as an artifact — a technology. These respondents did not form abstract desires with respect to the system. They mainly expressed desires concerning specific attributes.

We are very focused on all technology … because everything is very analytical in our respect. (President, Financial Firm)

On the other hand, those who saw the KMS in the wider context of an organizational KM practice expressed abstract desires, drawing on the promised benefits of KM to the organization (e.g., those expressed by Wiig [41]; listed in Section 2.3). These are illustrated by the quotes in Table 2. Specifically, respondents expressed the desire for outcomes such as better decision making, competitive advantage, market share, or a knowledge sharing culture.

## 4.3. Translating desires

The interviews provided very limited examples of concrete desires — product attributes perceived as necessary to attain higher level values. Specifically, the attribute ‘artificial intelligence’ was spontaneously brought up in this context by three respondents. Nevertheless, it was only loosely tied to a higher level value, specifically, business intelligence.

If it could be intelligent [it] would be great, as opposed to having the information manipulated by the user, so it becomes sort of artificial intelligence. (Vice President of Business Development, Service Firm)

What it needs would be an intelligent agent that could take that data or my spec … and would come [up] with not twenty-five forms that could work, but two that definitely will work. (Senior Vice President Sales and Marketing, Manufacturing Firm)

Other than these instances, there were no stated links between higher level values and concrete desires for product attributes to support these values. Thus, overall abstract desires did not translate well to product attributes, or lead to the formation of concrete desires that could be easily confirmed or disconfirmed upon the implementation of the system.

Summarizing the above three sections, we find that expectations and desires concerning KMS differ in their subject matter and in their formation. Expectations can be based on past experiences with IS in general; abstract desires require an understanding of the business domain of the technology (in this case, of KM); and concrete desires require a deep understanding of the role of each attribute of the technology and its impact on the organization. Linking this information back to the challenges identified at the beginning of this paper, there does not appear to be a difficulty in forming realistic expectations concerning the KMS. Similarly, abstract desires seem to emerge on topics relevant to the implementation and expected benefits from the system. However, concrete desires (desired product attributes) are largely missing.

The above findings are important. First, they confirm our suspicion that the poorly defined boundary between the practice (KM) and the technology (KMS) can lead to desires beyond the capabilities of the system itself. Practically this implies the need to better communicate the capabilities of the technology alone. More importantly, however, the above findings indicate an inherent difficulty of stakeholders to translate their desires into concrete product attributes. While we find a strong basis for expectation formation and a strong basis for abstract desires formation, stakeholders have no ‘anchor’ for concrete desires. There is no guiding information, such as the related managerial practice or past experience with similar products, to support the translation of desires into attributes. In practicality, this finding highlights the crucial role of a third party (such as the system’s analyst) in communicating and supporting the translation of abstract desires into concrete product attributes. We will further elaborate on this need in the final section of the paper.

Next, we examine the effects of expectations and desires’ disconfirmation, building on the prediction of the ECT.

## 4.4. Effect of expectations and desires

Another important insight obtained through the interviews concerns the impact of expectations and desires on KMS perceptions. ECT posits that consumers simultaneously evaluate the disconfirmation between perceived performance of the product and their initial expectations and desires ([7,32]; also recall Fig. 1). The theory further posits that the combined effect of expectations and desires disconfirmation leads to product satisfaction or dissatisfaction. For example, negative disconfirmation will lead to dissatisfaction. As the theory posits, our findings indicate that respondents indeed evaluate the confirmation between expectations and perceived performance, but there was no evidence of such evaluation with respect to desires occurring at the same time. More importantly, in contrast to what is predicted by the theory, our findings show that the negative disconfirmation of expectations (i.e., the7 product did not meet expectations) does not necessarily lead to overall dissatisfaction with the KMS. We attribute this effect to the long term impact of desires, as explained below.

First, we note that interview data on expectations alone largely support ECT and show that respondents do compare expectations to perceived performance in evaluating the system (positive or negative disconfirmation of expectations was brought up in 15 of the interviews):

We think we’re getting good metrics. We think we’re getting good tracking. We don’t think we’re getting the multimedia capabilities and report generation that we should be getting as management. And I don’t know that necessarily, at the end of the day, whether the price of the system can really be justified for what we got, from what we were typically expecting to get … does it have any of the other features that we would expect from a knowledge-based system? The jury is still out. (President, Financial Firm)

Obviously if it doesn’t deliver what the expectations are, there is going to be dissatisfaction. I think a lot of people get caught up in the hype. Most companies have not seen many information systems — they have what they have, so there’s always this burning desire to get off what they have and get something that looks better. Here people would be big time into a new system because they understand the deficiencies in the old system, but only until they migrate and they go “Geez, oh geez, this is actually worse, because I at least used to be able to get the data, but I can’t even get that here. Well this is of no use to me. But they promised me I was going to get that…” (Director of Finance, Food Services Firm)

Well obviously if what the sales people are telling me really doesn’t do it, that would be very upsetting to me; that would be a failure. (COO, Service)

However, contrary to the literature on the role of desires (e.g., [7]), interview data do not provide evidence that desires are used in the formation of immediate (short term) satisfaction from the KMS. None of the respondents brought up desires in this context. We do find, however, a strong indication that desires play an important role in forming long term satisfaction, as we explain below.

The ECT model posits that the disconfirmation of expectations leads to dissatisfaction. In addition, people are not likely to be satisfied when negative expectations are confirmed (e.g., when the product was not expected to work properly and it is – in fact – not working properly). Contrary to this prediction, during the interview process we revealed a somewhat different outcome of disconfirmation. In some cases where expectations were disconfirmed (e.g., “it took longer than expected”) and in cases were negative expectations were confirmed (e.g., “I expected such a long and complex implementation”) respondents did not express dissatisfaction with the system, but rather presented an optimistic view for the future. Indeed, one of the strongest themes identified in the interviews was the optimistic view towards the system, rooted in the future confirmation of abstract desires. (Optimism was identified in seven of the nine interviews indicating negative disconfirmation.)

We’re going to be able to have them access a program that will be able to help them out right at the bedside and be able to look up best practices so we’re on that track and we’re going down that road but we’re not there yet. (COO, Service)

Well, I guess my perception is, yes they can be successful, but it’s very labour-intensive and very time intensive. (Vice President of Business Development, Manufacturing/Software Firm)

It’s doing what we asked it to do but we’re missing the boat on its potential right now, so that [is] actually something I’m looking into. (Associate Director of E. Business, Financial Firm)

We’re still too early to evaluate whether or not the million dollars worth of savings are real, but I honestly believe it is there. (CEO, Transport)

Where we are today and where we expect to be? I honestly think we’ll be a four.<sup>8</sup> (CEO, Distribution Firm)

We’re still at a two<sup>8</sup> … I’m going to say in about three months time we’re going to be at the three and four. We know that because we feel that the system is good; it will do what we’re looking for so in other words it meets our demands… and I think the big gap is just purely education/training (Director of Sales, Manufacturing).

You believe that this is going to help you within your research to do things faster, then you have to be able to measure the time along the timeline — and that is a long, long time to measure, to take a product to market takes 10–15 years, to be able to get a product through research etc., so it’s not a quick hit return that you’re going to be able to see. (Vice President Marketing, Pharmaceutical)

Thus, somewhat contrary to what is predicted by ECT, we find that the disconfirmation of expectations, or the confirmation of negative expectations, may only have a temporary negative effect on satisfaction. Interviewees – likely building on the potential confirmation of their abstract desires – express optimism towards the system, being more tolerant towards disconfirmed expectations. We address this insight, along with previous ones, further in the next section, and propose a temporal view of expectations and desires as antecedents of satisfaction.

## 5. Discussion

In the introduction to this paper, we highlighted three challenges to understanding KMS users’ expectations, desires, and ultimately satisfaction. We noted that the formation of accurate and realistic expectations and desires might be difficult; that individuals may have difficulties linking specific product attributes with desired organizational outcomes; and that the effect of expectations and desires on satisfaction may be more sequential than simultaneous. Our interviews confirmed two of these three challenges. We have shown that expectations appear to be somewhat generic and easily formed based on prior experience in other organizations, with other organizational information systems, or by word of mouth. Thus, managers are able to generate realistic expectations with respect to the KMS. Similarly, abstract desires were generated based on the overall understanding of KM as a practice and how technology is incorporated within the KM initiative. A problem, however, arose with respect to concrete desires. We thus propose the following with respect to the formation of expectations and desires:

Proposition 1. Expectations from KMS are formed based on past experiences or external information.

Proposition 2. Abstract desires are formed based on the perceptions of outcomes from the business practice which the technology is intended to support.

As we anticipated, abstract desires were not easily translatable into concrete (product level) desires as this translation process required a deep understanding of the product and the specific attributes that could lead to the realization of abstract desires. Since KMS attributes and capabilities are often not clear to stakeholders (as discussed in Section 2) concrete desires are particularly difficult to form in the context of KMS. Because concrete desires are important for satisfaction, organizations should put much effort into clarifying the capabilities of the specific KMS and supporting stakeholders in linking abstract and concrete desires. Consequently we propose:

Proposition 3. The capability of stakeholders to form concrete desires is limited by the complexity and novelty of many KMS.

Proposition 4. The organization can impact satisfaction from the KMS by supporting the conversion of abstract into concrete desires.

Finally, our findings show that expectations and desires are not necessarily formed at the same time, nor do they necessarily work in tandem to affect satisfaction. While expectations disconfirmation can be evaluated immediately after the purchase and implementation of the system, disconfirmation of abstract desires is more difficult to evaluate. As shown in Table 2, abstract desires often concern business outcomes such as competitive advantage or employee retention. Such outcomes can only be confirmed or disconfirmed in the long run. Thus, we argue:

Proposition 5. Expectations and abstract desires are time dependent, with expectations affecting satisfaction in the short run and abstract desires having more futureoriented satisfaction effects.

This distinction can account for the existence of short-term satisfaction even when expectations are disconfirmed. Satisfaction in this case is forward looking, relying on the confirmation of abstract desires in the long run.

The last proposition is where our work most differs from prior ECT studies in the IS context. Several prior IS studies focusing on desires have appropriated the model proposed by Spreng et al. [32], which assumes that abstract desires can be accurately translated into concrete desires (i.e., desired product attributes). Consequently, both expectations and desires can be evaluated vis-à-vis perceived product performance at the time of purchase (or initial use). In contrast, we have demonstrated that, in the context of KMS, the translation of abstract desires into concrete desires is challenging, at best. Thus, at the time of purchase, individuals may only be able to evaluate expectations. The time lag involved with realizing abstract desires leads to their evaluation at a later point in time. As a result, satisfaction will initially form based on expectations (dis)confirmation and will be later modified based on desires (dis)confirmation. Thus, unlike consumption goods which can be immediately evaluated, KMS assessments are formed over a longer period of time.

## 6. Summary

The insights obtained in this paper have important theoretical contributions both to the KMS literature and to the general literature on information systems satisfaction. These results supplement the literature focusing on expectations as opposed to desires and clarify these two terms and the relationship between them, in the context of KMS.

In addition to this theoretical contribution, this paper also provides important contributions for practitioners. Specifically, we highlight that the inability of business executives to translate abstract desires to concrete ones challenges the applicability of the ECT model. We propose an alternative, time oriented, approach. Nevertheless, organizations can overcome this challenge by providing more support to the desires translation process.
