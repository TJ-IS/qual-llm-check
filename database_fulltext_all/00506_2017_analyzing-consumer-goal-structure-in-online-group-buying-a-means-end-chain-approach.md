## 1. Introduction

## A B S T R A C T

Given the enormous growth and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impacts of group buying on Internet business marketplaces, this study aims to understand consumer goal structure in online group buying (OGB) contexts using a means-end chain approach. Using the laddering interview technique, 52 OGB consumers were interviewed. From the interview data, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 27 goals consumers pursued and their goal striving paths that are summarized in the hierarchical goal structure. This study has the potential to make signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions to both information systems research and e-commerce by utilizing a different approach to explain consumer technology adoption behavior.

Online group buying (OGB) is considered an innovative ecommerce model that aggregates the buyers’ power, allowing them to obtain lower prices [56,110]. With the initial introduction of “Groupon” in the USA, OGB has experienced rapid growth and evolved into a massive e-commerce market worldwide, especially in China. According to a recent report from the China Network Information Center (CNNIC) [16], the number of users in the OGB market in China reached 180.22 million by the end of 2015 and accounted for 26.2% of the netizens. Despite the rapid growth of the market and the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts OGB businesses have gained, surviving in this market remains a challenge [56]. In China, more than 4670 OGB websites have shut down to date and the mortality rate is up to 75% as consumers have more choices among the numerous OGB websites available [120]. These circumstances raise the need for a solution that not only attracts consumers in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place but also retains them, enabling them to eventually engage and create value. To develop such a solution, however, it is indispensable to understand the goals consumers pursue when they visit the OGB websites as it is widely acknowledged that individual’s behavior is strongly affected by a person’s goals to action [84]; moreover, OGB consumers are goal directed, and their desire to achieve their speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals goes some way in explaining their technology use behavior [31,74,114]. In this regard, a goal is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the aim or the desired outcome of an action [84] and is used to guide people’s behavior. Thus, understanding these goals would be a useful framework for OGB businesses to understand why consumers use one OGB website over another and what consumers want to achieve with OGB behavior.

Despite the relevance and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of understanding consumers’ goals to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled when visiting OGB websites, researchers have paid little attention to this critical phenomenon, and a few signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gaps remain to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. First, although an increasing number of studies has paid great attention to understand the various factors leading to consumers’ purchase or repurchase intention in OGB contexts to enhance our understanding of OGB consumer behavior, most of them are mainly quantitative in nature and utilize traditional user-adoption theories such as the technology acceptance model (TAM), theory of planned behavior (TPB), and DeLone and McLean information systems (IS) success model [40,61,109], with the focus of understanding what IS (in this case the OGB website) can offer to the customers rather than identifying what customers really want from this technology use behavior. This resulted in fragmented and inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings about factors that are in<sup>fl</sup>uential in OGB consumer behavior. Consequently, it becomes dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to deduce generalizable <sup>fi</sup>ndings because of mixed and fragmented results [81]. Given that a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant feature of the OGB is its emphasis on “customer-driven commerce” [114], researchers [60,109] called for studies to explore OGB phenomenon from a user perspective. However, few studies have incorporated all or most explanatory variables to explain what goals consumers strive to attain in OGB behavior. In particular, given that much of the consumer behavior is goal directed, a more systematic framework, generated from a user perspective, regarding OGB consumer goals to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled when visiting the websites is timely and critical for OGB businesses to understand what customers’ requirements are and how to meet their needs to gain competitive advantage in emarketplaces.

Another disadvantage of these traditional user-adoption approaches in OGB research lies in their limited explanation and understanding of OGB consumer behavior and whether these identi<sup>fi</sup>ed factors are organized in hierarchical or other structures [6]. Researchers [34,84] have argued that multiple goals exist to provide the primary motivating and directing factors for consumer behavior, and these goals are interrelated and organized in hierarchies to facilitate their accomplishment. Only by identifying goal hierarchies can we uncover the theoretical mechanism of OGB user goal-satisfying process and develop an understanding of the reasons behind their behavior and the relative importance of each goal, thus enabling businesses to offer corresponding operational strategies to satisfy goals along these paths. Despite the certainty of the concept of a goal hierarchy in consumer behavior [84], few attempts have been undertaken to examine the underlying mechanisms OGB consumers follow in striving to achieve their goals, although some studies focused on the OGB user motivations but treated motivations in isolation [e.g.,59,113,114]. Consequently, we have little knowledge on not only WHAT OGB consumers want but also WHY they want them and HOW we should offer to meet their goals. As Pervin [83,p.475] states, “attention must be given to how goals become interrelated and organized in some hierarchical fashion” to understand the complex consumer behavior goal systems and develop good plans and strategies to gratify consumer goals.

To address these gaps, we adopted a goal-directed approach with the focus on what OGB users want rather than what technology (OGB website in this case) can offer to uncover the goals consumers pursue in OGB behavior and explore the goalstriving paths that explain the relative importance of each goal and elucidate consumer OGB goal ful<sup>fi</sup>llment behaviors. To achieve these objectives, we employed the means-end chain (MEC) theory, which elicits and places goals within a structure that includes a hierarchical system of interrelated goals [84]. This hierarchical goal structure, which is a summary outcome of the goal attainment paths derived from the analysis, provides richer information and offers more nuanced understandings of consumer goal-striving process. By inspecting the hierarchical goal structure, the different goal attainment paths and the relative importance of the goals can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. By uncovering this information, OGB website providers can not only foresee the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of each goal but also more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently allocate the resources when designing the OGB websites and making Internet-based marketing strategies.

This study has the potential to contribute to both emerging OGB literature and general e-commerce literature in a number of ways. First, it contributes to the current knowledge of OGB by using a systematic method to identify a comprehensive and coherent set of multiple-level goals that in<sup>fl</sup>uence OGB consumer behavior and uncover the relative importance of them, thereby expanding our understanding of the phenomenon of interest. Second, by integrating MEC with the hierarchical goal theory, we identify the cognitive goal attainment paths, which uncovers the theoretical mechanisms through which consumer goals are ful<sup>fi</sup>lled step by step, therefore deepening our understanding of consumer technology use behavior [82]. Third, it demonstrates that using a user-goal oriented approach can be a novel approach for exploring consumer’s technology adoption behavior in IS, in particular in e-commerce research, providing a very different but insightful lens for future research on understanding consumer technology adoption behavior.

We begin the paper with an overview of OGB, followed by a description of the goal-directed hierarchical approach and the MEC theory, which provides the theoretical foundation for the study. Next, we describe the sampling and data collection procedure. The hierarchical goal structure derived from data analysis is described in detail and interpreted in the results and discussion sections that follow. Finally, the implications of goal-directed consumer behavior in OGB highlighted by the structure are discussed, including implications from both theory and practice perspectives.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. OGB

The core concept of OGB is to offer cheap services or products by leveraging the buying power of individual consumers as a group [61]. The Groupon mode business has been introduced and widely adopted in many countries worldwide [69], particularly in China. According to the recent report, the OGB sales in China alone reached 74.75 billion Yuan (11.5 billion US dollars) in 2014, an increase of 108.3% compared to that in 2013 [15]. The users of OGB reached 180.22 million in 2015, an increase of 21.3% compared to that in 2014 in China [15].

The operation of OGB is simple: companies (i.e., products services suppliers) that aim to promote their products/services apply to OGB website providers; if they agree to cooperate, the OGB website providers announce the campaign via their website, including the actual price, discount rate, discounted price, number of minimum required buyers, transaction due time, and supplier information. Consumers interested in the deals pay in advance via the OGB website and receive the products/services from the supplier at a later date. OGB is different from traditional ecommerce in several respects [56]. First, compared with traditional e-commerce, OGB often offers a limited range of product types [69]. The service products such as restaurant coupons, entertainment-related coupons, and hotel coupons are the top products purchased in OGB [15]. Second, OGB websites typically adopt the discount business model and provide buyers with greater bargaining power so that they can purchase the products/services at a lower price [45]. Third, compared with traditional ecommerce, an OGB transaction has a time limitation for the products/services to be transacted [14]. Finally, the suppliers may set restrictions for consumption, for instance, that service is not available on public holidays.

With the booming of OGB worldwide, researchers started to pay attention to this new e-commerce model and took multiple theoretical perspectives for conceptualizing and explaining the use of OGB. These studies generally fall into three categories. The <sup>fi</sup>rst category of studies focused on examining technical factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing consumer OGB behavior from a theoretical perspective of TAM and the DeLone & McLean IS success model. Factors of perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and website quality were con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in<sup>fl</sup>uential in these studies [e.g.,40,61,104,109]. The second category of studies focused on social and psychological factors from a theoretical perspective of trust, theory of reasoned action, TPB, social capital theory, and social comparison. Factors such as trust, commitment, relational embeddedness, and social interaction tie were empirically examined [e.g.,13,14,96,114,115]. The third category emphasized transaction-related factors such as perceived price fairness, participation volume, Internet advertising, prior purchase experience, and satisfaction [e.g.,60,95,118], where theories of transaction cost economics, expectationcon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, and social identity were adopted.

Despite the contributions of these studies from different theoretical perspectives in explaining consumer behavior in OGB, there is a lack of studies investigating user behavior from a holistic, comprehensive, and integrative perspective. Wang et al. [110] recognized this and incorporated factors from technical, transactional, social, and psychological perspective. However, their contribution is still limited by examining limited number of factors from the e-commerce success model and commitment-trust theory perspective. Moreover, most of the extant studies focused on how various factors can in<sup>fl</sup>uence online group buyers rather than how online group buyers use OGB to pursue their various goals. Given the popularity and competitive intensity of OGB, it is important to look beyond customer’s adoption of OGB to explore what are OGB consumers’ goals to be grati<sup>fi</sup>ed. For instance, one key feature of OGB is the impulse purchasing behavior, which is ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed in the OGB context compared to traditional e-commerce applications as the majority of the deals on the OGB websites are available for a limited time, which only makes consumers more impulsive in participating in the buying event. It seems that lower price is the key motivation for consumers to be impulsive in OGB [e.g.,69,119]. However, research shows that low price is not the only goal consumers pursue in OGB, and impulsive shopping behavior is not sustainable in the long run and eventually harmful for the retailers as consumers may return purchased items or engage in negative word-of-mouth when they realize that the products/ services fail to meet their goals [56]. Thus, understanding the various goals online group buyers pursue from user-oriented perspective would offer more sustainable and practical value.

Moreover, understanding the various consumer goals is essential but not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for practitioners as OGB businesses can only serve their customers well if they know not only what customers want but also how to offer what they want and why they want what they want, i.e., the paths leading to the ultimate goals. This type of research is even scarce in e-commerce contexts, though a large number of studies have provided motivations of consumer online shopping behavior [e.g.,4,11]. These motivation studies considered online shopping motives in isolation, with no relationship being identi<sup>fi</sup>ed among each other. Given motivations are context speci<sup>fi</sup>c and hierarchical in nature and as OGB is different compared to other e-commerce applications, this deeper level of understanding is fundamental to our understanding of the drivers of OGB adoption. By employing a user-oriented goal hierarchy approach, our study adds additional insights into this phenomenon by not only identifying diverse consumer goals for this new e-commerce model adoption but also illustrating the goal ful<sup>fi</sup>llment paths. Such insights into consumer behavior and consumer needs would give businesses huge advantages in providing customer-centric service, the key to attract new and retain existing customers.

## 2.2. A hierarchical conceptualization of goal-directed OGB behavior

A goal is a desired outcome of an action [37]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a goal can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “a mental image or other end point representation associated with affect toward which action may be directed” [83,p.474]. According to this de<sup>fi</sup>nition, goal provides the primary motivational and directional factor for consumer behavior [34]. It in<sup>fl</sup>uences the direction of behavior by expressing what people are trying to accomplish and, in a broader sense, how they are planning to attain the goal in question and why they are pursuing the chosen course of action in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place. This goal-directed behavior model has been widely used to explain consumer behavior [84].

Drawing upon theoretical perspectives from psychology, including personal construct theory, human values, human personality, and cognitive structure [49,92], Pieters et al. [84] argue that a more useful way of studying goal-directed consumer behavior is to view it as a hierarchical model of action to facilitate accomplishment. They argue that a hierarchy of goal-directed behavior comprises three levels, corresponding to the how, what, and why of behavior, in terms of a hierarchical structure of increasingly more abstract goals [84]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this goal hierarchy assumes that goals below the focal level (sub-ordinate goals) serve as means by dealing mostly with the operational and more concrete aspects of attaining the focal-level goals (the how of behavior), while goals above the focal level (super-ordinate goals) provide the motives or reasons for pursuing a course of action (the why of behavior) [84]. Goals at some level can be further broken down into a series of sub-goals that have to be attained to reach goals at higher levels [84]. Thus, a goal hierarchy is considered as a means-end structure of sequences of sub-ordinate and superordinate goals [84], arranged by level of abstractness from the lowest to the highest [6]. The goal hierarchical approach is widely accepted in various <sup>fi</sup>elds, such as marketing [84], psychology [5], and, most recently, IS to explore consumer/user online behavior, such as in virtual worlds [47], social networking sites [79], and estores [74].

OGB consumer behaviors are goal directed, driven by multiplelevel goals organized in a hierarchy [107]. Thus, to understand OGB user behavior, a good place to start is to identify their goals to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled in this behavior. Understanding the hierarchical structure of OGB user goals would provide much deeper insights into consumers’ subjective understanding of their behavior and indicate both the means by which something can be achieved and the ends to which it is ultimately directed [84]. Despite the necessity for a better understanding of OGB user behavior across multiple levels of goals, few studies have incorporated more than one or two levels of goals into a theoretical framework in OGB research. To achieve this objective, the MEC approach, which presumes that consumer behavior is goal directed and the goal structure can be deemed as MECs, is adopted in this study.

## 2.3. MEC approach and consumer goal hierarchy

Using the expectancy-value theory, Gutman [32] developed the MEC theory to understand the consumer decision-making process by connecting products/services attributes, the consequences of using products/services, and personal goals or values achieved by the use of that product/service [88]. Attributes represent the observable or perceived characteristics of a product/service, consequences are the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts attained by the speci<sup>fi</sup>c attributes, and values imply highly abstract motivations that guide usage behavior [87]. The major assumption of this theory is that consumers are likely to select products/services that are more relevant for achieving their personal values. In other words, people do not use products/services for the sake of the products/services but for the positive consequences (bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) that their consumption can provide, which is, in turn, important for the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of their personal values [18]. Thus, MEC focuses on the cognitive linkages between the attributes of product/service (the “means”), the consequences these attributes provide to the consumers, and the personal values (the “ends”) these consequences help reinforce [88]. The model is hierarchical in nature as it connects the consumer’s end-state values to the basic attributes of products/ services representing the means [32]. The result of a MEC analysis is a hierarchical value map called MECs, showing the salient linkages between attributes, consequences, and values of consumers of the products/services studied [84]. In any MEC model, we would expect many “means” and few “ends” as the marketplace is full of many more objects than consumers have values [32].

Olson and Reynolds [77] modi<sup>fi</sup>ed Gutman’s [32] model by distinguishing <sup>fi</sup>ner gradation of attributes, consequences, and values according to the levels of abstraction in terms of its relationships to personal values, as shown in Fig. 1. Concrete attributes are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as being physical and concrete characteristics of a product/service that can be directly measured, such as the location of a store or color of a product. Abstract attributes are more subjective in nature, usually existing only in the minds of those who perceive the product/service, such as information quality of the website or service quality [77]. Functional consequences refer to the practical bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and performance outputs accrued directly to the consumers from consuming the products/service (e.g., save money), while psychosocial consequences refer to psychological feelings or social considerations (e.g., perceived risk, satisfaction) [20] derived from functional consequences. Instrumental values are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as preferred modes of behavior or ways of behaving (e.g., be loyal, be polite), while terminal values are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as preferred end states of being (e.g., self-esteem, sense of ful<sup>fi</sup>llment) [85,90]. Thus, the MEC model is also viewed as representing the relationship between self and products, in which the means represent aspects of product/service knowledge and the ends represent aspects of consumer selfknowledge [108]. Olson and Reynolds [77] emphasize that not all of these links are present in every situation as consumers may not be able to always possess the abstract meanings of the products/ services they use because of inactivation of self-relevant knowledge in the working memory during that decision situation [108].

Overall, the central idea behind MEC is that consumers are goaldirected decision-makers, choosing products/services that seem most likely to lead to the desired outcomes [18,87]. The attribute– consequence–value hierarchy explains that it is not only possible to uncover what are the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts required by consumers for purchasing/consuming a product/service but also how to provide these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (through attributes) and why these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are important for consumers (the values) [78]. This basic idea is quite similar to the notion of hierarchical structure of goal-directed behavior, in which goals at lower levels in the hierarchy serve as means to achieve higher level goals as ends, with speci<sup>fi</sup>c three levels of how, what, and why of consumer behaviors [84]. Thus, the notion of MEC theory has been extended to the domain of goaldirected consumer behavior [84] and has been adopted widely in various disciplines to study consumer goal-directed behavior [35]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the elements in a MEC can be considered to be elements in a goal hierarchy [34]. When the super-ordinate-level goals are at value level, relevant attributes and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be seen to play a role analogous to that of the goal hierarchy’s sub-ordinate and focal-level goals [20,84].

In the IS discipline, researchers have used MEC approach to elicit user goals for information technologies adoption intention and behavior. For instance, a study by Zumpe and Vander Heijden [117] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed user goals for e-Bay’s system, which not only referred to the key functions, i.e., bidding for a product and buying a product, but also included more high-level goals as end goals, e.g., enjoy using the product or bene<sup>fi</sup>t from its usage. Jung and Kang [47] applied MEC to investigate user goals in social virtual worlds and developed a hierarchical system of interrelated goals. Lin and Lin [68] examined the goal value sought by players of massively multiplayer online role playing games through MEC. Matook [74] investigated user goal MECs for the e-Bay online auction system and produced its structure as a goal network. Jung and Pawlowski [48] used MEC to understand consumer goals for virtual consumption in social virtual worlds. Collectively, these studies demonstrated the power of MEC in identifying user technology’s use of various level goals and their interrelationships.

As a powerful method to provide meaningful results and valuable insights, like other researchers who adopted MEC approach to study consumer goal-directed behavior, we also extend the notion of the MEC theory to study OGB consumer behavior. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this study adopted a similar notion of the six-layer model of MEC to understand OGB consumer goals to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled, considering each level goal can also be sub-divided into two sub-categories [35]. It is in this way that consumer goals are said to be hierarchically related sets of elements across levels of abstractness, with goals at lower levels serving as means to achieve goals at higher levels as ends [84]. Labels for each of the six-layer goals are adopted from previous studies [35,77]. The framework of such an interrelationship could provide a guide for understanding why OGB consumers select (or reject) speci<sup>fi</sup>c websites and help direct actions taken to motivate them to use that website to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their personal goals. ## 3. Research methodology

To generate goals and goal attainment paths in OGB contexts, the laddering technique, which is associated with MEC, is utilized and introduced in this section. A pilot study was conducted to test the laddering interview technique. Samples used for data collection, data collection procedure, and data analysis techniques are described in this section. Findings are provided in Section 4.

## 3.1. Laddering technique

Developed by Hinkle [39], laddering is “an in-depth, one-to-one interviewing technique used to develop an understanding of how consumers translate the attributes of products/services into meaningful associations with respect to self” [87,p.12]. It allows researchers to dig below consumers’ surface knowledge of the perceived product/service attributes and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, to the underlying beliefs and values motivating behavior [78]. Thus, its purpose is to reveal people’s underlying motives for choosing a particular product/service over another and paths connecting these motives at different layers [78].

One bene<sup>fi</sup>t of using the laddering technique in this study is that laddering is a method that avoids the use of a priori adoption of a theoretical framework and hence is a less biased approach to research [87]. In addition, this technique allows participants to express their views in their own words and yet, due to its systematic nature, allows researchers to probe deeper into the responses to derive consumers’ higher level goals [78]. In this section, the laddering interview technique is brie<sup>fl</sup>y introduced; then data analysis techniques are brie<sup>fl</sup>y outlined in Section 3.5.

In the MEC approach, the laddering interview is used to collect data required for the hierarchy model development. It commences with eliciting distinctions, which are the operational attributes and can be used as the bases for eliciting the respondents’ higher level bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and values [87]. The elicitation procedure is important for the outcome of a laddering study as it determines the relevance of the means-end structure to be extracted from subjects. The <sup>fi</sup>ve speci<sup>fi</sup>c methods for eliciting distinctions in laddering interviews, as summarized in Appendix A, include direct elicitation, free sorting, picking from an attribute list, ranking, and triad sorting, with different strengths and weakness for each method [7,87]. It is suggested that the interview outline generally includes at least two distinct methods of eliciting distinctions to make sure that no key distinctions are overlooked [87].

The distinctions obtained at the elicitation stage will then be used for probing to obtain higher level bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and values imbedded in consumer memory [87]. The interviewee is continuously probed with a variant of the questions such as “why is that important to you?” [78,87]. These questions were designed to provide gradual stimulation to help participants recall their memory so that participants could start with concrete attributes as the basis for exploring the higher level bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and values buried deep in their minds [78]. In the interviews, participants could freely describe their experiences, and the interviewer would keep the breadth of the interview in check until respondents utter “that’s it” or “I don’t know” or is unable to answer [78].

## 3.2. Pilot study

A pilot study was conducted before the main data collection for two purposes: (1) to test the elicitation techniques and select the most appropriate ones for data collection in the OGB context and (2) to identify the potential problems emerging during the interview process and adjust the research design. A sample of nine college students with OGB experience participated in the pilot study.

Among the <sup>fi</sup>ve interview techniques, we ignored the technique of picking from an existing attribute list from the literature for two reasons. First, we aim to explore consumer goals from a useroriented approach, which can con<sup>fi</sup>rm <sup>fi</sup>ndings in literature on the one hand and uncover new goals not veri<sup>fi</sup>ed in the literature on the other hand. Second, most of the factors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in OGB literature (e.g., perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, satisfaction, trust) are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed depending on IS adoption literature rather than a user-oriented approach, making it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to attain a truly comprehensive list of multiple-level goals to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled in OGB behavior from a user perspective. Thus, picking from an attribute list was considered inappropriate for this exploratory study and may compromise the research quality. In addition, as free and triad sorting involve similar sorting procedures and both techniques have similar strengths and weaknesses, triad sorting was adopted. In summary, triad sorting, ranking, and direct elicitation were used to elicit distinctions in the pilot study. Each of these three techniques was used in three interviews.

Preliminary pilot study data analysis showed that ranking and direct elicitation were more suitable to elicit the distinctions of this study as it can elicit more reasonable distinctions for the further probing process. The resultant distinctions were also more meaningful and distinctive. In addition, it was found that some participants could not clearly remember the corresponding attributes of speci<sup>fi</sup>c OGB websites when they were asked to rank or sort the websites. ## 3.3. Sample

The research participants were people who had purchased products/services through OGB websites in China. To recruit the subjects for interviews, announcements were posted on the bulletin board system of a few well-known OGB websites (www.dianping.com,www.bbs.tuan800.com) with the selection criteria listed, which included using at least four OGB websites and had used OGB for more than half a year. The potential participants who met the selection criteria and were interested in this study contacted the researcher through short message service (SMS) or online chatting tool (QQ). After con<sup>fi</sup>rming their OGB use experience, participants who were located in Shanghai and agreed to attend a face-to-face interview were selected. The time schedule and location for the interview were then discussed with the participants through the pre-interview communication. In total, 58 consumers were interviewed by considering that a pool of 50–60 participants could generate enough information in previous studies using MEC [87]. Fifty-two valid interviews out of 58 were considered for data analysis (four interviewees left during interview process and two interviewees were not able to express his/her abstract thoughts clearly).

Gender distribution was random, but the total sample resulted in 13 (25%) males and 39 (75%) females, which seems biased. However, this sample distribution can represent the sample characteristics of the online group buyers in China as we found that our sample distribution was similar to that in recent studies [41,110] in the OGB context in China. With 38.46% of respondents aged between 25 and 30, and 30.77% of them aged between 19 and 24, we found that the respondents were relatively young. In terms of education, 61.54% of them had a bachelor’s degree, followed by 28.85% who had attended college and 7.69% who had a postgraduate degree or above. Moreover, 34.62% of respondents monthly income was between 3001 and 5000 RMB (461 to 769 US dollar) and 30.77% of them had monthly income between 5001 and 8000 RMB (769 to 1230 US dollar). The most frequently purchased products were restaurant coupons, followed by entertainment products. More than half (69.23%) of them purchased products/ services more than 10 times using OGB in the last calendar year. Lastly, 36.54% of the respondents spent 301 to 500 RMB (46 to 77 US dollar), 26.92% spent between 501 and 1000 RMB (77 to 154 US dollar), and 25% spent between 101 and 300 RMB (15 to 46 US dollar) on OGB platforms each month.

## 3.4. Data collection procedure

All interviews were performed in a Café where the participants felt comfortable. Following Fig. 3, after step 1 was completed, the actual laddering interviews commenced, which lasted approximately 40–90 min. First, the frequently used OGB websites listed by participants were recorded on small cards that were presented to the participants. They were then asked to rank all OGB websites according to their preferences [87,105]. After ranking, they were asked “why do you prefer the <sup>fi</sup>rst website to the second one?” Normally, participants provided reasons for their preferred website with the unique features of the websites [87]. For instance, the preferred website had more product choices or provided more speci<sup>fi</sup>c product information. After receiving the answer, the interviewer proceeded with the question “why do you prefer the second website to the third one?” This question was repeated until respondents could not give further reasons. After obtaining operational-level goals using the ranking method, a direct elicitation method was utilized as a supplementary elicitation method to ensure that no key element was overlooked. Questions such as “what are the attributes or features of the website that appeal to you?” were asked [7,87]. At the end of this stage, a list of distinctions was obtained from each participant regarding his/her OGB use operational-level goals.

From the initial distinctions obtained earlier, the ladders began to form. Each distinction was presented individually to the participants, where the researcher discussed the reasons of preferences pointed out by the interviewee with probes in the form similar to “why is that important to you?” to enable the achievement of a higher degree of abstraction by the interviewee [87]. ## 3.5.1. Content analysis

## 3.5.2. Construction of summary implication matrix

The summary implication matrix (SIM) is used to summarize the connections between factors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in content analysis. It displays the number of times each factor leads to other factors for the respondent. It is a square matrix, Z, based on aggregation across respondents, in which the factors $( Z _ { \mathrm { i j } } )$ re<sup>fl</sup>ect how often factor i leads to factor j [87]. The SIM successfully transfers the qualitative data from interviews into quantitative data [87]. It summarizes all linkages among factors in different layers. By inspecting the SIM, it is possible to obtain information about not only which pairs of factors have strong linkages but also how many times each factor can lead to other factors in total or has been reached by other factors [87]. More detailed and sample description regarding how SIM was generated for this study is provided in Section 4.2.

## 3.5.3. Layers of factors and relative importance of factors

In typical MEC studies, after SIM is generated, factors can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into attributes, consequences, and values, and then chains of attributes consequence values are produced. There is no objective method introduced to classify factors into the layers. To mitigate classi<sup>fi</sup>cation errors, Pieters et al. [84] proposed an approach to determine the layer of factors by comparing the number of times each factor is mentioned as the means versus the ends, named as “abstractness.” Factors with high abstractness scores are predominantly ends, while factors with low abstractness scores are predominantly means [84]. Literature also suggested to refer to de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of attributes, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and values in MEC and refer to the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation used in previous literature [e.g.,68,107].

Moreover, the relative importance of each factor, which indicates the frequency of connection each factor has with other factors, can also be inferred from data in the SIM. When all interrelationships among factors are illustrated in the hierarchical structure, it is important to know which factor plays the most important function in the structural model. To provide this information, Pieters et al. [84] proposed an index of centrality, which represents the degree to which the factor occupies a central role in the structure. The higher the value of centrality, the more frequently the particular factor is involved in the linkages with other factors, meaning the more important the particular factor is. In this study, we calculated each goal abstractness and centrality based on the formulas proposed by Pieters et al. [84].

## 3.5.4. Generating the hierarchical structure of factors

The hierarchical structure of the factors visually illustrates the relationships between factors by showing how they link with each other and is gradually built up by connecting all the chains by considering the linkages in the SIM. By inspecting the hierarchical structure, the different dominant goal attainment paths can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

Usually, the hierarchical structure does not display all the factors and linkages in the SIM. ## 4. Results

Following the data analysis procedure introduced in Section 3.5 above, the results in each step are summarized in the following sections. Given that the <sup>fi</sup>rst two steps are used for step 3 and 4 analysis, we will discuss the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in Sections 4.3 and 4.4 collectively.

## 4.1. Goals identified in content analysis

By design, the “laddering” process allows participants to freely voice their opinions and hence achieve the greatest possible initial construct elicitation effect. As a result, the 52 interviewees produced a total of 250 raw constructs and 613 relationship nodes. Because there was considerable overlap between constructs across all participants, a data reduction process was conducted to consolidate similar constructs [31]. This involves two steps. First, codes that were the expressions of the same underlying idea were combined. For instance, “lower price” was the same as “higher discount” and therefore were combined into the same code of “product/service price.” In the second step, themes with different phrases but clear connections were consolidated and mapped to one unique construct. For example, “convenient to take transportation to go for consumption,” “place and time independence” and “without going out” were mapped to one construct of “access convenience.” In NVivo, when the constructs were combined, the relationship between constructs were combined and adjusted automatically. The consolidation of raw constructs ultimately yielded 94 unique constructs and 326 relationship nodes.

After data reduction, a categorization process was performed on the unique constructs identi<sup>fi</sup>ed to classify them into broader level goals, following the procedure described by Jankowicz [43]. During this process, relevant concepts and measurement items used in prior e-commerce and marketing literature were reviewed to facilitate an accurate and reliable categorization scheme. For instance, from the de<sup>fi</sup>nition and interpretation from the literature about “functional design,” “virtual design,” “security,” and “response time,” they were categorized into one goal dimension and named as “system quality.” In total, 27 goals were produced in this consolidation process, as shown in Table 2, in which goal de<sup>fi</sup>nition, unique constructs constituting the goal, and the number of participants mentioning the unique constructs and goals are provided. Appendix B provides examples for the entire content analysis process.

## 4.3. Layer of goals and relative importance of goals

Table 4 presents the abstractness, which is used to decide which layer each goal belongs to, and the centrality, which is used to determine the relative importance of each goal. From the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation criteria introduced in Section 3.5 and Table 4 results, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 11 sub-ordinate goals, 13 focal goals, and 3 superordinate goals. Of the 11 sub-ordinate goals, which acted as operational attributes used by consumers to reach focal goals, six were more concrete in nature and <sup>fi</sup>ve were more perceptual. The most frequently mentioned sub-ordinate goals were “product assortment” (45), “product price” (45), “information quality” (41), and “service quality” (35). Five of 13 focal goals were functionallevel bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to be sought by consumers to adopt OGB, called immediate goals, while the rest were more psychological and sociological bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, called intermediate goals. Among them, the most frequently mentioned immediate goals were “choice optimization” (48), “trust” (41), “convenience” (40), and “cost saving” (38), while “satisfaction” (42) was the most frequently mentioned intermediate goal. Finally, the three super-ordinate goals, including “loyalty,” “improving life quality,” and “selfactualization,” re<sup>fl</sup>ected both instrumental and terminal-level values, similar to those uncovered in previous personal values research [70].

In terms of the relative importance of goals, Table 4 shows that “product price” and “product assortment” were relatively more important concrete sub-ordinate goals, while “information quality” and “service quality” were relatively more important perceptual sub-ordinate goals with centrality ranging between 0.070 and 0.125, indicating that these elements were important operationallevel goals in attaining OGB consumers’ focal goals. In the focal goal categories, “trust,” “choice optimization,” “cost saving,” and “convenience” were more important immediate goals with higher centrality value, connecting between lower and higher level goals. Then “satisfaction,” with centrality of 0.164, was the most important intermediate goal OGB consumers pursued to reach higher level goals of “loyalty” and “self-actualization.”

## 4.4. Hierarchical goal structure

On the basis of the results in SIM and by referring to the abstractness value of each goal, a hierarchical goal structure can be created. Before creating the structure, the cut-off level was determined <sup>fi</sup>rst by using the sensitivity analysis proposed by Pieters et al. [84]. From the analysis, a cut-off level of 5 was deemed most appropriate in the present case as the hierarchical goal structure accounted for 67.55% of all connections between goals made by subjects using only 29.09% of all possible cells in the SIM. This decision is in agreement with Reynolds and Gutman’s [87] rule-of-thumb: that for a sample size between 50 and 60, the cutoff value may be set between 3 and 5, depending on the situation. This decision also complies with the suggestion that the correct cut-off level decision should make the structure cover at least twothirds of all existing relations in the SIM [87]. Consequently, the element of “entertainment” was removed from the hierarchical goal structure because of weak connections.

## 5. Discussion

This research yielded three useful <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, by utilizing semi-structured laddering interviews, this study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 27 goals embedded in consumer OGB behavior, with some unique goals that were not found in prior traditional e-commerce consumer behavior research. This indicates that context matters when discussing consumer online shopping behaviors, and this research is timely for providing a more comprehensive understanding of OGB goal-directed consumer behaviors. Second, 27 goals identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study were found to be hierarchically structured, spanning across three goal levels, ranging from the operational sub-ordinate level goals to focal goals and then to higher super-ordinate goals, indicating that OGB customers goals are structured hierarchically. Third, this study found that OGB customers were driven not only by “cost saving” but also by some other signi<sup>fi</sup>cant reasons, indicating that OGB consumers have various reasons to shop online, ful<sup>fi</sup>lled through different paths but for similar super-ordinate goals.

## 5.1. Consumer goals to be fulfilled in OGB contexts

Among the 27 goals this study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, we indeed found that a few key factors used in determining business to consumer (B2C) and consumer to consumer (C2C) e-commerce user behavior can also be applied to the OGB context, e.g., “information availability” [19], “product assortment” [4], “service quality” [50], “perceived risk,” “trust” [53,103], and, in particular, “satisfaction,” with the highest centrality index. These results, on the one hand, con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in prior OGB literature [40,41,109] and, on the other hand, demonstrated that although the underlying technologies will continue to evolve and new e-commerce models will emerge in the future, some key fundamental in<sup>fl</sup>uential factors in consumer behavior would always play a key role.

However, given that this study’s focus was to explore consumer goals from a technology user perspective rather than from what technology can do for the users aspect, it is not surprising that this study found some important goals that lacked attention in traditional e-commerce consumer behavior research. For instance, “network externalities,” which are less considered in e-commerce contexts as one of the consumer goals, were found to be important in the OGB context as one of the key operational-level goals (33 participants mentioned it) for both direct, peer, and indirect network externalities aspects. The “network externalities” were initially examined in economic studies [8]. In recent years, this factor has been widely applied to IS research to explain user pre- or postadoption of the information technology [17,21,122]. However, “network externalities” are mainly examined in contexts of computer-mediated communication [63,100], mobile communication [122], and social networking sites [17,66], although a handful of studies found that network externalities also play a role in online auction contexts [1,21,71,102,121]. From the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of this concept and the unique OGB business nature, it is logical that it will impact OGB user behavior, such as when there are more buyers participating in purchasing a deal in OGB, which the individual can sense from transaction volumes on the OGB websites, or more friends and relatives around the individual using OGB, the individual will be more likely to participate in OGB behavior. One interviewee’s comment supported this point:

“...The number of people who have purchased the deals is shown on the group buying websites. If I see that a large number of people have purchased from this website, then I will choose to buy from it... Because I think that everyone will compare the product/service quality and price on different group buying websites. Thus, this website should be the best if others have chosen it. . . . .It also indicates that the others trust this website. I don’t need to worry that I may be cheated. ...I don’t need to waste my time comparing websites anymore” (Participant no. 51).

Another interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding, different from what was found in ecommerce consumer behavior research, was that OGB consumers participated in OGB mainly for utilitarian reasons rather than for hedonic purposes. For instance, when researchers explored retail shopping or online shopping motivations, adventure, diversion, and entertainment value [11,53,76] were all con<sup>fi</sup>rmed to be important motivation factors from a hedonic perspective. However, we only found one goal of “entertainment” to be mentioned by 10 participants in this study. This may be because many OGB websites are simply designed for functional purpose rather than providing diversity interactive features that can offer adventure, excitement, and enjoyment to consumers during the browsing process. Thus, it is unlikely for consumers to use the OGB websites as a channel for hedonic purposes.

Furthermore, this study did <sup>fi</sup>nd that “cost saving” was a very important reason for OGB behavior, mentioned by the third largest number of participants (centrality is 0.100, top 3 among this level goals). This result is also consistent with motivation literature in the OGB context, in which, lower price was proven to be the key motive for consumers to be engaged with OGB [59]. Yang and Mao’s [113] study also demonstrated that price positively correlated with search and purchase intention in the OGB context. However, what was even more interesting was that this study also found that actually “trust” and “choice optimization” were even more important than “cost saving” as the key reasons OGB users perceived when deciding whether they would shop at OGB websites, with higher centrality and more number of participants mentioning it. Although the importance of “trust” in online consumer behavior is well understood, “choice optimization” is seldom discussed in e-commerce research in general, particularly OGB as a motive of consumer behavior. More discussion about the impacts of these factors are presented in Section 5.3.

Finally, we found that consumers utilized OGB to gratify their end goal for respect, self-ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, and sense of accomplishment, with 27 participants mentioning it. Although “self-actualization” was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in traditional e-commerce literature [62] as a consumer goal to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled, it was not found in OGB literature, indicating that through MEC and laddering, we were able to translate OGB websites characteristics and consequences of use into consumer self-relevant values, extending our knowledge about why consumers participated in the OGB behavior in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place.

Collectively, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings support Fulk and Gould’s [24] and Guo et al.’s [30,31] call for taking research contexts and technology features into consideration in technology research as context does matter when examining technology user behaviors. Our results

## 5.2. Hierarchical goal-directed OGB behavior

Further analysis of the laddering interview data showed that while all the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed factors were important goals for OGB consumers to pursue, these goals were actually interrelated and organized in a hierarchical structure. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, these goals were found to span across three different levels: sub-ordinate, focal, and super-ordinate level, each of which played different roles in consumer goal attainment journey. The operational sub-ordinate goals can be further subdivided into concrete and perceptual (e.g., “product price,” “information quality”) served as fundamental means to help consumers realize their focal goals (e.g., immediate goals of “trust” and “information availability” and intermediate goal of “satisfaction”), which in turn led to super-ordinate goals (e.g., instrumental goal of “loyalty” and end goal of “selfactualization”), the end-state in goal-directed behavior. These operational-level goals are important in OGB consumer goal attainment paths because they have an overarching effect on all other higher level goals that are considered as essential, although not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient, to achieve the end goals. “Product price,” playing as a salient factor, echoes with the core concept of OGB business model, leveraging the buying power of the group with a cheap price. Although “product assortment” is widely recognized as an important feature to be incorporated in traditional e-commerce [4,57], it has not been reported in OGB research as one of the key factors. “Information quality” and “service quality,” which were salient factors here, have always been important in determining ecommerce shopping motivations [46,53]. The 13 focal goals were next in the hierarchy and were imperative for bridging the connection between attributes and values layer goals, among which “choice optimization,” “trust,” and “convenience” were quite important as large proportion of connections in the hierarchy ran through these factors. In particular, “choice optimization” was not found as a reason of consumer behavior in previous ecommerce studies. “Satisfaction” was found to be the most important intermediate focal goal, with also the highest frequency at this level. Finally, although “loyalty” is one of the most important variables discussed in e-commerce research as an instrumental goal to be used to attract new and retain existing customers [40,41,118], OGB customer “self-actualization” end goal realized through “satisfaction” mechanism is not reported in OGB. Finally, although “cost saving” is discussed extensively in OGB research as one of the key reasons, its impact on “improving life quality” is not identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior OGB research as a terminal value to guide consumer behaviors.

The only incomplete path we found in Fig. 4 was from “information quality” “information availability” ”online impulsivity.” It not only showed a weak connection between “information availability” and “online impulsivity” but also showed no further connection with any super-ordinate goals, such as “loyalty” or “self-actualization.” This observation supported Kukar-Kinney et al.’s [56] argument that the impulsive buying behavior is not sustainable in the long run in the OGB context as consumers realize that the impulsive buying behavior fails to meet their goals. Thus, although consumers may purchase impulsively at one time, they will not be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed and be loyal to OGB eventually.

By uncovering the goal attainment process, this study corresponded to researchers’ call for more studies to explore the consumer behavior hierarchy in the e-commerce context by investigating not only consumer behavior goals but also their interrelationships [61,107]. This overlooked area in e-commerce research deserves attention as attributing consumers’ goals to only one isolated layer is to oversimplify consumer behavior considerably. The more speci<sup>fi</sup>c discussions about key goal attainment paths are provided next.

## 5.3.1. Economic-driven goal paths

As expected, we found that economic-related factors were one of the main goals for online group buyers. By depicting a clear and speci<sup>fi</sup>c goal attainment path, our results can more clearly clarify the different economic-related factors and describe how consumers utilize the attributes of price to reach their end goals and trigger their behavior.

## 5.3.2. Choice optimization-driven goal paths

Mentioned by 48 respondents, which accounted 92.31% of the sample, “choice optimization” was described as the desire to search for the right product to <sup>fi</sup>t consumer’s demands in OGB [112]. “Product assortment” was an important concrete goal that led to “choice optimization,” with 33 respondents mentioning this link. Westbrook [112] proposed that “choice optimization” is an important factor affecting consumer purchasing behavior in the shopping context, the self-ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, or self-actualization, and the need for achievement can be derived from <sup>fi</sup>nding the exact right product. However, few studies have empirically tested these relationships. Our results empirically depicted how the “product assortment” motivated consumers to search what they needed and made them happier, which further resulted in goals of “loyalty” and “self-actualization.” Thus, to retain customers and help them achieve “self-actualization,” the goal of “choice optimization” needs to be activated through the operational attribute of “product assortment.” One interviewee’s comment supported this point:

“The products on the OGB website are diverse for me to select. I’ll give you an example. When I select the restaurant category products, there are many restaurants offering OGB deals on the website. Some of the restaurants are the ones I go to often, even when there are no OGB deals available . . . . (For the deals) They offer many packages for consumers to select, which is very flexible . . . ?With so many selctions, I have a better chance to get the right product I want . . . ..I feel get a gratification, a sense of fulfillment” (Participant no.22)

Another way to reach the goal of “choice optimization” was through “information availability”-related goals. “Information availability” means consumers’ ability to obtain information from OGB websites such as the comments, pictures, price, supplier information, product information, etc. “Information quality” was the only attribute that contributed to “information availability.” It refers to a consumer’s general perceptions of the information content on the websites, covering currency, detail, accuracy, reliability, relevant, and completeness aspects of information in this study. The “information availability” helped consumers <sup>fi</sup>nd the right products and <sup>fi</sup>nally resulted in “satisfaction,” “loyal behavior,” and “self-actualization” value. This result con<sup>fi</sup>rms that OGB is also a channel for consumers to gather information to satisfy their higher level goals, as claimed in other e-commerce application literature [103], because the Internet can provide the most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient means for consumers to get information. This result also extends <sup>fi</sup>ndings in OGB literature that ignores the information seeking needs of consumers. One example interview comment that supported this <sup>fi</sup>nding is,

“As the group buying websites provide a large amount of information on local suppliers (restaurants), by browsing these websites I can get to know more suppliers that I didn’t know before and get to know more product information... Then I can easily compare different restaurants, which was not possible before with no OGB. In the past, it was also impossible to know the restaurant information in a new city. Even if I live in Shanghai, I have no idea of how many good restaurants there are... I think this comparison can help me make better decisions and choose the best one after comparison. I can try many new restaurants, which makes my life colorful and full of enjoyment” (Participant no. 25).

## 5.3.3. Convenience-driven goal paths

“Convenience” refers to consumers’ time and effort perceptions related to buying or using services [93]. Although “convenience” and “perceived usefulness” have been con<sup>fi</sup>rmed as important factors affecting consumer purchase decisions in a large number of e-commerce studies [91,103], research examining the underlying mechanism to generate the “convenience” and “perceived usefulness” is limited. When Lim and Ting [61] used TAM to explain consumer OGB behavior, they called for qualitative research employing interpersonal interviews to identify the antecedents of “perceived usefulness.” To respond to their call, our results demonstrated that the concrete goal of “marketing

communication,” “network externality,” and “relative advantage” were all means used by consumers to achieve higher level goals of “convenience” and “perceived usefulness,” while “system quality” served as an antecedent of “ease of navigation.”

“Ease of navigation” and “perceived usefulness” have been con<sup>fi</sup>rmed to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly drive consumer “purchase intention” in the OGB context [61,109]. Interestingly, in prior OGB studies or even in other technology adoption contexts, “perceived ease of use” and “perceived usefulness” are in parallel position even when “perceived ease of use” is veri<sup>fi</sup>ed to have a direct impact on “perceived usefulness.” However, instead of having “perceived ease of use” affecting “perceived usefulness,” we found that “ease of navigation” was in a much lower position compared to “perceived usefulness” along the goal attainment path. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, “ease of navigation” was a perceptual goal that described consumers’ subjective perception about how easily they can use OGB websites to navigate, search, and locate what they needed [47,79], while “perceived usefulness” was an intermediate goal that described the psychological bene<sup>fi</sup>ts consumers obtained, such as making the buying process more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. These two goals are thus at different layers, ful<sup>fi</sup>lling different purposes, at least as found in this study. The “perceived usefulness” in the higher focal goal layer can be achieved through the lower sub-ordinate layer goal of “ease of navigation” through perceived “convenience” of the websites.

## 5.3.4. Trust-driven goal paths

“Trust,” mentioned by 41 respondents, accounting for 78.85% of the overall number of respondents, measures whether the owner of the OGB website is reliable, competent, and benevolent [51]. Previous studies have shown that “trust” in OGB website and “perceived risk” are the key factors affecting consumer OGB behavior [40,61,110]. Consistent with the results in these studies, our results also indicated that “trust” in OGB website and “perceived risk” played signi<sup>fi</sup>cant roles in attaining higher level goals such as “satisfaction,” “purchase intention,” “loyalty,” and “self-actualization.” When Hsu et al. [40] explored the determinants of repurchase intention in OGB, they found two types of trust: trust in OGB website providers (sellers) and trust in product/ service suppliers; both types of trust exerted signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on consumer “satisfaction” and “repurchase intention.” However, different from their <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we only detected consumers’ trust in OGB website providers, rather than on the suppliers, indicating that consumers seemed to care less about trust in product suppliers in the OGB context. This may be because a majority of the OGB products are service oriented, i.e., consumers purchase online and consume it of<sup>fl</sup>ine. Such an online to of<sup>fl</sup>ine behavior may decrease the potential risks associated with the service suppliers. For OGB websites, consumers can only contact and make transactions with them in an online environment, in which high risks are involved. Thus, consumers may care more about trustworthiness of the OGB website providers rather than the product/service suppliers on the websites.

When Cheng and Huang [14] explored the antecedents of intention to participant in OGB, they found that “information quality” and “service quality” had no impacts on “purchase intention.” However, our results indicated that although “service quality” and “information quality” cannot directly result in “purchase intention,” they led to “purchase intention” indirectly through “trust.” Thus, by utilizing a more thorough research approach, this study extends existing literature by proving a more complete view to explain the relationships between OGB website characteristics (e.g., “information quality,” “service quality”) and consumer behavior. One example comment given by interviewee to support this <sup>fi</sup>nding is,

“They provide better customer service. If I come across some problems after purchasing the coupon on this website (dianping.

com), I can easily get a refund from this website. They have very quick response and handle the problems appropriately... I think it is a reliable website. I don’t need to worry about the potential risk of losing money... very satisfied with their honest service quality... This experience makes me continue to purchase from it in the near future” (Participant no. 47).

Among the sub-ordinate goals, we found that 22 participants mentioned “network externalities” affecting “trust,” which was not found in prior e-commerce literature. “Network externalities,” composed of the number of members (mass consumers), number of peers (impacts of friends), and perceived complementary, is not a new concept in IS research. From the individual perception level, there are two streams of research examining how “network externalities” in<sup>fl</sup>uence technology adoption through cognitive or affective beliefs. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stream focuses on the effects of “network externalities” on the perception of technology attributes, such as users’ perceived usefulness and ease of use [63,99,122]. The other stream examines how “network externalities” in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perception of social attributes, such as perceived enjoyment and social interaction [122]. Our results extend the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of prior studies by uncovering the impact of “network externalities” on consumer cognitive belief of “trust” in the OGB context—an online environment where the “trust” is relatively low, while “perceived risk” is relatively high. This relationship between “network externality” and “trust” can be further con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by quantitative studies.

## 5.3.5. Summary of goal attainment paths

By comparing the four main goal paths discussed above, three interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings emerged. First, we found that all the four paths had the end goal of “self-actualization” attached, indicating that it is the rationale behind all OGB consumers, regardless of the preferred paths they may pursue to realize it. It shows that OGB consumers draw a great sense of self-respect, self-ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, and self-accomplishment from engagement with OGB websites. Second, we found that all paths had “loyalty” as the instrumental goal, which was realized either directly or indirectly by the intermediate goal of “satisfaction,” proving the importance of making customers enjoy the shopping experience to encourage them to relate with their instrumental goal of being loyal, irrespective of the preferred paths they pursue in OGB behavior. However, the four paths did show the different mechanisms to satisfy the customers. Thus different marketing strategies may be needed in different scenarios. Third, as far as the complexity of the paths is concerned, the economic-driven path was the simplest path, with only one concrete attribute appearing at the subordinate goal level. It shows that the pricing of products can de<sup>fi</sup>nitely help to sell the products on the basis of the concrete goals and immediate goals of the customers, such that customers can improve their quality of life. The “trust”-driven goal path was the most complex decision process, given the number of not only concrete attributes but also perceptual goals incorporated to realize the higher level goals. Given that customers’ perceptions about “information quality,” “product quality,” and “service quality” may vary depending on customer experience [116], it increases the complexity of the consumer’s decision-making process. The “convenience”-driven goal path was relative less complex than the “trust”-driven goal path, while the “choice optimization”-driven goal path was the second simplest path among the four paths identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Overall, this demonstrates that different paths may require different levels of effort to realize higher level goals.

## 6. Implications

## 6.1. Implications for research

From a research perspective, this study makes three key contributions. First, the combination of hierarchical goal-directed consumer behavior approach and the MEC theory as theoretical perspectives and the laddering technique as a data collection and analysis technique have proved to be an effective way of understanding online consumer behaviors in areas where empirical studies are scarce. Through the use of the laddering technique to elicit a <sup>fi</sup>nite set of constructs from OGB customers, the study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed multiple-level goals OGB users aimed to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll, including sub-ordinate goals that describe how customer-focal goals can be achieved and end super-ordinate goals that explain why OGB users want to achieve focal-level goals. Although the goals identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study are not exclusive from those identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in other traditional e-commerce and OGB consumer behavior studies, this research is amongst the <sup>fi</sup>rst to identify some “new” goals across different levels and reveal them in a structured manner, opening the opportunity to synthesize knowledge from different research silos [81]. This <sup>fi</sup>nding not only demonstrates that context matters when studying human goal-directed technology use behavior but also indicates the importance of conducting this research to better understand consumer behavior in OGB contexts from a more systematic and comprehensive manner. Thus, rather than using goals derived from traditional e-commerce study contexts or technology user adoption perspective, future OGB studies that aim to understand consumer goal-directed behavior can adopt this study’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings to enhance their study’s validity.

Second, the application of the laddering yielded rich and relevant qualitative data from the interviews, allowing us to identify not only multiple-level OGB user goals but also their hierarchical structure that can be used to illustrate OGB consumer goal attainment paths, adding to our understanding of OGB consumer behavior by providing, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, an integrative theoretical explanation of howand why OGB consumers want to participate in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place. By adopting a MEC perspective of product/service knowledge to understand the hierarchical structure of goal-directed OGB consumer behavior, we categorized OGB consumer goals into “subordinate goals,” “focal goals,” and “super-ordinate goals” according to the MEC theory, allowing researchers to better formulate their views and disseminate them to others. We extend the current understanding of factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing consumer OGB adoption behavior by depicting the way factors are connected to each other. These goal attainment paths identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, on the one hand, provide deeper and solid insights into the speci<sup>fi</sup>c components of OGB platformsthatconsumersregardasusefulindirectingtheirbehavior. On the other hand, they constitute a solid basis for the design of future quantitative studies, aiming to uncover the interrelationships between OGB consumer behavior goals.

Third, from a methodological perspective, the novel approach taken in this study to investigate the research questions provides an alternative avenue for IS researchers to explore technology users’ motivations to use emerging technologies. Because technology use behavior is often depicted as goal directed, understanding the structure of technology users’ goals can provide insights into their behavior. By interpreting the goal hierarchical structure of their behaviors, incorporated with laddering techniques, researchers can understand not only the concrete technology (in this case, OGB website) attributes consumers prefer but also the desired bene<sup>fi</sup>ts they expect from the technology use ultimately for their end goals to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. Given that researchers are so devoted to understanding why technology users are so attracted to various emerging online systems, such as online games and social virtual worlds, researchers can adopt this study’s approach to understand their potential users’ goals to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled when they use the systems to understand their motivations for using technologies. In other words, a potential research area in IS is to adopt this approach to explore research questions of not only what bene<sup>fi</sup>ts technology users obtain from using IS but also why these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are important to them and how the IS should be designed to realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that the users want. Of course, the laddering technique itself engenders some challenges, in particular the complexity of data collection and subjectivity of data analysis [87,105]. Some suggestions about how to overcome the subjectivity of laddering technique are discussed in the Limitations section.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

This study also has practical implications for OGB website providers and products/services suppliers conducting or planning to conduct business through the OGB websites, or e-commerce in general, in their marketing strategy development and implementation.

First, the interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in our study regarding the relationship between the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts OGB users pursue and impulsive behavior indicates that this behavior may not be a sustainable strategy for OGB platforms. To encourage consumer impulsive shopping behavior, it seems that as long as consumers can access necessary information about the deals, such as the time left for the transaction and the number of people who have already purchased the deals, which are highlighted in bold and color words, consumers may engage an impulsive behavior, similar to what the literature suggested [56]. However, the incomplete ladder found in this study about online impulsivity indicates that this will not be a long-term strategy to promote business as impulsive behavior in OGB failed to connect to the achievement of any higher level personal goals. OGB websites should adopt other marketing strategies that would assist consumers to realize their personal end goals. On the basis of the hierarchical goal structure developed in this study, we recommend the following marketing strategies pertaining to various aspects in OGB.

1) Pricing strategy: product price should be set to match the value in order not to disappoint the customers with mismatch between the quality and price as it would affect customer satisfaction and loyalty. By saving the price, customers may also improve their life quality by using the savings wisely in other areas.

2) Product differentiation strategy: Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that OGB websites may focus more on product brand, variety, uniqueness, and multiple suppliers as customers usually view them as very important motivations for them to search for the goods they want. Their demand for self-actualization can be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed if they can get exactly what they want from the websites.

3) Content management strategy: In addition to product differentiation strategy, OGB websites should also pay close attention to the quality of content provided by the websites. By providing accurate, current, relevant, reliable, and detailed information about the products listed on the websites, customers can enhance their trust to the websites and reduce the perceived risk, consequently leading to more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed and loyal customers. High-quality information presented on the website can also motivate customers to choose what they want from the websites and make them realize their sense of ful<sup>fi</sup>llment and accomplishment.

4) Post-sales service strategy: How well customers are treated after the sales is also important to creating a better customer experience. OGB website providers should provide assurances to customers regarding product warranty; supplier information; and the need to be <sup>fl</sup>exible, responsible, and approachable when handling customer issues. Customers trust toward website providers can then be built and strengthened. Consequently, customers will perceive less risk when they deal with website providers, increasing their chance to do more shopping, to be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, and to become more loyal.

Another practical implication of this study is about the nature of OGB customer experience. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that OGB website providers should focus more on utilitarian aspects of the website attributes and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts rather than the provision of a hedonistic site usage experience as the majority of the goals in the hierarchical framework we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed are utilitarian based. This seems to be very different from traditional of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online business models, in which consumers are often driven by hedonic aspects of the businesses [28,76,103]. Most importantly, this study found that “cost saving” is one of the most important reasons for users to visit OGB websites. However, other factors, such as “convenience,” “trust,” and “choice optimization,” are equally important to the economic factor in constituting the goal hierarchy model. The multiple-level goal hierarchy model identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study also shows that OGB customers’ decision-making process relies not only on their concrete product and/or website knowledge but also increasingly on their more abstract level goals. This <sup>fi</sup>nding serves the OGB website providers a useful tool in terms of designing the websites, communicating with OGB consumers, informing product positioning strategies, and market segmentations, with four main reasons of OGB shopping behavior incorporated rather than only using the low price strategy to compete in the OGB market, as we have suggested above.

## 7. Limitations and future research

Despite contributing fresh insights to the OGB e-commerce literature, this study has some limitations. First, data were collected through a soft ladder technique, given the exploratory nature of this study, with a sample of only Chinese consumers. Although the applied sampling approach and given sample size are appropriate for this kind of qualitative research, the generalizability of the study can be improved by referring to the framework presented in this study and conducting a largescale quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation empirical study to validate the framework. Furthermore, given that personal value preference may be different across cultures because of political, social, economic, and cultural environmental differences [111], researchers who are interested in this area may conduct a similar study in other countries to compare whether and how the personal values relevant to OGB consumer behavior differ across cultures. However, as OGB is quite popular in China, with the largest number of users and e-commerce applications operating in the emarketplace, this study in the Chinese context can still contribute to e-commerce, especially the OGB industry in China.

Second, the inherent vagueness of ladder data collection and analysis techniques present some challenges to the researchers, and the approach is criticized for being subjective in nature [67,105]. First, as the interview requires to elicit personal aspects in different levels of abstraction through continuously probing questions, this data collection technique can present dif<sup>fi</sup>culties to the respondents, leading to be either unable to articulate or unwilling to answer [23]. Although the researcher who collected the data for this research was a very experienced researcher in the MEC method and applied various techniques suggested in the literature to overcome such a block [67,105], she may “still have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty bridging from data to strategy to executional design and implications,” as stated by Gengler and Reynolds $\left[ 2 6 , \mathsf { p } . 1 9 \right]$ . As a result, some important and more abstract level factors (personal values) may not be activated during the interview [33]. Second,

Lin [64] also criticizes the content analysis process that is more subjective in nature, leading to the possibility of elimination of relevant variables and linkages. To avoid this issue, we adopted the use of computer-aided content analysis methods of NVivo, as recommended by Grunert and Grunert [29]. We also revisited our codes after the initial analysis and even during our <sup>fi</sup>nal-stage discussions to minimize the negligence of important factors and or linkages. In addition, there are not many concrete and precise rules for determining how to clearly distinguish among three level goals. The cut-off point selection can also affect the content validity of an hierarchical goal structure [29]. Thus, the content analysis of the laddering data and hierarchy remains complex and subjective. In this study, we adopted various criteria when allocating all goals into different layers, aiming to make this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation process more rigorous and accurate. To overcome these issues, in the future, the interpretive structural modeling (ISM) technique, a well-established methodology for identifying and developing relationships within a system of related elements [106], can be employed to classify factors into different levels and to uncover the hierarchical structure of factors involved. The hierarchical structure framework developed by ISM is similar to the hierarchical goal structure generated by MEC. However, the ISM methodology is more objective in nature, utilizing a mathematic algorithm calculating procedure, having the capability to distinguish goals into different levels more objectively. Thus, studies can attempt to integrate ISM with MEC in the data analysis process in future.

Third, consumers who are heavy users of OGB may prioritize different goals compared to light users. This study did not consider the impact of involvement level on the hierarchical goal structure generated. Future research may try to divide subjects into light users and heavy users according to their level of involvement and compare the difference of the hierarchical goal structure generated by these two groups of online group buyers.

Finally, this study only represents an exploratory investigation purely based on qualitative methods. Although it is deemed appropriate for gaining valuable insights, the given sample size does not allow for any quantitative inferences about the online group buyers in a strict statistical sense. Therefore, it only represents a <sup>fi</sup>rst step that can be subsequently complemented by quantitative and con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory studies. Future research can seek to quantitatively validate the obtained hierarchical goal structure and provide more theoretical and practical implications regarding the relative importance of the goals identi<sup>fi</sup>ed.
