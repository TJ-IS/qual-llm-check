# Managing Knowledge Distance: IT-Enabled Inter-Firm Knowledge Capabilities in Collaborative Innovation

Tingru Cui <sup>a</sup>, Yu Tong <sup>b</sup>, Hock-Hai Teo <sup>c</sup>, and Jizhen Li <sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Computing and Information Systems, The University of Melbourne, Australia; <sup>b</sup>School of Management, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China; <sup>c</sup>Department of Information Systems and Analytics, National University of Singapore, Singapore, Singapore; <sup>d</sup>School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University, Beijing, China

## ABSTRACT

Collaborating with a partner in a distant area is widely endorsed in practice as a means for inspiring novel innovations. However, empirical evidence also reports signi<sup>fi</sup>cant innovation e<sup>fi</sup>ciency challenges when working with distant partners due to di<sup>f</sup>erences in technical knowledge domain di<sup>f</sup>erences. In this study, we conducted a multi-method approach by using a case study of 30 interviews to develop three information technology (IT)-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities and testing the research model with <sup>fi</sup>eld survey data from 258 <sup>fi</sup>rms. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research con<sup>fi</sup>rm our hypotheses that IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge exploration capability and IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge exploitation capability can help embrace the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness while IT-enabled social integration capability surmount process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency challenges inherent in collaboration with distant partners. This study adds a much-needed perspective to the IT and innovation literature and o<sup>f</sup>ers practical suggestions for <sup>fi</sup>rm managers to mindfully design and deploy IT resources in collaborative innovation projects.

## KEYWORDS

Collaborative innovation; ITenabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities; inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance; product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness; process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency

## Introduction

Firms are increasingly adopting a collaborative innovation approach to complement their internal e<sup>f</sup>orts for new product or service development [22, 37]. As collaborative innovation involves the recombination of knowledge from various collaborative parties, interfirm knowledge distance,<sup>1</sup> which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree of Research & Development (R&D) domain knowledge unrelatedness between two collaborating <sup>fi</sup>rms [44, 78, 110], is critical to innovation outcomes. A knowledge distant collaborator that possesses R&D domain knowledge from an unrelated <sup>fi</sup>eld has the potential to o<sup>f</sup>er fresh ideas to the innovation process through knowledge recombination or access to market insights that are otherwise unavailable to a <sup>fi</sup>rm [4, 95]. An industry survey reveals that most corporate executives deem working together with knowledge distant partners as critical to inspiring novel and high-quality collaborative innovations [34, 114]. For instance, P<sup>fi</sup>zer (a pharmaceutical company) collaborates with IBM for innovative remote monitoring tools for Parkinson’s diseases. Such tools, including components of sensors, mobile devices and computerized analysis, demand P<sup>fi</sup>zer’s expertise in the medical <sup>fi</sup>eld and IBM’s ability to analyze complex data [86].

Despite the enthusiasm for and touted bene<sup>fi</sup>t of collaborative innovation with a distant collaborator, <sup>fi</sup>rms continue to face the challenge of bene<sup>fi</sup>ting from it. The high interdependence between knowledge distant collaborators may potentially lead to di<sup>fi</sup>culties in integrating and coordinating their knowledge and task inputs [38, 84]. The challenges that arise from distant technological collaboration may result in low collaborative innovation e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, delay, or abandonment of the collaboration [118]. A collaborative innovation approach with knowledge distant partners is clearly not as straightforward as proponents expected. Although the approach confers on ideas that are novel and otherwise hard to envision, it also creates challenges for the collaborative innovation process [27, 68].

Collaborative innovation <sup>fi</sup>rms are dependent on IT for knowledge exchange and interaction. As such, IT may serve as a solution to extract potential value and mitigate the challenges induced by inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance in collaborative innovation. Prior literature has recognized that IT capability plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in enabling inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration (e.g., [43]) and supporting <sup>fi</sup>rm innovation development activities (e.g., [83, 57]). However, prior research has failed to explicitly address the challenges induced by inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance in the context of collaborative innovation. Enhanced theoretical understanding is thus needed to support the e<sup>f</sup>ective deployment of IT for collaborative innovation.

This study aims to answer the following research question: In the context of collaborative innovation projects involving two collaborating firms, what are the discriminating roles of IT capabilities in moderating the efects of inter-firm knowledge distance on project performance? Such consideration will <sup>fi</sup>ll the research gap in the literature. To obtain a satisfying project performance, a <sup>fi</sup>rm should concurrently achieve product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency [15, 109]. Based on the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions in prior innovation studies [13, 63, 80, 81, 83], we de<sup>fi</sup>ne product efectiveness as the ful<sup>fi</sup>lment of the technical quality indicators of new product or service development outcomes in a collaborative innovation project. For example, an e<sup>f</sup>ective product may lead to better technical quality than industrial standard or the product could be a breakthrough technical innovation [13]. Process eficiency refers to the extent of resources that are used in the process of the collaborative innovation project. For example, an e<sup>fi</sup>cient collaborative innovation process may consume less <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources compared to its budget for the project execution [83]. We conducted a multi-method approach with an expansion focus to investigate the research question, which allows researchers to understand the phenomenon of interest in a thorough fashion [2, 73, 108]. We <sup>fi</sup>rst used a case study of 30 interviews on six collaborative innovation projects in three <sup>fi</sup>rms to obtain an improved understanding of the collaborative innovation context. Such understanding, together with literature of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities in the intra-<sup>fi</sup>rm innovation, helped develop three IT-enabled inter-firm knowledge capabilities. We then extended the case <sup>fi</sup>ndings via a quantitative survey of collaborative innovation projects in 258 <sup>fi</sup>rms to test the hypotheses. Table 1 summarizes the steps and key <sup>fi</sup>ndings for this approach.

This study primarily contributes to the IT capability and collaborative innovation literature by demonstrating how di<sup>f</sup>erent IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities can leverage the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and surmount the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency challenges in the fundamental process of collaboration with knowledge distant partners. An appreciation of the di<sup>f</sup>erentiated roles of IT capabilities will help organizations design, deploy, and manage collaborative innovation projects mindfully in future endeavors.

## Stage 1: Theoretical Development

## Inter-Firm Collaborative Innovation

Inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaborative innovation refers to an inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaborative project that focuses on the joint development of technological innovation [28, p. 160]. This type of project uses a collaborative approach to combine di<sup>f</sup>erent knowledge across organizational boundaries to cultivate synergistic solutions and innovation [51]. The output of collaborative innovation is mainly technological innovation in the form of new product or service development, which is central to how organizations create value and achieve competitive advantage [16]. Collaborative innovation hence allows a <sup>fi</sup>rm to realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of an open innovation approach through external knowledge [22]. Moreover, a <sup>fi</sup>rm can follow a closed innovation approach that warrants the proprietary protection of innovative and high-quality outcomes using a contract [71]. The examples of collaborative innovation range from the project between Intel and Microsoft for the Wintel platform [18] to the collaboration of GE and Huawei for a smart IoT machine [116].

Collaborative innovation di<sup>f</sup>ers from the other types of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration by emphasizing the co-development of technological innovation as the collaboration outcome. The literature on collaborative innovation assumes the existence of an interorganizational team that performs a joint activity that requires high levels of interdependence between the collaborators [28]. Interdependence between collaborators can be characterized along two dimensions: (1) technology interdependence, which is the mutual reliance on R&D domain knowledge and resource exchange and integration for innovation and (2) behavior interdependence, which is the interaction, coordination, and communication dependence between collaborative participants [1, 8, 92]. Hence, the impact of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance<sup>2</sup> should be situated in the highly technology- and behaviorinterdependent inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm teams. The following section uses the extant literature to explain the impact of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance on the collaborative innovation project performance of product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

## Impact of Inter-Firm Knowledge Distance on Collaborative Innovation

Prior literature has utilized several related concepts to compare R&D domain knowledge bases between collaborating <sup>fi</sup>rms. For instance, knowledge diversity and knowledge heterogeneity are typically interchangeably used in prior literature. The terms emphasize on the variety of knowledge categories pooled from usually two or more partners [52, 107]. Knowledge complementarity focuses on the synergistic nature of related but contrasted knowledge possessed [104]. In contrast to these concepts, inter-firm knowledge distance emphasizes the unrelated knowledge that is, distant R&D domain knowledge between two collaborative innovation partners [107]. We select inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance as the focal construct in our study because it captures the paradoxical tension of collaborative innovation projects that involves a highly technology- and behavior-interdependent setting. On the one hand, varied extents of unrelated R&D domain knowledge bases from two <sup>fi</sup>rms might be combined and integrated to create value that did not exist in either <sup>fi</sup>rm before the collaboration to yield better collaboration outcomes [64, 110]. On the other hand, it can also undermine collaboration outcomes due to greater misalignment and absence of shared understanding (e.g., [44, 78]).

We propose that increasing the knowledge distance between two collaborative innovation partners yields an opportunity for increased product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness via raising the knowledge recombination bene<sup>fi</sup>t [54]. The extant inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm distance literature largely values the importance of unfamiliar R&D domain knowledge bases as input for problemsolving, learning and innovation [44, 78]. First, a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s cognitive structures, such as beliefs and premises of innovation solutions, are challenged by exploring unfamiliar R&D domain knowledge. Such knowledge can be induced from inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance, which promotes novel insights into new product development [87]. New research methods and perspectives from a distant partner may be introduced into the existing R&D processes of a <sup>fi</sup>rm [94]. The introduction hence encourages creativity and raises the potential of high product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness [84, 87]. Second, distant R&D domain knowledge provides collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms with unrelated problem-solving cues in new product development, which empowers the new combinations of R&D domain knowledge [7]. With the new combinations of R&D domain knowledge, <sup>fi</sup>rms can stimulate experimentation that may lead to high product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness [62].

However, the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>t of collaborative innovation can be undermined when the knowledge distance becomes excessively large [78]. In this situation, technology interdependence poses signi<sup>fi</sup>cant knowledge comprehension and knowledge integration challenges given the over-complexities and uncertainties associated with distant knowledge between collaborators [44, 97]. From the knowledge comprehension perspective, collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms are required to share a certain basic “interpretation system” or a “system of shared meanings” to su<sup>fi</sup>ciently align their knowledge competencies [111]. Studies have corroborated that mutual communication and understanding potentially decrease with an increase in the di<sup>f</sup>erence of knowledge base [41]. When the knowledge of two distant <sup>fi</sup>rms is unrelated, the insu<sup>fi</sup>cient understanding of the technology of a partner hinders the marshaling of various capabilities from partners. As for knowledge integration, inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance may impede the transfer of the tacit components of knowledge between collaborators with a low level of common knowledge base. It makes knowledge from distant R&D domains di<sup>fi</sup>cult to be absorbed and integrated by collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms [78]. Hence, the value of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance increases with product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness until a certain threshold, where it will degenerate the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm integration of knowledge for collaborative innovation product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. Following the arguments proposed in prior literature, therefore, we propose the following:

Baseline Relationship 1: Inter-firm knowledge distance has an inverted U-shaped relationship with product efectiveness.

Given the high behavior interdependence of collaborative innovation partners, task coordination and communication challenges become increasingly intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed between knowledge distant partners during the collaboration process. High behavior interdependence relates to increased needs for mutual adjustment and common decisions [8]. However, di<sup>f</sup>erent knowledge disciplines have unique premises and extensively divergent logic for their inner workings [31]. Firms with varied technological knowledge backgrounds, experiences, and skill sets (i.e., knowledge distance) tend to process information in di<sup>f</sup>erent ways in the knowledge exchange process [27]. Task coordination and communication challenges are likely to surface because of the lack of a shared code for information processing and inconsistency of work behavior styles [74, 92]. While collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms need work together on common tasks, many innovationrelated activities can be delegated to individual <sup>fi</sup>rms working parallelly. They need to coordinate common and individual tasks, schedules, and deliverables. Developing a shared situation model regarding tasks involved in their collaborative work is complicated and timeconsuming for knowledge distant <sup>fi</sup>rms [92]. Di<sup>f</sup>erent information processing and work behavior styles may lead to unanticipated problems or friction for the synchronization of these individual contributions [49]. Distant collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms may need to constantly make adjustments to develop common understanding related to how the up-to-date individual contributions can be interrelated [23]. Furthermore, collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms from distant knowledge domains may encounter operational incompatibility and procedural issues that assume salience in the working relationship setting [98]. Di<sup>f</sup>erent outlook and objectives increase coordination costs and incompatibility between partners, which may lead to a counterproductive working relationship [98]. Knowledge distance may also cause communication ambiguity and con<sup>fl</sup>icts for inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge transfer process [102]. It thus hampers the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the project execution [49]. Therefore, it can result in extra time to be taken for resolution as well as delayed knowledge exchange and agreement for new product development [50]. Therefore, we propose the following:

Baseline Relationship 2: Inter-firm knowledge distance has a negative relationship with process eficiency.

Recognizing the diminishing and even negative impact of large inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance on collaborative innovation, few prior literature has explored organizational characteristics to mitigate the negative impacts, such as the cumulative R&D capability of a <sup>fi</sup>rm or network position (e.g., [44, 78]). However, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant result has yet to be found. In the following section, we explore how IT may be the solution to extract the potential value and mitigate the challenges induced by inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance in collaborative innovations.

## Related Work on IT Capabilities

Prior research has recognized the roles that IT plays in inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration and <sup>fi</sup>rm innovation, such as enabling inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm social exchange [43] and supporting new product development activities [83]. We aim to identify suitable IT capabilities for enhancing the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and surmounting the challenges that are inherent in collaboration with distant partners. We conducted a literature review on three streams of research, namely: (1) IT in collaborative innovation (focal context); (2) IT in inter-firm collaboration, which includes the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration in supply chain management, business process outsourcing relationship, and inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm virtual teams to capture the nature of collaboration; and (3) IT in firm innovation, such as internal innovation and organizational learning areas of a <sup>fi</sup>rm to capture the nature of innovation.

Appendix A summarizes the literature review of related work on IT capability. The literature has a<sup>fi</sup>rmed that many prior studies on IT capability have focused on one speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of an IT tool (e.g., knowledge management systems or modeling technologies) (e.g., [33, 43, 89] or treat IT as an aggregate construct (e.g., IT usage, IT competency, and IT investment; e.g., [31, 32, 61, 67]). Limited studies on distinct IT constructs have focused on the roles of IT in supporting external knowledge sourcing for innovation (e.g., [25, 106]) without touching on the interdependent nature of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration. Other studies have discussed inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm learning without diving into actual innovation outcomes (e.g., [100]). In the context of collaborative innovation, the roles played by IT capabilities should address the nature of collaboration and the nature of innovation (i.e., high technology- and behavior-interdependence between collaborators). IT capabilities are expected to play discriminating roles in strengthening the potential value for improved innovation outcomes as well as addressing knowledge comprehension, knowledge integration, task coordination, and communication challenges. Therefore, the conceptualization of distinct IT constructs and the further systematic examination of the distinct roles played by IT capabilities in collaborative innovation are necessary.

## Absorptive Capacity and IT-Enabled Knowledge Capabilities

To <sup>fi</sup>ll the research gap, we re-conceptualized and examined IT capabilities in a collaborative innovation setting by selecting absorptive capacity theory as the theoretical lens. As this theory is widely used in the knowledge intensive context, this theoretical perspective may have the potential to bridge the knowledge gap among collaborators for inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm learning and innovation purposes. The absorptive capacity of a <sup>fi</sup>rm denotes its capability to “recognize the value of new, external knowledge, assimilate it and apply it to commercial ends” [24, p. 128] . In the intra-<sup>fi</sup>rm innovation context, three IT-enabled knowledge capabilities were conceptualized from absorptive capacity theory, namely, ITenabled potential absorptive capacity, IT-enabled realized absorptive capacity, and ITenabled social integration capacity in supporting intra-<sup>fi</sup>rm innovation [57]. These three IT-enabled knowledge capabilities are important for intra-<sup>fi</sup>rm innovation by supporting knowledge acquisition and assimilation; knowledge transformation and exploitation as well as building social capital.

Although <sup>fi</sup>rm-level absorptive capacity theory was initially proposed for intra-<sup>fi</sup>rm activities [24], it has since been extended to inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm levels and is re-conceptualized as relative absorptive capacity [66]. It suggests that the absorptive capacity of a focal <sup>fi</sup>rm highly depends on the relative relationship between the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s and its collaborator’s knowledge [66]. Relative absorptive capacity has been applied and found essential for inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm learning and collaboration (e.g., [10]). Based on this perspective, we decided to re-conceptualize and extend the theoretical foundation of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities in a collaborative innovation setting by focusing on its roles in addressing inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration and innovation nature. Doing so requires a deep understanding of the collaborative innovation context. The standard operationalization of the IT-enabled knowledge capabilities in this context is yet to be formulated. Therefore, we will initiate the exploration through a qualitative approach.

## STAGE 2: Understanding Collaborative Innovation via a Qualitative study

The qualitative study aims to (1) explicate and provide the contextual details of collaborative innovation project, (2) re-conceptualize and examine the IT-enabled knowledge capabilities in an inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaborative innovation setting, and (3) explore how IT capabilities can enhance the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and address challenges in a collaborative innovation project for hypothesis development. With the multi-method approach with an expansion focus adopted in this study, the qualitative study allows us to expand the depth and breadth of the research as well as understand the phenomenon of interest in a thorough manner [2, 73, 108].

We selected three high-tech <sup>fi</sup>rms that actively engage in collaborative innovation projects. These <sup>fi</sup>rms varied in terms of size and industry. Solar is a large and leading solar energy company in China; InteIT specializes in integrated IT services and ConsumerPro is a top multinational consumer goods company. This multi-case selection renders our <sup>fi</sup>ndings more robust and generalizable compared with a single-case selection. Six completed collaborative innovation projects, which cover various inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm distances between collaborators, were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from our three case companies. In the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed collaborative innovation projects, the two involved <sup>fi</sup>rms actively participated in the process of collaborative innovation to co-develop new products. The <sup>fi</sup>nal new products as well as new technologies and codi<sup>fi</sup>ed intellectual property such as patents created in the collaborative innovation project process were owned by both collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms. The collaborative new products include an integrated solar heating system, an intelligent robot platform and skincare beauty products. Appendix B.1 provides the details of these <sup>fi</sup>rms and collaborative innovation projects.

## Qualitative Data Collection

We adopted the critical incident technique [40] and asked interviewees to identify a collaborative innovation project that had been recently completed and that the two collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms were deeply involved in co-developing a new product together. This practice reduces recall bias and enhances accuracy [45]. We asked the interviewees to describe the collaborative innovation project, the challenges encountered, the IT tools used, and their roles in relation to the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed project. We addressed the potential informant bias in several ways. First, we followed the interview guides that guided informants on relating the chronologies of objective events, behaviors, and facts of the collaborative innovation project [11, 36]. Second, we triangulated the data collected through three sources: (1) onsite and follow up telephone interviews with key stakeholders, such as R&D directors, managers, and employees; (2) onsite observations of the workplaces and innovation products; and (3) archives, such as media and <sup>fi</sup>rm materials. We triangulated data collected from multiple informants in a <sup>fi</sup>rm. Approximately two pieces of evidence were used to support each <sup>fi</sup>nding [75]. Third, we provided our informants and their <sup>fi</sup>rms with anonymity, which encourages candor [36]. In total, we conducted 30 interviews. Each interview lasted 45–60 minutes and was taped and transcribed to ensure the reliability of the information [12].

We adopted the approach of soft positivist qualitative research for data analysis (e.g., [35, 103, 115]). The interview data coding and analysis were undertaken using the approach proposed by Miles and Huberman [72]. An initial set of coding themes was derived on the basis of our theoretical lens and our objective to understand the collaborative innovation project. The transcripts were then independently coded by three research assistants who were blind to the objectives of this research. We gave the assistants a one-page coding scheme that contains the de<sup>fi</sup>nition or description of each construct or theme (see Appendix C.1). On the basis of the coding scheme, a list of IT tools with their descriptive functions and roles in the collaborative innovation project was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, which was further compared and re<sup>fi</sup>ned with the measures of IT constructs based on the paper of Joshi et al. [57] (please see detailed discussion in the Measurement and Validation Process section). The three assistants <sup>fi</sup>rst coded one interview transcript, met to discuss any discrepancies, and developed explicit coding rules to ensure consistency in interpretation before proceeding to the rest of the transcripts. We iterated the <sup>fi</sup>ndings back and forth between the empirical data and the conceptual themes, until “theoretical saturation” was reached [35]. Thereafter, the inter-rater coding reliability was checked [72]. The <sup>fi</sup>nal Fleiss’s Kappa was 0.82, which is above the threshold of 0.70 for inter-rater reliability [65].

## Qualitative Data Analysis

On analyzing the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance between the R&D domain knowledge inputs of the collaborator and case company in the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed collaborative innovation projects, we claimed that distance can range from 1 to 10 points according to the research assistants’ ratings (see Appendix B.4). A 10-point distance implies that the R&D domain knowledge of the collaborator is highly unrelated to the case company. We found the largest distance in case projects 1, 2, 4, and 6. For instance, case projects 1 and 2 are two di<sup>f</sup>erent projects between the Solar and the same collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rm. The expertise of Solar (case company) includes solar energy water heaters and solar lighting, as well as various energy-saving and desalination technologies. Its collaborator specializes in pump motors and <sup>fl</sup>uid power machinery hardware. According to our interviewees, both of them are highly specialized and do not share a common technological background. In case project 4, the collaboration between an IT <sup>fi</sup>rm whose R&D domain knowledge mainly focused on cloud computing and big data analytics and a <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fi</sup>rm conformed to the emerging trend of <sup>fi</sup>ntech and the Internet of things to build a smart city. Case project 6 also shows high inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance between the case <sup>fi</sup>rm (manufacturer of home care products with odor clear technology) and its collaborator (with bamboo fabric material technology). Meanwhile, ConsumerPro (case company) did not limit itself as a company in one speci<sup>fi</sup>c industry, but as a company with a powerful core technology, that is, theoretically applicable in all industries to develop diverse product lines. This mindset opened up a potential solution space for ConsumerPro and enabled this collaborative innovation project to be positioned far from its existing markets. With respect to the other case projects in our sample, the distances are relatively small, which range from 3 to 4. The case companies used analogical solutions from a close-by industry.

On analyzing the performance of collaborative innovation projects, raters evaluated the product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of each case project on a scale of 1 to 10. We validated that three of the projects, namely, case projects 1 and 6, can be categorized as high in product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness with ratings above 6. However, these projects are poor in process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency with ratings equal to or below 5. Case projects 3 and 5, di<sup>f</sup>erently, are relatively low in product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness with ratings of 5. However, these projects were rated positive in process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency by the research assistants. Case projects 2 and 4 were the struggling projects for case companies with relatively low product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and with ratings equal to or below 4 (Appendix B.4). Our informants gave highly consistent designations to explain the impact of knowledge distance on increased product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness via raising the knowledge recombination bene<sup>fi</sup>t. For example, the R&D project manager at ConsumerPro stated that “Our expertise in diferent fields builds us a large pool of knowledge and resources to co-create the potential of an innovative and high-quality solution. This provides high knowledge variation for us to think about new options, to address problems, and to ensure new technical knowledge are efectively used to their maximum efect in delivering innovation.” On the other hand, our interviewees also revealed that the negative impacts of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance on process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Various challenges were raised during the collaborative innovation process, such as “experienced dificult in reaching agreement and led to delayed schedule” and “had struggle in reconciling their broadly diferent views on the requirements.”

The qualitative data also showed variation in IT usage across these six case projects. We consolidated a list of IT tools that are commonly used in collaborative innovation projects through our qualitative data (Appendix B.4). Our informants provided detailed examples and illustrations regarding how they used these IT tools in the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed collaborative innovation project. The following section uses these quotes to explain the arguments of our hypothesis development.

## Qualitative Findings: IT-Enabled Inter-Firm Knowledge Capabilities in Collaborative Innovation

Based on our qualitative data analysis, we developed three IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities, namely, (1) IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge exploration capability (IT-KRC), (2) IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge exploitation capability (IT-KIC), and (3) IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm social integration capability (IT-SIC) in a collaborative innovation setting. Appendix B.2 lists a summary of these capabilities with sample interview quotes. The qualitative <sup>fi</sup>ndings also enable us to identify the roles of IT capabilities in reaping the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and addressing the challenges in collaborative innovation for hypothesis development.

## IT-Enabled Inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm Knowledge Exploration Capability (IT-KRC)

As suggested by our informants, a set of ITs in the form of innovation platforms, data retrieval technologies, databases, and data warehouses<sup>3</sup> supported and enhanced the external knowledge sourcing capability of their <sup>fi</sup>rms by enhancing the speed, intensity, and directionality of external knowledge identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and selection. On the basis of our qualitative <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned IT-enabled inter-firm knowledge exploration capability (IT-KRC) as the ability to use IT to support the acquisition and assimilation of knowledge in the distant knowledge domain of its collaborator for inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaborative innovation. Firms that use these IT tools have enhanced the ability to absorb useful knowledge and build a knowledge base in the distant knowledge domain of its collaborator. On the basis of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the roles of IT-KRC in the collaborative innovation project can be summed up as creating more innovation opportunities and alleviating inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge comprehension and knowledge integration challenges, which have moderating impacts on baseline relationship 1.

Moderating Impact on Product Efectiveness. When IT-KRC is low, collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms encounter challenges with increased inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance, which makes enhancing product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness increasingly di<sup>fi</sup>cult. Under the condition with minimal support from knowledge sourcing ITs, learning and absorbing novel ideas and knowledge across the <sup>fi</sup>elds of R&D domain, distinct modes of reasoning, and varied problem-solving approaches for productive use are di<sup>fi</sup>cult for collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms [100, 106]. From our interviews, we veri<sup>fi</sup>ed that without su<sup>fi</sup>cient support from IT-KRC, an overly large knowledge distance undermines su<sup>fi</sup>cient mutual understanding (i.e., knowledge comprehension challenge) and poses a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant managerial challenge in knowledge integration between distant collaborators. One R&D employee at Solar also described a challenging process during case project 2, “ … we needed to design a water filtration prototype together, but we struggled in knowing what technical knowledge each of us can input and integrate. We did not use these innovation platforms and retrieval technologies to support our external knowledge sourcing. We had rounds of meetings without any progress” (IT Quote 2).

When IT-KRC is superior, increasing inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance will lead to enhanced product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. First, IT applications that provide IT-KRC enable collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms to acquire positive potentials of unique inputs from two distant R&D <sup>fi</sup>elds [25, 100]. ITs can provide collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms with high-quality sourced knowledge by scanning external environments from wide and distant external sources [26, 27, 93]. They are also instrumental in supporting the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of new product ideas and promising technologies in distant areas [30]. This creates a new environment with high knowledge variation for collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms from distant knowledge domains to think about new options, to engage di<sup>f</sup>erent parties, and to ensure that new technologies are used to their maximum e<sup>f</sup>ect in delivering innovation [26, 93]. As a result, the possibilities of knowledge recombination from collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms are increased.

Second, collaborators with distant domain knowledge encounter the knowledge comprehension challenge, which negatively a<sup>f</sup>ects product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. Such challenge can be reduced by high IT-KRC. The improved search and information processing capabilities with high speed, intensity, and directionality by high IT-KRC aid in the development of common knowledge base between partners for mutual knowledge comprehension [17, 39]. For example, innovation platform can assist <sup>fi</sup>rms in selecting and synthesizing knowledge from a wide range of sources [106]. ITs support <sup>fi</sup>rms in making practical sense of myriad external knowledge, which is not readily identi<sup>fi</sup>able without sophisticated data retrieval technology [60]. The R&D project manager at InteIT mentioned “these tools (data retrieval technology, databases and data warehouses) helped us with information search and information filtering from patent databases, web pages and scientific literature systematically. We are not only experts in our field but we know the technological developments even in our collaborator’s voice control and artificial intelligence technology fields and we can understand what they were doing and talking about. This really created a common ground and more opportunities for our innovation of skincare beauty product through our conversations of diferent technological backgrounds, which have helped both of us to identify and expand our common interests and achieve mutually beneficial outcomes” (IT Quote 4). The ability to use these ITs supports the assimilation of InteIT’s explored external knowledge from distant <sup>fi</sup>elds in their organizational memory for the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge comprehension of their collaborative partner.

Moreover, IT-KRC also resolves the knowledge integration challenge brought by the relationship between inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance and product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. IT-KRC creates the su<sup>fi</sup>cient mutual knowledge base necessary for knowledge integration to generate value-added output, which may lead to superior and novel products, that is, high product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. The mutual knowledge base o<sup>f</sup>ers rich, multiple knowledge exchange channels between collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms [3, 21]. They may have increased ability to analyze and interpret distant knowledge, thereby gaining insights into new market opportunities for new product o<sup>f</sup>erings as well as in providing opportunities to recombine distant knowledge for improved innovations [26]. These conditions can alleviate knowledge integration challenge due to an enlarged knowledge distance in the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm setting. As suggested by the R&D project manager at Solar, “these IT tools facilitated us to collect technical (R&D) information, and all these are stored in our well-developed organizational knowledge management systems. The systems enabled us to view information in intuitive and interactive dashboards, analysis, and reports. It helped us learn from our collaborator and understand each other’s technology, their pump motor technology and skills. This enlarged knowledge pool facilitated us to identify innovative and high quality knowledge creation opportunities. We could find convergence in our distinct knowledge to generate ideas of selling points and how to make use our capabilities for the integrated solar heating system” (IT Quote 5). In this case project, the two partners from two distinct knowledge domains successfully achieved high product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness for its collaborative new product (an integrated solar heating system), which has applied novel product concepts and introduced as an industry leading product. Therefore, the challenges of knowledge comprehension and integration from the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance on product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness are resolved by IT-KRC. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1: The nonlinear efect of inter-firm knowledge distance on product efectiveness is moderated by IT-KRC, in that the positive efect is enhanced while the negative efect is alleviated with high extent of IT-KRC.

Moderating Impact on Process Eficiency. Large inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance has a negative impact on process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency owing to the challenges of task coordination and communication, such as the inconsistency of the work behavior styles and the lack of a shared code of communication. IT-KRC can e<sup>f</sup>ectively moderate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of knowledge distance on product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness; however, we posit that easing the challenges of the collaboration process is unlikely. IT-KRC, which enables wide environmental scanning and the acquisition of further distant knowledge, does not directly address the high behavior interdependence nature of collaborative innovation. It does not build shared standards or <sup>fl</sup>exibility to facilitate the management of task coordination or con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution. Thus, we do not expect the IT-KRC to mitigate the negative impact of knowledge distance on process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

## IT-Enabled Inter-Firm Knowledge Exploitation Capability (IT-KIC)

Upon analyzing the six case projects, we found out that another set of IT tools consisting of a visualization suite or a 3D CAD system, a simulation and modeling tool, a visualization and prototyping tool, and business analytics was used in applying knowledge to the actual prototype and the <sup>fi</sup>nal product by collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms. Through our qualitative <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned IT-enabled inter-firm knowledge exploitation capability (IT-KIC) as the ability to use ITs to support inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge transformation and exploitation. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support that IT-KIC transforms tacit knowledge into an explicit prototype to create shared interpretations and meanings as well as facilities knowledge experimentation to alleviate knowledge integration challenge between distant external collaborators. IT-KIC also uses standardized tools and representations to deploy resources easily and quickly to alleviate behavioral complexity when coordinating group works between distant external collaborators (task coordination challenge).

Moderating Impact on Product Efectiveness. When IT-KIC is low, knowledge integration challenge occurs when knowledge distance becomes large, leading to reduced product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. The senior R&D manager at Solar and the R&D director at InteIT reported, “we experienced friction of our knowledge exchange quite often” (IT Quote8); “there certainly were some dificulties in combining knowledge” (IT Quote 9) for case projects 2 and 4 with relatively low IT-KIC (rated as 4 by our interviewees). In these projects without su<sup>fi</sup>cient support from IT-KIC, collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms must manually manage inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge exchange and verify proposed innovation solutions through experimentations. They do not have a digitalized channel to synthesize knowledge and ideas from multiple sources e<sup>f</sup>ectively. Hence, the product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of these project outputs is relatively low.

When IT-KIC is high, increasing inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance will lead to improved product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. First, IT-KIC facilitates distant collaborators by transforming and exploiting their distant knowledge inputs for further innovation opportunities. Computer simulation, business analytics, and rapid prototyping facilitate knowledge recombination and lower experimentation costs. For instance, the R&D project manager at InteIT con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that, “computer software prototypes gave us a common channel to easily verify and experiment our proposed solutions together. The alpha (beta, gamma, etc.) versions of the consumer credit information system can be tested by [company name] (the collaborator). Then we discussed with each other additional features to be added and reached consensus efectively. We also used tools called application simulation software to efectively simulate how the next version will behave once these changes have been made … It lowers the cost of experimentation and also can help us better converge each other’s idea or solution” (IT Quote 10). The knowledge from distant collaborators can be combined and incorporated into additional experimentations e<sup>f</sup>ectively [9, 58]. Such integration enables collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms to mobilize and deploy distant knowledge in support of innovation activities [25, 106]. It increases the opportunities to discover innovative and high-quality solutions from collaborators’ distinct knowledge inputs. Hence, IT-KIC strengthens the bene<sup>fi</sup>t brought by the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance, thereby leading to higher product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness.

IT-KIC also helps transform the distant tacit knowledge into an explicit prototype, which facilitates inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge integration cross boundaries [17, 100]. IT, such as the use of illustrations produced by business analytics and simulation tools, allows team members to easily share their interpretations of information and make consensual development highly e<sup>f</sup>ective [9, 105]. The use of CAD, visualization, and simulation tools a<sup>f</sup>ords a standardized architecture with extensive knowledge exchange capability between <sup>fi</sup>rms in the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration setting [43]. Team members can visualize their ideas or concepts to a working artifact or product prototype, make it available to all collaborators, and have it ready for knowledge integration. Such tools enable distant collaborators to leverage complementary assets and combine distant knowledge [100, 117]. As recalled by one R&D employee at Solar on case project 1, “we used the rapid prototyping technique to quickly engineer an initial model of the integrated solar heating system. The digital prototype explicitly showed the details of our proposed working concepts. Then [company name] (the collaborator) can integrate their pump chamber design onto the digital prototype for further development” (IT Quote 11). The challenge of knowledge integration due to the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance on product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness is addressed by IT-KIC. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2: The nonlinear efect of inter-firm knowledge distance on product efectiveness is moderated by IT-KIC, in that the positive efect is enhanced while the negative efect is alleviated with high extent of IT-KIC.

Moderating Impact on Process Eficiency. When IT-KIC is low, task coordination challenge occurs between collaborators when knowledge distance becomes large, leading to reduced process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Di<sup>f</sup>erent technological backgrounds, experiences, and skill sets (i.e., knowledge distance) may lead to a lack of a shared code for information processing and an inconsistency in work behavior styles. This situation causes the coordination con<sup>fl</sup>icts of interdependent works between collaborators [74]. The coordination challenge is salient as various complicated tasks are involved, and a <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot command another without a hierarchical control mechanism as in the case of a collaborative innovation project [28]. For instance, the R&D project manager at InteIT elaborated on case project 4, “the consumer credit information system module we completed and the parts they completed need to be updated with each other at any time. The inconsistency of the work behavior styles and various complex tasks involved lead to coordination dificulties. We did not have a unified coordination, unified planning, and the responsibility is unclear at that time” (IT Quote 13). Consequently, InteIT experienced a less e<sup>fi</sup>cient collaborative innovation process with its collaborator.

Unlike IT-KRC, IT-KIC can address such task coordination challenge in the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm context. When IT-KIC is high, collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms can maintain satisfactory process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency with increased inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance. IT-KIC fosters the use of standardized tools and representations such that collaborating <sup>fi</sup>rms can quickly mobilize and deploy resources through improved information visibility and enhanced <sup>fl</sup>exibility [89]. For instance, the use of CAD has made manufacturing and product development capability highly modular and <sup>fl</sup>exible [43]. It assists in digitizing a new product’s design and making it available throughout the innovation process. Collaborative partners can rapidly develop and jointly modify their mutual models and prototypes, from product conception to <sup>fi</sup>nal assembly [46]. Thus, IT capability facilitates the management of interdependence by properly routing available information between collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms [70]. In addition, IT-KIC provides a common channel for two parties to verify and experiment on proposed solutions, as well as discuss and reach consensus e<sup>fi</sup>ciently. Computer simulation and rapid prototyping lower the cost of experimentation and the time required to coordinate and test granular tasks from both parties [100]. This common channel allows collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms to focus on their respective tasks, which signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduces the time needed for task coordination and makes the innovation process highly e<sup>fi</sup>cient [17, 46]. Our proposition is also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by one R&D project manager at ConsumerPro for its case project 6 with relatively high process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, “the visualized digital air freshener prototype helped us organize a large project into a list of components that need to be completed. We used it to clarify the requirements from each side and input additional features needed, which are available to [company name] (the collaborator) synchronously. We managed to overcome the dificulties in task coordination due to our diferences in R&D skills. This indeed leaded to eficient progress during our collaborative innovation process” (IT Quote 15). Therefore, IT-KIC resolves task coordination challenge and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts distant <sup>fi</sup>rms on process e<sup>fi</sup>- ciency by creating modular and <sup>fl</sup>exible prototypes and lowering the costs for experimentation, which consequently leads to process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 3: The negative efect of inter-firm knowledge distance on process eficiency is mitigated with high extent of IT-KIC.

## IT-Enabled Inter-Firm Social Integration Capability (IT-SIC)

According to our interviewees, another set of ITs in the form of email, Web/video conferencing, electronic message software, and collaborative software was instrumental in cultivating social interactions and connectedness between collaborative R&D employees in two distant <sup>fi</sup>rms. Based on our qualitative <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we termed the capability provided by these ITs as IT-enabled inter-firm social integration capability (IT-SIC), which refers to the ability to use ITs to promote inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm connectedness, interaction, and communication. Our interviewees con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that IT-enabled capability facilitates interactions to create connectedness, which alleviates communication challenge between distant external collaborators.

Moderating Impact on Product Efectiveness. IT-SIC allows collaborating <sup>fi</sup>rms to have highly interactive communication and achieve mutual understanding but does not stimulate innovative and high-quality ideas that arise from knowledge disparities. IT-SIC does not enhance the knowledge pool for collaboration or address the knowledge comprehension or integration challenges between collaborators. IT for online communication only provides collaborating <sup>fi</sup>rms with easy and frequent communication, rather than actually assisting learning. To enhance the understanding and learning of a distant technological <sup>fi</sup>eld, the increased common knowledge base of recipients or the transformation of the tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge are required, making it ready for knowledge integration [66, 57]. However, the role of IT-SIC focuses on the social aspect of the collaborative relationship by building connectedness and cultivating social capital between collaborators. Thus, frequent communication may not contribute to a common base of distant technologies for knowledge comprehension and enhance the combination of knowledge from distant sources. When collaborating <sup>fi</sup>rms possess distinct knowledge expertise, having greater frequency alone does not augment their understanding of the technological issues. Thus, we did not expect IT-SIC to moderate the impact of knowledge distance on product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness.

Moderating Impact on Process Eficiency. When IT-SIC is low, the collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms rely heavily on face-to-face contacts for interaction, which easily facilitates frequent and convenient communication. When knowledge distance becomes large, <sup>fi</sup>rms from highly di<sup>f</sup>erent technological disciplines tend to have di<sup>f</sup>erent communication and working styles [98]. Such di<sup>f</sup>erences may cause communication con<sup>fl</sup>icts in this high behavioral interdependence and virtual communication context [100]. It may further delay e<sup>fi</sup>cient knowledge exchange and the timely attainment of consensus. The R&D project manager at InteIT recalled, “we relied more on face-to-face meetings and it was not a smooth process. We did not act as one team and had very diferent working styles. I remember our conflicts over the central module design of the credit system delayed the progress quite a while” (IT Quote 18). The communication challenge hence occurs between InteIT and its collaborator and makes achieving high process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency di<sup>fi</sup>cult.

When IT-SIC is high, it can alleviate the communication challenge between distant partners by signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increasing the communication frequency and involvement of partners. R&D employees from both collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms can exchange information with each other any time during the collaborative innovation process [91]. It helps augment the social capital of <sup>fi</sup>rms by creating electronic communication channels between collaborators to facilitate the socialization process [53, 59]. One R&D employee at InteIT con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed, “We used an application called yammer to communicate with [company name] (the collaborator). This private social network tool also allowed us segregate relevant information to and share posts with specific team members … We launched regular videoconference meetings to distribute information on the project development and its implementation. This channel allows eficient involvement of all team members from diferent companies to elicit our opinion and build team spirits. The understanding and reflections from these interactions foster our relationship … ” (IT Quote 19). InteIT hence managed to successfully complete this project under an economic budget and a project schedule. IT-SIC also expedites inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm communication and interaction by bridging physical, temporal, and organizational barriers [33, 89, 113]. Media synchronicity theory suggests that ITs with enhanced information transmission and processing capabilities support a shared pattern of coordinated behaviors among individuals when they work together [39, 100]. Emails and collaborative technologies support e<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>le sharing and provide a repository that records the process of each collaborator (e.g., date, sequence, and author of each contribution), as well as provides easy access and retrieval from the repository [69]. IT-SIC allows the transfer of idiosyncratic and privileged information and enables <sup>fi</sup>rms to develop in-depth knowledge about their partners [76]. Therefore, IT-SIC resolves the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm communication di<sup>fi</sup>culty caused by R&D knowledge distance between collaborators, leading to a smooth and e<sup>fi</sup>cient collaborative innovation process. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 4: The negative efect of inter-firm distance on process eficiency is mitigated with high extent of IT-SIC.

## STAGE 3: Verifying Hypotheses via Survey

## Measurement and Validation Process

A quantitative <sup>fi</sup>eld study was conducted using a survey methodology to test our hypotheses. We validated and adapted prior measures to operationalize IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities through our qualitative study. We followed Joshi et al.’s [57] mapping procedure to map IT tools and IT capabilities. To insure con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the categories, we employed two external experts who were well versed in knowledge management and IT. Based on interview transcripts, we (i.e., two authors and two external experts) went through iterations of individual to collective mapping until we reached consensus (in Appendix C.2). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, innovation platform was included in IT-KRC as suggested by our interviewees, the item of enterprise systems was removed, and data retrieval technology was used as the more popular term instead of data reading technology. Document management systems weree removed since it is just one type of database focusing on document management. The associated IT tools in our study hence include innovation platforms, data retrieval technologies, and databases (data warehouses). For IT-KIC, data mining and decision support systems were removed from Joshi et al.’s [57] list because these were not used during the collaborative innovation projects in our context. In our study, IT-KIC is evident in CAD systems, simulation and modeling tools, visualization and prototyping tools, and business analytics. For IT-SIC, our interviewees advised that collaborative software is a more appropriate and widely used term than groupware. Email is separated from messaging tools as they serve di<sup>f</sup>erent functions. The IT tools for IT-SIC include conferencing tools, messaging tools, email, and collaborative software. This gave us a re<sup>fi</sup>ned list of three categories of IT tools. We then measured IT-KRC, IT-KIC and IT-SIC by using a coherent and parsimonious formative model of the usage extent of these identi<sup>fi</sup>ed IT tools, given the composite and multidimensional nature of IT capabilities [29]. Besides IT-KRC, IT-KIC, and IT-SIC, we adapted the rest of the measurement scales based on previously validated questions. Following prior studies, product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness contains the quality and innovativeness dimensions of the collaborative innovation project outcome [83]. Process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency speci<sup>fi</sup>cally involves the time to market and project cost of the collaborative innovation project [63, 83]. The detailed measurements can be found in Appendix C.3.

We used the following steps to validate the survey instrument. First, we conducted a two-stage Q-sorting using ten Ph.D. students to ensure the conceptual validity. The results of one unlabeled (90 percent accuracy) and one labeled (94 percent accuracy) demonstrate good quality of the measures. The resulting questionnaire was then peer reviewed by several faculty members to correct any problems related to the questions framing and phrasing. A bilingual research associate <sup>fi</sup>rst translated the English version of the questionnaire into Chinese. To ensure comparability and equivalence in meaning, we adopted back-translation method [14]. The authors, another professor and two senior doctoral students then compared the original questionnaire with the translated one. Changes were made when necessary. The Chinese questionnaire was then pre-tested by R&D managers involved in managing collaborative innovation projects for face and content validity, clarity of instructions, and formats. We made minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to address any concerns raised.

## Survey Collection

We administered a cross-sectional survey to high-tech <sup>fi</sup>rms in China by mail. To make sure that <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample represent various industries, we followed the procedure of Rai and Tang [90] and chose high-tech <sup>fi</sup>rms from four industries that have a broad presence in the Chinese economy (electronic and other electrical equipment, chemical and pharmaceutical, industrial and commercial machinery equipment, and fabricated metal and other material industries). We randomly selected 750 <sup>fi</sup>rms for the survey from the high-tech <sup>fi</sup>rm directory provided by the China Science and Technology Commission. We employed the key informant approach, which collects data from one R&D manager for each <sup>fi</sup>rm, because managers are likely to be the most knowledgeable about the items in our questionnaire [88]. The managers were asked to identify a recently completed collaborative innovation project and answer the questionnaire based on their activities with their project partner (e.g., the IT tools used in the collaboration). Responses from <sup>fi</sup>rms that did not have a collaborative innovation project were eliminated from the sample. To increase the response rate, a follow-up letter was sent one month after the <sup>fi</sup>rst mailed survey to the <sup>fi</sup>rms from which we had not yet received responses. A total of 281 surveys were returned, of which 258 were useable for next analysis. We achieved an e<sup>f</sup>ective response rate of 34.4 percent. The R&D managers worked for 7.6 years in their position (SD = 5.3) on average. Appendix C.4 lists the summary of industry distribution.

To evaluate the issue of nonresponse bias and representativeness of the responding <sup>fi</sup>rms, we conducted two analyses [88]. First, we checked if early and late respondents di<sup>f</sup>er in their responses on principal constructs and their demographic characteristics. The early respondents responded in the <sup>fi</sup>rst two weeks. Our t-tests between the means of the two groups had no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence. Second, we compared the di<sup>f</sup>erence between the expected and observed number of responses. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test showed no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence across the four industries.

## Structural Model Tests

Given that our moderating variables (i.e., IT-KRC, IT-KIC, and IT-SIC) are formative, we <sup>fi</sup>rst calculated latent variable scores,<sup>4</sup> and we then used those scores in hierarchical regression analysis in Stata to test hypotheses with two-tailed 5 percent signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level. To reduce the chance of multicollinearity, we applied mean centering before creating the interaction terms [5].

We reported adjusted $\dot { R } ^ { 2 }$ to assess the explanatory power of the model. We entered control variables in Step 1, all centered independent variables in Step 2, the hypothesized moderating relationships in Step 3, and the non-hypothesized moderating relationships in Step 4. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant change in adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ between the two steps analysis was assessed using the change in F-statistic. Table 5 shows the path coe<sup>fi</sup>cient and the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of these four models. The change in $R ^ { 2 }$ value between Steps 2 and 3 was 7.4 percent $( F { \mathrm { ~ c h a n g e } } = 1 1 . 1 6 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ for product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and 4.9 percent $( F { \mathrm { c h a n g e } } = 5 . 2 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ for process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Thus, the outcomes of the interaction terms could be interpreted. Our test results supported hypothesis 1, 3, and 4 and rejected hypothesis 2. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the test results did not change in Step 4, which shows the robustness of our results. To further interpret the moderation on inverted U-shape (i.e., hypothesis 1), we conducted further tests according to Haans et al. [48]. The results indicate that the turning point of the inverted U-shape in the moderated relationship moved to the right and the shape of the inverted U-shape in the moderated relationship was <sup>fl</sup>attened as IT-KRC increases. ## Discussions and Conclusion

Situated in the context of collaborative innovation, our work, as an initial and original e<sup>f</sup>ort, constructed and validated a theoretical model of IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities that play di<sup>f</sup>erentiated moderating roles on the relationship of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance and collaborative innovation outcomes. The survey <sup>fi</sup>ndings generally supported our theorization. We observed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT-KRC on moderating the relationship between inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance and product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. IT-KIC and IT-SIC positively moderate the relationship between inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance and process e<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT-KIC was detected on the relationship between inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance and product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. A plausible explanation is that IT-KIC helps transform tacit knowledge into an explicit prototype for inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge integration and may lead to a potential trap of knowledge exploitation without fully understanding tacit knowledge from another discipline. With the support of IT-KIC, collaborators can e<sup>f</sup>ectively utilize it without the need to access the knowledge embodied within the prototype or the working artifact. The integration of specialized knowledge may impede the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of the <sup>fi</sup>nal knowledge combination outcome. Moreover, some contextualized cultural in<sup>fl</sup>uences may also explain this unsupported hypothesis. Nonaka and Takeuchi [77] corroborated that Western culture considerably focuses on externalization, which heavily emphasizes explicit knowledge and combination. Eastern culture emphasizes the internalization and sharing of tacit knowledge without explication for continuous innovation. This focus is due to the epistemological di<sup>f</sup>erence whereby “Westerners tend to emphasize explicit knowledge and the Easterners tend to stress tacit knowledge” [77, p. 243]. IT-KIC is instrumental in supporting inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm transformation and exploitation by externalizing, illustrating specialized knowledge, and making tacit understandings of speci<sup>fi</sup>cations explicit. Easterners may require less extensive articulation and are more oriented to exchange tacit knowledge. Hence, IT-KIC may not be e<sup>f</sup>ective in transferring and integrating tacit knowledge to meet the knowledge integration requirements of the collaborators in the Chinese context.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study has a few limitations, which provide directions for future research. First, crosssectional data were used to empirically assess our theoretical model. Although the proposed research hypotheses were derived theoretically, the results still re<sup>fl</sup>ect associations rather than causality. Future studies could adopt a longitudinal focus to yield insights into the exact nature of IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities, how they develop and evolve, and how they can be leveraged for improved collaborative innovation performance. We also acknowledge the limitation of the survey method (e.g., desirability bias and vested interest of respondents) and encourage the examination of this research topic through other research methods in the future. Second, our measurements of IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities only captured the extent of IT tool usage, which are consistent with one stream of the IT capability literature, which looked at the extent usage of IT tools (e.g., [57, 89]). Future research could explore other ways of measuring inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm IT capabilities and attempt to measure the e<sup>f</sup>ective use of IT tools or their usage for business processes. Third, our study does not focus on the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT on collaborative innovation project performance or the interdependent relationships of di<sup>f</sup>erent IT capabilities. We developed a managerial solution to extract the potential value and mitigate the challenges induced by being collaborative with a distant partner. Hence, we focused on examining their independent moderating roles. However, we acknowledge the potential direct e<sup>f</sup>ects or interdependent relationships and encourage future research to further explore IT capabilities in this important context. Fourth, in this study, we did not measure or identify the circumstances for <sup>fi</sup>rms failing to achieve innovation outperformance because it cannot well assimilate the knowledge in its own domain. This one is another interesting angle for future research to compare the assimilation ability of a <sup>fi</sup>rm for its own domain and for the distant domain. Lastly, as the focus of this study is only on inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance, future research may explore the collaborative innovation context through other related concepts, that is, knowledge complementarity, knowledge diversity, and knowledge heterogeneity, as well as may compare the di<sup>f</sup>erent in<sup>fl</sup>uential roles of these various constructs.

## Implications for Theory

This research contributes to the literature on IT capability and collaborative innovation by building a theory on the roles of IT in addressing the challenges induced by inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance in the context of collaborative innovation in following three aspects: (1) understanding the collaborative innovation context and its challenges, (2) exploring di<sup>f</sup>erent IT capabilities, and (3) adapting absorptive capacity in the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm innovation context. First, this study adds to the literature by understanding the collaborative innovation context and exploring the solutions to resolve the challenges brought by large inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance from the IT capability perspective. Collaborative innovation entails high technology interdependence between partners, which poses signi<sup>fi</sup>cant knowledge comprehension and knowledge integration challenges, while high behavior interdependence intensi<sup>fi</sup>es task coordination and communication challenges. Prior studies have explored the moderators from the organizational characteristic perspective, such as its cumulative R&D capability or network position on eliminating the technology integration challenge (e.g., [44, 78]), but supporting results were not found. The challenges have not been addressed thoroughly, and no other solutions have been proposed from the organizational managerial strategy perspective. The present study systematically establishes that knowledge capabilities resolving collaborative innovation challenges can be created and augmented through IT. It further adds to the discussion of divergence versus convergence tension embedded inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaborative relationships: divergence promotes the discovery of new ideas, but it also can lead to di<sup>fi</sup>culties in integrating the ideas, while convergence promotes integration but reduces discovery [4, 56]. Therefore, our study extends the extant work by giving attention to the tension-ridden process of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance on product and process performance.

Second, this study adds to the IT and innovation literature by identifying and di<sup>f</sup>erentiating the most suitable IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities (i.e., IT-KRC, IT- KIC, and IT-SIC) for enhancing the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and addressing the challenges caused by inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance in the collaborative innovation context. Our literature review on the streams of IT capability in inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration and the innovation of <sup>fi</sup>rms has revealed the lack of in-depth discussion on the di<sup>f</sup>erent types of IT capabilities and the associated roles. We hence <sup>fi</sup>ll the gap and broaden the theoretical base of IT capability and its relationship with external knowledge absorption for inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm learning and innovation [39, 93]. To this end, our study expands the extant literature on the business value of IT and broadens the theoretical base of IT and its relationship with inter-organizational collaborative innovation (e.g., [20, 82]).

Third, this work contributes to the absorptive capacity literature. It adapts absorptive capacity theory in inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm innovation context by conceptualizing the ITenabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities and identi<sup>fi</sup>es new roles played by IT capabilities in collaborative innovation. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings in collaborative innovation are di<sup>f</sup>erent from prior <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the intra-<sup>fi</sup>rm innovation context [55, 57, 93, 99]. Such di<sup>f</sup>erences indicate that the salience of certain IT-enabled knowledge capabilities can vary in di<sup>f</sup>erent innovation contexts. Our study paves the way to a profound understanding and rich appreciation of its role in the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm relationship. The empirical results of the model a<sup>fi</sup>rm the conceptualization of IT capabilities as crucial inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities in the collaborative innovation context. In doing so, we tie absorptive capacity closer to the nomological network of IS research and reiterate the importance of IT use as an important component of organizational absorptive capacity [93].

## Implications for Practice

Our study also has important implications for practitioners. Despite the widespread belief that collaborating with distant partners is imperative for inspiring novel innovations, limited practical guidance is available to assist managers in understanding and managing the associated bene<sup>fi</sup>t and challenges. First, this study <sup>fi</sup>rst helps <sup>fi</sup>rm managers become aware of the challenges and negative impacts in addition to the bene<sup>fi</sup>t obtained through distant knowledge combination in collaborative innovation. To avoid negative spirals that reduce the potential that knowledge distance o<sup>f</sup>ers, managers must understand the nature of the tensions and their e<sup>f</sup>ects and how to e<sup>f</sup>ectively function with the embedded ones.

Second, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study provide practical solutions for managers to strategically manage a collaborative innovation project and mindfully invest in IT applications. IT-KRC can enhance the positive impact from an inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance, and IT-KIC and IT-SIC can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly mitigate the negative impact from an inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance. Thus, a list of IT tools is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for managers to address the challenges and bene<sup>fi</sup>t from an inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance. For instance, <sup>fi</sup>rm managers can reach the maximum bene<sup>fi</sup>t of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance by prioritizing the investment on related IT tools, such as an innovation platform and data retrieval technology, to enhance knowledge recombination bene<sup>fi</sup>t and avoid knowledge comprehension and knowledge integration challenges. In our interviewed case project 4, the case <sup>fi</sup>rm is an integrated IT solutions provider that specializes in cloud computing and big data analytics. Meanwhile, its collaborator is in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial sector that focuses on credit rating and <sup>fi</sup>nancial securities. According to our interviewees, they have relatively high inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance but managed to achieve high product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness for its collaboratively developed new product, that is, a consumer credit information system. One R&D employee at InteIT commented on the role of IT-KRC, “ … data retrieval and other knowledge sourcing technologies helped us identify and collect knowledge from our partner’s financial credit rating system area with fast pace. We now understand previous unfamiliar rating evaluation policies and financial security issues, etc. … With the enlarged knowledge base, we can better know how to synergize our distinct fields for new solutions and recombine our distinct R&D knowledge inputs in this project. So the developed consumer credit information system performance was excellent, with high quality and innovative design and idea” (IT Quote 3).

Firms that experience task coordination challenges with a collaborator during a collaborative innovation project should exploit the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of ITs, such as simulation and modeling tools. From our qualitative <sup>fi</sup>ndings, one interviewed R&D employee at ConsumerPro commented that, “we had a better idea of our collaborator’s requirements and current tasks with the illustration of a CAD design of the new product, even when their requirements changed quickly. The CAD drawing was altered accordingly and be demonstrated to us to facilitate the tasks on our side. We can also access and edit at the same time, which makes the coordination easier” (IT Quote 14).

Firms that wish to cultivate a mutual understanding with a partner can deploy communication ITs to address communication challenges during the collaboration process. Management must be receptive to opportunities and create a culture for exploiting IT capabilities for a collaborative innovation project. Based on our qualitative study, the R&D project manager at ConsumerPro claimed: “We used collaborative software, like cloud-based file sharing tool. The service we used is called Box to share project documents with [company name] (the collaborator). Another useful tool we used is called RealtimeBoard, which also made our communication more convenient and eficient and it speeds up the exchange of information. When we have a problem at any time, we can discuss with our collaborator in real-time and resolve conflicts together. We know each other much better and we also had small talks to maintain a strong relationship” (IT Quote 20).

## Conclusion

Collaborative innovation has become an important strategic initiative for <sup>fi</sup>rms to stay in an increasingly competitive environment. Given the growing trend and the remaining challenges, there is a clear need to understand the mechanisms that enable <sup>fi</sup>rms to create value through collaborative innovation. This paper takes a contextual look into the IT conditions under which the innovation process would be e<sup>fi</sup>cient and the product would be e<sup>f</sup>ective when collaborating with a distant partner. This e<sup>f</sup>ort is the <sup>fi</sup>rst step toward developing an insightful theory into the tension of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge distance and how IT capabilities can e<sup>f</sup>ectively support. With a multi-stage multi-method approach, we discovered how to leverage di<sup>f</sup>erent IT-enabled inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge capabilities to enhance the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of product e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and surmount the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency challenges in the fundamental process of collaboration with knowledge distant partners. We hope that our theorizing and empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings set the stage for the future research aimed at understanding how <sup>fi</sup>rms create value through collaborative innovation.

## About the Authors

Tingru Cui (tingru.cui@unimelb.edu.au) is a Senior Lecturer in the School of Computing and Information Systems, at the University of Melbourne, Australia. She holds a Ph.D. in Information Systems from the National University of Singapore. Dr. Cui’s research interests include digital innovation, IT-enabled innovation, and data analytics. Her work has been published in such journals as Information & Management and Journal of Global Information Management, and in the proceedings of conferences such as International Conference on Information Systems.

Yu Tong (tong\_yu@zju.edu.cn; corresponding author) is a ZJU-100 Young Professor in the Department of Data Science and Engineering Management, School of Management, at Zhejiang University, China. Her research interests cover the use and management of information systems in organizations, healthcare information systems, and emerging forms of IT-enabled collaboration. Her work has been published in such journals as Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and Information & Management.

Hock-Hai Teo (teohh@comp.nus.edu.sg) is a Provost’s Chair Professor of Information Systems in the Department of Information Systems and Analytics at the School of Computing, National University of Singapore. His research interests lie in the areas of IT innovation adoption, assimilation and impacts, information privacy, electronic market institutions, and virtual communities. Dr. Teo has published broadly in journals such as the ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interactions, MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Information Systems Research. He is serving or had served on the editorial board of Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, and others.

Jizhen Li (lijzh@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn) is an Associate Professor in the School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University, China, and the director at the Research Center for Competitive Dynamics and Innovation Strategy. He holds a Ph.D. in Management Science from the Tsinghua University. Dr. Li’s research interests include management of technological innovation; science and technology policies; as well as project management. His work has been published in such journals as Strategic Management Journal, Research Policy, and Information & Management.
