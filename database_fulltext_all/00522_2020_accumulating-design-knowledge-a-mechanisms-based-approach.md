# Accumulating Design Knowledge: A Mechanisms-Based Approach

Hannes Rothe<sup>1</sup>, Lauri Wessel<sup>2</sup>, Ana Paula Barquet<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Corresponding author, Freie Universität Berlin, Germany, hannes.rothe@fu-berlin.de <sup>2</sup>University of Bremen, Germany, lauri.wessel@uni-bremen.de <sup>3</sup>Technical University Berlin, Germany, a.barquet@campus.tu-berlin.de

## Abstract

We develop an empirically grounded understanding of how design knowledge accumulates over time. Drawing from theory on knowledge creation, we conceptualize accumulation along the goals and scope of knowledge in DSR as a distinct knowledge creation problem. Through two empirical studies, we theorize knowledge accumulation in DSR by unpacking (1) three knowledge creation mechanisms, and (2) explaining how their interplay forms different patterns of knowledge creation over time. We contribute a theoretical framework conducive to integrating knowledge produced through different methods by introducing the knowledge creation processes as a distinct unit of analysis in DSR and by pointing out that extant procedural models may need revision to account for the continuous knowledge creation occurring in potentially multiyear DSR projects.

Keywords: Knowledge Accumulation, Design Knowledge, Knowledge Contribution, Design Science Research, Information Network, Mechanisms

Alan R. Hevner was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on November 30, 2017, and underwent three revisions.

## 1 Introduction

In recent years, design science research (DSR) has moved toward the center of the IS discipline (Goes, 2014; Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Markus, Majchrzak, & Gasser, 2002; Peffers, Tuunanen, & Niehaves, 2018; Walls, Widmeyer, & El Sawy, 1992). Substantive methodological advancements such as the explication of methods for doing (Hevner et al. 2004; Peffers et al., 2007; Sein et al., 2011) and theorizing DSR (Gregor & Jones, 2007; Gregory & Muntermann, 2014; Mandviwalla, 2015) lie at the core of this development, as they provide valuable standards for producing design knowledge (Rai, 2017). These recent discussions point to the importance of considering how knowledge is accumulated through DSR studies (henceforth “design knowledge”). The importance of this issue stems from the fact that the proliferation of various methods for doing and theorizing DSR calls for scrutinizing how knowledge is systematically developed and tested (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Gregor & Jones, 2007).

We set out to address this issue by conceptualizing accumulation of design knowledge as knowledge creation (Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka & von Krogh, 2009). The notion of “knowledge creation” stems from research on knowledge management where the concept captures how organizations create and revise a knowledge base (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Chatterjee & Sarker, 2013; Pentland, 1995). Building on this idea, we cast accumulation of design knowledge as a distinct knowledge creation problem arising at the intersection of organizational knowledge creation theory and DSR. In particular, organizational knowledge creation theory and DSR complement each other in that we can understand DSR projects as temporary organizations (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Mathiassen, 2002; Otto & Osterle, 2012; Rai, 2017; vom Brocke & Lippe, 2010).

Furthermore, accumulation of design knowledge can be seen as creation of explicit knowledge existing in or emerging from problem and solution spaces (Baskerville et al., 2017). However, both literatures also contradict each other in terms of the levels to which they apply, the epistemic stance they take, and their emphasis on context-specific knowledge. These contradictions render DSR projects an interesting case for exploring knowledge creation because they operate under different scope conditions than commercial organizations. Thus, there is the potential not only to investigate knowledge creation in DSR but to explore knowledge creation theory in a new setting, particularly with regard to microlevel mechanisms that explain knowledge creation and that help DSR scholars reuse, develop, and communicate the knowledge they create. We thus link knowledge creation with DSR by treating it as a unit of analysis in its own right and asking the following research questions:

RQ1: What mechanisms explain knowledge creation in DSR?

RQ2: How do these mechanisms impact accumulation of design knowledge over time?

We conducted two studies to investigate these questions. The first study was an in-depth case study of three DSR projects that we worked with for more than two years (Study 1). Study 1 addressed knowledge creation at the level of a DSR project and enabled identification of three mechanisms of knowledge creation in DSR, i.e., “injection,” “folding,” and “enhancement.” Study 2 was an information networks study on knowledge contributions that used text mining to reveal three patterns reflecting how these mechanisms built on one another to create knowledge that unfolded across publications. Study 2 thus traces knowledge creation at the level of an accumulation process comprising multiple publications through which researchers draw on and extend a knowledge base. We reveal three patterns that illustrate how our mechanisms interact and associate each pattern with distinct impacts on the accumulation process.

Our study contributes to DSR in four important ways. First, to our knowledge, this is the first study to theorize the process of knowledge accumulation in DSR. Conceptualizing these phenomena through a knowledge creation lens (Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka & von Krogh, 2009) enabled development of a theoretical framework that explains knowledge creation in DSR.

Second, introducing knowledge creation as a distinct unit of analysis in DSR renders extant discussions about appropriate means for doing DSR a question about the levels at which arguments on deduction versus induction apply (Iivari, 2015) and not a “once and for all” question. Third, we show empirically that knowledge creation is a continuous process that may prompt reconsideration of procedural models (Peffers et al., 2007; Sein et al., 2011) in order to support continuous knowledge creation. Finally, we empirically explore mechanisms of knowledge creation operating under different scope conditions than those of conventional knowledge creation theory.

## 2 Toward an Understanding of the Accumulation of Design Knowledge as a Knowledge Creation Problem

The goal of this section is to suggest that accumulation of design knowledge is a distinct knowledge creation problem. We set the stage for this idea by first introducing the notion of “knowledge creation” and discussing related IS literature. We then relate theory on knowledge creation to DSR by discussing the complementarities and contradictions between these streams of research.

## 2.1 Theory of Knowledge Creation

Knowledge, “a justified belief that increases an entity's capacity for effective action” (Alavi & Leidner, 2001), has attracted significant scholarly attention in fields such as information systems (IS) (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Chatterjee & Sarker, 2013), strategic management (Argote, McEvily, & Reagans, 2003; Grant, 1996; Kogut & Zander, 1992), and organization science (Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka & von Krogh, 2009). Research in these areas builds on the idea that firm performance depends on how the members of a firm create, combine, and use tangible resources (Barney, 1991; Freiling, Gersch, & Goeke, 2008; Penrose, 1959). Thus, the capacity to effectively manage knowledge holds the promise of sustaining competitive advantage (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Spender, 2014). Knowledge creation as one area of knowledge management has received significant scholarly attention (Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka & Toyama, 2003; Nonaka, Toyama, & Konno, 2000; Spender, 2014) resulting in a distinct theory of organizational knowledge creation (for an overview see, e.g., Nonaka & von Krogh, 2009).<sup>1</sup>

Knowledge creation concerns the development of new content as well as the replacement of existing content within an organization’s knowledge base (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Chatterjee & Sarker, 2013; Pentland, 1995). Even though researchers generally agree that such modifications are characterized by interplays of tacit knowledge, which is hard to explicate and codify, and codified, explicit knowledge (Chatterjee & Sarker, 2013; Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka & von Krogh, 2009), there is a tendency among researchers to value tacit over explicit knowledge and to focus on the former type (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). Some of the central topics of IS research on knowledge creation are thus related to how IS affect the processing of complex information (Li & Kettinger, 2006), how IS relate to risks inherent to knowledge creation processes (Chen & Edgington, 2005; Yang et al., 2017), and how digital technologies change knowledge creation (Kallinikos & Tempini, 2014; Lyytinen, Yoo, & Boland Jr., 2016; Shollo & Galliers, 2016; von Krogh, 2012). A general contention in much of this research is that IS improve various knowledge creation outcomes (Eservel, 2014; Faraj et al., 2016; Kleis et al., 2012; Ransbotham & Kane, 2011) so that models of knowledge creation now assign IS a central strategic role (Datta, 2007; Lee & Choi, 2003).

Widespread interest in knowledge creation has led to development of a “theory of organizational knowledge creation” (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka & Toyama, 2003; Nonaka et al., 2000). We build on it to conceptualize accumulation of design knowledge for several reasons. First, the theory highlights knowledge as dynamic, continually revised, and linked to an overall knowledge base (Nonaka 1994), which fits well with recent advancements in DSR that have highlighted how design knowledge builds on and extends the DSR knowledge base (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Gregor & Jones, 2007; Gregory & Muntermann, 2014). Second, DSR is typically organized in research projects (e.g., vom Brocke & Lippe, 2010) and projects can typically be seen as temporary organizations across which knowledge arises and develops. Third, organizational knowledge creation theory sees knowledge creation as a collective activity that aligns well with the idea that DSR teams cycle through designing and evaluating artifacts iteratively over time (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Rai, 2017). Finally, the “wicked” character of many design tasks calls for integration of design knowledge by project team members from different disciplines (Iivari, 2015) or communities-of-practice (Markus et al., 2002; Otto & Osterle, 2012).

Organizational knowledge creation theory places the “SECI” process at its center (Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). It comprises a circular sequence of four activities: sharing tacit knowledge within groups (socialization), articulating this tacit knowledge (externalization), systematizing and applying explicit knowledge (combination), and acquiring new tacit knowledge by putting the external knowledge into practice (internalization). A mechanism coined “ba” (for “place”) is cast as the driver of the aforementioned dynamics (Nonaka et al., 2000). “Ba” can be seen as an “existential place where participants share their contexts and create new meaning through interaction” (Nonaka & Toyama, 2003, p. 7) with the intent to create sustainable competitive advantage for the organization (Nonaka & Teece, 2001; Nonaka et al., 2000).

Similar to empirical studies on knowledge creation, SECI strongly emphasizes tacit knowledge (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Such knowledge is particularly prone to drive competitive advantage but it must be formalized and transformed into “explicit knowledge” so that an organization can function (Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka & von Krogh, 2009). SECI thus integrates tacit with explicit knowledge in seeking to understand how the former becomes manifest in the latter.

## 2.2 Complementarities Between Knowledge Creation Theory and DSR

As indicated before, a cornerstone of knowledge creation theory is the distinction between tacit and explicit knowledge (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Chatterjee & Sarker, 2013; Nonaka & von Krogh, 2009). This distinction matters for DSR insofar as recent discussions about the relationship between DSR and the types of knowledge that it draws on and yields (Baskerville, Kaul, & Storey, 2015; Winter, 2008) can be understood as conversations about explicit knowledge.<sup>2</sup> The rationale is that the codification of knowledge, which characterizes explicit knowledge and its communication (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Peffers et al., 2007), is evident in recent discussions in DSR. Scholars in DSR extend, revise, or enrich general design knowledge by explicating knowledge contributions (KC) that address rigor and relevance (Pettigrew, 1997; van de Ven, 2018), meet the needs of business and society (Hevner et al., 2004), and are novel and interesting (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Truex, Holmström, & Keil, 2006). Several established procedural models call for grounding DSR projects by reviewing scientific literature and justifying suitable kernel theory (Hevner et al., 2004; Mandviwalla, 2015; Peffers et al., 2007; Sein et al., 2011; Walls et al., 1992; Gregory & Muntermann, 2014). For KCs to be accepted, they usually need to be published in peerreviewed journals, conference proceedings, or research reports. Hence, creating KCs calls for codification; i.e., a process of creating explicit knowledge.

Design knowledge differs in terms of goals and scope (Barquet, Wessel, & Rothe, 2017; Baskerville et al., 2015). Goals highlight whether knowledge belongs to solution or problem spaces (Hevner et al., 2004; Purao, Rossi, & Bush, 2002; Simon, 1996; Venable, 2006b). Solution spaces generally include knowledge about how artifacts should be designed, as well as whether and why particular designs are desirable (Österle et al., 2011; Winter, 2008). Such “prescriptive knowledge” thus explicates means-end relationships and affords researchers an understanding of the consequences of their decisions (Simon, 1990). Problem spaces include “descriptive knowledge” about current versus desired situations (Hevner et al., 2004); they are oftentimes drawn from theories that make cause-effects statements (Baskerville et al., 2015; Gregor, 2006; Gregor & Jones, 2007; Venable, 2006b; Venable & Baskerville, 2012).

In comparison, scope refers to the boundaries of the applicability of design knowledge (Gregor, 2006). KCs depend on context or are generally applicable (Baskerville et al., 2018; Gregor & Jones, 2007). Thus, scope asks whether knowledge is abstract or situational (e.g., Baskerville et al., 2018, 2015). Situational knowledge focuses on particular contexts whereas abstract knowledge applies to a broad class of problems or solutions (Gregor & Hevner, 2013). Regardless of scope, differentiating between situational and abstract knowledge remains a distinction between two instances of explicit knowledge because both situational and abstract knowledge need to be explicated in order to be related to the knowledge base.

Taken together, discussions about problem and solution spaces as well as abstract and situational knowledge are different but not contradictory. Instead, they can be seen as orthogonal to each other because both problem and solution spaces can include situational and abstract knowledge.

## 2.3 Contradictions Between Theory on Knowledge Creation and DSR

Considering the accumulation of design knowledge, the creation of explicit knowledge also reveals contradictions between the theory of knowledge creation and DSR. These are rooted in the level at which these discussions apply, the epistemic stances that these discussions take, and the context specificity of the knowledge that they highlight.

First and foremost, there is a critical difference between the levels of analysis at which knowledge creation theory and accumulation of design knowledge apply. Knowledge creation theory’s SECI and “ba” underscore integration of tacit and explicit knowledge into knowledge bases at the level of the organization (Nonaka & Teece, 2001; Nonaka et al., 2000). Even though DSR projects can be seen as temporary organizations, they accumulate knowledge through building, testing, and extending KCs such as theory across different projects and publications (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Gregor & Jones, 2007). It is difficult to generalize “ba” to this process because “ba” builds on implicit, firm-level knowledge as the input, throughput, and output of knowledge creation. In contrast, well-established approaches in DSR highlight explicit knowledge as key input, throughput, and output (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Gregor & Jones, 2007; Iivari, 2015; Peffers et al., 2018; Gregory & Muntermann, 2014). Deductive models such as Peffers et al. (2007) and Hevner et al. (2004) showcase how abstract knowledge in the problem space guides normative actions (Winter, 2008). In this context, situational knowledge serves purposes of demonstration, evaluation, and communication (Peffers et al., 2018; Winter, 2008). Inductive strategies such as action design research (Sein et al. 2011) or heuristic theorizing (Gregory & Muntermann, 2014) draw on abstract knowledge to structure and predefine a problem space. At the same time, knowledge creation unfolds by grounding insights in situational knowledge first and then formalizing them as abstract knowledge (Sein et al., 2011; Gregory & Muntermann, 2014).

Second, there is a difference in the epistemic stance toward knowledge. Corporate knowledge creation serves the purpose of generating competitive advantage in economic settings (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Chen & Edgington, 2005; Choi, Lee, & Yoo, 2010; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka & Teece, 2001). Such competitive advantages stem from barriers to imitation, which are a result of (design) knowledge being kept private, ambiguous, or tacit (Barney, 1991; Dyer & Singh, 1998). The underlying epistemic stance is thus that knowledge drives financial performance and that this effect will be particularly strong for tacit knowledge. In stark contrast, in order to be seen as trustworthy, the accumulation of design knowledge demands explication of what knowledge was used and how. The rationale is that evaluators such as reviewers and editors need to be able to follow how KCs emerge through a study. Further, when future researchers (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Gregor & Jones, 2007), or even the same team of researchers (Chandra Kruse & Seidel, 2017), aim at reusing earlier knowledge (Holmstrom, Tuunanen, & Kauremaa, 2014), insights into how it was developed are important. Accordingly, the epistemic stance is that knowledge may drive scientific performance but that this effect will be particularly strong for explicit knowledge.

A third contradiction applies to the importance of context-specific knowledge. SECI and “ba” strongly emphasize the importance of context (Nonaka et al., 2000), in fact, they rest on the premise that knowledge is context-specific (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). However, creating design knowledge calls for reusing KCs across contexts, including multiple disciplines (Tarafdar & Davison, 2018) or practical problems (Otto & Osterle, 2012). DSR scholars regularly follow one of two strategies. They either “transform” a general knowledge base to fit their particular problem domains and contexts (Carlsson, 2007) or they approach the empirical material first, and suggest that more general knowledge be derived from it (Sein et al., 2011). Both strategies, however, demand objectification of knowledge to the extent that future work can draw on it (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Iivari, 2015).

This contradiction extends to the scope and stability of abstract knowledge. In line with knowledge creation theory, abstract knowledge has to be contextualized each time it is applied. It needs to be transformed into tacit knowledge to be eventually reexplicated (Hildreth & Kimble, 2002; Nonaka et al., 2000). In contrast, design knowledge is abstract if it remains stable. Prescriptive knowledge guides future behavior. Hence, proposing abstract design knowledge in the problem space, such as a meta-requirement, assumes stability beyond singular, known contexts. In comparison, design knowledge in solution space is considered to be projectable (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2014) if it applies to changing problem spaces, i.e., new classes of problems. DSR captures the dynamics of knowledge in multiple contexts within the scope of knowledge.

To summarize, theory on knowledge creation and DSR are complementary in that they enable viewing the accumulation of design knowledge as the creation of explicit knowledge. However, differences in terms of levels of analysis, epistemic stances, and emphases on context-specificity render generalization of SECI and “ba” to DSR difficult. This makes the accumulation of design knowledge a distinct knowledge creation problem operating under different scope conditions than conventional knowledge creation theory.

## 3 Empirical Findings

The first important step in our study was to treat knowledge creation as a distinct unit of analysis in DSR. This meant that our study would analyze processes through which knowledge is drawn on and contributed to. To explore knowledge creation as our unit of analysis, we followed Nonaka and Toyama (2003) and attempted to identify the generative mechanisms of knowledge creation in the DSR context. Thus, although we were pre-informed by the theory of knowledge creation, we approached the creation of design knowledge in a scientific context. Therefore, we combined deductive with inductive elements, a combination commonly referred to as “qualitative-abductive research design” (as proposed by McGrath, 2013, 2016, Mingers, 1994, 2004). Consistent with earlier studies that sought to explore mechanisms (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Henfridsson & Yoo, 2013; Henningsson & Kettinger, 2016), we integrated two studies with one another. The first study was dedicated to identifying the generative mechanisms of knowledge creation in DSR through indepth case studies (Research Question 1). The second study was devoted to exploring how our mechanisms would impact knowledge creation over time; i.e., across multiple publications (Research Question 2). Thus, we analyzed a sample of published DSR papers by means of text mining (Kobayashi et al., 2018) and information networks (Geerts, 2011; Vaishnavi & Kuechler, 2007) in order to capture the impact of our mechanisms on knowledge creation over time.

## 3.1.2 Data Analysis

The level of analysis in Study 1 is an individual DSR project that spans multiple years and codifies contributions throughout the documents and reports. It serves as research site to explore mechanisms of knowledge creation before their impact over time was further assessed in Study 2. Our exploration of these mechanisms echoed a process of abduction (McGrath, 2013, 2016, Mingers, 1994, 2004) that combined earlier theory with insights emerging from data (Locke et al., 2008). In terms of deduction, we first descriptively coded all knowledge contributions in our material. We deduced a list of all types of knowledge contributions referred to in DSR literature (see Appendix C) and applied it in a content analysis that enabled investigation of the patterns therein by mining text (Agar, 1996; Krippendorff, 1980). KCs were coded according to goals and scope (Barquet et al., 2017; Baskerville et al., 2015; Hevner et al., 2004) as well as content (for example: “Targeting should be carried out by determining the value based on the customer lifetime value” as “customer lifetime value”). Coding for goals, scope, and content helped track relationships between KCs.

Our inductive analysis built on the deductive steps described above. Like Henfridsson and Yoo (2013), we organized our coding in a temporal display in order to see how the KCs changed over time (Langley, 1999). Consistent with Klein and Myers’s (1999) principle of abstraction and generalization, we then iterated between our initial coding and the existing literature in order to abstract the dynamics that we were observing. In keeping with Henfridsson and Yoo (2013), we sought to identify the mechanisms that drove this process. Once we had arrived at intermediary versions of our mechanisms, we increasingly challenged the emerging interpretations by discussing our work within the authorship team and with colleagues (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2013). Finally, we synthesized the insights that resulted from these steps into “injection,” “folding,” and “enhancement” mechanisms.

## 3.1.3 Knowledge Creation Mechanisms in DSR

“Injection” is a mechanism through which knowledge, which is made available in problem and solution spaces, is declared to have a profound impact on the gestalt of a concrete DSR project. As such, declarations normally occur in the early stages of a DSR project, “injection” starts with explicit knowledge and not with tacit knowledge as knowledge creation theory would predict for firms. Figure 1 casts the mechanism, including its dimensions, as “locational” and “teleological” injection. Appendix B provides the corresponding representative data.

“Locational” injection captures the importance of existing design knowledge for the knowledge creation process at the level of a DSR project. DSR researchers include various chunks of abstract and situational knowledge from the knowledge base in their projects although their importance varies. When surveying the literature to design an artifact, a theory might be extended while the method to survey the literature is not altered. In this case, the theory represents abstract problem-space knowledge that is central whereas the method to survey literature represents abstract solution-space knowledge that is peripheral.

Accordingly, injection into the core occurs when researchers declare knowledge to be a central building block of their work. In terms of our cases PredictAD, ContextAD, and PredictBackPain, members of all projects declared that abstract problem space is central.

Figure 1. Representative Knowledge Contributions for the “Injection” Mechanism

For example, for PredictAD, the ideas of “customer lifetime value” and “real time advertising” were central and drawn from abstract marketing knowledge. The combination of these ideas was deemed a crucial differentiator of the artifact:

The prototypical implementation of a demand side platform for the purchase of advertising space in real time advertising, using the customer lifetime value as an economic control parameter, can give advertisers a competitive advantage through more efficient use of the marketing budget. (Doc. 20, PredictAD)

Abstract knowledge that was injected into peripheries of DSR projects typically fulfilled support functions. A case-in-point is that PredictAD, ContextAD, and PredictBackPain relied on vom Brocke et al.’s (2009) procedural model for conducting literature reviews that informed the design of their artifacts. Although the model represented abstract solution space knowledge that played an important role for all design projects, no team attempted to alter the original model by vom Brocke et al. (2009).

Even though our findings associate injection into the core with problem-space knowledge and injection into the periphery with solution-space knowledge, we believe this association to be an empirical matter that may play out differently when more DSR projects are scrutinized. It seems fully possible to inject problemspace knowledge into peripheries and solution-space knowledge into centers. Thus, conceptually, injection into the core versus the periphery is agnostic toward whether problem or solution space knowledge is injected.

Teleological injection refers to the intent to elaborate on problem- and solution-space knowledge by carrying out a DSR project. Elaborations on means-ends relationships (solution space) or cause-effect relationships (problem space) featured prominently in our data. For example, in the case of PredictAD, abstract knowledge on predictive analytics and customer lifetime value were injected into the core (see above). The intention was to elaborate on them by showing that the combination of these knowledge chunks would drive the likelihood for further purchases and the overall retention rate:

Using a logistic regression based on the purchase indicator, the purchase probability that a customer will make another purchase in the next period can be regressed by specifying the covariables “recency” and “frequency.” In the next step, the probability for a customer can be predicted using the recency and frequency for the next period. (Doc. 23, PredictAD)

This exemplifies the general point that DSR researchers may intend to elaborate on abstract solution-space knowledge. Similarly, PredictBackPain injected abstract problem-space knowledge as well as situational solution space knowledge from its industrial partner with the intent to elaborate on situational solution-space knowledge, in this case, narrowing down PredictBackPain’s target group:

2016’s CHARISMHA study showed that relatively younger people would use health apps (Albrecht, 2016). Building on the experience of our partner “the health fund” [synonym], we also found that students use apps for primary prevention whereas working people use it for secondary prevention. (Doc. 13, PredictBackPain)

We also found that injection into cause-effect relationships is salient across our cases, often when teams linked their ideas to general concepts stemming from the problem space. For example, PredictAD intended to elaborate on situational solution-space knowledge (their platform) by leveraging abstract problem-space knowledge (a lock-in effect from economic theory):

The self-learning engine and the resulting continuous improvement in result quality create a lock-in effect. As the database grows, the precision of the engine increases and, from the customer's point of view, so does the monetization of its own customer base. (Doc. 10, PredictAD).

Whereas our findings associate injection with meansends relationships by drawing on the solution space and associate injection with cause-effect by drawing on the problem space, we believe that this matter is also empirical. Conceptually, teleological injection is agnostic toward what knowledge is drawn on because the mechanism concerns the intent to elaborate on problem- and solution-space knowledge. Such elaborations can draw on either space (see also, Winter, 2008). Moreover, injections into prescriptive versus descriptive relationships are often intertwined in practice; however, it is important to separate them conceptually because the end to which a project caters may differ from the knowledge that it creates.

To summarize, “injection” links DSR projects to a scientific knowledge base that explicates how research draws on knowledge existing in the general knowledge base, thereby shaping particular DSR projects. This happens through injection of knowledge into the core and periphery of a project as well as through intending to elaborate on problem and solution spaces.

“Folding” is a mechanism that explains how problem and solution spaces are intertwined over time at the level of a DSR project. Folding can build on injection when the injected knowledge is processed. Moreover, folding highlights how abstract and situational knowledge are transformed over time by doing DSR, which leads to new chunks of knowledge in problem and solution spaces. While knowledge creation theory predicts that this process converts tacit into explicit knowledge and vice versa, folding highlights changes in the goals and scope of explicit knowledge. “Partitioning” and “latching” are the dimensions that characterize folding (Figure 2). Appendix B provides representative data.

“Partitioning” is a process through which design researchers specify the solution space of their projects, for example, by deriving situational specifications for the design of artifacts. Our evidence shows two patterns of this process related to components and capabilities. Partitioning components seek to identify situational knowledge in the form of concrete components that are needed for an artifact. All of our cases provide evidence for such a dynamic. PredictAD is built on the CLV and predictive analytics are central concepts intended to boost monetization per customer for each ad space (see above). Project members folded this abstract solution-space knowledge into situational solution-space knowledge by identifying components that would help execute this broad idea: “data drawn by a ‘required connection’ between first party data (‘segmentation’), e.g., with partners such as Oracle and SAP, and third party data (‘bidding’), e.g., with partners such as Doubleclick and The Trade Desk” (Doc. 3, PredictAD).

![](/api/attachments/C5WPU26G/fulltext/images/688bdc73be0588ed92bc18955a7b1a74a18baef15f63eeb397797b1ee2290cbc.jpg)  
Figure 2. Representative Knowledge Contributions for the “Folding” Mechanism

Moreover, ContextAD, which intended to display ads based on abstract problem-space knowledge about weather, performance marketing, machine learning and an optimization model (see above), folded this knowledge into situational solution-space knowledge by identifying components such as a particular weather service, several ad networks, and hosting services as important for the execution of their idea. Finally, PredictBackPain folded abstract problem-space knowledge on posture, machine learning, and physiotherapy into components such as a “a Bluetooth adapter, which transmits the posture data (angles) to our smartphone app, [and an] accelerometer, which fuses the collected axis values and generates a more precise angle (Digital Motion Processor)” (Doc. 20, PredictBackPain).

Partitioning capabilities means spelling out the artifact’s intended capabilities. Our data show evidence of partitioning capabilities across all our design teams. For example, ContextAD folded abstract problem-space knowledge on machine learning into situational knowledge by proposing that the team’s machine learning algorithm should be capable of proposing a trigger that leads to the display of a particular ad strategy to marketers. This trigger was envisioned to operate using different data sources such as purchase history, historical data on weather (temperature, rainfall), as well as the location of end users. Ads would thus fit the weather surrounding a user:

The aim is to use historical data to create a model that identifies any dependencies between the conversion of products and the local weather context at that time. On this basis, optimal weather triggers should be determined for each campaign. (Doc. 14, ContextAD)

Similarly, PredictAD folded abstract problem-space knowledge about the CLV into situational knowledge by specifying that its model should be capable of predicting how the CLV of a customer would change upon seeing particular ads: “The model should be able to predict how the CLV of a customer segment will change through a specific advertising contact. Based on this, an optimal bid decision should be made and real time advertising can be made possible.” (Doc. 11, PredictAD) Finally, team PredictBackPain folded abstract problem-space knowledge in the form of 41 ideal type postures into the capability to measure and predict the posture of an individual via machine learning.

“Latching” is the second dynamic of folding (see, e.g., Figure 2). Latching captures how researchers combine knowledge that results from partitioning, oftentimes leading to new knowledge in problem and solution spaces through “additive latching” as well as “causal latching.” Additive latching refers to adding chunks of situational knowledge by combining components. For example, ContextAD added situational knowledge in the form of a self-developed engine to other components like web services such as Google Adwords and Mailchimp. PredictBackPain relied on a “conception of the product and its manufacture [that] has been aimed at the sensible use of resources. The shirt is modular and designed for a long service life and suitability for everyday use” (Doc. 8, PredictBackPain).

Causal latching relates problem and solution spaces by explicating how one component (see above) leads to changes in one or more other components. It is not unusual for this process to lead to changes in abstract, problem-space knowledge as ContextAD showed. The team intended to minimize waste circulation, a common marketing problem. To this end, the team proposed weather-based targeting to moderate the effect that other targeting methods had on waste circulation. This was important because combining the ability to recognize the position of a potential customer enabled advertisers to target ads based on specific environmental factors. Furthermore, PredictBackPain had

to ensure medical correctness, [so that] we estimated the position of each sensor within a workshop with physiotherapists of the [insurance company]. The main requirements for the sensor position are the seamless integration of the shirt into daily life and a correct estimation of postures, especially postures that cause back pains (Doc. 21, PredictBackPain).

Thus, PredictBackPain folded situational knowledge on the position of sensors on shirts into abstract solution-space knowledge about how to estimate posture. Taken together, folding explains the intertwining problem and solution spaces over time through the specification of solution spaces (partitioning) and by combining knowledge (latching) at the level of a concrete DSR project.

“Enhancement” is a mechanism that explains how knowledge, which is created at the level of a DSR project, is fed back to the level of the scientific knowledge base. Similar to injection, enhancement thus links the levels of the DSR projects with the knowledge base. However, whereas injection explains how the former “pulls” knowledge from the latter, enhancement explains how the former “pushes” knowledge into the latter. Therefore, enhancement extends problem and solution spaces that exist outside DSR projects. Whereas knowledge creation theory predicts that this process is about integration of deeply contextualized knowledge into a knowledge base, our evidence shows that decontextualization of knowledge is the key to enhancement. The dimensions of “abstracting” and “evaluating” characterize this mechanism (Figure 3). Representative data can be found in Appendix B.

“Abstracting” refers to generalizing situational to abstract knowledge in problem and solution spaces by (1) identifying misfits between both spaces for classes of problems and ranges of solutions and (2) attempting to fit them together. As for identifying misfits, PredictBackPain reviewed literature about how IT would facilitate management of chronic back problems. However, this abstract problem-space knowledge failed to offer a solution for prevention of chronic back pain through IT. Most applications that were developed in medical informatics or informatics addressed self-management of chronic back pain. Hence, this knowledge offered little insight into how back pain could be prevented: “Compared with existing wearables, which only give general recommendations, our shirt recommends particular actions based on data about the wearer's movements, which are constantly collected” (Doc. 8, PredictBackPain).

![](/api/attachments/C5WPU26G/fulltext/images/fd4fd79a4c7b2055077759319543a9e0c41152aa7a804429dc02199481c9b400.jpg)  
Figure 3. Representative Knowledge Contributions for the “Enhancement” Mechanism

PredictAD and ContextAD also identified such misfits. PredictAD found that existing artifacts in the solution space fail to comprehensively assess the value of a customer in real-time as first party user data were not sufficiently utilized: “Existing standard algorithms of the DSPs (Demand Side Platform) use the advertiser’s own data only insufficiently and bid decisions are based on 3rd party data” (Doc. 8, PredictAD). ContextAD, on the other hand, found that current ad bidding systems lack granularity when considering contextual information. This, however, was necessary to lower customer acquisition costs.

All teams also proposed to improve the fit between expanded problem and solution spaces. The pattern that we observed was that teams generalized from their artifact to abstract knowledge in problem and solution spaces. For example, ContextAD built abstract problemspace knowledge by hypothesizing that weather would affect a person’s purchasing decisions. Their artifact was designed to confirm the hypotheses. Thus, a contribution was made to abstract knowledge about consumer behavior by introducing weather as an explanatory variable in purchasing decisions. Similarly, PredictAD proposed that the results of their project would provide a new perspective on the “customer lifetime value” concept. Abstract problem-space knowledge, in this case literature, cast CLV as an effective tool for steering customers. PredictAD contributed to this view by showing how real-time data provided marketers with new perspectives on customers. Finally, PredictBackPain proposed its combination of sensor data and machine learning as an effective remedy for the lack of ICT support for preventing chronic back pain. Hence, PredictBackPain generalized from situational solution-space knowledge to abstract solution-space knowledge.

“Evaluating” captures evaluation of knowledge generated in a DSR project in order to ensure adequate abstracting. The patterns of “evaluating” that we found concur with the functional purpose of evaluation in DSR (Venable, 2006a; Venable, Pries-Heje, & Baskerville, 2016). “Formative evaluation” occurred when teams used confusion matrices on test data (ContextAD) or simulation methods (PredictBackPain) to evaluate artifacts during design processes. “Summative evaluation” occurred toward the end of design when, for example, PredictBackPain made final interviews with prospective users and communicated the results as testimonials to claims made by the team.

In summary, “enhancement” links the levels of a DSR project and the knowledge base by revealing how the latter extends knowledge in problem and solution spaces through generalization (abstracting) and ensuring appropriate generalization (evaluating). To conclude our first study, we found three mechanisms that characterize knowledge creation in DSR: “injection,” “folding,” and “enhancement.” These mechanisms are important because, to our knowledge, they are the first empirically grounded conceptualizations of how knowledge is created in DSR.

## 3.2 Study 2: Impacts of Mechanisms on Knowledge Accumulation over Time

Our second research question asks how mechanisms of knowledge creation impact knowledge accumulation over time. Like Henfridsson & Bygstad (2013), we conducted a second study to address this question that focused on assessing how mechanisms impact knowledge creation across a sample of DSR publications (see also, Archer et al., 2013; McGrath, 2013).

## 3.2.1 Data Collection

The goal of Study 2 was to assess the impact of knowledge creation mechanisms on knowledge accumulation unfolding across papers published in several DSR publications. In Study 2 we chose publications by two research teams on the basis of three inclusion criteria (IC), i.e., a set of publications by a recognizable group of DSR scholars (IC1) that dealt with the same problem or solution space over time (IC2) leading to publication of a design theory in a Basket of Eight journal (IC3). IC1-IC3 ensured a sample of studies reflecting a cumulative development of design knowledge. Team 1 was led by Legner and Giessmann, who worked on “business models for [a] platform as a service.” Team 2 was led by Seidel and worked on “creativity in business processes.” Both teams applied different DSR strategies. Team 1 repeatedly executed both ADR and qualitative inductive research. Team 2 relied on methodologies commonly found in more deductive approaches to DSR. The exclusion criteria (Table 2) we used to filter publications of both teams did not exclude any articles by DSR Team 1 but did exclude 15 articles by DSR Team 2 (14 papers remained in the sample). ## 3.2.2 Data Analysis

Our initial coding was a descriptive coding of the KCs in the articles mentioned in Table 3. Appendix E shows the code distributions. Based on the coding, we ran an exploratory analysis using the artifact and information network in DSR proposed by Vaishnavi & Kuechler (2007) and operationalized by Geerts (2011). This set the basis for exploring how our mechanisms impacted knowledge accumulation across multiple publications by visualizing efforts of researchers over time (Vaishnavi & Kuechler, 2007). To this end, we operationalized the impact by measuring how KCs developed over time and linked this development to our mechanisms. We used text mining and network analysis (Kobayashi et al., 2018) because they helped us to (semi)automatically identify semantic relationships between KCs. In the following, we briefly outline this method. Figure 4 highlights the different steps (1 to 8) taken. Following descriptive coding (Steps 1 to 3), we applied typical means of data preparation in text mining, such as stemmification, removing special characters, or punctuation. Subsequently, we assessed the semantic distance between all KCs (Step 4) using the cosine similarity of the term-frequency and inverse document frequency (IT-TDF) to weigh terms within each KC. Cosine similarity (Salton, 1991) assesses the angle between document vectors without depending on vector lengths—here, the length of a KC citation. Thus, it returns the strengths of relationships between KCs (Evangelopoulos, Zhang, & Prybutok, 2012), which we interpreted as distance in the information network. Based on TF-IDF vectors, cosine similarity was shown to be a robust and efficient method for comparing documents (Adomavicius & Tuzhilin, 2005; Larsen & Bong, 2016; Li & Larsen, 2011).

Because lower levels of similarity tend to become meaningless, we derived minimum threshold values at Step 4 (J. Li & Larsen, 2011). Such threshold values need to be empirically derived using domain knowledge (Evangelopoulos et al., 2012). We assessed these values for each case individually by comparing the similarity measures with the manually coded topics for each pair of KCs. If these topics matched (i.e., KC1 and KC2, both coded as CLV but not real-time advertising), we found measured similarity potentially meaningful. We calculated the mean accuracy of similarity and topics for multiple threshold values and used an elbow criterion to pick one (accuracy between 75% and 80%).

Eventually, the similarity measures enabled us to derive adjacency lists for each relevant pair of KCs, which could be used for representing information networks for each DSR team (Step 5). We visualized the networks (Steps 6 and 7) and based upon them, we applied a typical community detection method (Step 8a), the Louvain partition algorithm (Blondel et al., 2008), to identify groups of interlinked KCs. These communities were selected for further qualitativeabductive inspection (Step 8b). We used several python libraries to perform our analysis, including pandas, ntlk, networkx, forceatlas2, and floweaver.

## Impact 1: Solution Space Knowledge as Trajectory

The first impact that we found is that solution-space design knowledge can act as a trajectory. We found this when injection and enhancement build on one another sequentially. Initial injection of design knowledge defined a trajectory into which subsequent DSR papers fell as they enhanced the knowledge that was initially injected (see Table 4). Due to the emphasis of enhancement on abstracting and evaluating, KCs that emerged from this sequence were largely abstract and retained their goals. In contrast, situational knowledge was rarely picked up in subsequent studies.

In terms of our analysis, in Cluster 5 (Team 1, see Table 4), the team began with statements such as: “[PaaS] requires a shift to at least two-sided business models” (Doc. 1, Team 1), marking an injection of platform and business model research into the core of the project (Doc. 1, Team 1). DSR Team 1 evaluated this knowledge in different contexts of “developers or ISV [independent software vendors] on the one side and end consumers on the other side” (Doc. 2, Team 1). Finally, the team enhanced general design principles for platforms-as-a-service business models (Doc. 4, Team 1) and deductively derived the role of customer ownership in PaaS business models (Doc. 6, Team 1).

Cluster 0 of DSR team 2 sits close to the core of the network. Such centrality displays the relevance of this knowledge. The frameworks created in this cluster were applied by the DSR team in other clusters, e.g., to analyze pockets of creativity in Cluster 5. An early version of the framework for “creative environments” was proposed in (Doc. 1, Team 1) and therefore injected creativity and knowledge management literature into the project. The DSR team enhanced this framework into a “framework for information retrieval to support Pockets of Creativity within business processes” (Doc. 3, Team 2) with instantiations (Doc. 6, Team 2) evaluating the frameworks during the process. Finally, the accumulation concluded in a prescriptive model to “guide the development of new process modeling technique” (Doc. 10, Team 2).

## Impact 2: Solution Space Knowledge as Driver

The second impact that we found is that solution space knowledge can act as a driver of knowledge accumulation. We found this when folding and enhancement built on one another in a pattern of iteration. Iteration occurs when KCs in the solution space change views on concrete problems or classes thereof, which leads to KCs in the problem space. These KCs in the problem space again attract further solution-space knowledge. Hence, knowledge already developed in the solution space is further enhanced. For example, in two of the clusters of DSR Team 2 (see Table 5), abstract solution-space knowledge emerged in later stages, indicating that folding and enhancement built on one another in iteration. We found that knowledge is folded in the early stages of a study into abstract problem design knowledge, which for instance led to new requirements. At a later stage, the KC clusters are revisited to enhance design knowledge in the solution space.

In Cluster 4, the role of knowledge management systems for creativity were expressed (Doc. 4, Team 2). These insights were then folded into concrete cases in order to highlight shortcomings in the current systems. Such failures of the solution space informed new requirements and propositions for a remedy (Doc. 7, Team 2) that were enhanced into hypotheses formulated as general causal relationships (Doc. 15, Team 2). Thus, testing design knowledge led to formulation of abstract knowledge.

Cluster 3 shows how design knowledge about information structure and retrieval was created. Multiple prescriptive statements expressed “how multiperspective, hierarchical navigation structures can be applied to … support creative persons in their effort to develop product-service bundles” (Doc. 3, Team 2). This knowledge was folded with situational knowledge from interpretative studies on concrete instantiations (Doc. 6, Team 2). Building on that, this situational knowledge was tested and finally enhanced into a general prescriptive model with general design requirements (Doc. 7 and 15, Team 2).

Taken together, these insights suggest that design knowledge cannot only be seen as setting a trajectory; it is also a force driving accumulation forward through iteration. Through combining design knowledge, folding and enhancement serve as a driver of DSR.

Based on a literature review, (Doc. 1, Team 1) presented an initial definition of PaaS as an “execution environment, wherein external developers deploy and run their complementary components” as part of Cluster 6. This definition was subsequently reused mostly without change. The only exemption was one publication (Doc. 2, Team 1) that narrowed its context to a “web-based development platform.” In particular, the definition informed the design principles, for instance that “PaaS must offer complementary components and applications” (Doc. 4, Team 1). Knowledge accumulation in Cluster 6 (Team 1) followed an enhancement of mostly descriptiveabstract knowledge. It was predominantly discussed in a conceptual manner and informed the development of design principles. Comparably, the abstractdescriptive statement that “positive network externalities on one of the customer sides is expected to initiate positive network externalities on the other customer side” (Doc. 2, Team 1) in Cluster 7 extended the problem space and informed metarequirements on leveraging network effects expressed in Doc. 4 (Team 1). These metarequirements, as well as directly related consideration of multisidedness, were enhanced and a solution evaluated in a case study (Doc. 6, Team 1).

In summary, these insights show that design knowledge marks the outcome of an accumulation process that spans many studies. At the same time, we found that the mechanisms leading to its emergence differ. When design knowledge is built firmly across multiple publications, it follows the same mechanism, either folding or enhancement. Our observation that knowledge contributions in proximate clusters tend to be activated by the same mechanisms (see Figure 6 and Figure 7) supports this argument.

## 3.3 Synthesis: A Process Model of Knowledge Creation in DSR

We draw from Studies 1 and 2 to propose a process model of knowledge creation in DSR (see Figure 8). The arrows in Figure 8 depict knowledge creation in a process through which design knowledge accumulates over time. DSR constantly produces such knowledge, which is why the boxes labeled “design knowledge” should not be understood as endpoints. Instead, we see knowledge as continuously injected, folded, and enhanced and chose boxes merely for purposes of representation. Furthermore, our model differentiates between the levels of singular publication(s), wherein injection, folding, and enhancing occur at the level of the overall knowledge creation process. The arrows capture the processes and the circles publication(s). We deliberately speak of “publication(s)” to indicate that more than one mechanism can play out in one paper and that mechanisms and their interplay can interact across multiple papers. Moreover, the processes depicted by the horizontal arrows do not operate in a sequence but, as shown by Study 2, unfold in parallel and in different constellations over time. Finally, the coloring of the horizontal arrows depicts the different patterns of interactions between our mechanisms that are associated with the three impacts that we described above.

Figure 8. Impacts of Mechanisms on Accumulation of Design Knowledge Across Publications

The first pattern that we found is one of sequence characterized by injection predating enhancement. Because such predating leads to enhancement addressing the knowledge that was injected, we associate this pattern with our first impact—“solution space knowledge as trajectory.” Our data reveal that injection of abstract knowledge from problem and solution spaces often leads to enhancements of this knowledge. Goals of knowledge thus remain constant by and large, as researchers tend to expand knowledge to new classes of problems or an enhanced solution space.

The second pattern that we disclosed is iteration. We found iterations between folding and enhancement that played out in two ways. Either situational knowledge was folded into a DSR publication and later enhanced into abstract knowledge or knowledge emerging during a DSR project modified the problem space and enhanced existing solutions. Given that these iterations between the mechanisms drive what knowledge is created and how, we associate iteration with the impact of “solution-space knowledge as a driver.” This impact means that the goals or scope of knowledge change more often over time, which is likely when knowledge emerges in a DSR project after abstract knowledge was previously proposed in the solution space.

The final pattern that we found was mutual exclusion. It revealed that folding or enhancement could yield outcomes separately from one another. This was particularly prone to occur when the scope and goals shifted throughout the accumulation process. In these instances, folding mainly changed the solution space. When the scope and goals remained constant, enhancement was more likely to change the problem space. Given these effects on solution and problem spaces, we associate the pattern of mutual exclusion with our third impact, “design knowledge as outcome.”

To close, literature on organizational knowledge creation highlights the “ba” mechanism as a “place” where contextualized, tacit knowledge is created through human interaction (Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). In contrast, our model of knowledge creation in DSR highlights three mechanisms (injection, folding, and enhancement) through which explicit, decontextualized knowledge is created by interacting with a scientific knowledge base. Moreover, whereas organizational knowledge creation theory builds on a circular pattern in which knowledge creation operates, we were able to disclose three patterns: sequence, iteration, and exclusivity. These differences are likely akin to the different challenges involved in corporate knowledge creation versus scientific knowledge creation. Corporate knowledge creation focuses on combining established tacit with new explicit knowledge to create tacit knowledge (Nonaka & von Krogh, 2009), suggesting that an end of the conversion process is reached once knowledge is contextualized and tacit. In sharp contrast, input, output, and the process to extend, challenge, and refine knowledge are explicated in DSR.

## 4 Discussion

We suggested that knowledge creation in DSR is a distinct problem at the intersection of organizational knowledge creation theory and design science research. As it is largely unknown how knowledge creation unfolds in the latter context, we explored empirically which mechanisms explain knowledge creation in DSR and how they impact knowledge accumulation over time.

This study contributes to DSR in four ways. First and foremost, our framework is the first to theorize knowledge creation in DSR as a distinct knowledge creation problem. This is important because the widespread use of well-established procedural models in DSR (Hevner et al., 2004; Peffers et al., 2007; Sein et al., 2011) calls for improving our understanding of how knowledge, which is produced by using these models, accumulates over time. Through theorizing mechanisms that drive accumulation of design knowledge, we were able to uncover a set of important insights in this regard. We found that the more inductive ADR project in our study created situational and abstract knowledge and reused both, while the more deductive DSR project largely created and reused abstract knowledge. Even when members of the latter project encountered situational knowledge, they tended to enhance knowledge by abstracting and formalizing. Our findings thus reveal general patterns in knowledge creation in DSR. Earlier works on DSR have not taken up this question because recent debates have largely centered on the nature of knowledge in DSR (Baskerville et al., 2018; Gregor & Jones, 2007; Hevner et al., 2004; Venable, 2006b), on what would constitute legitimate knowledge contributions, and on how to communicate (e.g., Gregor & Hevner, 2013) and evaluate them (e.g., Venable et al., 2016). Notwithstanding the importance of these debates, they fail to reveal the diversity in extant DSR. This diversity is, however, important (Baskerville et al., 2015; Peffers et al., 2018). It is equally important to understand how these diverse approaches build upon each other and contribute to a joint knowledge base for DSR. We were able to capture this because we theorized knowledge accumulation as a knowledge creation problem.

Our second contribution is that we introduce knowledge creation as a distinct unit of analysis. Our process model differentiates between three different patterns that play out in knowledge creation and, hence, our model gives knowledge creation analytical weight in its own right. This is important because several recent conceptual discussions in DSR have circled around questions of whether knowledge creation should be deductive or inductive (Goldkuhl, 2012; Gregor, 2006; Hevner et al., 2004; Iivari, 2015; Niehaves, 2007) with distinct implications for how knowledge should be created (Iivari, 2015). However, there has previously been no empirical investigation into knowledge creation. By undertaking one, we were able to analyze the knowledge creation process in its own right and to disclose the crucial insight that researchers use inductive and deductive methods over time. While researchers may have used induction or deduction in individual papers, they used induction and deduction at the level of the overall knowledge creation process. This renders knowledge creation an “abductive” process (van de Ven, 2007) as opposed to either an inductive or a deductive process. This implies that we need to keep the levels of a publication and the knowledge creation process apart when we ponder what approaches are more suitable for DSR. We were able to unlock these insights because we explored how mechanisms impacted knowledge accumulation over time.

Our third contribution is to show that DSR projects tend to continuously generate knowledge throughout a project. Extant procedural models often suggest that knowledge occurs at a late stage in the design process (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Peffers et al., 2007). However, our findings challenge this logic of sequentiality, which is expressed explicitly in the notion that design knowledge is derived from deductive reasoning to relate problem and solution spaces (Simon, 1996) or assumed implicitly in procedural models (e.g., Peffers et al., 2007) that regard “conceptualizing, designing, building of prototypes (as proof-of-concept, proof-bydemonstration) and/or formal (mathematical, logical) proofs and description” (Gregg, Kulkarni, & Vinzé, 2001, p. 173) as a sequence. In contrast, our study shows that DSR projects often span multiple years during which many papers are published and contributions explicated. This calls for research to refine extant procedures in order to account for continuous processes of creating explicit knowledge through the mechanisms we found. Platforms such as mydesignprocess.com provide valuable resources in this regard.

Fourth, we explored knowledge creation under a set of scope conditions that differ from the corporate contexts from which organizational knowledge creation theory emanates (Nonaka & Teece, 2001; Nonaka & von Krogh, 2009). This allowed us to explore knowledge creation under conditions in which explicit knowledge drives performance and knowledge that is detached from its initial context determines project success. For corporations, although explication of knowledge can be considered a threat, assessments of rigor and relevance in DSR demand transparency. By using organizational knowledge creation in an abductive fashion, we were able to disclose mechanisms that operate in such settings.

Despite our efforts to apply the utmost rigor, our study is not without limitations that open up fruitful avenues for future research. The first is that we only explored knowledge accumulation within teams. This was important because earlier studies have not addressed the knowledge accumulation process empirically in DSR and we expected to be able to deliver fundamental insights on this process from intrateam analyses in which we assumed that a great deal of knowledge would evolve systematically (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka & Toyama, 2003). While we tried to substantiate our claims by studying five teams across two studies, future research is warranted to explore how knowledge accumulates across teams. For example, how injection, folding, and enhancement drive the role that procedural models such as Peffers et al. (2007) or Sein et al. (2011) play as researchers relate to one another would be a fruitful avenue. In order to explore this, future research may complement microlevel analyses of KCs with meta-analytic and citation analysis methods.

A further limitation is that because our data stem from the European design science community, our findings are subject to cultural limitations. There were several good reasons for our sampling choice. On the one hand, European scholars have been particularly active in DSR, thus rendering European projects a particularly good sample for capturing state-of-the-art ways to create knowledge in DSR. On the other, the projects we sampled reflected how DSR is done more globally. All of our projects built on a particular procedural model and were thus comparable to bestpractice DSR in the United States, Asia, and other regions of the world. At the same time, all of our projects mainly used qualitative data for design and naturalistic evaluation. This stands in some contrast to works that have put quantitative methods (Morana et al., 2019; Walls et al., 1992) or artificial evaluation (Venable et al., 2016) at the center of DSR. Future research is thus needed to (1) control for cultural influences generally and then (2) for testing our insights on DSR studies that made use of quantitative and artificial methods.

Last, we coded more than 212 knowledge contributions from primary data in our first study and 303 KCs from secondary data in the second study. With all members of the author team coding and discussing the codes, we did our best to mitigate subjectivity biases and to create a “shared interpretation and understanding” (Weston et al., 2001) of KC which was further informed by a codebook with a list of the KCs mentioned in the DSR literature. We also tested multiple configurations of data preparation for text mining in our second study. In our second study we tested for changes to text preparation, which includes adaptations to stemming or lemmatization. They had no significant effect on the formation of the clusters presented.

To conclude, by providing insights into the abductive process of knowledge creation, this study suggests new thinking about an underlying epistemology that strikes a balance between DSR’s methodological pluralism and its potency for generating knowledge that can be systematically accumulated. This advances DSR by disclosing mechanisms that drive knowledge accumulation and the varying roles that design knowledge plays during its accumulation. While much remains to be done, we believe that our study provides a new way of seeing knowledge in DSR.

## About the Authors

Hannes Rothe is an assistant professor at the Digital Entrepreneurship Hub, Freie Universität Berlin. He earned his doctoral degree at Freie Universität Berlin in 2015, where he has been responsible for coordinating entrepreneurship education since 2013. He has been a visiting researcher at University of Cambridge (UK) and TU Graz (Austria). Hannes’s research interests focus on organizing data and knowledge, digital entrepreneurship, and digital infrastructures. His work has been published in journals such as Information Systems Journals, Communications of the Association for Information Systems and presented at leading information systems conferences. He was runner-up and winner of multiple paper awards, including the Claudio Ciborra Award for the most innovative paper.

Lauri Wessel is an associate professor of management and organization at the University of Bremen, Germany. His work focuses on using organization theory in order to understand and design health care IT. Accordingly, he has published in a range of journals like Information Systems Journal, Business and Information Systems Engineering, Research in the Sociology of Organizations, Alzheimer’s & Dementia: Translational Research & Clinical Interventions, and others. He serves as associate editor for Communications of the Association for Information Systems and sits on the editorial board of Information and Organization. He has won awards from the International Conference on Information Systems and the Alcatel-Lucent Foundation.

Ana Paula Barquet has a PhD in industrial engineering with a focus on product-service systems from the University of São Paulo and received postdoctoral training at the Technical University of Berlin and Freie Universität Berlin, Germany. She also holds an undergraduate degree in pharmacy and food technology from the Federal University of Santa Catarina, Brazil, where she completed her master’s in industrial engineering with a focus in product development. In the course of her academic career, Dr. Ana Paula Bezerra Barquet has been involved in the coordination and execution of international projects and consulting projects. She has published in journals such as Industrial Marketing Management, Journal of Cleaner Production and Business Process Management Journal and presented her research at conferences in Brazil, Chile, Sweden, Denmark and Germany. Her research interests and teaching skills include topics such as product-service systems, product and service development process, business models, design science research and remanufacturing.
