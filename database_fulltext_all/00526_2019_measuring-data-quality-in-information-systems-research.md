## A B S T R A C T

Although contemporary research relies to a large extent on data, data quality in Information Systems research is a subject that has not received much attention until now. In this paper, a framework is presented for the measurement of scientific data quality using the principles of rule-based measurement. The proposed framework is capable of handling data quality problems due to both incorrect execution and incorrect description of data collection and validation processes. It is then argued that uncertainty can arise during the measurement, which complicates data quality assessment. The framework is therefore extended to handle uncertainty about the truth value of predicates. Instead of a numerical quality level, data quality is then expressed as either a probability distribution or a possibility distribution over the ordinal quality scale. Finally, it is also shown how quality thresholds can be formulated based on the results of the quality measurement. The usefulness of the proposed framework is illustrated throughout the paper with an example of the construction of a possible survey data quality measurement system and, subsequently, the application of that system on a realistic example.

## 1. Introduction

The existence of user-friendly storage media such as hard disks, together with universally accepted database models such as the relational model [1] enabled the creation and storage of enormous amounts of data in the past few decades. Consequently, many organizational processes currently in place rely on information drawn from data. It is clear that in order for these processes to be of high quality the underlying data should be of high quality as well. However, obtaining data of high quality is not trivial. Since data quality became an active research field in 1995 [2], a lot of research has been performed regarding the meaning of data quality and how to measure it. This has lead to the emergence of diferent philosophies of how to look at data quality and, consequently, to the development of diferent measurement procedures. However, the question that should be answered when measuring data quality is in fact always the same: “Are data of high quality or not?”. The measurement procedures only difer in the way this question is answered.

An important domain where data quality is a topic of main interest is scientific research. Indeed, contemporary research relies to a large extent on data. Because of this, several important scientific research domains have given special attention to the quality of research data in the past few years [3,4]. However, it seems that the importance of the data quality problem has not yet reached the Information Systems (IS) research world [5]. This lack of attention has lead to various problems with data quality in IS research. First, scientific data collection and validation processes are not always performed correctly. This possibly leads to low quality scientific data. Consequently, the scientific results that are largely based on these data are also of low quality. Second, even if the data collection and validation steps are performed correctly, the performed processes are not always qualitatively written down in the resulting scientific paper. Not only does this lead to scientific literature that is very dificult to understand, but also to scientific studies that are dificult to reproduce. This lack of reproducibility is a growing concern in various research areas [6,7]. However, attention to reproducibility of IS research has been limited so far.

As such, it may be concluded that the time has come to revitalize scientific data quality research within the IS world. This paper wants to aid with that revitalization. The main contribution of this paper is to provide a general framework for the measurement of IS research data quality and to show how decisions according to the usefulness of the data can be taken based on such measurements. The framework basically produces numbers on an ordinal scale and is capable of handling uncertainty that arises during the measurement procedure. Because of its clear interpretation, the framework allows for very intuitive declaration of data quality thresholds. We aim at measurement procedures with a clear fundamental ground and reject those that produce numbers without any meaning. For that purpose, the measurement procedures presented in the following are limited to those that fit in the framework of Representational Measurement Theory [8].

The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, the principles of rule-based measurement are discussed. With these measurement tools in place, we explain in Section 3 how they can be used in the context of IS research data quality measurement. This explanation is supported by an example of the development of a quality measurement system fo survey data. Section 4 illustrates how quality thresholds can be defined based on the results of such a measurement procedure. Section 5 illustrates the practical usefulness of the proposed framework by measuring the quality of a realistic survey dataset. Finally, the conclusions of this paper are given in Section 6.

## 2. Background: rule-based data quality measurement

As already stated in the introduction, measuring data quality in essence comes down to answering the following question: “Are data of high quality or not?”. The answer to this question is of Boolean nature: either the data is good enough for its intended purposes and the answer is true, or the data do not sufice and the answer is false. An important class of data quality measurement procedures exploiting this Boolean philosophy is the class of rule-based measurement procedures. These measurement procedures rely on the construction of a set of quality rules to which the data should adhere in the ideal case [9]. Over the last couple of years, the philosophy of rule-based measurement has received a lot of attention. This has lead to the development of diferent types of rules, including those based on regular expressions [10], functional dependencies [11], control digits [12] and association analysis [13].

Formally, all rule-based procedures rely on a set of predicates $P = \{ p _ { 1 } , p _ { 2 } , . . . , p _ { k } \}$ where each p<sub>i</sub> maps a data item to a value in $\boldsymbol { \mathbb { B } } = \{ T , F \} \ [ 1 0 ]$ . These predicates represent rules that good quality data should adhere to. Assessment of the quality of data d comes down to assigning a number to d when we observe the set $\{ p | p \in P \land p ( d ) \}$ , which simply consists of those predicates that evaluate to true. The number that is assigned to d represents a quality level. It naturally follows that there is a totally ordered set $\mathbb { S } = \{ s _ { 1 } , s _ { 2 } , . . . , s _ { l } \}$ consisting of such quality levels. For simplicity, indices are assumed to indicate the order, which means that $s _ { 1 } < s _ { 2 } < \ldots < s _ { l } .$

The assignment of numbers to data is achieved by a capacity functionC: $2 ^ { P } \mapsto \mathbb { S }$ which basically maps each subset X ⊆ P to a quality level reached when all predicates in X are satisfied. As such, measurement of quality of d is now simply given by:

$$
Q (d) = C (\{p | p \in P \land p (d) \})\tag{1}
$$

The main role of the capacity function is to connect the set of predicates with an associated quality level. To be meaningful, the capacity should be monotonic w.r.t. ⊆. Moreover, because of the boundary conditions of C, we impose that data not adhering to any of the proposed quality rules are associated with the lowest quality level: $C ( \varnothing ) = \operatorname* { i n f } ( \mathbb { S } )$ ). Similarly, data that satisfy all available quality rules are of the highest quality level: $C ( P ) = \operatorname* { s u p } ( \mathbb { S } )$ . Data that satisfy some of the quality rules have an associated quality level in between. Based on the capacity function, for each quality level an associated logical expression of predicates can be derived representing the requirements to reach at least that quality level. These logical expressions typically consist of conjunctions and/or disjunctions of predicates from P. Simplification of these expressions is possible by using logical calculus. Note that the obtained expressions for the diferent quality levels are mutually coherent due to the properties of C.

It is of key importance to understand that rule-based measurement induces an ordinal scale. This implies that arithmetic operations such as calculating the average data quality have no meaning. Comparison of the quality of diferent data sets is possible as long as the same measurement system is used, i.e. the same rule set, capacity function and measurement scale.

## 3. IS research data quality measurement

With the appropriate measurement tools in place, this section presents the necessary steps that should be performed in order to obtain a useful rule-based measurement system for data quality in IS research. To illustrate the usefulness of rule-based measurement within the given context, an example system is constructed for the measurement of survey data quality. It should be noted that the main purpose of this example is to show how the theoretical rule-based measurement philosophy can be practically applied, rather than claiming the development of a perfect measurement system for the quality of survey data.

## 3.1. Rule-based measurement of data quality in IS research

To construct a framework for the rule-based measurement of IS research data quality, we start from the seven aspects or seven W's of scientific data collection and validation, as identified by Marsden and Pingry [5]:

1. What provides an explanation of exactly what is captured in the data.

2. When refers to the time at which the data are collected.

3. Where refers to the location (virtual or real) where the data are collected.

4. How describes the precise process(es) of data collection.

5. Who details the individual(s) involved in the data collection.

6. Which details the instruments or artifacts used in collecting the data.

7. Why provides the set of reasons or goals for collecting the data.

In order for each of these aspects to be performed qualitatively, several requirements should be met. The more requirements that are met, the higher the measured quality level should be. Failing to perform any of the seven aspects correctly should lower research data quality. Similarly, failing to correctly describe in the resulting scientific paper how the research steps belonging to each of the seven aspects were performed also leads to scientific work of lower quality. Finally, it is also important to mention that some requirements may be more important to data quality than others. This should also be taken into account in the development of a measurement system.

The specific requirements for the correct execution and description of the data collection and validation processes depend on the type of data that is gathered. Indeed, surveys require for example diferent quality standards than field experiment data. Depending on the specific data type, diferent quality requirement guides were developed in the past [14,15]. By transforming each rule into a corresponding predicate, a rule set can be constructed that can be used to build a rule-based measurement system. As such, it is clear that rule-based quality measurement is indeed a good fit in the context of IS research data. In its simplest form, quality measurement of a given IS research data type thus comes down to the construction of a set of predicates P representing the requirements of qualitative data collection and validation for that data type, the construction of and the construction of a capacity function C that maps a set of predicates satisfied by a given piece of data to an associated quality level in .

## 3.2. Presence of uncertainty during quality measurement

The approach presented in Section 3.1 assumes that the correct execution of each of the seven W's can be translated into a subset of predicates that can take on either the truth value true or false. However, the simple mapping of quality requirements to predicates that evaluate to a truth value in B is not always possible. Indeed, uncertainty about the truth value can be present, which can complicate quality measurement. If the binary truth value of a predicate cannot be determined during its evaluation, the determination of the quality level of the data is not possible as well. The reason for this is that the capacity function requires a set of predicates evaluating to true as an input. Simply translating each of the seven aspects to several predicates that should be satisfied and constructing a capacity function based on these predicates will thus not sufice: rule-based measurement should be extended to be able to cope with uncertainty.

Uncertainty about the binary truth value of a predicate can exist due to diferent reasons [16]. In the context of IS research data, three spe cific causes of uncertainty are worth mentioning. The first possible cause is that not all information about the data is known. Marsden and Pingry indeed claim that one problem with IS data management is that scientific papers often do not provide enough information about how data is collected and validated [5]. As not all information about the data is known, predicates that require this information in order to be evaluated indeed severely complicate the assignment of a binary truth value and, consequentially, data quality measurement. As such, the link between an incomplete description of research data and data quality measurement is revealed: poor description complicates IS research data quality measurement. Although the description of data related processes in a paper does not directly afect the quality of the research data, it influences the extent to which we are capable of exactly assessing its quality level. This observation emphasizes once again the importance of the role of correct data description in the process to establish a data quality research culture.

Second, uncertainty can be introduced because predicates are too complex to evaluate. If we consider the principal predicate a measurement system should evaluate, i.e. “The data are of high quality”, it is immediately clear that it is impossible to evaluate this predicate to a Boolean value directly. The reason for this is that this predicate encompasses too many diferent aspects of the data collection and validation processes at once. This kind of uncertainty can however be prevented by decomposing complex predicates into predicates that are easier to verify. The above mentioned predicate can for example be decomposed into seven diferent predicates referring to the quality of the execution of each of the seven aspects, obtaining predicates such as “The Who-aspect of the data collection and validation processes is performed in a correct way.”. While these kinds of predicates are still too complex to be evaluated in a Boolean way, it is clear that un certainty can be removed by further decomposing these freshly obtained predicates on their turn. Their subpredicates then represent aspect-specific requirements. By repetitious decomposition, a hierarchical rule set can be finally created of which the lowest, most detailed level of predicates can efectively be used as a set of predicates to measure the quality of the data.

Such a hierarchical rule set can for example be constructed for the measurement of survey data quality. Indeed, the requirements that survey data should meet in order to be acceptable as research data were investigated by diferent authors in the past [15,17,18]. By further decomposition of the seven aspects in the predicates associated with these requirements the hierarchical rule set depicted in Fig. 1 can be obtained. In this figure, the predicates that are surrounded by a green oval are the predicates residing at the lowest tree level, i.e. the predicates that should be evaluated and on which a capacity should be built. As already indicated before, we do not claim this predicate set to be the perfect, complete basis for future survey data quality measurement. However, we believe this example to be useful to illustrate the practical implications of the proposed measurement framework.

The predicates indicated in red are predicates that are too complex to evaluate and are thus further decomposed into simpler subpredicates. However, one can see that although uncertainty is definitely removed by this repeated process, predicates in the lowest tree level exist that can still not be mapped to a Boolean value. This brings us to a third possible cause of uncertainty that arises during quality measurement: vagueness. Fig. 1 indicates vague predicates in yellow. As an example, consider the following predicate referring to a requirement of the Which-aspect: “The survey does not contain leading questions”. Although this predicate cannot be further decomposed, it is still not possible to map it to a binary truth value without any doubt. The reason for this is that the predicate is vague. What if for example a question in the survey is not fully leading, but only leading to a certain extent? Should this predicate be mapped to the truth value true or false? Clearly, we need a dedicated strategy to handle this problem.

## 3.3. Expressing predicate uncertainty

To be able to still assess data quality under uncertainty, the proposed rule-based framework should be extended. To this end, the result of a predicate evaluation should express the uncertainty about its truth value, rather than its exact truth value. This can be accomplished using uncertainty theories, of which the most well known are probability theory [19] and possibility theory [20-22]. In case of probability theory, $\operatorname* { P r } ( p ( d ) = T )$ expresses the probability that the data d satisfy the predicate p. The probability $\operatorname* { P r } ( p ( d ) = F )$ is then simply given by $1 - \operatorname* { P r } ( p ( d ) = T )$ . Similarly, possibility theory is capable of expressing the extent to which it is possible that p takes on the truth value true (Pos (p(d) = T))) or false (Pos(p(d) = F)) when evaluated on the data d. The two possibilities together then form a possibilistic truth value (PTV) $( \mathrm { P o s } ( p ( d ) = T ) , \mathrm { P o s } ( p ( d ) = F ) )$ . In the past, diferent techniques were created to assign numerical values that reflect uncertainty [16,23,24]. Instead of a binary truth value, the result of a predicate evaluation under uncertainty is thus a probability or possibility distribution over the binary truth values. It should also be noted that although probability and possibility theory are capable of handling uncertainty, both theories can still correctly express the results of predicate evaluations of which the truth value is known exactly. In case of possibility theory, this is done by stating that $\operatorname* { P r } ( p ( d ) = T ) = 1 { \mathrm { ~ o r ~ } } \operatorname* { P r } ( p ( d ) = F ) = 1$ for those predicates. Similarly, in case of possibility theory, we can say that (Pos( ( ) ), Pos( ( ) )) (1, 0)p d T p d F= = = or (Pos( ( ) ), Pos( ( ) ))p d T p d F= = = (0, 1).

Although both uncertainty models can be useful in the context of predicate evaluation, both can be preferred in specific situations. Possibility theory is a very conservative theory that is especially wellsuited to express uncertainty in case absolutely no or very few information is present. This can be for example due to the fact that information that is needed to evaluate a predicate is simply not mentioned in the scientific paper. The situation where we have no information can be formally represented by the PTV (1,1): it is completely possible that the predicate takes on the value true, but it is also completely possible that the predicate takes on the value false. Expressing this situation is much more dificult (if not impossible) using regular probabilities<sup>1</sup>. One might think of using a uniform probability distribution over the truth values, i.e. Pr( ( ) )p d T= = $\Pr ( p ( d ) = F ) = 0 . 5 \qquad $ . However, this is not equivalent to stating that no information is available. Indeed, assuming that the truth values of two independent predicates p (d) and $p _ { 2 } ( d )$ are both modelled as a uniform distribution, the probability that both are satisfied can be calculated as $\operatorname* { P r } ( p _ { 1 } ( d ) \wedge p _ { 2 } ( d ) ) = 0 . 5 \times 0 . 5 = 0 . 2 5 $ . This suggests that in a scenario of no information at all, we infer that quality of data is lower. Uniform probability distributions are thus no correct representation of a scenario with missing information. We point out that, via experiments in which large amounts of scientific papers are investigated, one could possibly obtain a probability distribution about the truth value of a predicate, conditional on the fact that the information needed to evaluate the predicate was not present in the scientific paper. Provided that such data is available, probability theory is the best choice to build an uncertain measurement system on, but one should also realize that collecting such data would be a very costly operation.

## 3.4. Constructing capacities under uncertainty

Once the (possibly uncertain) evaluation of each predicate in the low-level predicate set is performed, the quality level of the IS research data should be determined. As explained in Section 2, this is done by the construction of a capacity function. The easiest possible capacity function simply maps the research data to the number of rules that are satisfied, which implies that the satisfaction of each predicate is assumed equally important to obtain high quality data. However, typically this is not the case: some rules are more important to the data quality than others. This should thus be reflected in the behaviour of the constructed capacity. It should be noted that the construction of the capacity function is complicated by two diferent factors. These are discussed in the following.

1. Domain size of capacity function. The cardinality N of the power set of the predicate set P increases exponentially with the number of predicates in the predicate set: $N = 2 ^ { | P | }$ . This implies that the number of possible inputs of the capacity also increases exponentially. Manually constructing a capacity thus rapidly becomes infeasible. As an example, the capacity function for the measurement system depicted in Fig. 1 would have $2 ^ { 1 8 }$ predicate subsets as possible input. This number can however be drastically reduced by identifying subsets of predicates related to similar data quality aspects and constructing smaller capacity functions for each of these subsets. In the example of Fig. 1, a possible approach is to group all lowest-level predicates belonging to the same parent predicate. This leads to the construction of nine diferent smaller capacities, each associating an ordinal scale with the quality measurement of that subaspect. Subsequently, capacity functions can be defined that are based on the quality values coming out of the previously evaluated capacity functions. The predicates on which these capacity functions are built can then be of a form similar to “The data processes related to subaspect x have a quality level of at least $y . \stackrel { \triangledown } { }$ Finally, an encompassing capacity can be constructed that assesses a single quality value to the data based on the obtained quality levels for each of the seven W's. This approach drastically simplifies the construction of the measurement system. In case of the survey data quality measurement system, the number of inputs for the final capacity function decreases from $2 ^ { 1 8 }$ to only 194 (i.e. 62 predicate combinations that serve as inputs for the nine lowest-level capa cities, 4 possible inputs in total for $C _ { h o w }$ and $C _ { w h o }$ and $2 ^ { 7 } = 1 2 8$ possible inputs of the final decisive capacity function). This number can be reduced even more by assuming a natural order in the eva. luation of predicates, such that predicate subsets only form a possible capacity input if for each predicate that is satisfied, all pre dicates that are considered to be less strict are also satisfied [10]. Using the strategies detailed out above, a simplified capacity structure can thus be built on the predicate set of Fig. 1. 2. Uncertainty about predicate evaluations. A second problem with the construction of the capacity is that the uncertainty about the exact predicate values propagates to the ordinal quality scale. While the rule-based measurement framework of Section 2 is capable of assigning one quality level to a piece of data, this is not possible anymore under uncertainty. Instead, the uncertainty model that was used to express the uncertainty about the truth value taken on by a predicate should also be used to express uncertainty about the exact quality level of the data. As a simple example, consider the predicate $p _ { w h a t , 1 }$ of Fig. 1. Table 1 indicates that $C _ { w h a t }$ maps the survey data to the highest possible quality if this predicate evaluates to true, or to 0 otherwise. However, Boolean evaluation of this predicate is not possible as it is vague. As such, the evaluation of the predicate should express the uncertainty about its truth value. To this end, a possibilistic approach is used in the context of this paper. For exemplary purposes, in this paper the assigned values are the result of the subjective assessment of the authors. More specifically, ordinal categories regarding possible outcomes of a predicate evaluation were defined. In the case of $p _ { w h a t , 1 ; }$ , the categories “the content of the survey is unknown”, “Some parts of the survey are detailed out in the paper.”, “All questions in the survey are mentioned in the $\mathrm { p a p e r } . \mathrm { } ^ { \mathrm { \prime \prime } }$ and “A link to the full survey is given” can for example be defined. These were then mapped on possibilistic values indicating the extent to which the authors believe these categories can be interpreted as ${ \bf \ " { a } }$ clear description”, e.g. respectively 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1.0. Based on these values and the optimistic calculation technique described in [16], a PTV can then be derived from the performed measurement.

Based on the possibility (PTV) or probability distributions over the truth values of the lowest-level predicates, new possibility (PTV) or probability distributions can be generated over the truth values of higher-level predicates. In general, these can be obtained by using the minimal requirements for each quality level, such as the ones indicated in Table 1. Indeed, because we have an uncertainty estimate for each of the basic predicates, we can calculate for each such logical expression the probability or possibility distribution that it evaluates to true. In other words, we can calculate for any level of quality of any subaspect the PTV or probability distribution over the truth values of the logical expression. As such, possibility or probability distributions are obtained over the truth values of predicates similar $\mathbf { t o } \ ^ { \ast } Q _ { t e s t i n g } \geq 1 ^ { \prime }$ . The obtained distributions can then again be combined in order to obtain new distributions over the truth values of higher-order predicates. This process can be repeated until the possibility or probability distributions are obtained over the truth values related to the diferent quality levels of the total quality $Q _ { t o t a l } .$ Finally, from the obtained distributions, a possibility or probability distribution over the ordinal total quality scale can be inferred.

## 4. Defining quality thresholds

Depending on whether uncertainty is present in one of the predicates or not, the result of the proposed measurement procedure is either a quality level or a probability/possibility distribution over quality levels. Remember however that the essential question that is asked in a data quality investigation is “Are data of high enough quality or not?” To answer this question, a final binary decision should be made based on the outcome of the quality measurement. This can be done by defining quality thresholds. In case no uncertainty is present, this simply comes down to defining a minimum quality level the data should reach. In case uncertainty is present however, instead of only a minimum quality level, the quality threshold should be composed of both a minimum quality level and a given certainty with which this quality level should be reached

By defining quality thresholds, one is capable of expressing very precisely which quality standards research data should meet. The minimum quality level that should be reached by the data can for example be based on the specific requirements that should be fulfilled in order to reach that quality level. In the context of survey data, it can be argued that the data should fulfill at least the most basic requirements belonging to each of the quality aspects (except why). This corresponds to a quality threshold at level 2. However, the developed measurement system for survey data quality has uncertainty associated with it and thus the defined quality thresholds should take this into account. As such, it should also be decided how much uncertainty one allows regarding the quality level of the data. Information about the companies and how to contact their CIO was obtained by renting a dedicated database from a third-party consisting of 3500 tech companies. ## 6. Conclusions

In this paper, a rule-based framework for the measurement and assessment of the quality of data in Information Systems research is presented. The framework is able to handle both problems due to the incorrect execution and description of data collection and validation. It is shown how a set of predicates, a capacity function and an ordinal quality scale are essentially enough to be able to measure the quality of a data set. Moreover, the practical implementation of a measurement framework is illustrated in the case of survey data. It is then argued that in some cases uncertainty arises during quality measurement. While uncertainty due to too complex predicates can be removed, this is not the case for uncertainty because of lack of information about the data collection and validation and uncertainty due to vaguely formulated predicates. This uncertainty is handled by extending the predicate formalism to express the certainty with which predicates take on truth values. It is shown that the result of the measurement procedure in case of uncertainty is not a single quality level anymore, but rather a probability or possibility distribution over the ordinal quality scale. Attention is also given to the importance of quality thresholds in order to be able to decide on the quality of the data. Finally, the constructed system for the quality measurement of survey data is applied to an exemplary scientific paper and its associated survey data.
