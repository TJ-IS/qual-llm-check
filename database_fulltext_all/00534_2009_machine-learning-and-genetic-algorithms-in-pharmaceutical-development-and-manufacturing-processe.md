## a b s t r a c t

We develop an adaptive Automated Intelligent Manufacturing System (AIMS) for Case 1:to a wellunderstood-pharmaceutical-process to demonstrate our methodology, Case 2:with clustering, to a not-wellcontrolled or understood-process for seemingly identical experiments yielding disparate results, Case 3:to scale-up a process from development to manufacturing, and Case 4:to deploy AIMS adaptively, to modify the process model and reoptimize the system contemporaneously, when predictive errors are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The results showed AIMS had both explanatory and predictive power. We have developed the following methodological extensions: a random probe method for feature selection, a simulation approach to establish tolerances for target inputs, and an adaptive capability integrated with statistical-process-control to modify the model.

## 1. Introduction

Our objective is to demonstrate the feasibility, viability, and value of applying computational intelligence (machine learning) and evolutionaryalgorithms to respectively model and optimize real development and manufacturing processes by intelligently and novelly applying such algorithms to an actual commercial pharmaceutical process. We call this integrated approach of modeling and optimization by machine an “Automated Intelligent Manufacturing System (AIMS).” A major motivation for proposing such an approach is a natural consequence of the information revolution, whereby Information Technology (IT) has made it possible, indeed routinely natural, to provide process data in real-time to process owners to improve system behavior and performance. IT thus has been a major driver of increased productivity in the manufacturing and service sectors. As a consequence, IT has helped to spawn the popular discipline and execution of ‘Six Sigma’ as an enterprise strategy, which generally requires training individuals in the organization to use univariate and multivariate statistics to facilitate process improvement, often at substantial cost. Of speci<sup>fi</sup>c interest to the pharmaceutical <sup>fi</sup>rm in this study was to demonstrate a complementary approach, namely the value of using machine learning and automated optimization tools as an alternative means of enhancing process knowledge by integrating quality by design with process analytical technology tools for improving the quality and productivity of manufacturing processes in the pharmaceutical industry [34]. Currently, process improvement in progressive manufacturing companies (including those in the pharmaceutical industry) is achieved by applying such well-known rigorous ‘Six Sigma’ analytical tools as Statistical Process Control (SPC) and Design of Experiments (DOE)/response surface design and optimization. Unfortunately, DOE can be very time intensive, operationally disruptive, as well as requiring competent statistical expertise (which may be a limited organizational resource). We propose the following complementary approach to formal experimental design to facilitate process improvement: (a) automate, integrate, and deploy data mining tools such as machine learning and evolutionary algorithms to respectively model and optimize an actual system, and (b) employ these automated algorithms dynamically and continuously to allow the models and optimal target input settings to adjust and adapt contemporaneously to additional data being generated by a process, irrespective of whether the system is stable or changing. Conceptually, our vision is to use such algorithms to foster more effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient system decision making by letting humans do what they do best: perform the creative part of decision making in a ‘Six Sigma’ sense, de<sup>fi</sup>ne and measure (e.g., de<sup>fi</sup>ne inputs, outputs and associated metrics); and letting machines do what they do best: namely based on data, perform the analysis (viz model, optimize) … analyze, improve, and control the process. While it is true that such data mining tools may be less well known than the traditional multivariate statistical techniques the fact that they can be easily automated and deployed unobtrusively without disrupting operations can provide a considerable competitive advantage.

Machine learning and optimization algorithms have to a degree been applied previously to manufacturing systems, including pharmaceutical manufacturing [18,21]. Other examples of applying machine learning and genetic algorithms to model and then optimize a manufacturing system include [7,12,13,15,35]. Here, statistical tools including DOE, chemometric models, and other process analytical technologies have also been applied to such pharmaceutical processes as crystallization, which is the focus of our current application. Our approach is differentiated from these more traditional multivariate statistical process tools in the following sense: our Automated Intelligent Management System (AIMS) mines real-time and/or historical data, using statistical and computational intelligence algorithms (based on statistical learning theory) in concert with evolutionary algorithms to simultaneously model and optimize process performance.

We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally address each of the following non-mutually exclusive situations of interest and characteristics of the pharmaceutical manufacturers and the industry: (a) the input variable stream is rich in complexity involving a possible variety of interacting variables, where the potential exists for redundant and/or irrelevant controls that are not apparent from empirical design, (b) there may be crucial input variables that are not apparent and missing from the dataset that, if known, would enhance process control strategies or simplify process design (this is particularly relevant in the early stages of product development), and (c) there are control parameters that may be speci<sup>fi</sup>c to equipment type, size, or operation that may change as a result of transitioning from the laboratory to pilot and then to commercial manufacture (scale-up), thus in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the robustness of the overall manufacturing operation. These are often referred to as site-speci<sup>fi</sup>c or equipment-speci<sup>fi</sup>c manufacturing issues, and typically indicate a lack of robust process understanding and control that would be expected, indeed demanded, by regulatory agencies. The data mining and evolutionary algorithms employed here to deal with these issues involve (a) the development and use of a regression Support Vector Machine (SVM), (b) a novel feature selection method called random probe, (c) clustering for model construction and analysis, and (d) an adaptive algorithm to integrate continuously generated additional process data, in concert with a Genetic Algorithm (GA) for dynamic model optimization. To allow for variation in the system inputs, a simulation routine is also developed and applied to obtain tolerances around the optimal input targets generated from the SVM and Genetic Algorithm (GA) that would satisfy output speci<sup>fi</sup>cations to a given level of desirability.

We have previously and successfully applied AIMS to model and optimize a simulated product development and manufacturing environment of a <sup>fi</sup>rm in the consumer electronics industry, and to a company's supply chain network in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce supplies industry [10,11]. In this paper, we next apply and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly extend the development and application of our AIMS methodology to an actual commercial pharmaceutical development and manufacturing process to address issues of interest to pharmaceutical manufacturers, as well as demonstrate the viability, potential advantages, and universality of using machine learning and evolutionary algorithms to effectively and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently model and optimize processes under a variety of situations experienced in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Three extensions of our methodology developed include (a) a novel feature selection method called the random probe, to perform model reduction (removal of insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant terms) ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently, and (b) a simulation approach for establishing tolerances on target inputs obtained from AIMS, and (c) an adaptive AIMS methodology integrating statistical process control with engineering control to provide feedback for triggering model and parameter changes based on additional acquired process data.

## 1.1. Objectives

Our research objectives were as follows:

a. Model validation and veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of AIMS: to gain initial credibility, assess the validity of applying AIMS to model and optimize data in an actual commercial pharmaceutical manufacturing process setting.

b. Setting tolerances (speci<sup>fi</sup>cations) on optimal input targets: based on target input settings obtained from the SVM and GA, develop a methodology to determine tolerances for the target inputs to allow for the inability to control such targets precisely due to inherent variation, yet assure satisfying product speci<sup>fi</sup>cations to a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed level of desirability.

c. Model reduction — validation and veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of random probe method: develop, verify, and validate the use of a novel random probe method for model reduction, and compare the results to the parsimonious (reduced) model obtained via DOE, using a common dataset (Case 1).

d. Clustering — model improvement from seemingly identical datasets yielding different results: demonstrate the ability of clustering algorithms such as k-means in conjunction with AIMS to improve explanatory and predictive power of an aggregated dataset comprised of observational and seemingly identical experimental data subsets performed during product development over time, where the same input variables yield dissimilar outputs, suggesting the existence of missing relevant input variables (Case 2).

e. Scale-up–scale-independent modeling from development to manufacturing: demonstrate how AIMS can be deployed to assess/ verify whether and which product development models can be ‘scaled up’ to manufacturing models (“scale-independent/robust models), potentially saving considerable cost (Case 3).

f. Adaptive AIMS — implementation on-line: using feedback in concert with statistical process and engineering control, develop and demonstrate an adaptive AIMS capability which dynamically updates a process model and optimal target input settings in realtime as new process data is generated, under conditions of system stability, instability, or change (Case 4).

The paper consists of <sup>fi</sup>ve sections. In Section 2, the pharmaceutical process application is considered and its various operational parameters are described. In Section 3, the machine learning, model reduction, clustering, and evolutionary algorithms employed in AIMS are presented. In Section 4, the methodologies for each of the above cases are developed, applied, and compared (as appropriate) to the results obtained from DOE. Conclusions and future research are presented in Section 5.

Although there are numerous reports of successful machine learning applications in the pharmaceutical industry, such as in drug discovery and design [2,5,22], and the prediction of various adverse drug reactions (ADRs) [33], our research described in this paper focuses in pharmaceutical manufacturing, and differs from the prior research in the following aspects: uses real data from several commercial pharmaceutical processes, attempts to model and optimize a real pharmaceutical processes using SVM and GA, uses machine learning techniques to estimate the tolerances for target inputs, veri<sup>fi</sup>es scale dependency from drug development to manufacturing, employs clustering techniques in situations where seemingly identical datasets yielding different results to improve model predictability, applies adaptive algorithms with machine learning techniques to continuously adapt and monitor system outputs.

## 2. Background on pharmaceutical process

The manufacturing of an Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) or bulk drug substance involves a series of chemical manipulations that may involve bond formation and bond breakage, and crystallization followed by isolation of the desired chemical entity. The synthetic route can vary from as simple as one to two chemical transformations with only a few chemical reactants to those that involve in excess of ten isolation and puri<sup>fi</sup>cation steps with numerous starting materials and intermediates. The development of a chemical process for the commercial scale manufacturing of an API relies on deep technical expertise in chemical synthesis, strong interaction between engineering design and control as well as appropriate analytical technologies and expertise to measure the process output. The intent of the pharmaceutical process is to deliver the intended chemical entity with the desired quality attributes (purity, assay, identity and crystal form), and do this with the highest yield that is attainable while minimizing the cycle time from batch to batch. However, the detailed data generation, data mining and interpretation, and subtle complexities (interactions) that must be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, understood, and controlled are often underestimated in achieving the goal of a manufacturing process that meets the objectives. Thus, even a simple salt formation process can become quite dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to control if it is not properly developed.

Robust chemical processes are achieved in the pharmaceutical industry by developing information about the process that enables suitable manufacturing control strategies to be employed. These control strategies or levers are developed based upon scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory and principles, experimental data obtained throughout development of the process, suitable engineering evaluation, and analytical methods that ultimately deliver a body of knowledge that supports the understanding of the process requirements.

For the process under investigation, a single step, acid/base crystallization which produces a desired salt form of the API is considered. The process has several distinct operations within one chemical reaction including dissolution, crystallization, isolation, and drying. The procedure concerning how each operation is carried out or controlled can impact the overall quality of the desired product. The key quality attributes of an API are identity (physical form), purity (the desired compound with minimal impurities) and crystal size as measured by laser light scattering techniques. These quality attributes are often interrelated and dependent on how the process is achieved. The crystallization process modeled here is no exception. Several variables that have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in potentially controlling a spontaneous acid/base crystallization can include, but are not limited to, the crystallization temperature, concentration of the reaction before crystallization, the amount, duration, temperature or mode of addition of the antisolvent used to effect crystallization, and mixing parameters (agitation, <sup>fl</sup>uid dynamics) within the crystallization device. Data obtained from this process served as the basis for system modeling and optimization using our machine learning and evolutionary algorithms. A listing of the process inputs are enumerated subsequently.

## 3. Structure of the proposed algorithms

The proposed machine learning and evolutionary algorithms are data mining and optimization tools respectively capable of modeling a pharmaceutical development and/or manufacturing process and specifying and adjusting the input settings and con<sup>fi</sup>gurations to optimize system performance. These algorithms complement, and under certain circumstances have the potential to substitute for classical multivariate experimental/statistical techniques, such as DOE, response surface design and optimization, or regression analysis. Unlike DOE, the proposed algorithms do not require the creation and execution of experimental designs to generate useful data, but instead can exploit available on-line real-time or historical data. Moreover, the fact that such observational input and output data are often highly collinear (correlated) is not a drawback for our machine learning algorithms, as may be the case, for example, when employing multivariate statistical methodologies, such as regression analysis.

The structure of our proposed algorithms consists of two major components: (a) training a machine learning algorithm using historical or real-time data (e.g., a 2nd-order (or higher order) polynomial Support Vector Machine (SVM) for regression) to model the process (constructing an explanatory and predictive model), and (b) applying a global optimization methodology such as a Genetic Algorithm (GA) to obtain target input settings that would yield optimum system performance (the optimization process). ## 3.1. Support Vector Machines (SVMs) for regression

Support vector learning is based on a machine learning algorithm which originates from the structural risk minimization principle in statistical learning theory, using an induction principle to minimize a bound on generalization error (i.e. error rate on future unseen data) [29]. It involves implicitly mapping the input vectors to the feature vectors through various kernel functions in such a way that computation in the high dimensional feature space is avoided, and then constructing a linear function with the ε-insensitive loss function in the feature space. Extensive recent research has shown that SVMs have many attractive features and exhibit promising empirical performance relative to other conventional learning algorithms currently used to solve many real-world problems. For example, SVMs for regression have been used successfully in medical image segmentation [31], signal processing [30], time series prediction [23], and <sup>fi</sup>nancial forecasting [28]. In [14] the authors use type of domain knowledge where input space is inside of a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed convex polytope, to obtain tighter error bounds on SVM classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. In [4] learning for principal–agent models which employ utility based, adversarial, and strategic learning, is studied whereas in our paper we have an adaptive model which learns based on additional data which is used to modify the model. SVMs have been used in credit rating prediction in the USA and in Taiwan [19], when to buy and sell stocks [26], in assessing the internal sovereign rating system and its associated risk [16], in currency exchange rate forecasting [20], to assist clinicians in diagnosing pain [6], in forming clustering patterns of on-line surveillance methods and to track incremental changes over time [8]. The concept and methodology of SVMs can be found in [3,10,27].

## 3.2. SVM model reduction using random probe method

A 2nd-order polynomial SVM always produces a linear output equation with coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for all linear, quadratic, and interaction terms. However, in real situations, many of these terms, including some of the interaction and quadratic terms, will not be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in modeling a process. In fact, via model reduction by removing the insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant terms, the overall prediction accuracy of the SVM model can often be improved, due to the bias-variance trade-off [17]. In addition, since a mathematical equation with fewer terms is more parsimonious, requiring less data and allowing easier analysis and interpretation, a simpler and more compact equation, which is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to model a process, is obviously preferable. Analogous to model reduction in statistical analysis, we develop and apply a novel feature selection algorithm called the random probe method to the 2nd-order polynomial SVM to remove insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant terms.

## 3.3. Cluster analysis for missing (unidentified) input variables

In our pharmaceutical data for Case 2, k-means clustering was used to partition an aggregated dataset comprised of both observational and experimental data collected over time during development that had identical input settings but different output values, into different clusters (based on the output values). Initially the data from these experiments were treated as one dataset which yielded a model with poor predictive and explanatory power.

## 3.4. Global optimization using a Genetic Algorithm (GA)

Global optimization attempts to identify/locate the conditions or targets that result in the highest desired objective function value. Traditional optimization methods such as gradient ascent/descent, search in the direction of the local gradient vector, and thus can easily get mired at a local minimum in an application with a multimodal objective function. We employed a Genetic Algorithm (GA), a popular and widely-used technique for global optimization, because of its several advantageous features.

## 4. Case results: performance of AIMS

We now present the results of applying our AIMS methodology with the random probe procedure for feature selection/model reduction to the various case situations of interest determined and provided by the pharmaceutical manufacturer. These included data from (a) a well-understood process (ready for manufacturing), (b) an early development process undergoing evolving ad-hoc experimentation, and (c) two crystallization control approaches used to scale-up from development to manufacturing (i.e., to ascertain whether the models (and therefore processes) created in development were scale independent).

We have also developed additional methodology to address two important practical issues: $\left( \mathsf { a } \right)$ how to determine tolerances around the optimal target input settings to assure output speci<sup>fi</sup>cations are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed to a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed level of desirability, and (b) how to make AIMS adapt over time to additional data generated by an ongoing process. This involves dynamic remodeling, re-optimization, and re-tolerancing of optimal input targets based on feedback and process control principles.

4.1. Case 1: data from a well-understood process — validation of AIMS methodology

Data from a well-understood commercial pharmaceutical manufacturing process described in Section 2 was <sup>fi</sup>rst used to validate the credibility of our methodology in a real application. The process contained 12 input variables (three of which were binary (0, 1) and nine continuous variables) and 3 real-valued con<sup>fl</sup>icting output variables (the performance metrics). The continuous input variables were water amount $\left( x _ { 1 } \right)$ , water addition time (x ), stir rate (x ), addition temperature $\left( x _ { 4 } \right)$ , stir time $\left( x _ { 5 } \right)$ , water temperature for second water addition (x ), batch addition temperature (x ), duration of second water addition $\left( x _ { 8 } \right)$ , and cooling duration $\left( x _ { 9 } \right)$ . The output variables include Solvent % $\left( y _ { 1 } \right)$ , XRPD $\left( y _ { 2 } \right)$ , and d90 (y ).

This dataset involved 109 training samples and 14 validation samples. Originally unknown to us, it was obtained from a previous DOE-created design conducted, analyzed, and modeled by the pharmaceutical manufacturer. Our goal, using the data with our SVM and GA technology, was to <sup>fi</sup>rst develop a parsimonious model, and then search for a set of input target settings that yielded an optimum for the three con<sup>fl</sup>icting output variables. The target input results from the AIMS methodology were to be compared with that from the manufacturer's DOE-created design.

## 4.1.1. Modeling using SVMs and random probe method

All training input variables were <sup>fi</sup>rst standardized to zero mean and unit variance. Using the data from the company's small scale manufacturing process (in development) described in Section 2, a 2nd-order polynomial SVM system was trained to formulate the X–Y relationships, i.e.,

$$
\begin{array}{c} y = w _ {0} + w _ {1} x _ {1} + \dots + w _ {m} x _ {m} + w _ {m + 1} x _ {1} ^ {2} + \dots + w _ {2 m} x _ {m} ^ {2} \\ + w _ {2 m + 1} x _ {1} x _ {2} + w _ {2 m + 2} x _ {1} x _ {3} + \dots . \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

Usually, before training a SVM, only a few decisions (kernel type and its associated parameters, as well as the regularization parameter C) must be chosen to ensure good prediction performance. This is called model selection, which is problem speci<sup>fi</sup>c. The typical method, which is computationally intensive and time-consuming, involves an automated grid search over a wide range of the parameter space to identify the function and parameter values which yield the lowest error on a validation dataset. However, in our application, since a 2ndorder polynomial kernel function is often suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to reasonably model a physical manufacturing process, the only parameter needed to be chosen was the regularization parameter C. This reduced the model selection problem to a one-dimensional search. The value C was selected using the validation method, which involved training the SVM on the training data, validating it on a separate set of test data, and then selecting the value of C that gave the lowest validation mean squared error. Once the 2nd-order polynomial SVM was trained, the random probe feature selection (or model reduction) method was applied to remove irrelevant/insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant terms.

Table 1 presents the modeling results for Case 1. Table 1a lists the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of all 12 input variables including the interaction and quadratic terms for the 3 outputs (performance metrics) by the SVM with the random probe method with respect to the standardized dataset. In using the random probe method, ten 2nd-order polynomial SVMs were trained independently to estimate the means and variances of $w _ { 1 } , . . . , w _ { \mathrm { n } }$ and $w _ { \mathrm { r } }$ with the threshold and $\gamma ^ { 2 }$ (a parameter to allow <sup>fl</sup>exibility to control the level of sparsity of the remaining input features (see Online Supplement)) set to 0.50 and 1, respectively. Some of the linear, interaction, and quadratic terms were also regarded as insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, as their corresponding coef<sup>fi</sup>cients were zero, and thus were removed from the <sup>fi</sup>nal equations. As it turned out, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the linear terms $x _ { 5 } , x _ { 9 } , x _ { 1 0 } , x _ { 1 1 }$ and $x _ { 1 2 }$ were all zero for all three outputs, indicating that these input variables were unimportant in predicting any of the three outputs (although some of the interaction terms containing these input variables turned out to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant). The training process took less than 5 min on a Pentium 4 machine with 512 MB RAM.

Except for the validation mean squared error for XRPD, the SVM with the random probe method achieved lower mean squared errors than the full SVM for all outputs, indicating more accurate predictions were obtained using the random probe method, and verifying the validity of applying the random probe method to remove irrelevant terms. The fact that XRPD had a higher validation error was probably due to statistical variation, and it could be lower if a different value of $\gamma ^ { 2 }$ was used to adjust the number of terms selected in the <sup>fi</sup>nal equation.

## 4.1.3. Feasibility analysis: setting tolerances (specifications) on target inputs

In addition to obtaining the optimal target input settings, it is often desirable, indeed necessary, to determine the range of the input settings (viz, tolerances) that satisfy the output speci<sup>fi</sup>cations in Table 2a. Process inputs in general, and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in pharmaceutical manufacturing, are susceptible to variations and therefore dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to control precisely; moreover, they may drift over time. Hence, it is important to allow for such variation and still satisfy the output speci<sup>fi</sup>cations to given levels of desirability. This is critical in the highly government-regulated pharmaceutical industry, where out-of-speci-<sup>fi</sup>cation product can be extremely costly. Unfortunately, the feasibility region of the input parameters is often irregular and may even be disjoint. It is therefore dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to analytically solve for, as well as to describe the range of all the feasible input settings in a compact and interpretable way. To overcome this problem, we have developed a simulation approach that speci<sup>fi</sup>es a “possible” feasible subset of input settings around the desirable output targets as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst locate the optimal input settings (targets) using the GA, then randomly sample data points within a distance ±l along each dimension from the optimal input set point. A data point is feasible if its corresponding outputs satisfy all speci<sup>fi</sup>cations to a pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed level of desirability. The probability of having a feasible input setting can be computed by taking the ratio of the number of feasible points to the total number of random-sampled points within this hyperbox of distance l from the optimal input setting. The distance l is measured as a fraction of the largest possible range of each input parameter. For instance, an l of 0.1 corresponds to a hyperbox with length equal to twice the 0.1 of the input range in each dimension, and with the optimal input setting as the centroid. Instead of sampling inside a hyperbox, we could have collected samples uniformly inside a hypersphere [24]. We chose the former method because it is simpler and easier for practitioners to implement and analyze. Moreover, this method can be applied to determine the region around an optimal input setting where the individual output desirabilities, or the aggregate desirability, exceed a speci<sup>fi</sup>c value. In this case, the average desirabilities over all random samples were computed instead of the feasibility probability.

In this simulation experiment, the sample size was set to 1000. The results are shown in Fig. 3. Fig. 3a depicts the probability of a feasible point versus the distance l. As l gets larger, more data points tend to exceed the speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of the outputs called d90 and solvent %. In order to ensure all three outputs always satisfy the speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, the input settings can only deviate a distance l of 0.15 (shown by the vertical dashed line) from the optimal input settings, according to Fig. 3a. A distance l of 0.15 corresponds to a range of 0.0139 in $x _ { 1 } ,$ 129.75 in $x _ { 2 } ,$ 127.5 in $x _ { 3 } ,$ , 3.75 in $x _ { 4 } ,$ 12.9 in $x _ { 5 } ,$ 8.4 in $x _ { 6 } , 2 . 8 5$ in $x _ { 7 } ,$ 8.41 in $x _ { 8 } ,$ and 13.5 in $x _ { 9 } .$ It is also shown that d90 was the <sup>fi</sup>rst output that would most likely fail the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation as l increased beyond 0.15. This provides an idea of which output is the most critical in meeting the speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, and how much control and drift can be tolerated in the inputs in the pharmaceutical manufacturing process. Fig. 3b shows the plots of the individual and overall desirabilities versus the distance l. The desirability of d90 dropped off rapidly, which agrees with the rapid drop-off in the probability of feasibility in Fig. 3a. The probability and desirability of XRPD remained constant in both Fig. 3a and b, implying that XRPD was the least critical output that always met the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation for all possible values of input parameters within the given range.

## 4.1.4. Remarks

In practice, data would ordinarily not be organized/collected from a DOE-created design but rather from observational data to train the proposed machine learning algorithms. In fact, variation in the observational data (for each input variable in the training and validation set) is important, in order to fully represent the input space and to obtain appropriate parameter estimates. Moreover, machine learning algorithms can easily accommodate collinear observational (input) data. Notwithstanding the use of a DOE design, the results of these experiments demonstrate the validity and potential of machine learning and evolutionary algorithms to relatively seamlessly model and optimize data-intense systems. In managerial terms, let the analysis and optimization components of decision making be performed by an “intelligent” machine, and the creative part (e.g. determining the performance metrics, input variables) by a human. Moreover, if there is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient variation in the process data, these algorithms can be automated to unobtrusively model processes in real-time.

## 4.2. Case 2: early process development data — reconciling seemingly identical experiments yielding disparate results

The goal in this phase of our study was to apply our AIMS methodology to a pharmaceutical process in an early stage of development that was not-well controlled. The process, generally described in Section 2, contained 3 continuous input variables and 7 continuous output variables.

This dataset (as given to us) had 72 training samples, which were collected in ad-hoc experiments (some of which were seemingly identical) performed at different times. As previously noted, initially, the data from these experiments were treated as one dataset, which, when analyzed, yielded a model with poor predictive and explanatory power. In order to attempt to explain the variation, namely to identify differences between the various ad-hoc experiments and to try to discover missing critical input variables that were responsible for these differing results, k-means clustering was used in concert with SVMs, as discussed below.

## 4.2.1. Modeling using SVM and k-means clustering

The clustering/modeling results are shown in Table 3. These data clusters, which were collected during different phases of development, were probably obtained under different unknown experimental conditions, since mostly ad-hoc experimentation was conducted by the pharmaceutical manufacturer. The clustering resulted in different and better models for each cluster, and motivated the pharmaceutical company to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on why each set of data yielded different results, by attempting to account for the uncontrollable missing variables responsible for this phenomenon (e.g., use of different equipment).

## 4.3.1. Scale-up modeling using SVMs

A regular 2nd-order polynomial SVM was used to model the input– output relations of the two processes after standardizing the training input data to zero mean and unit standard deviation. The regularization parameter C was chosen by testing on the validation dataset in Process 1, using the LOO cross-validation method in Process 2, and choosing the one with the smallest validation mean squared error. Table 4 presents the results. Table 4a and b gives the training and testing mean squared errors, together with the factor of increase (i.e., the ratio of large scale test mean squared error to that of small scale) for both small and large scale data for Process 1 and Process 2, respectively. In Table 4a, the large scale errors were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly larger than the small scale errors for some outputs (especially Impurity 1 and Potency), while in Table 4b, the large scale errors were essentially the same magnitude as the small scale errors. The results in Table 4c suggest that Process 2 was much more scale-independent than Process 1. Moreover, feasibility analysis (as described in Section 4.1.3) on the Process 2 dataset revealed a set of horizontal lines at feasibility probability equal to one for all values of l, which corroborated the robustness of Process 2 with respect to being insensitive to changes in the inputs. Process 2, which was intrinsically controlled, was scaleindependent and not impacted signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly by equipment differences or other input (e.g. physical, environmental) factors. Process 1 however demonstrated that upon scale-up, it lacked suf<sup>fi</sup>cient robustness to continue commercialization using this control strategy approach. AIMS modeling con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that there was a high degree of inaccuracy in predicting the large scale outputs from small scale outputs, indicating dependence on extrinsically controlled factors such as equipment used. The large factors of increase for Potency and Impurities in Table 4c, two key quality attributes for both processes, allow these potential pitfalls to be readily observed leading to changed decisions regarding future development needs. Thus, machine learning techniques such as SVMs in verifying scale independence in transitioning from development to manufacturing provide another useful application of these algorithms.

We next focused on developing an adaptive AIMS model, so that the model and input settings it generates can be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed dynamically as additional process data is acquired (as would occur realistically with ongoing systems), fed back, and added to the original database.

4.4. Case 4: implementation: adaptive AIMS — adjusting to additional data

Real systems are dynamic, constantly generating new data. These data can emanate from a stable (one in control), unstable (one out of control), or changed process as a result of an improvement, failure, vendor change, etc. Accordingly, AIMS should be designed to be dynamic, capable of adjusting to feedback in the form of new data, if it is to be implemented in practice. Our objective was to develop and illustrate a procedure for implementing AIMS dynamically, by allowing it to adapt to additional data generated from a manufacturing process. The new data generated could either result from a stable process or one where known or unknown system changes have occurred (e.g. special causes due to failures, system improvements). In the case of a stable process, more data should result in more accurate SVM model parameter estimates and presumably minor adjustments to the optimal input settings. In the case of an unstable or changed process, the model and the optimal input settings could be altered signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly.

Based on feedback from new data, adaptive AIMS was designed to have two principle functions:

1. Detection … Has the model and hence the manufacturing process changed?

2. Adaptation … How should the model and its parameter estimates as well as the optimal input settings and tolerances change to re<sup>fl</sup>ect incorporation of the new data?

4.4.1. Detection of model changes using Statistical Process Control (SPC) charts

Statistical Process Control (SPC) charts are used to monitor the individual errors (residuals) between the actual outputs and the predicted outputs (i.e., y–ŷ) from the SVM model so as to detect any change in the actual manufacturing process. If there is no system change, the error should presumably decrease, as more data becomes available, yielding more accurate parameter estimates and model predictions. If there is a change in the actual process, the error will increase because the machine learning model would no longer be valid and thus no longer predict the outputs accurately. Here, a system change refers to an internal change in the actual system (e.g., as a result of a process improvement or vendor change), so that the exact same input settings now yield output values different from the values before the change. It therefore requires re-modeling of the machine learning model, re-optimizing the changed model to obtain new target inputs, and re-tolerancing of the new target inputs to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the new change. The usual statistical tests used in SPC can be employed to detect any system changes (see, e.g. Minitab).

## 4.4.2. Adaptation using Least Mean Squares (LMS) algorithm

Adaptive AIMS attempts to continuously update the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the machine learning model to realign it to the actual process, as new operational data is generated. As previously indicated, there are the following two possible general scenarios:

a. No system change: if there is no actual system change, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the SVM model will still be updated to re<sup>fl</sup>ect more accurate parameter estimates simply because more data is available. Therefore, the prediction error should tend to decrease or remain about the same.

b. System change: if a system change occurs, adaptive AIMS would adjust the model coef<sup>fi</sup>cients to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the new system, assuming there is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient variation in the input data settings.

It would be most desirable for the model to be highly responsive to (i.e. detect) system changes with as few new data as possible. In either case, the GA can then be used to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the optimum input settings with simulation to determine new target tolerance settings after an appropriate amount of data is collected.

There are several adaptive algorithms that can be used to update the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of a 2nd-order polynomial representation iteratively whenever new data samples are available. Examples include a LMS algorithm, Kalman <sup>fi</sup>ltering, and a recursive update algorithm for least squares SVMs [9]. All three methods were tested in our preliminary experiments, but LMS was ultimately selected because of its simplicity, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and relative better performance with respect to the other two methods. Below, we provide a simple demonstration of adaptive AIMS using LMS; other adaptive techniques such as Bayesian estimation will be studied more thoroughly in the near future.

LMS is derived from iteratively minimizing the squared error between the actual and predicted outputs using the method of gradient descent [1]. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient update equation is as follows:

$$
w _ {k + 1} = w _ {k} + \mu e _ {k} x _ {k}\tag{2}
$$

where $w _ { k + 1 }$ is the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient vector in the (k+1)-th iteration, $w _ { k }$ is the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient vector in the k-th iteration, µ is the step size whose value is between 0 and $1 , e _ { k }$ is the error between the actual output $y ( x _ { k } )$ and the predicted output $\hat { y } ( x _ { k } ) , \mathrm { i . e . , } y ( x _ { k } ) - \hat { y } ( x _ { k } )$ , and x is the k-th input vector. The value of the step size must be chosen carefully. A large step size will cause the LMS algorithm to diverge, while a small step size will converge slowly. The step size can also be made to vary from iteration to iteration (variable step size LMS).

## 4.4.3. Illustrative applications of adaptive AIMS

Both situations (a) and (b) in Section 4.4.2 were investigated in this illustrative experiment. The dataset used was identical to that employed in Case 1. Initially, a full 2nd-order polynomial SVM was trained using the initial dataset of 10 samples, and then the LMS algorithm was applied when an additional dataset of 159 samples was generated sequentially. Of these 159 data points, the <sup>fi</sup>rst 99 were obtained from the exact same process without any system change (corresponding to situation (a)). The next 60 samples were obtained after a sudden and sustained change of the system was induced at the 100th sample. This sudden and sustained change was simulated by arti<sup>fi</sup>cially changing the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the 2nd-order polynomial representation of the original system (corresponding to situation (b)). The step size µ in the LMS algorithm was determined experimentally to be 0.002.

Fig. 4a shows the I-MR SPC chart of prediction error for this experiment. From the <sup>fi</sup>rst to the 99th data point, the system was under control, and no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant deviation was detected. However, the individual error tended to decrease gradually because additional sample data were used to update and train the model, yielding more accurate estimates of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients using the LMS algorithm. The crosses that appear after the 100th data point indicate that the system went out of control as revealed by one (or more) of the eight statistical tests used in SPC (see, e.g. Minitab). This observation corresponded precisely with the sudden and sustained change that occurred at the 100th data point and beyond, as shown in the I-MR SPC chart. Thus, an SPC chart of model prediction error was very responsive in detecting a model change, implying a system change.

Situation (a) is further depicted in Fig. 4b, which plots the individual squared error of each additional data sample between the actual output and the predicted output versus the sample order. A trendline is shown to illustrate how the squared error decreased gradually as more data samples became available, indicating the ability of the LMS algorithm to adapt the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, to re<sup>fl</sup>ect a more accurate model of the system. Similarly, situation (b) was further exhibited in Fig. 4c. A trendline is also shown to illustrate how the squared error rapidly diminished and eventually saturated, supporting the validity and responsiveness of the LMS algorithm in adjusting the model coef<sup>fi</sup>cients to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the changed system.

## 5. Conclusions and future research

Our main goals were (a) to propose the use of machine learning (based on statistical learning theory) and evolutionary algorithms as a potentially effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient mechanism for modeling and optimizing processes, (b) to demonstrate and validate their applicability and desirability using data from real processes, and (c) to novelly extend and customize the methodologies to make the algorithms even more useful in practice. Our approach can be applied in any available data driven/intensive process environment. In pharmaceutical development and manufacturing, we have further extended our approach to parsimonious data environments as well, for example to a pharmaceutical milling process consisting of several stages, where there exist many potential explanatory variables but limited amount of data. Here, we developed a method analogous to Partial Least Squares, producing a number of orthogonal features based on all original variables, then performing SVM regression based on these features and the use of a GA to help identify the appropriate functional form for each orthogonal feature. We have also applied our approach to other related and non-related applications: (a) to supply chain management [11], (b) physiological and psychological human processes such as (1) in homeland security application responses to bioterrorism events, (2) breast cancer detection (involved development of an interactive clustering and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation machine learning algorithm [32]). Contrary to physical processes, requires “personalized models” as “one aggregate model does not <sup>fi</sup>t all”. Another pharmaceutical application for future research is drug ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy; namely, the same drug may help one individual but not another.

Based on this study, the proposed machine learning algorithms show considerable promise as a potential methodology for semiautomated system modeling and optimization, and pose several bene<sup>fi</sup>ts usually not obtained by more traditional multivariate statistical DOE methods, which can be disruptive to ongoing operations, as well as virtually always requiring scarce human statistical expertise.

Two main virtues of our proposed approach are: (a) although it is data intensive, it is virtually automatic and relatively seamless, generally requiring little human intervention, and (b) it can be designed to be adaptive in dynamic process environments. Information Technology has not only made the deployment of machine learning and evolutionary algorithms feasible, but also indeed a potentially highly desirable management and decision making tool, which can improve process understanding and hence product quality and productivity while simultaneously saving companies considerable time and money by reducing the need to often perform formal experiments for process improvement and optimization. The proposed machine learning and evolutionary algorithms can easily be extended to many management practices and applications in manufacturing, service, and healthcare. Our next step will be to implement the proposed machine learning and evolutionary algorithms as an automated, adaptive system on-line in an actual, real-time process environment.

With respect to future research, the proposed machine learning algorithms can be further modi<sup>fi</sup>ed and expanded as follows:

Multistage regression SVM

• Develop an automated multistage SVM (SVM Regression Tree structure) that will develop different models for an aggregated dataset that may contain datasets from different unknown processes, obviating the need to perform cluster analysis explicitly to partition the data into distinct subsets.

This dif<sup>fi</sup>cult problem would involve nonlinear optimization theory and methodology, including approximation techniques to de<sup>fi</sup>ne feasible regions, and simulation tools to ascertain and re<sup>fi</sup>ne such tolerances. As further considerations, the tolerances would need to take into account SVM modeling and measurement errors.
