# HOW AI-BASED SYSTEMS CAN INDUCE REFLECTIONS: THE CASE OF AI-AUGMENTED DIAGNOSTIC WORK<sup>1</sup>

Benjamin M. Abdel-Karim, Nicolas Pfeuffer, K. Valerie Carl, and Oliver Hinz Chair of Information Systems and Information Management, Goethe University Frankfurt am Main, GERMANY {BenjaminM.Abdel-Karim@gmx.de} {nicolaspfeuffer@gmx.de} {kcarl@wiwi.uni-frankfurt.de} {ohinz@wiwi.uni-frankfurt.de}

This paper addresses a thus-far neglected dimension in human-artificial intelligence (AI) augmentation: machine-induced reflections. By establishing a grounded theoretical-informed model of machine-induced reflection, we contribute to the ongoing discussion in information systems (IS) regarding AI and research on reflection theories. In our multistage study, physicians used a machine learning-based (ML) clinical decision support system (CDSS) to see if and how this interaction can stimulate reflective practice in the context of an X-ray diagnosis task. By analyzing verbal protocols, performance metrics, and survey data, we developed an integrative theoretical foundation to explain how ML-based systems can help stimulate reflective practice. Individuals engage in more critical or shallower modes depending on whether they perceive a conflict or agreement with these CDSS systems, which in turn leads to different levels of reflection depth. By uncovering the process of machine-induced reflections, we offer IS research a different perspective on how such AI-based systems can help individuals become more reflective, and consequently more effective, professionals. This perspective stands in stark contrast to the traditional, efficiency-focused view of MLbased decision support systems and also enriches theories on human-AI augmentation.

Keywords: Machine learning, reflective practice, grounded theory, health information technology, physicians, verbal protocols

## Introduction

One necessary condition for becoming an “effective professional” is learning from previous decisions (Schön, 1984; van Manen, 1977). In other words, effective professionals need to engage in self-reflection and critique, reevaluate their own actions, and maintain constant interaction with the rapidly changing modern environment to stay relevant and work effectively (Neumann Jr., 1999; Schön, 1984). Since it is difficult for professionals to engage in selfreflection without additional input, typical proposals for reflective practice involve a human (expert) coach assisting the reflecting individual (e.g., Neumann Jr., 1999).

Such reflective practice is of immense importance for effective analytical processes, especially for critical decisions in, for example, the field of medicine (Pearl & Mackenzie, 2018).

Much of the knowledge that differentiates effective professionals is tacit (Polanyi, 1987). Consequently, physicians may find it difficult to not only articulate their “theory in use” (e.g., Schön, 1984) but also to reflect on their own reasoning, especially during diagnosis and under time pressure (Mamede & Schmidt, 2004). Given the frequency of diagnostic errors (e.g., Singh et al., 2014), the numerous possible types of diagnostic errors (e.g., Pinto et al., 2016), and the fact that many of these errors may occur in time-sensitive situations, there seems to be value in exploring novel methods for encouraging professionals to reflect on their own actions in critical decisionmaking environments (such as healthcare).

Recent advancements in machine learning (ML)—a subset of artificial intelligence (AI)—have prompted research on how to best combine the capabilities of humans and ML-based systems. Research indicates that ML-based systems differ vastly from previous technologies discussed in information systems (IS) research; in fact, “ML may require massive rethinking of significant portions of the corpus of IS research” (Teodorescu et al., 2021, p. 1483).

Interestingly, prior research on the interplay between humans and ML-based systems, particularly in the realm of healthcare, has surfaced a paradox: One stream of literature examining possible new applications of ML technologies—especially as decision support systems (DSS) in, e.g., the clinical setting (e.g., Esteva et al., 2017)—has shown that these systems can fulfill tasks that usually require a human expert. In the healthcare sector, ML-based DSS in radiology already excels at providing diagnostic support and thus contributes, in practice, to alleviating the burden on physicians (e.g., lack of experts, limited time for reflection and learning). However, another stream of literature has argued that machines cannot substitute for human experts because machines lack (1) tacit knowledge, (2) the capability to reflect upon previous decisions, and (3) the ability to discuss their actions (e.g., Pearl & Mackenzie, 2018). Based on previous literature in the area of reflection theory, these human expert properties would be especially important for inducing reflection in the human user. However, while machines can potentially induce reflections in humans in ways that human-to-human reflections cannot, reflection theory has not devoted much attention to this possibility. If this is the case, using such systems as a reflection partner<sup>2</sup> could constitute the next stage of a fruitful human-machine collaboration.

Thus far, we know that ML-based DSS can effectively support decision-making in the professional context: for example, serving as rapid and accurate image-interpretation tools for physicians in the medical context (e.g., Jussupow et al., 2021; Lebovitz et al., 2022). However, previous research on this topic has focused on the actual decision-making rather than the accompanying process. The current research base is therefore inconclusive and has not provided an empirical foundation for the existence of machine-induced reflections. Thus, it remains an open question as to whether interactions with these systems can have any further transformative effects on analytical and high-stakes decision makers, such as physicians, above and beyond the direct influence on decision-making. From research on algorithms, medical imaging, and human-AI hybrids, it is apparent that ML-based systems could potentially support the discovery of relevant information that we would normally miss. Since these systems develop their own corresponding theoretical account of reality and build their own data-based computational models of this reality (e.g., Bauer et al., in press; Rook, 2013; Seidel et al., 2018), they might also be able to stimulate fruitful reflection on the part of the human user.

Given prior research on the abilities and boundaries of MLbased systems, we believe there is value in examining whether these systems can function as a reflection partner. Doing so would extend prior research on reflection, which has generally focused on human-induced (or expert-induced) reflection (e.g., Neumann Jr., 1999; Schön, 1987); specifically, it could illuminate a machine’s ability to induce reflections in humans despite its lack of distinct human properties. Thus, an important step toward establishing the efficient and beneficial usage of ML would be to examine prior research on reflection to support the idea of ML-based reflection partners. Therefore, our study poses the following research question:

RQ: How do ML-based systems stimulate reflective practice in professionals?

We explore this question in the context of healthcare and examine physicians’ machine-induced reflective practice. Since there is only an indirect theoretical foundation and little empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of such an approach, this topic requires an intensive, immersive, integrative, and idiographic study in a natural setting to establish sound theoretical insights. Building on prior research, we conducted an inductive study analyzing verbal protocols that serve as the core data source for our theory building process: We recorded six think-aloud protocols (pilot study) and 23 semi-structured interaction protocols (main study) while observing physicians engaging in an X-ray diagnosis task with and without an ML-based clinical decision support system (CDSS). In this way, we were able to learn if and how ML-based reflection partners could trigger reflections and how to integrate them into clinical practice.

Relating our findings to existing reflective practice and to the use of ML-based systems in healthcare, we contribute to research in several ways: First, we offer a novel theoretical model that explains how machine-induced reflections emerge. Second, we show different dimensions of machine-induced reflections, thus enriching theoretical knowledge on both human-AI collaboration and reflective practice. Third, we illustrate how these systems could lead to not only a more effective practice but also to professionals who are more reflective in the long run. Lastly, we offer IS research a different perspective from the traditional efficiency-focused view of ML-based systems (i.e., as DSS or recommender systems that usually aim to speed up a decision or reduce users’ cognitive efforts). By incorporating our findings into a generalizable model of machine-induced reflection, we lay the foundations for future research on MLbased reflection partners and on the enhancement of reflective practice in analytical professional work.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: First, we briefly summarize the related literature and connect it to our study. Next, we introduce our research approach. Then, we describe the results of our study, focusing on interactions between physicians and the ML-based reflection partner. Following this, we discuss the findings, the theoretical integration, and the machine-induced reflection model. We conclude by presenting some limitations and outlining how future research and practice could benefit from our study.

## Related Research and Theoretical Background

## Related Research

Reflections allow us to learn and thus expand our knowledge. This process is crucial in professional environments that involve continuous decision-making, such as medical practice. Learning through reflection typically involves interacting with human experts. Certain technical systems have attempted to support this learning process: In the general field of learning, intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) served as learning tutors for students and facilitated the learning of standardized knowledge (e.g., Anderson et al., 1985). However, ITS were not always suitable for supporting learning in practice because they were founded on cognitive learning theories that viewed knowledge as a prescribed and predefined form of information (e.g., Anderson et al., 1985). Thus, ITS were not ideal for “ill-defined” areas (Lynch et al., 2006, p. 3) such as clinical practice and other areas involving complex, emergent analytical work because the application of standardized knowledge in such areas may not result in efficient or effective decision-making. This shortcoming of rule-based expert systems like ITS (e.g., Anderson et al., 1985) can be eliminated through the use of ML-based systems, which excel in solving ill-defined problem sets. MLbased systems develop their own data-based computational models, while rule-based systems rely on predefined rules to solve such fuzzy problems (Berente et al., 2021). Beyond the logical and computational advantages of ML-based systems, they also have the potential to invoke fewer hierarchy conflicts between the machine and users compared to ITS (e.g., due to providing confidence measures; Lebovitz, 2019). Using MLbased CDSS for reflection thus resembles a relationship between reflection partners rather than a teacher-student relationship, which is typical for ITS.

Many of the more influential IS papers on the impact of algorithms on individual decision-making and learning were published about two decades ago (e.g., Gregor & Benbasat, 1999). These studies excluded potentially different effects of

ML-based systems from their theoretical explanations. For instance, the deployment of ML-based approaches, and especially deep learning (DL) ML-based systems, enabled higher predictive power, but their substantial black-box issues made explainability a salient issue (e.g., Rai et al., 2019). However, it is important to note that the experts publishing two decades ago used knowledge-based systems; thus, their explanations primarily served to verify knowledge rather than expand it (Gregor & Benbasat, 1999).

Recent research on physicians’ interactions with ML-based systems has made similar observations about this issue of verifying prior knowledge versus expanding it (Lebovitz, 2019). Beyond confirming and illuminating that experts experience conflicts in comparing their own “know-how” knowledge with the “know-what” knowledge of ML-based systems (Lebovitz et al., 2021), this type of research has revealed important theoretical nuances in the cognition and perception of ML performance and trustworthiness. Different studies have suggested that working with ML and related data during implementation can trigger reflections—and even mutual reflection cycles in stakeholders (Van den Broek et al., 2021) that benefit developers, experts, and the ML model at the same time. Research spotlighting radiologist-AI decisionmaking has discovered well-known decision patterns (e.g., automation or confirmation bias), including interesting cases of “deliberate consideration” (Jussupow et al., 2021). This decision pattern may be one of the most useful ones with the least potential for error because participants critically juxtapose their own opinions with that of the AI, considering both opinions at the same time (Jussupow et al., 2021). This decision pattern closely resembles what research describes as reflection (e.g., Schön, 1984).

Although the concept of reflection consistently appears as something useful in the above-mentioned research, IS theory has thus far assigned a secondary role to the cognitive process behind the emergence and effect of reflections. Recent research has focused on sensemaking under the influence of AI (Jussupow et al., 2022) but not from the perspective of reflection. While it is certainly important for theory to look at users’ reasoning for accepting or rejecting AI advice (Jussupow et al., 2021), we are interested in an end-to-end inspection of the reflective process triggered by the input of an ML-based system. We believe that by critically examining how AI input stimulates cognition and induces reflections, we can potentially uncover different reflection dynamics in this cognitive process. Such revelations may not only lead us to a greater understanding of individuals’ cognition but can also help us design interactions to effectively improve reflective practice and harness the full potential of ML-based systems.

This paper extends research on reflection by introducing the idea of ML-based systems as reflection partners. Current AI research (e.g., Jussupow et al., 2021; Lebovitz et al., 2022) has investigated AI mostly in the light of assisting and recommending roles in human-AI augmentation. In these contexts, humans rarely have the opportunity to make up their own minds before their judgment is augmented or tainted by the AI’s input. This situation may be reminiscent of previous research on the phenomenon of anchoring (i.e., a cognitive bias whereby humans tend to rely heavily on the information that is available first in the decision-making process—in this case, the AI’s input) (Epley & Gilovich, 2006). Being exposed to the AI’s input before forming a more impartial judgment makes it difficult for users of AI systems (and researchers alike) to disentangle the AI’s influence from their own judgment. Thus, users may struggle to reflect on their own reasoning and learn from interactions with the AI. Our paper addresses this issue by introducing a novel perspective on human-AI augmentation: using the AI as a reflection partner. Reflections between human reflection partners typically proceed as follows: One individual makes an initial judgment call. The reflection partner then responds to that judgment with their own opinion, and both individuals then compare, discuss, and reflect upon the issue at hand. In this process, individuals can make adjustments based on their own initial judgment and subsequent cognitions. This sequential process is similar to the reflection process induced by our ML-based system: Professionals first form their own assessment before being exposed to the AI’s input. This is both in contrast to DSS— where the different assessments occur simultaneously—and with recommender systems—where the recommender system makes the recommendation first.

To summarize, this paper differs from current AI research in terms of its subject (i.e., reflection about the decision rather than the decision outcome itself), the timing of the focal interaction (i.e., after an initial judgment has been made instead of during the judgment), the similarity of the examined ML-supported decision-making process with collaborative human diagnosis, and the type of influence that the AI system has on the human decision-making process. In short, this research contrasts with most prior IS research by focusing more on the decision process and less on the decision outcome.

## Theoretical Background

While making mistakes is undoubtedly human, medical errors can have major negative impacts on patients and can even lead to death. For example, approximately 12 million U.S. adults are harmed because of diagnostic errors every year—many severely (Singh et al., 2014). This situation is even more problematic when physicians make mistakes but do not reflect upon them or reflect upon them when it is too late to draw appropriate conclusions about how and why they happened. More importantly, many of these errors are due to time pressures. While more reflective work practices could mitigate many of these issues, such practices may be deemed too time-consuming (Mamede et al., 2020). Our research is based on the idea that ML-based reflection partners can support analytical workers, particularly physicians, in their diagnostic work by implicitly invoking reflective practice. Indeed, research indicates that reflective practice may help improve diagnostic work if it is augmented by the use of MLbased reflection partners. Below, we briefly discuss the theories on reflective practice in order to explain how MLbased reflection partners differ from traditional DSS and other learning systems (such as ITS) and to clarify how the former may be useful augmentations to reflective practice.

## Research & Theory on Reflection.

In reflective practice theory, several authors have pointed out that simply hypothesizing about different reflective methods is not good enough (e.g., Larrivee, 2008). Instead, research in education and nursing has analyzed reflective practice and categorized reflection into varying levels of depth (e.g., Hatton & Smith, 1995; Larrivee, 2008; Smyth, 1989; van Manen, 1977). Classifying reflection into different levels of depth provides a better look at how reflection is in reality: The behavior associated with reflection varies greatly, ranging from relatively automatic responses to critical, deep reflection (Larrivee, 2008; Smyth, 1989). Notably, each level of reflection is useful for reflective practice despite the inherent differences regarding the quality of the reflection (Larrivee, 2008). Even if there is no uniformity in the names given to these levels, most established frameworks of reflective practice employ three to four comparable depth levels. Figure 1 gives an overview of the different levels of reflection in terms of their depth.

Interventions, and especially interactions, can help practitioners reach deeper levels of reflection than they would on their own (Hatton & Smith, 1995; Larrivee, 2008). In the literature, human-human interaction dominates these encounters, whereas research in this domain has so far neglected human-AI interactions in the context of reflection theories. Accordingly, this study aims to remedy this situation by investigating the influence of an ML-based CDSS on reflection depth and thereby evaluating ML-based systems as potential reflection partners.

## Reflection in Practice and Organizations

As we theoretically examine the effects and outcomes of reflection, we wish to consider not only the depth of reflection but also how and when individuals engage in it. To this end, we turn to professional reflection theory (Schön, 1984), which is an important epistemological theory in the context of practitioner learning. The design of reflection practices is important to making implicit knowledge consciously accessible. Accordingly, Schön differentiated between two methods that he called “reflection-in-action” and “reflection-on-action.” Reflection-in-action is a caserelated, situational process of reflection. It takes place in the course of action: for instance, by “reflecting teams” during a counseling session. Reflection-on-action concerns reflection about an action that happens after (or at least not during) the action itself (Schön, 1987). This form of reflection involves a subsequent analysis of one’s own actions. Both forms of reflection are necessary to unleash the full potential of reflective practice within professional learning and to improve a professional’s “theory of action” (i.e., experts narrating how they would do something).

The theory of action is closely connected with two notions of learning: “single-loop” (adaptation) and “double-loop” (change) learning (Argyris & Schön, 1978). Single-loop learning focuses more on finding “hot fixes” to problems and treats erroneous results as issues to be handled rather than opportunities to learn. Individuals engaging in double-loop learning aim to remedy problematic parts in their mental account of the decision process (often referred to as the “mental model”; Rook, 2013) by learning through reflection, detection, inspection, and the correction of errors in the learning process.

Professional medical education research has concluded that scholars need to thoroughly analyze and identify methods that can motivate relevant stakeholders to engage in double-loop learning (e.g., Rouse et al., 2017). Thus, scholars have begun integrating professional reflection theory into the clinical context (e.g., by adding feedback and reflection to medical teaching methods) (e.g., Schei et al., 2019). In this context, Schön (1984) pointed out that little is gained by experts narrating how they would do something (i.e., theory of action) because this rarely resembles how they really act (i.e., theory in use) (e.g., Neumann Jr., 1999). Instead of prescribing knowledge, coaches, instructors, or assistants in reflective practice should guide the learner to not only reflect on their actions but also reflect in action (Schön, 1987). Doing so can contribute to professional growth and foster a change in the theory in use (Neumann Jr., 1999). Historic DSS learning types, such as ITS (e.g., Anderson et al., 1985), follow the prescribing teacher or expert model, which has been deemed too rigid to help students thoroughly reflect (e.g., Schön, 1984). Thus, such methods are highly unsuitable as assistants in reflective practice and difficult to integrate into clinical practice. It is already a challenge for physicians to pause and reflect on their own without also having to think about the interruptions that traditional (i.e., rigid and inflexible) interactions with ITS would bring (Schei et al., 2019). Mamede et al. (2020) showed that physicians rarely reflect critically about a case without outside triggers, despite the potential for reducing diagnostic errors and improving learning.

Considering these identified characteristics of reflection and reflection partners, an ML-based CDSS may be able to implicitly stimulate reflective practice; thus, it could likely be integrated into clinical practice more effectively than traditional ITS. The use of an ML-based reflection partner would not only constitute a new element for reflection theory but would also be a useful extension for the digital transformation of healthcare.

Differences in Human-Human and Human-AI Interactions in Reflection

Clarifying the differences between human-human and human-AI reflection can help to explain the need for ML-based reflection partners. Research has long challenged rationality in human decision-making, given that humans are susceptible to biases and emotions when making decisions (e.g., Kahneman, 2011). As such, (reflective) interactions between two rational (human) individuals will also tend to succumb to these ramifications of sociality, leading to opinions that either converge over time (Van Swol & Ludutsky, 2007) or diverge for irrational reasons (Kahneman, 2011).

In general, humans’ pseudo-rationality often leads them to reinforce their cognitive beliefs in the face of compatible information and engage in defense mechanisms when confronted with conflicting information (Gillespie, 2020). These defense mechanisms may appear as avoiding, delegitimizing, or rationalizing the opinion of the other (Gillespie, 2020). “Unlike contradictory experiences in the material world ... the truth of the social world is that it contains many perspectives on what is true” (Gillespie, 2020, p. 384), leading to defense mechanisms that deteriorate learning opportunities. In this sense, social structures motivate behavior to quickly remove cognitive dissonance caused by someone else’s perspective on what is true (Stone & Cooper, 2001). These other perspectives are believed to threaten one’s own cognition and thus one’s sense of self (Gillespie, 2020).

In contrast to pseudo-rational humans, ML-based systems learn from data about the past to make predictions and inferences in the present and future. ML-based systems follow a learned logical representation of the decision problem, which involves logical functions used for reasoning. When humans interact with ML-based systems that are devoid of socially persuasive or influencing factors (such as those in recommender systems), they face an interaction counterpart that is inherently logical. In their interactions with IS, humans still often desperately seek the normality of social interactions by anthropomorphizing IS (i.e., assigning human traits and behaviors) (Pfeuffer et al., 2019). The learning aspect of MLbased systems and intelligence is often associated with humans, unlike traditional DSS with deterministic programming. Thus, ML-based systems are susceptible to anthropomorphization, which in turn may evoke defensive interaction mechanisms on the human side.

Humans learn from different sources and primarily develop expertise from experience—specifically, from social experience (Schön, 1984). However, unlike humans, non-recommending and non-patronizing ML-based systems mainly use data for their reasoning and representation (different from, e.g., the case of Jussupow et al., 2021). A machine intelligence can thus seem much less anthropomorphic if interactions with it are largely stripped of social components. In such cases, users would be expected to not perceive the reasoning of an ML-based system as potentially irrational or emotional. This, in turn, would nudge humans to engage in discourse and defense mechanisms, should they emerge, mainly at the logical level. This difference between machine- and human-induced reflections may be important to boosting logical thinking. In short, there are opportunities for reflection and learning in the face of cognitive dissonance.

## Benefits of Using ML-based Reflection Partners

The current reflection literature broadly accepts that the learner’s role is founded on constructivist learning. That is, the learner is an active participant exerting some influence over the knowledge to be learned through situational interpretation and integration (Bada & Olusegun, 2015).

This is not the case in ITS: The tutoring system usually takes on the role of a guiding teacher (Anderson et al., 1985), follows predefined rules, and does not have self-learning capabilities. Furthermore, while prior research has shown that interactive double-loop ML settings in creative fields are effective (Seidel et al., 2018), analytical professional fields such as healthcare cannot apply such experiments without additional cost.

In this case, a promising method for employing ML-based systems as reflection stimulators is to take advantage of the systems’ computational power and equip them with the ability to implicitly stimulate reflection. Research strongly recommends that physicians use differential diagnosis to help mitigate cognitive errors and produce more accurate judgments (Croskerry, 2003). Doing so can also lead to reflection and learning. However, because reflections are often conducted superficially (Croskerry, 2003), they can be problematic without appropriate stimulators (Ferguson, 2018). Therefore, a system that is able to produce differential diagnostic predictions may implicitly stimulate reflective practice by pointing out alternative diagnoses to physicians. The system thus takes on the role of a reflection partner. However, given that prior research has not investigated this combination, our study examines this possibility.

## Data and Research Methodology

The efficacy of ML-based systems as reflection stimulators only has an indirect theoretical foundation and little empirical evidence. Hence, there is a need for an intensive, immersive, integrative, and idiographic study that can establish sound theoretical insights into the cognitive process of machineinduced reflections. We implemented this phenomenon-driven theory development study from 2019 to 2021, with data collection and analysis occurring across multiple points in time.

The foundation of our study is our core data collection (also called reflective practicum), consisting of physicians interacting with ML-based reflection partners. In a pilot study, we recorded think-aloud protocols during the reflective practicum to explore if ML-based reflection partners implicitly stimulate reflection. We collected data through six physician-AI interactions. The data analysis showed that ML-based reflection partners were able to stimulate reflection-in-action. In the main study, we built on the findings from the reflective practicum in the pilot study, delved deeper into the theoretical realm, and analyzed how ML-based reflection partners stimulate reflections. In the main study, we again conducted the reflective practicum with 23 (newly recruited) physicians and recorded the interactions on video. The immersion led us to an in-depth understanding of the still-neglected phenomenon of machine-induced reflections.

We used complementary data to further enrich the development of our theory: namely, decision-making data from the reflective practicum and questionnaire data from before and after the reflective practicum. Furthermore, considering group-learning theory (Lave & Wenger, 1991), we collected data from a group reflection with seven physicians who discussed predictions from the AI and the errors made by the physicians during the reflective practicum. For this reflection group, we included several participants who had also participated in the pilot reflective practicum. Additionally, to make the group-reflection round more dynamic and enable a less biased sample, we included several physicians who did not participate in any of the stages of the reflective practicum. For data collection and analysis, we employed a methodological approach informed by grounded theory methodology (GTM), with the goal of inductively developing a new theoretical model (Gioia et al., 2013) of machine-induced reflections.

In the next sections, we present our research methodology. First, we explain the development of our reflective practicum, which lays the foundation for our core data collection. We likewise describe how we developed the CDSS, which served as a proxy for future ML-based reflection partners. We then describe our core (i.e., the thinkaloud protocols and semi-structured interaction protocols) and complementary (i.e., the reflection-group think-aloud protocol, decision-making data, and survey data) data sources. Finally, we explain our qualitative coding and grounded theory-informed approach.

## Core Data Collection Foundation: The Reflective Practicum

To develop an applicable theoretical account of how MLbased systems can implicitly stimulate reflections, we anchored our close examination in practice. Consistent with practice learning theories and Schön’s idea of reflection partners, we designed a typical scenario that examined physicians’ interactions with an ML-based CDSS (i.e., the reflective practicum), with the aim of inducing reflections to augment diagnostic work.

For the purpose of this study, we developed an ML-based CDSS that had the ability to learn to discern different diagnoses from a large, labeled Chest X-ray dataset for lung disease diagnostics (see 1 in Figure 2). As pointed out in the introduction, a good reflection partner should induce reflections in a manner similar to a reflection coach (as described by Schön, 1987). Like real reflection partners (e.g., physicians discussing a case together), the physician and the ML-based reflection partner may come up with the same probable diagnosis. Based on a self-selected subset of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) dataset, we trained a CDSS based on the AlexNet architecture (Krizhevsky et al., 2012). In line with Szegedy et al. (2015) and Esteva et al. (2017), we used the common approach for testing, training, and validation and data augmentation techniques. Our test results showed the highest accuracy for “No Finding” (0.944) and “Atelectasis” (0.923), while the average accuracy was 0.632 for the 10 diagnosis labels on the unseen test set. The CDSS classified all X-rays for the reflective practicum set up (see 1 in Figure 2). We specifically selected 20 cases from our data for which we could guarantee a correct prediction of the system.

Hence, we built a modern ML-based CDSS prototype similar to the models in other works (Rajpurkar et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017). Although we obtained adequate accuracies for several diseases in this ML task, the performance of our model—and even of those superior to ours (e.g., Rajpurkar et al., 2017)—could not justify the use of these systems as fully automated tools in healthcare (e.g., Topol, 2019). Nevertheless, these systems are likely to stimulate reflections in physicians, as we will show in this study.

To assess whether and how ML-based systems can induce reflections in practitioners, we implemented a pilot reflective practicum and an accordingly modified main reflective practicum evaluating interactions of physicians with the ML-based reflection partner. Each participant in the pilot and the main study completed both stages of the reflective practicum (see 2 and 3 in Figure 2).

Figure 2. Overview of the Reflective Practicum Process

In Stage 1, we let physicians rate X-rays<sup>3</sup> for the presence of abnormalities that potentially indicate one of nine lung diseases (see 2 in Figure 2). We recorded their thoughts and inner workings using verbal protocols. We only recorded audio in the pilot study, but video-recorded the physicians and their interactions in the main study. In addition to the diagnosis, physicians also presented their certainty about the diagnosis. For their diagnosis, physicians could select from nine diseases and “No Finding” (i.e., no abnormality related to any of the other available diagnoses). The results from Stage 1 provide initial observations about how the physicians used their own perception and cognition to make personal judgments, thus laying the foundation for Stage 2 of the reflective practicum.

In Stage 2, we performed what Schön (1987, p. 296) called a “joint experimentation” study. According to Schön, professionals are most likely to engage in effective reflection-in-action when they learn alongside a coach (i.e., expert) (Schön, 1984, 1987). The expert should present guidance rather than solutions, with learners being active participants in knowledge formation (Neumann Jr., 1999). In our case, we provided the same task, X-ray cases, and environment as in Stage 1, except we added an ML-based CDSS that could predict the most likely diseases based on the X-ray. We again showed the physicians their evaluated lung X-rays from Stage 1 and provided the classification of the CDSS in Stage 2 of the reflective practicum (see 3 in Figure 2). In each X-ray case, the CDSS provided probability scores for each of the available ten labels (nine disease labels and one label for “No Finding”) in the form of a bar chart visualization. With the help of the input from the ML-based CDSS, the physicians had the opportunity to reevaluate the chest X-rays. The physicians were not restricted in their reevaluation step, i.e., they could maintain their previous diagnosis, adjust their diagnosis confidence, or change their diagnosis completely. In this step, we captured the change in the physicians’ diagnosis patterns through verbal protocols. We assumed that the ML-based counterpart’s additional input triggered an inner cognitive process involving a variety of reactions: uncertainty, confirmation, contradiction, and confusion. This additional input especially served to activate reflection-in-action. While Stage 2 provided a second chance to evaluate the diagnosis, the physicians did not know the ground truth about the X-rays; thus, this potential second-stage reflection still constitutes reflection-in-action and not reflection-on-action.

To sum up, we collected two types of data: core data (i.e., the think-aloud protocols, semi-structured interaction protocols both within the reflective practica) and complementary data (i.e., the group-reflection think-aloud protocol within the group reflection, as well as decisionmaking and survey data within the reflective practica) (see Table 1 for a summary of our verbal protocol data sources). To collect the core data, we recruited physicians with various degrees of experience<sup>4</sup> in the field of pneumology. We opted to recruit participants from both clinics and private practices because physicians typically work primarily in one setting and there may be systematic differences in the workflows and methods applied between them.

Below we briefly describe the value of the core data (i.e., physician-AI interaction protocols) in laying the fundamentals for building our theoretical model. We also describe how the complementary data (i.e., decision-making, survey, groupreflection think-aloud data) were pivotal in enhancing the observations from the core data.

## Core Data: Protocols of Interactions

We collected the core data (physician-AI interaction protocols) for the pilot study in the summer of 2019 and for the main study in the summer of 2021. Both studies involved different physicians in the field of pneumology. While the data collection in the summer of 2019 consisted of a reflective practicum with six individuals, the main reflective practicum in the summer of 2021 included 23 newly recruited individuals. We were able to recruit a relatively large number of physicians with specializations relevant to the field of pneumology. Having so many specialists at our disposal for our physician-AI observations allowed us to analyze these experts’ emergent reflective patterns and consider the fully developed (i.e., more complex) specialist cognitions in our theoretical concerns.

To gain insight into the subjects’ cognition, we used variants of think-aloud protocols rated as effective tools for tracing cognitive processes in interactions with IS (e.g., Todd & Benbasat, 1987). The think-aloud protocol is an established data collection technique that focuses on the cognitive process and content of a problem-solving procedure, while the subject verbalizes the cognitive process (Vitalari, 1985). We recorded and transcribed the verbalizations accordingly. In the pilot study, we captured standard think-aloud protocols. To intensify the theory building process in the main study, we extended the basic think-aloud protocols to what we call “semi-structured interaction protocols.” This variant of the think-aloud protocol includes both audio and video recordings of the interaction, as well as additional situational exploration (i.e., sporadically asking what and how some information affected the subject’s consideration). The semi-structured interaction protocols employed different data collection approaches during the two stages of the reflective practicum: In Stage 1, we recorded think-aloud protocols; in Stage 2, we recorded think-aloud protocols while also asking some semi-structured interview questions during and after the interaction phase. This adjusted protocol allowed us to not only record think-aloud data but sometimes also encouraged participants to more precisely verbalize their reflective process, which was not easily traceable with conventional think-aloud protocols. Furthermore, using recorded video, we were able to examine participants’ thought processes through an integration of facial expressions, gestures, and interaction behavior (e.g., cursor and screen activity). Lastly, the post-interaction interview questions allowed us to collect information on the general effect of the ML-based reflection partner on the physician’s thought process, selfawareness, and expectations about future interactions.

## Complementary Data

In general, we augmented the core data using complementary data to strengthen the theory-building process. As mentioned above, these complementary data featured the decision-making data, survey data, and think-aloud data from a group reflection.

We analyzed the decision-making data from Stages 1 and 2 of the reflective practica. In these data, we found indicators of the physicians’ confidence level for the different cases, how they judged the cases on their own, and how the AI affected their final decisions. Furthermore, we analyzed the data to calculate the difference in diagnostic errors between Stage 1 and Stage 2. We used the ground-truth information in the NIH dataset as a basis for this calculation (Wang et al., 2017).

Before physicians engaged in the reflective practicum, we asked them to complete a survey containing demographic (i.e., age, work experience in years, patient contacts per day) and psychometric (i.e., self-reported competence, knowledge, and experience; adapted from Flynn & Goldsmith, 1999; Van den Broeck et al., 2010) questions. Physicians completed the psychometric part of the survey again after the interaction. We elicited this survey data to find out if and how interactions with the ML-based reflection partner affected their self-perception.

To achieve theoretical saturation, we conducted an additional reflection round in the form of a group reflection. Given learning theories such as double-loop learning (e.g., Argyris & Schön, 1978) and experiential group learning (e.g., Lave & Wenger, 1991), this interaction seemed a natural next step in the reflection process. Through a departmental session in clinical practice, seven physicians discussed the X-ray diagnoses in the reflection round where (1) the physicians did not diagnose the actual disease and (2) the diagnoses of the ML-based CDSS coincided with the ground truth. As before, we recorded the inner workings and dynamic processes of the group with the help of think-aloud protocols. The data analysis of this group’s reflection protocol indicated the potential of ML-based reflection partners to induce reflection-on-action in group settings.

## Grounded Theory-Informed and Qualitative Coding Approach

Since our research question has high theoretical relevance but requires critical input from practice, we employed a GTMinformed approach (Gioia et al., 2013) to derive theoretical insights from the observations in our study. GTM has demonstrated its usefulness for making theoretical and inductive contributions in IS research (Wiesche et al., 2017). Therefore, we opted for a GTM-informed methodological approach and drew on several GTM elements in the qualitative analysis.

We followed the guidelines for GTM as set out by Gioia et al. (2013) and conducted our GTM-informed process in five steps: (1) getting lost in the data, (2) seeking similarities and differences, (3) theoretical aggregation, (4) building a data structure, and (5) elaborating and theorizing (Gioia et al., 2013, p. 20ff.). To analyze and evaluate the verbal core and complementary data, we employed a qualitative data analysis (QDA) methodology. Furthermore, we utilized the program MaxQDA as a computer-assisted qualitative data analysis system. Figure 3 illustrates our procedure.

Our approach follows the argument that, through careful coding and extraction of the data, one can make inferences from textual data from inductive content analysis (Rivard & Lapointe, 2012) and generate new knowledge and insights (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). We followed rigorous procedures during the coding process by having two different coders code the transcripts. For the initial coding step, we applied inductive content analysis, which is a well-documented procedure (Schamber, 2000). We followed the general steps for developing a coding scheme: “(1) Determine the recording unit (word, phrase, sentence). (2) Develop categories. (3) Code data sample” (Schamber, 2000, p. 738). Technically, we followed Saldana’s (2013) recommendations: The two coders in the first step developed codes independently, in parallel, and based on terms or phrases used by the physicians in the protocol. These initial in vivo codes formed the basis for code discussions that built a common coding frame. After assembling and discussing the codes, and then specifying the rules of application on the common codebook, the two coders double-checked their individual coding against the common coding basis. The two coders did their coding in multiple iterations.

Figure 3. Overview of the QDA Approach

After each coding phase, the coders gathered together and assessed their agreement using the Kappa coefficient (Brennan & Prediger, 1981). For each set of qualitative data, we iterated the common coding until the coding converged toward high coding agreement among the two coders.<sup>5</sup> During our iterative coding process for each of the protocols, we found similar codes and assembled them into first-order concepts, being very careful to use the language of the physicians as much as possible. We followed the guidelines provided by Birks et al. (2013) to manage our preconceptions and avoid being deceived by existing theories into discarding obvious and subtle insights. The first-order concepts are the intermediate results of our iterative coding process.

We followed common rules in QDA and analyzed an adequate subset of the data from the main study (\~20-25%, 5 protocols in our case) (O’Connor & Joffe, 2020) with this intertwined two-coder approach (see Figure 3). Furthermore, to build on the findings of the core data from the pilot study (where we used all six protocols for the intertwined two-coder approach), we ported the previous codebook from that study to the main one. After reaching adequate agreement on the codebook in the final iteration of the common coding block, we achieved a highly refined codebook that showed much more distinctive ramifications of the initial codes in terms of results. At this stage, we coded the remaining protocols independently. We then proceeded with our structured inductive analysis of the core data (e.g., Gioia et al., 2013), which involved determining connections in the categories of the verbal protocols. This analysis was necessary for developing the second-order themes. These themes represent distinctive concepts that raise the collected insights to a more abstract, theoretical level. Because these themes were identified by researchers—and thus connected to theoretical knowledge—we used labels extracted from the protocols where suitable (Gioia et al., 2013). From the second-order themes, we composed several aggregate dimensions that constituted the core influence factors in machine-induced reflections.

Next, we analyzed the data and built a data structure to enhance the transparency of our concepts (e.g., Pratt et al., 2006) and elevate the established codes to higher aggregate dimensions of inductive interpretations (see Figure 4). This data structure served as a guideline for constructing the theoretical model.

For the final step, we followed Gioia et al. (2013) in using our findings to develop a sound theoretical understanding of how machine-induced reflections emerge. We explain our findings using the theoretical dimensions (as suggested by Urquhart et al., 2010, and Gregory et al., 2018) in our data structure. ## Results: A Process of Machine-Induced Reflection Stimulation

To answer our research question, we analyzed verbal protocol data using QDA and evaluated decision-making and survey data by means of tentative quantitative analyses. Jointly, our analyses revealed that machine-induced reflection nurtures the reflective practice, similar to a crop that needs to be appropriately cultivated. In fact, the plant metaphor effectively captures the different dimensions that shape the process of machine-induced reflection: In Stage 1 of the reflective practicum interaction, physicians revealed different facets as predispositions to decision-making. While physicians varied in their initial judgment certainty, we observed stringent analytical decision-making, on the one hand, and rapid and perceptionfocused decision-making on the other. These facets formed the foundations for the further development and the potential growth of reflections induced by machines. We refer to these facets as reflection soil. In Stage 2, physicians interacted with the ML-based reflection partners—with the initial interaction largely determining how the reflections evolved later on. Reflections became deeper or shallower depending on whether the results of the ML-based reflection partner would deviate from or coincide with the physicians’ judgments. Based on our observations of both cases, this interaction served to implicitly stimulate reflection—that is, it seemingly planted a seed of an idea in the physician’s cognition. We refer to this interaction as reflection seed. During this study, we discovered several different effects of reflection. We observed very shallow interactions due to confirmation, but also highly complex interchanges between different modes of thought due to conflict. Participants struggled with their own sense of competence and judgment in opposition to the ML’s judgment. Physicians tried to reason about the differences in their judgment, sometimes even questioning their own abilities. Because reflection grew in this stage of reflection, we refer to this overarching concept as reflection growth. Whether the reflection led to a change of judgment or not may be a function of how realistic the ML judgment appeared to be, the extent to which the ML judgment conflicted with the physician’s, how certain the physician was, and how much the ML appeared to confirm the physician’s initial judgment.

In our case, we observed varying outcomes in each specification of reflection depth. We call this outcome of the reflection process the reflection harvest. Following the reflection harvest, we asked the participants in downstream interviews and questionnaires how the AI affected their selfperception. Tentative analyses indicated that ML-based reflection partners influenced the physician’s self-perception of competence, potentially leading to a more reflective practice. We call this dimension reflection residue. Lastly, in analyzing the potential of ML-based reflection partners in group settings, we observed increasingly dynamic reflections. Participants also highlighted different facets regarding algorithmic transparency and the characteristics of ML-reflection partners. These facets, if properly integrated, can serve as reflection fertilizer. The next section discusses each of the above-presented theoretical dimensions.

## Reflection Soil

To be able to explain the effects of the ML-based reflection partner on the judgments of the physicians, it was necessary to first observe how the physicians used their own perception and cognition in making judgments on their own. The observations from Stage 1 of our reflective practicum helped us examine aspects of the physicians’ individual judgment and decision-making processes that mirror their procedural focus in the face of reflective practice. We observed three aspects of decision-making, each depending on the individual. These aspects—namely, perception focus, analysis focus, and initial judgment certainty—occurred semi-consistently, often fluctuating based on the particular decision cases. The first aspect (perception focus) involved making perception-driven judgments, while the second (analysis focus) involved making more systematic judgments mirrored by the initial level of reflection. Although the physicians’ (situational) confidence in their judgment often depended on the case under inspection, some of them showed tendencies toward lower or higher levels of judgment certainty in general. Below we discuss the theoretical concepts of the two foci more comprehensively.

## Perception Focus

In perception focus, physicians are guided by the things they perceive to be dominant, which may evoke certain prominent scripts in their knowledge (i.e., easily accessible knowledge often leading to automatic responses to stimuli, e.g., illness scripts in medicine; Schmidt & Rikers, 2007), which in turn may lead them to higher certainty regarding their initial judgment. However, at the same time, this certainty removes the possibility of delving deeper into the case. The physicians’ strong reliance on perception became visible in different facets of judgment behavior. Sometimes, it took physicians only seconds to move from the perception and description of highly prominent patterns in the X-rays to the formation of their initial judgment.

[Interaction: Subject scrolls up and down in X-ray] [Long pause in speech] I don’t see any significant Effusion here, but the pleura certainly appears thickened, at least on the right side. [Interaction: Subject selects the diagnosis “Pleural Thickening” and diagnostic confidence of 70%]

In other cases, physicians followed the path of their initial suspicion to further consolidate it in short, narrow trains of thought:

So what’s immediately noticeable here is, ... you definitely have a huge heart here, on both sides. And probably strongly congested vessels. … [Interaction: Participant zooms into middle third] Especially with this upper field here, [Interaction: Participant points from upper to lower field, left, close to middle] clear vascular congestion. I think that’s relatively clearly the cardiomegaly. [Interaction: Participant selects the diagnosis “Cardiomegaly” and diagnostic confidence of 80%]

One salient pattern in perception focus was the narroweddown perspective on the available diagnosis options. In many cases, physicians tried to bolster their decision-making confidence by going through the data using the exclusion principle (i.e., by going through the possible diagnoses and excluding the implausible ones).

[Interaction: Subject scrolls to diagnostic options] I would resort ... to congestion, which is not really available as a possible diagnosis. [Long pause, looks at diagnostic possibilities and X-ray] Well, what else is there to choose from? Well, there is no atelectasis, cardiomegaly is not present, pleural effusion is not present, fibrosis, that cannot be excluded here if it is additionally congested, that is in principle a possibility. But what speaks against fibrosis is [the fact] that it completely spares the upper lobes. Infiltration. ... sure, infiltration is also possible. [Interaction: Subject zooms into middle third] There is this line, which is a questionable bronchiopneumogram. [Interaction: Participant points to dark small line in left center field] ... Nodes except for this area, [Interaction: Subject points to left midfield] not visible. Pleural thickening ... not visible.

## Analysis Focus

We observed that physicians in this mode of decision-making resorted to more sophisticated ways of analyzing the X-rays and potential diagnoses. For instance, some of the physicians demonstrated stringent systematic analysis behavior.

Alright, this image is reasonably centered. [Interaction: Subject scrolls up and down in X-ray] Acceptable depth of inspiration. First of all, if I look at this systematically, the hilary structures, [Interaction: Subject moves cursor, starting from the middle center, slightly down to the right] the mediastinum, are apparently okay, on both sides. Diaphragm, [Interaction: Subject moves over diaphragm] then orthotopic, costophrenic angles are clear and to the parenchyma. Right lobe cleft, [Interaction: Subject points to left white filament, in midfield, close to center] [Head is close to the screen] Lobe cleft goes to the edge. But here I see on the left side, [Interaction: Subject points to right outer edge of the corpus, in the upper half] that the lobe seems to be partially detached here, [Interaction: Subject zooms further into the upper right corner] [Head is close to the screen again]. So no, that’s the stance, that’s the edge of the scapula here. [Interaction: Subject points to the center-right, rather outward] That’s a little irritating. [Interaction: Participant scrolls up and down in the range-zoomed X-ray] Like that. [Long pause in speech] I would think that’s a relatively normal image. At least respecting these possibilities [of diagnosis].

While the thought environment of individuals engaged in perception focus appeared to revolve around immediate perceptions and easily accessible knowledge, our observations of analysis focus revealed exploratory yet systematic analysis behavior. In contrast to individuals strongly employing perception focus, individuals engaged in analysis focus went through the cases as thoroughly as possible. In the end, this represents the field of tension: Do I accept the approximated solution and the associated risk, or do I try to analyze as carefully as possible and minimize risks? In this mode, individuals showed even deeper levels of reflection by thinking about alternative working diagnoses and hidden patterns.

But the lung tissue itself, on the right side ... I’ll have to take a closer look at what’s in the lower field and in the middle field. PC image is okay for now. Diaphragm is also sharp. Hilus is okay, heart is normal in size. Um. [Interaction: Subject scrolls to upper third] [Mumbles unintelligibly] [Interaction: Subject scrolls up and down alternately] And ... So I’m looking a little bit here in the area ... , in the right lower field. [Interaction: Participant points from right lower field to center field] ... because it seems a bit denser to me, there’s another line here, a very narrow one.

## Initial Judgment Certainty

Participants’ level of certainty about their judgments (i.e., diagnoses) varied on a case-to-case basis, depending on their expertise and experience<sup>4</sup> with similar cases. Participants who engaged in prolonged back-and-forth analysis, and sometimes even in reflection-in-action, occasionally ended up entangled in their own thoughts.

Oh God, well, it’s also difficult now to call a spade a spade ... Middle lobe atelectasis, no I do not see either ... looking for signs of lung fibrosis now in the middle field ... I have to gain a little clearance there again. [Interaction: Participant zooms out a bit again] Whereby these peripheral regions are of course again what is most interesting. [Interaction: Participant zooms back into the right lower field] I can’t see it now either. [Long pause] [Interaction: Participant scrolls down the X-ray again] Well. [Exhales loudly] Well, I’m a bit lost. Means, it could be that it’s [Long pause] I don’t think it’s clear, I must say. [Interaction: Participant selects the diagnosis “No Finding” and diagnostic confidence of 50%] Not so certain.

Furthermore, the initial judgment certainty about the diagnosis tended to be higher in individuals with higher levels of experience compared to novice physicians. Nevertheless, some individuals appeared to more strongly rely on their own experience than others, which also resulted in higher levels of initial judgment certainty. Such physicians sometimes reached a high level of certainty quickly and handled the case in a matter of seconds.

This is now a tumor, probably, so mass. But you can never be certain. Especially with these lungs. [Interaction: Participant selects the diagnosis “Mass” and diagnostic confidence of 90%].

## Switching the Initial Foci

Regarding the interaction of these three aspects of physicians’ decision-making in reflection soil, we observed that both perception and analysis focus not only occurred in isolation but were also rather intertwined. For example, there were cases when the physicians first engaged in analysis focus, closely examining the things that stood out on the X-ray. Often, they continued on this path using systematic analytical heuristics and even sometimes reflected on the action on their own. In other cases, physicians first relied on a perceptive process and then examined the details in an analytical manner.

Okay, so I’ll look at this again in the overview. The shoulder blades are not turned out nicely in the shot. Then I see in the right lower field a few small compressions, similarly on the left, but we’ll look at that up close in a moment. ... Now I’ll start again at the lower right. I see a little, a little roundish condensation in the left center field. Probably though, either something old or a vascular incision after all [Subject continues to slowly scroll up and down again, narrating on the systematic analysis of the lung sections]. So here I see a couple of small round nodules, mini small. [Interaction: Participant selects diagnosis of “Nodule”] But they possibly also just correspond to vascular incisions. [Interaction: Participant selects diagnostic certainty of 90%] ... I never have 100% certainty of what I do and what I see, that’s why I have a hard time giving a 100%. [Laughs] But ... Of course I see nodules. So that’s why I might say 100% this time.

In some cases, physicians switched from analysis to perception focus when they relied on the most salient, readily available knowledge to quickly draw conclusions. While this was certainly important in making quick decisions in cases of emergency, it nonetheless appeared to replace further systematic analyses.

## Reflection Seed and Reflection Growth

In our reflective practicum, the two reflection seeds, i.e., conflict and confirmation, stimulated reflections in physicians and led to the emergence of three interesting cognitive modes that jointly took place in the physicians’ decision-making process: self-assurance mode, challenge mode, and deep dissonance mode.

## Self-Assurance Mode

Physicians who engaged in the self-assurance mode used the information provided by the reflection partner as a tool to support their existing beliefs. This observation is fairly expected in light of decision-making research (e.g., Kahneman, 2011) and recent research on human-AI interactions (e.g., Lebovitz et al., 2021). Both research fields consider confirmation bias to be one of the basic decisionmaking patterns and heuristics present in advice taking.

Cardiomegaly. In that case, she [the AI] recognized it. That gives me a little more certainty. [Interaction: Respondent selects the diagnosis “Cardiomegaly” and diagnostic confidence of 100%] Well, that is only logical when someone says “I see it the same way.” Then you feel more certain.

Carefully analyzing such occurrences through the lens of reflective practice showed that the confirmation seed (i.e., cases in which the judgment of the physician and the AI concurred) led to reflections, although very shallow ones. Besides the first manifestation of this mode, which hardly suggested reflection, we observed some cases in which the confirmation again suggested reflection, albeit at a strongly perceptual and descriptive level.

Here I have said with 75% [certainty] that there is “No Finding” and the AI says it with ... 68%. Since I already know the images and once evaluated them myself, I would now take a look at the differential diagnoses of the AI, which occur with less percentage ... the nodules I see here at least not prominently. I don’t see atelectasis here either. Pleural thickening... [Shakes head] The AI is not sure about that either, I don’t see that here either. Also, no infiltrative changes, so I would stick with my, um, my selection. And ... the certainty with which I made the diagnosis has increased a little bit, because not only I see it that way, but also the AI ... from 75 to 80 percent.

In conclusion, the self-assurance mode seemed to be largely triggered by confirmations and led to rather superficial reflections in the best case. Nevertheless, we still found that the ML-based reflection partner stimulated (quite shallow) reflections on the part of the physicians. Regarding the influence of this mode on reflection depth, we found strong relations with what Larrivee (2008) identified as prereflections, i.e., the shallowest type of reflections. Our data indicated that, in the majority of cases, there was little opportunity to pursue deeper levels of reflection with the self-assurance mode.

## Challenge Mode

In the self-assurance mode, physicians appeared eager to confirm their own beliefs by pointing to the shortcomings of the AI and the superiority of their own assessments. The challenge mode is somewhere between the deep dissonance mode and the self-assurance mode in terms of reflection depth. Often, being surprised about the differential diagnosis of the AI triggered the challenge mode. In some cases, the physicians were shocked that the AI did not find any indication for the physician’s diagnosis or that the AI proposed an unexpected but severe diagnosis when the physician had not paid any attention. Sometimes, when the AI proposed a different solution, the physicians sought ways to synthesize this piece of new information with their own proposed diagnosis. In other cases, physicians responded to the AI challenging their diagnosis by thinking through the conflict. They then constantly compared their own diagnosis with the AI’s, often asserting that this was what they had meant with their own diagnosis but were just too uncertain.

Aha, atelectasis. Ah, well, I had called it dystelectasis. And pleural effusion. Aha. Well, I don’t see a pleural effusion. So, I think they mean by pleural effusion, anyhow, congestion. I think they kind of packed that together because that’s really acutely angular. [Interaction: Subject points to the gap between the diaphragm and the outer wall, on the left, then also on the right] So I, I would think, at least in the PA scan, the criteria of a pleural effusion would not be met like that. And ... to me this is a dystelectasis. ... I have to be honest now, I would google now and look again from what percentage one would call it “atelectasis.” What did I say? Ah well so that’s influencing me now. Because I think that’s just the AI’s way of finding the best fit. And I would also put that on atelectasis because there is a partial atelectasis so to speak. So that confirms me and then I go up to 90% [Grins].

Clinical research (e.g., Borrell-Carrió & Epstein, 2004) recommends finding measures to remind physicians to resort to sophisticated, effortful decision rules rather than faster alternatives. Although such measures can be difficult to find, we observed that ML-based reflection partners were able to partly motivate physicians to elevate their reflection activities to a higher level. This motivation for deeper reflection was evident in the physicians’ rethinking and questioning of the initial judgment when it conflicted with the AI. For example, physicians engaged in perception focus during their initial judgments, narrowing their train of thought, and then faced a challenging opinion that urged them to reflect.

And here he [the AI] said pneumothorax, I actually missed that, so I would have to go back and look. [Interaction: Subject zooms into upper third] And would look at the lung tissue on both sides. [Interaction: Subject moves over upper fields left and right, then scrolls to lower third] [Long pause in speech] But this image ... in which the heart is actually huge in the thorax. Now, if I was to assume that only one diagnosis is correct. [Interaction: Participant scrolls the X-ray in zoom] Now I am looking at the lung tissue again to see if I discover a crack somewhere. [Long pause, pauses in the upper third] But I actually find that difficult. [Long pause, still in the upper third, then zooms out] Yes, the AI says that there is a relatively high degree of certainty that this is a pneumothorax. I’m not so sure. Maybe I’m overlooking it completely, but for me the most pathological thing about this picture is the heart. That’s why I would stick with cardiomegaly for the time being. But I am extremely confused by the fact that the AI says that there is a pneumothorax. [Interaction: Participant selects the diagnosis “Cardiomegaly” and diagnostic confidence of 20%]

In comparison, physicians who initially adopted a perception focus often reacted by getting involved in the conflict and trying to resolve it through at least shallow reflections on diagnostic alternatives, especially when it only took them seconds from recognizing highly present patterns to their initial judgment.

[Interaction: Subject scrolls up and down in the X-ray] Now, of course, I’m looking for the nodulus that the AI provides. … That may be correct. Good. Of course, if I’m looking at it correctly, it could also just be a vascular incision. [Interaction: Participant points to right center field] I can’t tell, but when in doubt, of course, it’s always better to take the more severe diagnosis. [Interaction: Participant chooses the diagnosis “Nodule” and diagnostic confidence of 40%]

These observations allowed us to infer a higher-order reflective mode: the challenge mode. This mode (see Figure 1) signals interest in the available alternative insights and the possibility of ascending toward medium-deep reflections when the individuals question their own perceptions. The challenge mode is the first of two modes that are triggered by the conflict seed. As seen in our data, this mode particularly emerged in situations where an elevated decision certainty and the perception focus dominated the initial judgment. In most of the cases, individuals who engaged in this mode maintained at least a shallow level of reflection. This enabled the individuals to reflect more deeply on alternatives even if they decided not to adjust their opinions. In contrast, we also observed conflict cases where subjects initially engaged in the challenge mode (medium-deep reflections) and then switched to the self-assurance mode (shallow reflections).

So, now I’m looking for atelectasis again and I simply can’t find it. No. Nope. [Shakes head slightly] Funnily enough, now I find it [is infiltration] even more. [Interaction: Participant selects the diagnosis “Infiltration” and diagnostic confidence of 70%] So I really don’t find any atelectasis, I don’t see it. [Interaction: Subject looks at X-ray again] But I’m also willing to learn. [Interaction: Subject zooms Xray] [Mumbles] Nah, not behind it either. Not behind the heart either, no, I can’t find it. No, no atelectasis. [The conflict] rather increased [my own certainty]. Strangely enough. I don’t know. Nah, I think that’s really infiltration, not atelectasis.

In the beginning, the conflict led to interest; the train of thought started, but the subject found no reliable indications, which then caused the reflection to dwindle. This happened even if the individual was inclined to think about it. It seemed that the output of the AI, which was too distant from the physician’s own considerations, led to a dwindling of the reflection. As some physicians noted, explainable AI (XAI) methods would potentially help encourage further reflection.

## Deep Dissonance Mode

In some cases, physicians went beyond the challenge mode and reacted with deeper reflections. These were cases when the reflection partner’s differential diagnosis shook the basic framework of the physician’s initial judgment and caused a deep cognitive dissonance (Hernandez et al., 2013). Physicians pointed to the diverging differential diagnoses as motivators to engage in medium to very deep reflection.

So, he [the AI] says: “Atelectasis.” [Interaction: Participant moves mouse over corresponding part of X-ray] And I said: “No Finding.” Because it’s ... I think this is what he means. [Interaction: Participant circles a spot in the center field with the mouse] Or this. But that’s the hilus again. And this is a striped atelectasis. ... So this would be the first time that I would change my mind. And I would say: Yes, I saw it, I also thought there was something there. But what bothers me a bit is that if you look at it closely, it goes through here. [Participant continues to zoom in and out of the X-ray, articulating and critically juxtaposing his perception and cognition with the analysis of the AI. Finally, participant looks at X-ray very closely, brings hand to chin] Well, you’re allowed to. One is allowed to change one’s mind. So, this is atelectasis now, but then at most ... 50%, because you simply need a better image.

In other cases, participants initially resisted the contrasting diagnosis to preserve their competence and autonomy. But in some cases, the fissure that the AI’s statement caused in the physician’s thought framework became too large. Once the switch to the deep dissonance mode happened, reflection deepened to previously unobserved levels. We found that MLbased reflection partners stimulated reflections even in very analytical physicians, while those with a strong predisposition to reflect went to an extremely deep level. The reflection had to first shake the foundation of the judgment for the deep dissonance to be resolved with a final, strongly reflectedupon, and reconciliatory decision (see Figure 5).

Because I wasn’t particularly sure from the beginning, and because that was also a differential diagnosis that existed until the end, um, the AI would guide me to get to the bottom of that further, and in that case, [would even guide me to] say that I can imagine that in principle, this is more likely atelectasis.

Physicians were most likely to perceive the differential diagnosis of the ML-based reflection partner as an impetus to question their own judgment when the contradiction was intense and they expressed low confidence in their initial judgment. Some physicians experienced stress about the additional input, while others were thankful and gave their new “colleague” nicknames:

Well, I’m always grateful. I like it when someone says: Can’t it be something else? ... And if I then hear from someone, whether this is a good radiologist or a good AI ... so the AI. The AI, AIA. [Laughs] AIAs, so I’ll call it the AIA. So, when I get help from them, and I always assume that they are not that bad, I find that quite exciting, because then you try to compare things. Maybe you didn’t pay attention initially. She says there is nothing here at all. I say there is Fibrosis. Take a closer look. Yes, I would still do it.

## Reflection Harvest

Finally, in the best case, interaction with an ML-based reflection partner results in a reflected decision. Thus, the pathway for reaching a conclusion (i.e., the decision-making process) is just as important as the final decision itself. At the metalevel, we decided to characterize the resulting decisionmaking outcomes as either adjusting or persevering. Here, we introduce the different expressions of decision-making outcomes because they reveal interesting linguistic subtleties that point to different mental connotations.

## Adjusting Outcome

With this outcome, we observed a subpattern that we called being guided by the AI. In this subpattern, reflections led the participants to recognize potential weaknesses in their own perceptions and thought processes, which resulted in them changing their mind.

Okay. So pleural thickening [that] may well be the case ... such that I’m missing the hilus gatherings, so to speak. [Interaction: Subject scrolls up and down in Xray] Thought out loud. Hmm, I think that he probably had surgery and that maybe this is more like pleural thickening then. So in that respect, I now agree with the system and change my mind. And would say, yes that is so. [Interaction: Participant selects the diagnosis “Pleural Thickening” and diagnostic confidence of 100%].

In several cases, when the participants reflected on why they made a change, they said that in their initial judgment they had doubts and that the AI now reinforced those doubts.

How did it influence me? ... So, what I was thinking about was the atelectasis story. That would be an explanation. What bothered me was the hilar gathering, which I didn’t see somehow ... which was kind of missing, to be certain [in the diagnosis]. A pleural thickening is much more likely, of course, because he’s had surgery.

Other cases appeared to be quite complex: Physicians could not resolve the cognitive dissonance—whether stemming from the AI or the underlying X-ray data—through the reflection process.

## Persevering Outcomes

If the output by the ML-based reflection partner was insufficiently convincing, participants decided to adhere to their initial judgment. Sometimes, the conflict with the AI led to reflections about whether the AI was trustworthy, which then often led to retaining or even reinforcing the initial assessment:

And effusion, there are criteria for that. The edge angles should then not taper, as they do on both sides here. [Interaction: Subject scrolls up and down in the lung shot] That’s why I would honestly stick with infiltration, persevere. [Interaction: Participant scrolls to answer choices] And even if the AI wanted to steer me down a different path ... I would stick with ... infiltration. I had 80%. I’d be even surer. I would go up to 90% now.

Relativizing the AI—a psychological technique used to avoid taking the opinion of another seriously (Gillespie, 2020)—also happened in higher-order reflections, even when participants were actively reflecting on their insecurity over their decision and the potential of other diagnoses.

Pleural effusion. This is wrong. [Interaction: Participant moves mouse over corresponding part of Xray] A pleural effusion ... well, at least you don’t see it like that. Often, perseverance outcomes were also accompanied by explanations that if the AI had explained its diagnosis or if the physician had more information, the AI’s “argument” would have been more believable.

I will continue to not refer to this as an infiltrate. I would continue to call this a suspected nodule. Now, if I knew how this AI actually works, then it would be easier for me to maybe take this [AI] diagnosis more seriously. Yes, you actually need an X-ray where this patient inhales properly. ... But it’s not easy for the AI or for that [case]. [Shakes his head slightly] It’s also possible that he’s right. I’m not going to let him, not going to let him dissuade me.

## Reflection Residue

After the active reflection (i.e., reflection-in-action) process stopped when the decision was final, the process of theoretical clarification continued with the help of the following questions: What is the aftermath of this reflection process? What remains after the reflection crops have been harvested (i.e., after the final decisions have been made)? To analyze and establish the longterm impact of interactions with ML-based reflection partners, we inspected several data sources (i.e., decision-making, postinteraction stance, and survey data) in the following sections.

## Decision-Making Data

The analysis shows that the physicians incorrectly classified the X-rays in 80.2%<sup>6</sup> of the cases in the first diagnosis round. This result is expected, given that the physicians had to make a diagnosis based on only one X-ray and in the absence of other parameters, such as patients’ medical records. In the second round, the average value for the incorrect classification with the support of our CDSS was 58.2%.<sup>6</sup> This result indicates that our CDSS provided valuable information and guidance for the diagnostic process. Overall, the ML-based reflection partner encouraged the physicians to change their diagnosis on average for the better.

## Verbal Post-Interaction Stance

In the interviews, the participants revealed that different aspects of interacting with the ML-based reflection partner shaped their self-perception. In particular, participants discussed their relationship with the reflection partner and whether and how the latter could influence cognitive processes in general. One interesting response was that the participants compared the MLbased reflection partner and its effects on users to a human colleague:

So, what I said is that if you think this is a reasonable approach ... then you’re just as likely to be swayed by it as you are by colleagues who say “Woah! Look. That could be this and that.” That’s the background, which is why you might look twice.

Some physicians also expressed worries about the strong influence the AI may have on inexperienced and less critical colleagues. Physicians who were more self-confident and engaged than others in both challenge and self-assurance modes were especially critical of the AI (although the prediction of the AI corresponded to the ground-truth values in this rather difficult diagnosis task):

But the mix between agreement and differences shows me that this kind of AI would still worry me ... how should I say, that the AI, really completely fails in some places from my point of view and, and brings agreement in some other places. It shows me actually the high uncertainty and that you just can’t rely on it.

When we asked the physicians if interacting with the AI altered their perception of their own professional abilities, they had diverse reactions. Some admitted to increased uncertainty regarding their professional future:

Yes, I’m a bit afraid of that. [Laughs] ... At least according to the works that have been published, you get a very meaningful statement from the AI. And also, which is supposed to be better than the assessment of the pneumologist. ... [This] is then of course also when you see your job in danger. And there is the possibility to actively deal with it or to say [Laughs] or to pull the blanket over your head. Well, that doesn’t work either.

However, many of the participants strongly stated that the ML-based reflection partners did not impact their selfesteem at all. Often, participants argued that although the ML-based reflection partner was proficient in pattern recognition, it was nevertheless just a system with its own limitations.

Many colleagues are afraid of that. But these are assistance programs ... so, this is not something where I would say that it scares me or influences my self-esteem as a doctor.

No, not at all. ... I would be happy if we had AIs. Because I find that totally awesome, because of what the AI can do. It can’t think, it’s difficult for it to put things into context. But what the AI can do extremely well is pattern recognition, because that’s totally easy. ... But that would not affect my professional ego that I am just a stooge of the AI or something like that. No, I would see that as assistance.

Lastly, we asked participants whether they thought MLbased reflection partners would influence their future professional work and, if so, how. Many participants welcomed the idea of having such a system as a support to help uncover all the small details and to approach the work in an even more professional and reflective way.

Most likely, it would make me feel supported and more professional in my everyday life.

I think it will make life easier. Because you have timely advice, can learn with the system to some extent or you know the system … I mean, it’s going to be with AI, like with colleagues. There are some, they’re twerps. Yeah, if they say something, you know, it’s certainly not that. And I think with AI, there’s going to be something similar or that they’re just going to say, for certain medical conditions, it’s not a job for the AI.

## Survey Data

In contrast to the post-interaction interview data, a tentative analysis using t-tests on the available pre- and post-survey data revealed an interesting insight into the potential shortto long-term effects of interactions with ML-based reflection partners on self-perceived competence. We first created two pairs of independent groups using a median split (i.e., one pair concerning competence and one pair concerning experience with ML-based DSS). Next, we calculated the differences between before and after the interaction with the ML-based reflection partner regarding the competence selfassessments within each group. Positive (vs. negative) values indicated that the subject became more (vs. less) selfconfident through the experience with the AI. Then, we applied a series of independent t-tests on these pairs of groups indicating two interesting patterns. First, individuals who considered themselves very competent prior to the interaction with the ML-based reflection partner (using a median split for grouping) looked at themselves more critically afterward<sup>7</sup> (p < 0.01). Second, individuals who had a high level of experience with ML-based DSS (also using a median split to form two independent groups) questioned themselves (even) more<sup>8</sup> (p < 0.1) after the interaction.

## Reflection Fertilizer

The reflection process can be supported through methods we call reflection fertilizers. Based on our observations, several factors (both social and technical) were necessary to enrich and strengthen the reflection process and drive up the potential of machine-induced reflections: group reflections, deep subject information, and algorithmic transparency.

## Group Reflections: Reflection in a Hospital Department

In a group-reflection session with a university hospital pneumology department, we presented physicians with the Stage 2 results of the reflective practicum. We selected the particular cases with clear disagreements between physicians and the AI and showcased the X-rays for the group to evaluate. During the feedback and reflection round, the physicians partly justified their positions. This was congruent with the findings from the reflective practicum in which individuals— especially those with a high level of expertise—tried to preserve their perception of competence.

Here, you have support that ... provides an automated feedback to you, which, in the first place, is neutral. Yes, because, uhm, I don’t know of such a machine, if you then receive too much negative feedback, then there might be a lot of different doctors who are not going to use it, because they say “She [the system] always says something different than me, I don’t want that.” Well, there is something like that [such behavior] already; that is my perception.

Furthermore, it was evident that the physicians found it important to retain control over their decisions; a factor that is consistent with the previous remark.

I’d like to see it [first] myself ... you have to look at the pictures yourself. I could imagine, if one uses the system this way [for learning], then I should be required to make my own diagnosis first [Approval in the background from the group], before the machine provides its own assessment … ! If I am able to use it and get an assessment right away, I would automatically move in that direction.

The last quote is very significant because this was not only obvious in our core data but also repeated in the group discussion. We performed careful contextual analysis of the impressions gained from the study and the statements on retaining control over decisions, which is obviously a crucial feature of the decision set. We concluded that the reaction to the ML-based reflection partner was a combination of the fear of being patronized by the machine (of falling victim to an automation bias) and the will to receive feedback (to be shown where the contradictions lay). It appears that when physicians retained control over decisions, they did not perceive that the system was patronizing them when they adjusted their decisions and cognition. In this way, they engaged in modes that allowed for deeper reflections. We argue that an important feature of cognitive refinement in such ML-based reflection partners may reside in individuals’ perceptions of autonomy. As our observations and the physicians’ testimonies revealed, the desire for control over decisions exists independently of individuals’ lack of interest or desire to refine their own reasoning further.

Not if it is that or that or that, but. ... Here could be an infiltration, look at it for a second. Yes, or “There is no infiltration with high certainty here.” This [system] could definitely never conduct a diagnosis [by itself], but it can stimulate learning processes. I think it ... is necessary, to use it for this.

The physicians apparently did not appreciate the system as a primary decision-maker. Instead, they preferred the system in a stimulating role. This desire became even clearer in the course of the reflection group.

Yes, and the question is what this is [Points on the monitor]. I’ve noticed that the whole time. Why is there such a line here? [Vehemently points on the monitor] We don’t know of it like that!

Sure, I think, if you ... know now, there should be an infiltration somewhere here, you definitely look at it differently. Well, in any case and you examine it more closely ... and try to comprehend it [the reasoning of the system].

Motivated in part by the impassioned reflection discussions, we found that, in principle, individuals valued collaborations with ML-based CDSS and particularly wanted to exploit the system’s stimulating input. We perceived a fundamental desire to reach a higher level of knowledge by integrating conflicting information with the theory-in-use while interacting with intelligent machines.

It was particularly clear that the ML-based reflection partner was able to stimulate the reflective practice of the group, inducing dynamics into the reflection, particularly through its contradictions with the human experts. During the groupreflection round, the participants criticized many issues concerning the current condition of daily medical life. First, the physicians stated that they generally thought that group discussions of patient cases would be helpful for refining their diagnoses. In practice, this tended not to be possible due to the time-sensitive nature of daily clinical decision-making and economic restrictions. Second, especially the healthcare sector features cultural habits that hinder learning. As the head of the pneumology department stated:

Because we actually cultivate a culture of ignorance and [a culture of] “I am always right” ... and if I do not admit any mistake. ... I cannot improve myself.

This critical observation means that the necessary feedback culture, especially in practices, is difficult to implement. This may be because physicians work alone and therefore have no access to feedback from other physicians. The pneumology department head continued:

In the clinic it is automatic ... , You always get feedback and discuss together. ... But as soon as you are in a practice, and you have been working on your own for 10 years [you are not used to doing that anymore]. This feedback culture is insanely important, and even more important than in the clinic; it is important outside the clinic for the colleagues who muddle along on their own ... so that they are reminded that it [the diagnosis] could be something different.

## Deep Subject Information

The physicians further explained that the ML-based reflection partner would need certain characteristics and features in order to be embedded in clinical routines. In particular, physicians would need clinical data or the patient’s medical record before and during the actual confrontation with the AI.

Well then, and I would ask this person to tell me ... whether he is seeing a cardiologist and whether he has a pre-X-ray image. And then I would compare that, whether this is somehow an old finding, right pleural, or whether this [the abnormal pattern] has newly occurred. And then I would decide whether I would find cardiomegaly or mass ... more important.

## Algorithmic Transparency

Many of the physicians mentioned the issue of algorithmic transparency. They seemed to already have concrete preferences concerning the additional information that the system should provide. For instance, an ML-based CDSS could reveal additional statistical or contextual information, which would ensure better traceability. In this case, information about an increased inflammation value or information from the clinical admission of the patient could satisfy this requirement. Particularly salient was the demand for XAI.

Well, it ... could have pointed out with arrows ... well I would still like to know: What are the criteria of why it did what. So, I would be able to understand it. So, you understand, I look at it this way. The thing is searching. The thing searches very effectively and very schematically and pulls everything through and then tells me why it came to such and such a decision. It should not only be a decision, it should also explain why.

According to the physicians, it was equally important to have background information about how the AI really works.

Now you have said it has been trained based on some medical records, but, well ... If I knew now how this AI has been trained, actually [also] on CT findings and bronchoscopy findings and all such things have been incorporated, I would say, yes, alright. So that also increases the probability for me. Then I would be more willing to let myself go or accept that completely. But I don’t know that. I don’t have that piece of information, so sometimes this leads to inner conflicts.

## Discussion

## A Model of Machine-Induced Reflections

Through abductive reasoning, we constructed the model of machine-induced reflection based on six reflection dimensions: reflection soil, reflection seed, reflection growth, reflection harvest, reflection residue, and reflection fertilizer (see Figure 6). Each of these dimensions is an important pillar in our theory. To build our framework, we followed recommendations to carefully scaffold reflective practice and to ensure sensitivity to the fact that individuals engage in analytical thoughts differently and with varying depth (e.g., Larrivee, 2008). Thus, we conceived machine-induced reflection as similar to the cultivation of a crop—nurturing our reflective practice.

Reflection soil includes such analytical assumptions in the interchange of the two initial cognitive phases (analysis and perception focus) and in the assessment of initial judgment certainty. This dimension of our model aligns with the concept of “systems of thinking.” Kahneman (and his colleague Amos Tversky) referred to two systems of thinking: fast (i.e., heuristic, sometimes biased) and slow (i.e., analytical) thinking (Kahneman, 2011). As we observed with analysis and perception focus, different predispositional factors—i.e., individual experiences, characteristics, and emotions— certainly influence (Campitelli & Labollita, 2010) how we engage in either of these systems (Kahneman, 2011).

The previous literature indicates that individuals’ willingness to reflect on themselves is the result of analysis more than perception, intuition, or impulsive behavior (e.g., Littrell et al., 2020). Quite naturally, comparing perception focus to fast or heuristic thinking and analytical focus to slow-paced or analytical thinking is simplistic because an analytical focus may also drive a tendency to suppress heuristic but potentially incorrect responses, which may galvanize a stronger tendency to reflect (Travers et al., 2016). However, the perception vs. analysis focus is a simplistic dichotomy: Individuals tended to switch between them based on how certain they were in their initial perceptions. Related research has connected increased certainty on initial judgments with shallow reflectiveness and being unaware of one’s own shortcomings (Thompson et al., 2011). Other research claims that unease or uncertainty while making heuristic decisions is a sign that the person is aware of incorrect heuristic responses but may be unable to inhibit this decision-making in favor of a more elaborate response (Travers et al., 2016). Switching between perception and analysis focus signals an attempt to update one’s own cognition with new information, which may constitute a positive influential factor for reflections.

With reflection soil in mind, we now turn our attention to the interactions between humans and the reflection partners. Here, the actual initiation of machine-induced reflection takes place through one of two processes (i.e., the reflection seeds). Agreement between the human’s initial judgment and the MLbased reflection partner activates the confirmation seed, whereas divergence activates the conflict seed. It is noteworthy that humans’ interpretation of conflict depends on system properties and their own human cognition. For example, if the system offers multiple predictions with varying probabilities (as in our case), the user may find their answer in one of the presented predictions. Based on our observations, users appeared to interpret probabilities below 50% for their initially selected diagnosis option as the AI having a diverging opinion from their own. However, even if the AI displayed their initial diagnosis with a probability below 50%, subjects also tended to treat this as some sort of confirmation of their own judgment, although only a small one.

In our results section, we present qualitative evidence that the conflict seed can induce deeper reflection than the confirmation seed. The confirmation seed inhibits the potential for reflection growth because it may activate the rather shallow self-assurance mode. Individuals with an active self-assurance mode may only superficially reflect on the new stimulus induced by the MLbased reflection partner, using the results to confirm their initial judgment rather than reflect on the reasons that led them to this judgment. In contrast, the conflict seed may activate the challenge mode, especially in cases where individuals made their initial judgment under high certainty and with a perception focus. Individuals engaged in the challenge mode followed a shallow reflection strategy, and in the process of reconciling their cognition, they often thought a lot about the conflict between their initial judgment and the information from the AI. On several occasions, individuals also switched from challenge mode to deep dissonance mode, and in doing so, reached deep, critical levels of reflection.

We found the deepest and most elaborate reflections when the conflict seed activated the deep dissonance mode. This happened particularly in cases where individuals expressed a stronger analysis focus and higher uncertainty about their initial judgment. Activation of the deep dissonance mode made individuals critically analyze their initial judgments while trying to integrate the diverging information from the ML-based reflection partner so they could identify the best possible solution.

Scholars have highlighted that dissonance (as a form of ambiguity induced by machines in diagnostic decisionmaking) can be a problematic result of human-machine interactions (Lebovitz, 2019; Lebovitz et al., 2021). Indeed, if ML-based systems function purely as DSS and not as reflection partners, ambiguity and cognitive dissonance could be unwanted side effects. However, our study suggests that intentionally incorporating ML-based DSS as a reflection partner could leverage these ambiguities by encouraging individuals to reach the deep dissonance mode. This is an interesting finding in light of existing research.

Putting this finding in the context of reflection and cognition (e.g., Gillespie, 2020; Kahneman, 2011; Mann et al., 2009), we want to emphasize that individuals who engaged in the challenge or deep dissonance mode did not simply refer to their previous perception or cognition, as is often the case with confirmation; instead, they tried to actively process the new information and update their mental model. Accordingly, machines could actually induce reflection despite their lack of human capabilities. This may be a revolutionary finding regarding the potential of AI to serve as reflection partners, given the extant research on the difficulties in updating one’s cognition in the face of cognitive dissonance (Travers et al., 2016) and on human defense mechanisms during conflicts (Gillespie, 2020).

If reflection fertilizers are applied appropriately—i.e., indepth information on a subject, algorithmic transparency, or group settings are available—the reflection may grow deeper (e.g., challenge mode to deep dissonance mode) and lead to more elaborate and dynamic reflections. Thus, reflection fertilizers may indirectly influence the reflection harvest by enhancing both reflection soil and reflection growth. For example, providing differential probabilities for the classes (in our case, differential diagnoses), or an XAI feature showing why the ML predicts the way it does, may induce users to reflect on the case and the judgment of the ML-based reflection partner (Bauer et al., in press). The ultimate decision-making that results from the reflection process in the reflection harvest depends on the machineinduced reflection process, the awakened reflective modes, and individual heterogeneities.

Finally, reflection residues show how machine-induced reflection stimulates both reflective practice and self-perception in the short- and long run. The physicians in our survey were eager to adopt ML-based CDSS as a general aid for reflection. While some individuals claimed that the interaction did not affect their self-assessment of competence, we found indications to the contrary when quantitatively analyzing psychographic data from the questionnaires. T-tests indicated that subjects who were highly confident about their own skills, as well as those with high levels of experience with AI systems before the interaction with the ML-based reflection partner, were significantly less self-confident after the interaction. Although these patterns are quite interesting, this quantitative analysis has limitations (apart from the relatively small sample size for statistical tests). The above-mentioned patterns are in contrast to the interview data: The participants stated that the interaction with the AI had little to no influence on their selfperception. Some of the participants claimed afterward that the reflective practicum was somewhat artificial because, unlike in real clinical situations, they lacked clinical data and patients’ medical records were missing. Therefore, the significance of the t-tests and the initially low level of diagnostic accuracy in Stage 1 of the reflective practicum could probably be attributed to the fact that the physicians had to work with only a portion of the tools usually available to them (e.g., patients’ medical records and clinical data), which may have also impacted selfconfidence.

Despite these limitations, we decided that the t-test results provided an interesting perspective on how ML-based reflection partners could potentially influence reflective practice in both the short- and long term. Potentially, interactions could counteract instances of individual overconfidence, while also boosting the certainty of lowconfidence individuals who feel supported by the system. Furthermore, individuals reporting previous interactions with ML-based DSS were more inclined to question themselves, suggesting that repeated exposures may induce stronger reflections.

## Contributions and Implications for Theory

Recently, there have been interesting theoretical discussions regarding human-AI augmentation in IS research (e.g., Fügener et al., 2021; Jussupow et al., 2021; Lebovitz et al., 2021, 2022). Studies closely examining the interactions between physicians and AI have highlighted their growing benefits and disadvantages, thus providing insights into the augmentation process and extending IS theory. By clarifying a previously neglected aspect of AI augmentation, we make several contributions to the “massive rethinking” process of IS theory (Teodorescu et al., 2021, p. 1483).

Previous research has not devoted much attention to reflective practice in the context of human-AI augmentation; however, some studies have found hints that machines can prompt decision-makers to reflect (e.g., Lebovitz et al., 2021, 2022). While some research has focused on metacognitive patterns that physicians themselves follow in utilizing AI advice (e.g., Jussupow et al., 2021) or on how augmentation processes are influenced by the opacity of AI systems (e.g., Lebovitz et al., 2021, 2022), we contribute to the ongoing debate on AI augmentation by highlighting the potential of machine-induced reflections to stimulate a more reflective work practice—a novel perspective.

Our model shows how the process of reflection stimulation emerges, how it can be nurtured by fertilizing factors, and how such interactions may affect decision makers in the long term. Although our model is based on a study in the healthcare sector, our findings and model can be generalized to other analytical contexts. Our results suggest that the effects of such systems might be different from those of traditional DSS or recommender systems. While DSS and recommender systems are designed for decision support and persuasion, respectively, both may trigger various types of biases that deteriorate decision quality (e.g., automation bias; Goddard et al., 2011). Using ML-based systems explicitly for reflection constitutes a promising alternative.

Regarding the observed reflection modes, recent research has cautioned that reflections in time-critical decisionmaking contexts, such as diagnostics, may unnecessarily slow down organizational processes; thus, the use of reflection must be balanced (Jussupow et al., 2021). Prior studies have critically examined cognitive dissonance—in the form of ambiguity induced by interactions with ML— and pointed to the stalling of cognitive processes (e.g., Lebovitz et al., 2021, 2022). Concurrently, our findings support criticisms against opaque systems that obscure the logic of a system’s predictions; indeed, the physicians in our study were unanimous in demanding algorithmic transparency. Nevertheless, the aim of our study and model of machine-induced reflections is to foreground the positive aspects of machine-induced thoughtfulness, especially as they relate to the deep dissonance mode. By exposing humans to an opposing view, machines can awaken deeper cognitive structures that are rarely activated without this stimulus and can trigger a reflective process that ultimately helps subjects become more effective professionals.

By explaining how machine-induced reflections emerge and situating this in the context of existing research and insights on reflective practice, this study also contributes to research on reflective practice. This study complements reflection theories so far focused on human interaction triggering reflection by employing ML-based systems as reflection triggers, thus showing that a reflection partner does not need human expert properties to stimulate reflections. Indeed, such ML-based systems can induce reflection in a different, but equally viable way. They are useful for triggering different cognitive modes that are necessary for reaching deeper levels of reflection. These modes are activated depending on dispositional factors (reflection soil, reflection fertilizer), individual characteristics (difficult for us to observe in this study), and an “element of surprise” (reflection seed, i.e., conflict or confirmation) (Schön, 1987). Within the context of a complex reflective process, these modes can alternate and lead to profound reflections. Consistent with research on reflective practice (e.g., Larrivee, 2008), we observed that the involvement of the ML-based reflection partner is an effective means for deepening reflection regardless of the individual’s experience. According to clinical research, the more experienced individuals are, the less likely they are to engage in deeper, critical levels of reflection. This is probably due to their extensive base of readily available knowledge (Mamede & Schmidt, 2004). In our study, individuals with a higher level of expertise<sup>4</sup> tended to make initial judgments more frequently with a perception focus than with an analysis focus. Moreover, senior professionals (i.e., those with high levels of expertise) were (on average) more impulsive and confident than junior professionals, signaling low levels of reflection (Littrell et al., 2020). Prior research has considered overconfidence and impulsiveness to be negative influences on reflection (Littrell et al., 2020). However, as our t-tests indicated, interactions with our MLbased reflection partner could perhaps remedy this situation.

Our study reveals another promising aspect of ML-based reflection partners in effectively augmenting high-expertise individuals in their analytical work. When interacting with our ML-based reflection partner, even senior professionals frequently engaged in the challenge mode and even in the deep dissonance mode, thus allowing them to engage in deep reflection. Accordingly, this study demonstrates that MLbased reflection partners can induce reflection in different ways than human experts.

Lastly, research on clinical education has suggested that evoking cognitive dissonance for the purpose of challenging one’s cognition can help resolve biases (Hernandez et al., 2013). We add to this literature by showing that ML-based systems can be helpful in triggering stark dissonance that compels deeper thinking, thereby helping professionals refine their decision-making and cognition.

## Implications for Practice

Apart from its contributions to theory, our study and model of machine-induced reflection also has important implications for practice. In particular, our findings supplement knowledge about the theoretical facets of human-AI augmentation (e.g., Fügener et al., 2021; Jussupow et al., 2021; Lebovitz et al., 2021, 2022). Thus, IS developers and managers now face a critical challenge in integrating this knowledge into their AI design and adoption strategies. For managers, the success of ML-based reflection partners will partly depend on establishing and affirming an organizational culture that values reflection and learning from errors (Argyris & Schön, 1978). Specifically, in assessing which kind of systems to integrate into the organizational workflows, managers should be aware of the danger of individuals becoming AI-directed “borgs” (i.e., blindly following the AI as individuals or groups) or falling victim to decision-making biases when using traditional ML-based DSS (Fügener et al., 2021; Jussupow et al., 2021). Such scenarios may hamper the creativity and effectiveness of professionals’ decision-making. Thus, managers need to assess which systems perform best with ML-based reflection partners. By nurturing an environment that values reflection, managers can help counteract the deterioration of human performance (Lebovitz et al., 2021).

In small- and large-scale organizations alike, such as private healthcare practices and hospitals, time and resource challenges may prevent individuals from reflecting with human reflection partners who are also experts. ML-based reflection partners provide a scalable technological solution to this problem; for example, in healthcare, the emergency command hologram “holo-doc” in the TV series Star Trek Voyager comes to mind. The use of ML-based reflection partners would allow IS managers to complement managerial cost-efficiency strategies by supporting analytical professionals in maintaining high levels of reflective practice.

IS developers face the exciting new challenge of effectively supporting the process of machine-induced reflections: namely, by integrating fertilizing factors and developing advanced designs for ML-based reflection partners. In crafting such systems, especially ones with algorithmic transparency, designers may need to experiment with different visualizations of XAI. Granted, the effectiveness of XAI in supporting reflection depends on the task at hand. Furthermore, designers must find effective XAI designs that appropriately highlight model correctness and failure, thus striving to prevent excessive and misguided reflections (e.g., Tomsett et al., 2020). In this manner, designers may also effectively shift the user’s focus from wondering about opacity (Lebovitz et al., 2022) to achieving more effective goal-oriented or creative types of reflection. Furthermore, our participants wanted to have more information on the training process and the data used. Inexperienced users would probably not want this kind of information, but individuals with high levels of expertise would want to assess whether the system is comparable to a highly esteemed colleague or if it is just a “twerp.” Providing thoughtful algorithmic transparency may also blunt overly optimistic performance estimates and add a layer of security to the reflection-inducing process.

## Limitations, Future Research, and Conclusion

The findings should be viewed in light of some limitations and implications for future research. First, we focused on one subdiscipline in medicine. Thus, it would be beneficial to determine whether there are differences between specialists from other disciplines. Second, a larger sample size would be valuable for generating more nuanced findings on a quantitative scale. The interactions in our study were set in a typical and time-pressured environment, which prevented us from assessing the actual deployment and effectiveness of ML-based reflection partners in the “wild.” Similarly, the MLbased CDSS we employed was only able to stimulate reflection by providing input. Next-generation ML-based CDSS should combine features from XAI with human-in-theloop concepts to enable real dialogues. Such advanced systems could provide decision-makers with both input (in the form of classifications, e.g., here, diagnoses) and explanations of results. The human decision maker could then mark certain parts of the data (e.g., an area on an X-ray) to compel the system to reexamine that focal area. Such interactions would allow the system and the human user to produce more valuable insights, which would likely lead to better results (e.g., in terms of diagnosis accuracy).

This study paves the way for future research to address the above-mentioned issues through longitudinal, large-scale, and/or more hypothesis-driven studies. We are convinced that research on the effective integration of ML-based reflection partners will yield many novel and exciting insights. Although our study was in the important field of imaging in pneumology, our model of machine-induced reflections may nonetheless be generalizable to various kinds of ML-based systems and fields of practice (e.g., finance, marketing, engineering). In the course of our study, various healthcare professionals envisioned many different kinds of helpful CDSS, ranging from radiology tools for X-rays or CT to diagnostic tools for electrocardiograms to systems that analyze bone structure for anomalies and patterns. We believe that our model of machine-induced reflections could transform reflective practice in many professional fields where humans conduct analytical work. During the course of our project, we met and worked with numerous physicians who were likely to embrace the idea of an AI-based reflection partner as more than just another analytical tool that simply provides classifications. The idea spread through the field, and we were fortunate that the umbrella organization of German pneumologists found the idea intriguing enough to help us acquire a decent sample of physicians.

In short, our paper illuminates a process where ML-based systems help humans extend their own cognitive capabilities by stimulating reflection. In this way, this paper illustrates the missing link in the effective integration of ML-based systems in analytical work. Our model of machine-induced reflection delivers an extensive framework wherein the reflections induced by ML-based reflection partners can transform the reflective practice of analytical professionals (in our case, physicians). From a theoretical perspective, we lay the foundation for augmenting reflective practice using ML-based reflection partners, which will hopefully inspire future research on human-machine collaboration.
