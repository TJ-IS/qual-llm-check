## Highlights

· There are risks associated with websites that rely on user-generated content

· We study a protest by the users of Digg, a social news site

· We identify the context, development, and consequences of the protest

· Site administrators must be aware of the possibility of unrest among users

## Abstract

This study concentrates on online protests in websites that rely on usergenerated content. We develop a case study on Digg, a website powered by user-generated content that encounters backlash from their community. Through an interpretive analysis of the case we identify how the context and interactions among key players lead to what we call a "catch me if you can" game, where users and administrators of the website battle in an effort to outsmart and outmaneuver each other. The findings reveal a tension between users and administrators that intensifies during the protest, and should be considered carefully by site administrators.

Keywords: user-generated content; online protest; online revolts; web 2.0

## INTRODUCTION

The most highly trafficked sites on the World Wide Web, such as Facebook, YouTube, and Wikipedia, are produced with user-generated contributions. Sites that rely on user-generated content provide their users tools to contribute data, whether through a profile, video, comment, or other means; these contributions ultimately determine the site’s value. This type of interaction and the technologies that facilitate it have been labeled by practitioners as web 2.0 [O’Reilly, 2007]. User-generated content websites have experienced rapid growth and high valuations: by the time of Facebook’s initial public offering, the company was

valued at nearly 100 billion dollars [Sengupta and Rusli, 2012] with over one billion active users [Zuckerberg, 2012]. Other user-generated content websites have been the target of acquisitions: Google purchased YouTube for \$1.65 billion less than 2 years after it was founded [LaMonica, 2006] and YouTube is now the third most visited site in the world [Alexa, 2016]. The recent rise and explosive growth of startup companies that are driven by user-generated content has contributed to what some consider a second dot-com bubble, called Bubble 2.0 [Ibrahim, 2011].

However, while users typically contribute with good intentions and their contributions are central to a site’s success and growth, there is a need for a greater understanding of the risks associated with user-generated content. As we see in this study, dissenting actions by a user community can harm the site’s integrity and reputation. In the context of our research, we deem protests as a type of dissenting action that can quickly spread, and one in which users actively rebel against the site’s administrators and owners. The effects of protests can be long lasting and can potentially tarnish a site’s reputation. While some scholars have concluded that the Internet has limited impact for protests [Earl et al., 2010], evidence discussed below suggests otherwise [e.g. Gurak and Logie, 2003].

Protests, and how groups engage in collective action in general, have evolved considerably with the emergence of new communication methods [Bimber et al., 2005; Lupia and Sin, 2003]. Thanks in part to new information technologies, the resources required to participate in collective action have declined dramatically [Lupia and Sin, 2003], rendering previously well-accepted theories of collective action [Olson, 1965] as no longer completely accurate. The rise of onlinesupported protests in recent years has led to the “The Protester” being selected as Time Magazine’s Person of the Year in 2011 [Stengel, 2011; González-Bailón et al., 2013]. While the media tends to focus on protests that occur offline that in part are mobilized using online resources, we believe that another fruitful area of research is protests that occur online, particularly those that have been enabled by newer web 2.0 technologies.

We aim to make sense of online protests in the context of organizations that adopt user-generated content as a key source of value creation. The empiric content of our study concentrates on user protests and administrative retaliation that occurred on and against a popular social bookmarking site called Digg. Digg allows users to submit links to interesting content on sites and other users vote on the content’s newsworthiness. Digg was one of the first web companies to empower users with the ability to share news with others and actively create content, leading to the emergence of news sharing via sites such as Facebook and Twitter [Lynley, 2012]. Because Digg, Facebook, and other social sites primarily monetize through advertising, continued user contribution and engagement is critical. Accordingly, we have two research objectives. First, we aim to empirically study how these protests unfold and second, by interpreting our empiric account, we propose a theoretical generalization of this phenomenon. Our theorization consists on analytical generalizations [Lee and Baskerville, 2003] of the context of the protests and of the main properties that characterize the interactions between users and administrators which in turn define the protest.

The findings of our paper reveal a tension between the users and administrators of web 2.0 websites that is not adequately addressed in the practitioner or researcher literature. This tension intensifies and becomes salient during an online protest, as users continue to protest and administrators continue to penalize, eventually leading to one side capitulating. In this study, what began as a website user’s submission about the leaking of an encryption key quickly transformed into a protest against the website itself, using the same tools and technologies that users had been equipped with to provide a more democratic platform of content creation and curation. Administrators must be aware of the conditions, processes, and consequences of these online protests, and carefully monitor and adjust their actions in face of potential unrest among its users. While researchers have studied protests both online and offline, there has been little research on how web 2.0 technologies contribute to the conditions and development of a protest. The conclusions of this study suggest that there is still more work to be done to understand how and why these protests begin, develop, and conclude.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Following this introduction, we present an overview of online protests in the literature including past empirical cases and key properties. We identify a gap in the literature of online protests, particularly regarding empirical cases where a protest occurs completely online. We follow with an account of our research approach: an interpretive case study. We then present the case, which is divided into two parts: (1) a context of Digg, including its history, functionality, and a discussion of its business model (2) a narrative of a series of protests that occurred on Digg’s site. The case is followed by the results of its interpretation. We conclude by reflecting on the implications of our study.

## ONLINE PROTESTS IN THE LITER ATURE

In this section we concentrate on the literature that discusses the emergence and main properties of online protests. We first propose a working definition of online protests and provide a general background on how these protests have originated. We deem an online protest as an event that occurs when a significant number of site’s users strongly oppose the administration and, in doing so, manifest their discontent by taking belligerent actions against the site, such as defacing web pages or participating in a denial of service attack. Before usergenerated content sites were widespread, protests against Internet-based services were not as straight-forward; i.e. an act of protest such as defacing a site or causing serious damage required technical competency to ‘hack’ and change the content of the site. Since users did not always have the means or tools to voice their discontent in such a radical manner, they engaged in milder forms of rebellion, such as through online petitions and boycotts [Earl, 2006]. As the barrier of technical proficiency has decreased, the nature of online protests has changed as well. We begin our analysis of the literature by examining empirical and theoretical investigations of online protests.

Since Internet related protests can range from Internet-supported to Internetbased [Van Laer and Van Aelst, 2010] it is worth further demarcating what we mean by the term online protest. An Internet-supported protest may involve an action as simple as an online petition expressing discontent for a change in a corporation’s privacy policies. In these type of protests, the Internet is a vehicle for coordinating, but not the primary source of action. Internet-supported protests continue to become more prevalent, most notably in 2011, where the Internet was used to coordinate activities in the Arab Spring, and consequently in other countries where other protests emerged (González-Bailón et al., 2013). Conversely, Internet-based protests are aimed at the site itself, occur completely on the Internet, and may include hostile activities such as defacing of the site [Van Laer and Van Aelst, 2010].

For the purposes of our study, we focus on protests that are completely Internetbased, with very limited or no offline presence. Specifically, we are interested in protests within the context of what has been referred to as a hacktivist rhetoric [Wray, 1999], whereby the protesters attempt to deface a site or bring the site down through, for example, a denial of service attack. The site is not only the location of the protest, but also the reason that the users are protesting; faced with a potential threat to their beliefs and values, users express their discontent using no new tools or resources. Rather, the users creatively repurpose the existing tools and platform they are given as members of the website’s community.

To position our contributions in the section below we discuss what the extant literature has found and proposed when attempting to explain the nature of these types of protests. Specifically, we focus on the properties that characterize online protests. We do not claim such properties are exhaustive, rather we present them with the purposes of organizing our literature review and, as mentioned above, to position our theoretical contributions.

## Properties of online protests

Online protests reflect the properties of the Internet, and more specifically, the World Wide Web. In this sense, after examining the literature that concerns online protests, we found that this phenomenon is enabled by the following social properties of online participation: (1) the rapid diffusion and availability of information [Garrett, 2006; Gurak, 1999; Gurak and Logie, 2003], (2) the ability for groups to self-organize through computer networks [Arquilla & Ronfeldt, 2001; Bimber et al., 2005; Garrett, 2006; Leizerov, 2000], and (3) low costs of participation [Earl and Kimport, 2011; Garrett, 2006; Van Laer and Van Aelst, 2010]. Each property along with its relationship with online protests is discussed in further detail below.

The rapid diffusion and availability of information [Garrett, 2006] has created an environment where actions by corporations are more visible, leading to greater accountability and what Garrett [2006] labels a "reversal of the Foucaldian panopticon". The reversal of the panopticon occurs, Garrett argues, when organizations realize that they are being monitored through groups such as corporate watchdog sites, thereby inducing them into self-censorship. Instances of protests leading to tension between organizations and protesting groups can be traced back to the early days of the Internet. For example, empirical studies of online protests demonstrate how self-organizing online groups have successfully convinced large organizations such as Intel [Leizerov, 2000] and the U.S. government to reverse actions related to consumer privacy [Gurak, 1999; Gurak and Logie, 2003].

One example of a protest and the reversal of the Foucaldian panopticon occurred in 1999, when Geocities, a web site hosting company owned by Yahoo!, made changes to its terms of service that indicated any content hosted on their servers could be used by Yahoo! for whatever purposes they deemed fit [Gurak and Logie, 2003]. In response, web page owners who had content on Geocities mobilized together and “grayed out” their web pages, making the content difficult to read, while also putting a short notice that indicated their anger towards the terms of service changes. The defacing of their own sites sent a powerful message, and combined with an online petition and an e-mail campaign, resulted in Yahoo! modifying the offending clauses in the terms of service [Junnarkar, 1999].

The swift and effective development of the Geocities protests is representative of another key property in online protests: in an era of user-generated content, the presence of computer networks facilitates the self-organizing of groups of individuals who do not know each other. The actions of self-organizing groups result in new networked organizational forms that are unlike conventional organizations [Arquilla & Ronfeldt, 2001; Bimber et al., 2005; Garrett, 2006; Leizerov, 2000]. These groups tend to lack traditional governance elements such as a formal leader [Earl and Schussman, 2003] and do not suffer from increased

organizational costs as the group size grows [Lupia and Sin, 2003]. In these self

organized groups, an individual may decide to pursue or push an agenda that other users, who often do not know the individual personally, choose to join their cause [Earl and Schussman, 2003]. Often this involvement is temporary, as the users who participate in Internet-based protests typically disband after fulfilling their objectives [Earl and Schussman, 2003]. However, in some instances, it is also possible that these protests are part of a larger social movement that develops over time as well [Snow et al., 2007].

Multiple studies have also noted the ability of online protests to spread very quickly [Ayres, 1999; Earl, 2010; Gurak, 1999; Gurak and Logie, 2003]. This is mainly because the participation costs on the Internet are relatively low [Earl and Kimport, 2011; Garrett, 2006; Van Laer and Van Aelst, 2010]. We consider the low costs of participation a relevant property in the development of protests. Protesting online is also usually easy and immediate [Leizerov, 2000], leading to the rise of keyboard activism [Van Laer and Van Aelst, 2010], which can be defined as users engaging in a protest through a simple action, such as clicking a button on a website. However, protests can be more technically sophisticated. In the Geocities/Yahoo instance, protestors had to be technically proficient in editing the HTML code of their website to include the protesting message the graying out of the page. The introduction of sites that focus on user-generated content and democratic participation has made it easier to change a page’s content, thereby providing the foundation for engaging in rebellious activities that were not previously possible.

Below we summarize the Internet properties that enable online protests. In our study, our empiric objective is to advance this body of knowledge by studying an actual protest. Specifically, we concentrate on the tensions that exist between a site’s users and its administration and focus on the antecedents and processes that lead to online protests. Accordingly, our theoretical objective addresses the online protests phenomenon from a more general perspective as it proposes an analytical generalization of the possible antecedents of this type of protests as well as their dynamics, i.e. explaining how these protests are manifested, and outlining what their consequences are.

## RESEARCH METHOD

For this study we adopted an interpretive approach. An interpretive study is fitting for this research, especially due to the data’s textual nature and our study’s objectives, which concentrates on power struggles [Silva 2007; Walsham 1993]. Interpretivist epistemology posits that we know the world as though it were a text that we make sense of through a hermeneutic interpretation [Taylor, 1971, Ricoeur, 1981]. Our interpretation focused on articulating the context that engender this type of protests, as well as the processes that produces them, including their antecedents and consequences. The interpretation is hermeneutically framed by going back and forth from data to theory, which in our study consisted in the literature about protests found in the IS literature and other disciplines (see Table 1).

## Data Collection

We focus our data collection on the unit of analysis of our study – a user protest as described in the case below. The main data for our case comes from multiple sources of data collected in the Spring of 2007: posts on Digg as well as blog posts from key individuals in the protest. The Digg posts include a salvaged copy of a resubmitted story that was deleted from Digg, and consists of almost 900 comments related to the incident. The story was dugg (voted up) over 15,000 times. We will explain the concept of “digging” stories in the case. The second post, which quickly became the most popular post on the website with over 46,000 diggs (votes) on the website, is a link to a blog entry on Digg’s corporate blog, written by one of Digg’s founders, Kevin Rose. In the blog entry, Rose contends that they retract their decision to delete stories related to a copyrighted key. The blog entry has over 1,700 comments, representing opinions from both sides of the controversy.

## Analysis

We began our analysis by thoroughly reading the data and creating an initial narrative of the case. This developed from a thorough familiarity with the incident that occurred on Digg. One of the researchers has been a member of Digg since 2005 and observed the event unfold, archiving data before, during, and after the protest. Following this observance, the other researcher immersed themselves in the event, not only reading Digg articles, comments, and responses, but also researching discussion of the phenomena by popular press articles and the blogosphere. Each researcher worked on their interpretations separately. Once each interpretation was developed, the researchers compared their findings to identify and resolve differences. A consensus was developed based on the findings of both interpretations. Afterwards, the narrative was structured according to what we consider to be the most relevant events regarding the protests. The criterion for selecting relevant events was based on the online protest literature.

Thus, we articulated the narrative of the case by structuring the storyline of the protest in its main parts: conditions (context), processes, and consequences. The second step was the identification of the context and process that produce these types of protests that occur completely online. ## THE CASE OF DIGG

This section is divided into two parts. We begin by presenting the antecedents of Digg’s protest, including a description of Digg, its functionality including the reliance on an algorithm that is central to the site’s operation, Digg’s business model, and the users’ suspicion of bias within the administration. We then describe the controversy immediately before, during, and after the protest.

## Antecedents of Digg

Kevin Rose founded Digg in November 2004. Since its inception, Digg has developed and been promoted as a social bookmarking site, whereby members would submit articles and content to the site, and other members would digg it if they considered the item newsworthy. A digg is essentially a vote; by voting for a submission, a user is indicating that they believe the submission is important and should be a part of the Digg front page. In the beginning, Digg focused on technology-related story submissions, but later expanded to cover all news.

The popular press has suggested that Digg has “transformed the way people consume news online” [Waters, 2006] through “bottom-up media” and “citizen journalism” [Lacy and Hempel, 2006]. Digg’s users and supporters consider the site as a way of removing the editorial process and empowering the users. Digg’s “How Digg Works” page stated that “Digg is democratizing digital media. As a user, you participate in determining all site content by discovering, selecting,

#

sharing, and discussing the news, videos, and podcasts that appeal to you. Digg’s former CEO, Jay Adelson, declared that Digg is “leveraging the collective wisdom of the Internet masses to sift through these stories and apply their interests to it” [Waters, 2006].

In their first town hall meeting, Kevin Rose and Jay Adelson indicated that Digg has over 2.5 million users with more than 10,000 submissions per day [Digg, 2008]. However, the number of site visitors far exceeded this figure since one does not need to be registered to view submissions. At one point, some suggest that as much as 80% of the community is not registered [Bogatin, 2006], and the number of unique visitors each month, which exceeded 23 million, appears to validate this claim [Williams, 2010]. Prior to Digg’s eventual sale, Alexa’s traffic rankings ranked Digg as the #165 most visited web site on the Internet and #156 in the United States [Alexa, 2012].

## How Users Participate in Digg

When a user wants to submit content to Digg, they can go through the submission process in two ways: by submitting from a “digg this” or “digg” button already on a web page (such as those commonly found on news sites), or by submitting the URL on Digg’s website. By submitting content, the user is suggesting that the submission will be of value to the community. If the story has been submitted before, the user will be given the opportunity to digg it, email it to a friend, see who has “dugg” it, read comments, and/or join the discussion. Like submissions, comments can be dugg and given a positive vote; comments can also be voted negatively, and multiple negative votes for a comment can result in it becoming buried and hidden from display when viewing the submission page.

After submission, the story enters the ‘Upcoming’ area. ‘Upcoming’ consists of all submissions that have not yet become ‘Popular’ and entered the front page. At this point, the submission is visible to others, and people can digg the story. It is expected and understood that a user who sees an interesting submission in the ‘Upcoming’ area will visit the link, and if they believe it is newsworthy, give it a digg (positive vote).

Digg relies heavily on its users for content, creating a do-it-yourself environment where the users are in charge of finding news. Digg also incorporates social networking features; users can create profile pages, add friends, and communicate and share stories with others. This is a fairly common feature for sites with user-generated content, and is meant to encourage virality and increase engagement.

An extensive discussion of Digg’s business model is outside the scope of this case study, but it is important to note that Digg, much like Facebook, YouTube, and other user-generated content sites, does not primarily make money directly from its users. Instead, Digg and other sites are multi-sided platforms [Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010]. In a multi-sided online platform, a website usually serves as an intermediary with which to connect users, who do not pay to access the site, with advertisers, who seek to get exposure to the users and pay the website for advertising. Because the platform is multi-sided, revenue is largely contingent on both sides having critical mass: there needs to be enough users to attract advertisers, and there need to be enough advertisers to become a profitable venture. Keeping both users and advertisers satisfied and engaged is a critical activity for Digg.

Because continued user engagement is a central to Digg’s success, the egalitarian discourse of Digg, wherein every user’s vote has the same power, seems to epitomize the site’s fundamental values and promotes a sense of community. Although some users have suggested that the process may not be democratic because of the administrators’ moderation powers, Digg’s administrators contend that they are not actively involved in the voting process. In the next section, we describe events preceding the protests that indicate the users’ suspicion of bias.

## The Digg Algorithm and the Suspicion of Bias

The Digg algorithm determines at what point popularity is reached, but the exact algorithm is not known. In a post about upcoming algorithm changes, Digg founder Kevin Rose briefly described that popularity is determined by digging diversity and not by a straight tally of the votes [Rose, 2008]:

One of the keys to getting a story promoted is diversity in Digging activity. When the algorithm gets the diversity it needs, it will promote a story from the Upcoming section to the home page. This way, the system knows a large variety of people will be into the story.

Therefore, two submissions may exist with the same number of diggs and only one reaches the front page. Digg’s algorithm for the promotion of submissions is continually evolving, and changes have been made in the past to combat spammers and gamers who have attempted to artificially improve a submission’s ranking [Calore, 2006].

Even with the algorithm’s ambiguity, censorship allegations and controversy regarding Digg administration had been relatively sparse prior to the protest. Blog entries, with titles such as “Digg Corrupted: Editor’s Playground, not User-Driven Website,” “Digg Army,” and “Suspicious Digging,” appeared in the blogosphere as early as April 2006, suggesting that articles were being falsely promoted to the front page and that administrators were participating in automated digging:

...one article showed that the first nineteen diggs of each article were identical. What made this really interesting was that the 17th digger was none other than Kevin Rose, aka celebrated creator and founder of Digg. I’ve read that Digg gets anywhere from 500,000 to 800,000 readers a day. 16 (or 19) identical diggs for two articles by the same author? 22 of the first 24 diggers being the same for both articles? Somehow I don’t think that is a coincidence. [ForeverGeek, 2006]

Kevin Rose responded to allegations in an entry on Digg’s corporate blog [Rose, 2006]:

Recently it was brought to our attention that several users have created accounts to mass digg and promote stories. While these accounts appear to be valid, they have in certain instances been used for automated in-order (scripted) digging. This is a violation of our terms of service and the accounts have since been banned.

...On a personal note: It has been pointed out that I too have dugg these fraud stories. I digg stories I enjoy reading and currently track over 40 users within digg. If it's good content, I digg it.

In addition, multiple domains were banned from story submissions for unexplained reasons, and some bloggers protested that some story submissions would mysteriously disappear.

You will note that the one on the front page [Top 10 Free AdSense Tools] has less diggs than the other [Digg Corrupted]. This is despite the fact that it was submitted almost a day ago whereas the other was submitted less than an hour ago. This means that the “Digg Corrupted” submission has received almost 24 times as many diggs per minute. And yet it has not been promoted to the front page. I smell ‘hierarchical editorial control’. The articles are buried so they’ll never make the front page, I guess they could’ve been user buried. But this doesn’t detract from the fact than mine and other’s submissions were deleted (not buried). [Splasho, 2006]

Nevertheless, although these protests circulated among a few blogs, they did not mobilize the Digg user base to take collective action until the HD-DVD controversy described below.

## The Controversy

In early 2007, Digg experienced a dispute regarding the allegations of censorship that can be considered one of the most critical moments in the site’s history. On February 11, 2007, a hacker with the user name arnezami posted a cryptographic key on a public forum on the site Doom9.org that would unlock the encryption present on HD-DVD and BluRay discs, two high definition video formats. The 16-digit hexadecimal key could unlock all high definition discs, whereas previous decryption methods had only been able to unlock some titles.

Over the course of the next two months, the 16-digit hexadecimal key spread throughout the Internet and appeared on many sites focused on technology, including Digg. To stop the spread of the key, the Advanced Access Content System consortium, the group that held the copyrights to the encryption scheme, began to take steps to remove the key from offending sites. By the end of April 2007, the AACS sent DMCA cease-and-desist letters to many sites requesting that they remove the key or incur legal action.

In response to the cease-and-desist letters, a blog entry about the key titled “Spread This Number. Now” was submitted to Digg by username RuddO on April 30<sup>th</sup>. The user recollects his motivation in a blog entry following the controversy [Briz, 2007]:

Many sites were already carrying the key; therefore, never in my wildest dreams I imagined what would happen later on. At that moment, I wrote the (now kind of famous) story named Spread this number, partly because I was angry at Big Media’s continuing attempt to screw with our lives, and partly because I knew that the story warranted more attention than just a cease-and-desist.

The submission quickly gained diggs:

Then, (if I recall correctly) after half an hour of submitting the story, I couldn’t access my site. I just couldn’t. Why? It turns out the Digg community had started to vote for the story, at a dramatic pace — a Digg every three seconds, or so.

Shortly after the story spread, Digg moderators removed the submission and RuddO’s username was banned with no apparent explanation. A second user, chesterjosiah, who had dugg the original story but noticed that it had been removed, resubmitted the story with the title “Spread This Number. Again.”

This second submission was dugg at an astounding rate, bringing Digg’s servers to a crawl and eventually crashing the system for a few minutes. Within hours, the submission became the most highly dugg story in Digg’s history. However, after reaching this milestone, the submission was also removed and the submitting user was banned.

Moments later, Jay Adelson, Digg’s CEO, posted an entry on the corporate blog stating why Digg was removing stories [Adelson, 2007]:

We’ve been notified by the owners of this intellectual property that they believe the posting of the encryption key infringes their intellectual property rights. In order to respect these rights and to comply with the law, we have removed postings of the key that have been brought to our attention.

The Advanced Access Content System consortium, the organization that holds the copyrights for the HD-DVD code, had sent a DMCA cease-and-desist letter to Digg asking them to remove all instances of the code on its site or else face legal consequences. However, when Digg administrators attempted to comply, they soon discovered that their site, built on user-generated content, was impossible to control. Users began submitting countless stories that contained the key. Not only did the users defy the administrators’ request, but they did it in the name of Digg’s original values of democracy and freedom through the very powers they were originally given as Digg users: story submission, diggs, and comments. As the hours passed, the stories increased in creativity:

Digg deleted my hard drive for posting the HD-DVD KEY!

Now my hard drive refuses to write in binary. I get Error Code 09-F9-11-02- 9D-74-E3-5B-D8-41-56-C5-63-56-88-C0. Oh noz.

Furthermore, the cries for action intensified as users interpreted the administrators’ actions as censorship. Users insisted that the community fight to protect their rights:

I truly cannot believe how many people decided they were going to stand up (while sitting down mind you) for a cause that 50% of them were not going to actively participate in anyway. If ‘protesting’ or ‘sticking it to the man’ as they call it, is in fact possible by simply clicking your mouse a few hundred times then I for one welcome our new carpal-tunnel ridden overlords.

Actually no, you should fight, every last one of you, till you are all banned. There is no solution save ultimatum: none of you will go quietly. If you truly believe in free information and loathe the DRM, you must be prepared to fight for it. Wholeheartedly.

This is about your liberty. ## The Sale of Digg

After the protests, Digg’s traffic began to decline dramatically; site visits dropped to half by 2010 [Walker and Ante, 2012]. The decline is attributed to multiple reasons: a botched redesign attempt in 2010 further exacerbated the tension between the users and the administrators; there were technology problems with the implementation, and some popular features were removed while other new undesired features were added. The redesign, called “v4”, shifted the power from

users to publishers selected by Digg [Newman, 2015]. It did not take long for users to leave Digg, many of whom moved to Reddit. Rose later acknowledged that Digg failed to understand how to respond quickly to user concerns [Ante, 2012]. Others agree that Digg mishandled the site – an ex-Digg employee who posted their thoughts anonymously to Reddit blames power users, gaming, and the algorithm for the site’s decline [Reddit, 2012]; a Digg power user corroborates these claims [Siegler, 2012]. Meanwhile, the growth of Facebook and Twitter as tools for sharing news with others online lessened the need for sites like Digg [Gruber, 2012; Lynley, 2012]. Eventually, in July 2012, Betaworks, a company that focuses on new medium technology development, acquired Digg for a sum rumored to be around \$500,000 [Lynley 2012]; this number is remarkably low, especially considering that the company was valued at nearly \$160 million just four years earlier in 2008 [Ante, 2012]. The Betaworks team explains their motivations in a blog entry:

“that readers of news no longer just read, they participate; they no longer just consume, they create; that the traditional roles of the editor can be dispersed and democratized. Digg was new and it was different, and it was like nothing that had come before…..We acquired Digg because we all need a product to help find, read and understand what the Internet is talking about right now.” [Levine et al., 2012]

## RESULTS

In this section, we present the results of our interpretation of the case. We first present the context and conditions that may trigger a user protest such as the one that occurred on Digg. Second, we discuss the process and dynamics that such protests take. As stated above this process is likely to resemble a game of “catch me if you can”. The third part of our results section presents what we conjecture could be the consequences of protests in user-generated content websites.

## Context

Before we discuss the protest’s dynamics, we must delve into its context: the conditions, relations and actors that are relevant in engendering an online protest. This context is particular to our case and we do not propose it as a general model for all types of protests; only for those with similar conditions. Yet, understanding the context will help us to understand the conditions that trigger the process presented below. Our unit of analysis and the phenomenon under study is an online protest. We identify four conditions: community rights (freedom of speech), web 2.0 technology (user-generated content), powerful players who perceive a threat (corporations), and legislation.

The most immediate condition is a threat to the set of rights valued by a community of users. In our case, we found that the threatened right was freedom of speech on the Internet; a central tenet since the early days of the Web has been the belief that “information wants to be free” [Barlow, 1996] and that the spread of information on the web should be uninhibited [Wray, 1999]. Our case showed how a community of users defended these rights when they perceived threatened by powerful agents; i.e. the site administrators and entertainment corporations. This tendency of online groups to react defending their values and beliefs is in line with previous studies in the literature of society and ICTs [e.g. Leizerov, 2000; Gurak, 1999; Gurak and Logie, 2003]. These studies have shown, for example, how users reacted and forced Intel [Leizerov, 2000] to revert a policy that was deemed to invade users’ privacy. Likewise, the sociology literature confirms a similar disposition in groups to react aggressively when they perceive they are being deprived of a good or right they deem belongs to them [Miller et. al., 1977; Walker and Smith, 2002].

The next condition is web 2.0 technologies; e. g. wikis, blogs and mashups [O'Reilly, 2007]. These technologies are fundamental to our context as they constitute the space in which organizations such as Diggs establish their businesses and at the same time enable users’ actions during protests. Technologies associated with social networking are the resources that allow users to add content to sites while enabling communications and interactions among themselves. These technologies facilitate the self-organizing of users; a key attribute of these type of protests. Instances of this condition have been documented in previous cases such as the aforementioned protest in Geocities [Gurak and Logie, 2003; Junnarkar, 1999]. In these protests, protesters carried out their actions through online communications while directing their protest activities against the same site, i.e. Geocities. The speedy delivery of news online is vital to having users join the cause and grow the size of the protest [Ayres, 1999; Gurak, 1999]. Thus, our proposition here states that web 2.0 technologies are key conditions for users’ protests and that they constitute the target and the means through which protesters execute their actions.

The need for resources in carrying out protests is also confirmed in the sociology literature, especially in situations of social unrest, where participants in protests must have access to resources to execute their actions (see Jenkins, 1983). Likewise, the access to resources presupposes previous organization for gathering and distributing them, which in turn implies the presence of leadership [Snyder, 1978]. We argue, then, that in this sense, online protests are different from those in the physical world, as the former does not require leadership or gathering of resources. This may help explain how quickly online protests can emerge and spread.

#

Another condition is powerful players who perceive serious threats to their interests. It has been widely documented that corporations monitor social networking sites not only with the purpose of learning about users patterns of behavior but also to watch that their corporate interests are not threatened by any means [Gandy Jr., 1993; Ogura 2006; Fuchs. 2010]. This surveillance occurs in a Panoptican way in which those under scrutiny do not see those who observe them [Foucault, 1977]. History of the Internet shows that corporations do not hesitate to act when their interests are seriously threatened; for example the file sharing service Napster was shut down in the early 2000s as a result of litigation from record labels [McCourt and Burkart, 2003; Ku, 2002]. Although corporations may act in opposition to individual users as in the Napster case, their primary strategy is to take legal actions against the site owners hosting illegal content [Stelter and Stone, 2009]. In our case, the threat of spreading a code that could crack encryption of valuable intellectual property triggered possible legal actions against Digg, who responded by banning those members divulging the code. Thus, we argue that powerful agents, in this case entertainment corporations, who perceived their interests being threatened would be a necessary condition – when combined with the others – to start an online protest.

Finally, the last condition is legislation, which in our case legitimized the corporations’ and the administrators’ actions against the users. There are several laws and regulations that protect intellectual property from being illegally distributed through the Internet [Engleman, 2011; Schatz, 2012]. Research on social unrest has identified institutional factors as being fundamental in explaining collective aggressive behavior [Snyder, 1978; Jenkins, 1983]. In our case legislation protecting intellectual property represents a clear example of an institutional factor playing a major role in the Digg’s online protest. Such legislation provides the regulatory framework that allows a powerful player, in our case entertainment corporations, to bring social control on the online service. Moreover, our data showed that users not only protested against the site administrators and the corporations exercising influence to control postings, but also against the institutional setting that empowers both, administrators and corporations. The presence of legislation provided a foundation to legitimize the administrator’s actions. In the case of Digg, existing laws allowed the AACS to submit cease-and-desist letters to websites like Digg, putting the site administrators in a difficult situation where they had to choose between their community’s free speech rights and the threat of legal action.

Thus far, we have concentrated on the necessary conditions for an online protest to occur. However, the structural conditions of the phenomenon are both reinforced and modified by agents’ actions [Giddens, 1984]. Thus, we argue that the protest itself would strengthen the tendency of online users to react defensively to threats and punishment from powerful agents. Knowledge of a particular protest would likely reinforce the beliefs and feelings of deprivation that may be latent sources of future protests. In addition, we argue that the occurrence of a widely known protest such as the one that happened in Digg would compel corporations to continue lobbying for stringer laws regulating users’ contributions.

In sum, the interpretation of the context allows us to articulate the necessary conditions for an online protest to occur similar to the one we studied in our case. The first condition is the sociological disposition of groups to react and defend their beliefs and rights when faced with a perceived threat. This also requires a threatening powerful player, who is supported by a legal framework, is strong enough to mobilize resources, and can trigger sanctions for transgressors. Furthermore, the protest would not occur if the users would not have access to web 2.0 technologies that enable self organization and at the same time would permit them to creatively transform a contribution to the site into a defiant act of protest. Our contribution to the literature here consists in expanding a definition of online protests, similar to the one we studied, by specifying the conditions required for them to occur. In the next subsection we discuss in detail the different aspects that characterize the protest itself.

## Protest Interactions: “Catch me if you can”

We propose that in sites with user-generated content, an online protest is very likely to occur in three phases. In the first phase, there will be an interaction between a user and the administration. This phase would begin when a user contravenes a rule or regulation of which the administrators are aware could bring risks for them and the site in general. Consequently, the user is disciplined. To potentially trigger a protest, the disciplining would require a set of conditions. The act of disciplining (1) has to be public so the site’s users can realize it, (2) has to be severe (e.g. banning the account and deleting the post) and (3) must have an immediate effect. Moreover, the act of discipline has to be related to a tension of which users are aware of; hence, the disciplining measures would be considered unfair and would be associated to a threat to a specific right that would alert other site’s users and predispose them to protest such a punitive action. The propensity of collective acts of aggression to start after a specific event – similar to the one we found in our case – has also been observed in the sociology literature; see for example, Watt’s riots in 1965, Los Angeles Riots in 1992 and more recently the riots in England in 2011 [Sears and McConahay, 1973; Waddington, 2013; Myers, 2013; Miller et. al., 1977; Useem, 1998].

The second phase occurs when other users realize the severity and unfairness of the disciplining measures so they react aggressively, in our case it was by defiantly repeating the action that caused the punishment. We propose that the dynamic between administrators and users, whereby they engage in acts of disciplining and protest, may be similar to a game of “catch me if you can” (see Figure 1 below). Our data showed that users found the experience of posting in support of the protest and bypassing administrative controls as exciting and fulfilling [Wray, 1999]. Their experience was fed not only by outflanking controls but also as a result of joining protests against what they perceived to be a powerful agent – e.g. entertainment corporations – threatening to deprive them of a right. The sociology literature observes a similar propensity in urban riots. Once a violent public protest has started, agents of social control arrive and attempt to discipline the protesters. The arrival of the social control agents seems to incite the group of protesters to gather and confront them with even more force than at the beginning of the protest [McPhail, 1971].

In our case, the means for conducting the aggressive actions against the site were posts containing the HD-DVD code. We identified the main attributes of these posts, which were characterized by anonymity, low cost, and low effort. We found anonymity to be fundamental because it facilitates the users’ avoidance of possible responsibilities and potential risks [Gurak and Logie, 2003]. Anonymity is a key factor in urban riots as individuals before acting aggressively calculate the low risk of being identified by social control forces. Once rioters establish they will not be the target of retaliation from social control forces, they feel safe to act [Donnerstein et. al., 1972]. In addition, the posting does not require any effort or investment; all that is required is clicking on a link. The low threshold of participation is attractive to users who would otherwise not be associated with protest-related activities that require further effort [Van Laer and Van Aelst, 2010]. The protests are self-regulated; they do not require following elaborated instructions or coordination. This is interesting as resource mobilization theory, a well-accepted theory for explaining social unrest, states that among the conditions for collective aggressive behavior to occur are mobilization and access to resources, which entails a high degree of organization and leadership [Jenkins, 1983]. We did not find any of these conditions in our case.

Conversely, the attributes of online protests discussed here (low cost, low effort and anonymity) facilitate their spread and make them difficult to control. The only barrier for participating in the protest seems to be technological. We found that protesters have to be technologically savvy and, as suggested above, be aware of the threatened rights and the main actors in the power struggle. They need to be technologically savvy in the sense of understanding that their actions constitute a contribution towards a protest. It is this level of knowledge and sophistication that allows them to pick up the rules of the “catch me if you can” game. The third phase of the protest is the resolution. In this phase, the winner

#

of the game is established; either the administrators were successful with punitive actions and survived the protests, or, on the contrary, the protesters achieved their objective and were allowed to continue post messages of the type that ignited the initial disciplinary action.

These properties are analytical generalizations as we arrived to them through induction after interpreting our data [Lee and Baskerville, 2003]. Accordingly, we argue that it is very likely that an online protest, with similar conditions as those discussed above, would exhibit these properties. Punitive actions would be characterized by being: visible, of immediate effect, perceived as unfair and severe, while the protesting actions would be characterized as low risk, low effort, of immediate effect and having a defiant tone. The arrows from the attributes to disciplining suggest a relationship between the degree in intensity (of the attributes) and the perceived strength of the action (disciplining). The more visible, immediate, unfair and severe the disciplining measure is deemed to be, the stronger the protests are likely to be. Conversely, in the case of the protests, we propose that the more defiant they are, the more likely they are to incite retaliation from administrators. In addition, we argue that as long as the protesting actions continue to be low risk (anonymous), to represent low effort and to have an immediate effect, the more incentives the protesters will have to continue with their actions. The positive feedback loop between punishment and protest depicted by the arrows and the plus sign represent what we call a “catch me if you can” game played by the protesters. Escalations in disciplining measures result in escalations in protest, and vice versa. Put simply, the more the users protest, the more the administrators would attempt to punish users protests. The more administrators punish, the more users protest.

## Consequences

Figure 2 depicts the possible consequences of a protest on a site with usergenerated content. The matrix shows the different combinations of the main interactions during a protest: the interaction between the degree of intensity of protests and punishments (Table 4 presents data excerpts that support and illustrate each scenario). We have identified four possible outcomes. One possible outcome would be characterized by an intense exchange of protests and punishments, while in another harmony is defined by virtually no protests and punishments. The other two cases are also interesting; one would be characterized by the administration's capitulation, which we observed in Digg's case. The other case is when the administration overcomes the protesters by maintaining the status quo; this case would suggest the effectiveness of the administrators.

Thus, there is a paradoxical result for the administrators. On the one hand, administrators empower users to provide content but on the other when the content is protest-related, empowerment turns into a force that disrupts the site. In our case, eventually, the administrators realize that they cannot ban all the users and capitulate. This is similar to what occurred in the UK in May of 2011 when John Hemming, a British MP, publicly stated that "you cannot jail them all". He was referring to nearly 75,000 Twitter users who protested an injunction from a judge that prohibited making public the name of a British celebrity involved in a scandal. The Twitter users massively protested in an overt and defiant manner by "tweeting" the celebrity’s name.

The most appropriate strategies for the administrators seem to be those that would diminish the intensity of or eliminate the protests. One strategy could be to monitor possible accumulation of feelings of deprivation and frustration from their users. This is what van Noort and Willemnsen [2012] call a proactive strategy to avoid negative word of mouth reputation in the Internet. One of these strategies consists in constantly searching the web to identify and address negative

#

comments from users [Willemsen et. al., 2013]. By spotting and addressing sources of users’ frustration early on, possible protests may be prevented. The second strategy – a reactive one – could consist in stopping any punitive actions against users such as limiting access to resources – especially those used for the protest. The latter would be difficult because the limiting of access to resources, like banning accounts, could increase frustration in users and exacerbate the protests even more. Moreover, as it has been shown, the lack of social controls does not necessarily entail the stopping of riots [Useem, 1998]. Therefore, we conclude that the optimal strategy may be that of being alert to prevent sources of frustration that may lead to protests.

## CONCLUSION

This study had two main objectives. One was the empiric study of an online protest while the second was a theorization regarding the conditions and dynamics of an online protest. The in-depth case study of Digg allowed us to achieve the first objective and the second one was addressed in the results section. A summary of our theoretical contributions is presented in Table 4. Table 4 contrasts the current body of knowledge with our contributions, specifically regarding the context and processes of online protests.

Our study presents some limitations. One is that we concentrated on the written interactions between users and administrations. Our data does not provide a direct perspective from the users in terms of their motivations. A study in which participants would be interviewed and asked about their feelings and emotions would inform their psychological motivations and would provide a more comprehensive view of the whole phenomenon. Another area of further studies could consist in confirming or rejecting our theoretical formulation. In this sense, other online protests could be identified and then researchers could establish whether there are differences in terms of context and process in relation to our findings. The generalizations suggested in the models do not pretend to be a generalizations of all online protests. They are not positivist models. Instead, they represent a conceptual generalization of this case in particular. This type of generalization to concepts is what Lee and Baskerville call generalizations to theory, which they point out to be one of the essential characteristics of case studies (Lee and Baskerville, 2003).

In addition, more research may be required in the area of management and prevention of online protests. Administrators would welcome an analytical tool that would help them to recognize signs of an impending protest, and avoid getting caught up in a “catch me if you can” game. Ultimately, these games may result in capitulation by the administration or complete loss of the site’s integrity. Moreover, further research could be conducted on the development of indicators for identifying and measuring frustration in online posts. This could consist on counting number of complaints posted online, identification of the main themes found in complaints, and inquiring about the degree of frustration in the messages. These measurements as well as the means to identify the posts could help administrators to prevent protests.

It would also be helpful to identify psychological factors that contribute to the willingness of a user to engage in a protest. Are certain characteristics or attributes of a user likely to lead to them joining a protest? Which types of users join a protest early on, and which types join once the protest has already gained momentum? Such an analysis would aid administrators in identifying the types of users to target during different stages of a protest.

The primary objective of our study was to identify conditions that could lead to the development of protests on sites powered by user-generated content. Protests in social networking sites are an emerging phenomenon, and they are becoming increasingly relevant as more sites rely on user-generated content. As organizations and businesses continue to leverage these technologies, they risk placing the vitality of their business at the whim of users. Often times these users are passionate about certain values, and will attack at any indication of perceived unfairness. By maintaining transparency in these situations, and responding diligently to calls for protest, an administration can help avoid a potentially costly mistake. We hope our study informs practitioners on how to avoid this dilemma, and invite further research on this growing and relevant issue.

<table><tr><td>Current Body of Knowledge (from Table 1)</td><td>Our Contributions</td></tr><tr><td>Context of online protests• Rapid diffusion and availability of information (Garrett, 2006; Gurak, 1999; Gurak and Logie, 2003)• Networking capabilities that facilitate the creation of new organizational forms (Garrett, 2006; Gurak, 1999; Gurak and Logie, 2003; Lupia and Sin, 2003)</td><td>Context of online protests• Online protests would likely originate when a feeling of deprivation grows among users, this will lead to an accumulation of frustration in users.• Online protests would involve users against a powerful agent. We suggest that the latter would likely be empowered by a regulatory and legislation framework.</td></tr><tr><td>Process of online protests• Low participation costs (Arquilla &amp; Ronfeldt, 2001; Bimber et al., 2005; Garrett, 2006; Leizerov, 2000; Earl and Schussman, 2003)</td><td>Process of online protests• An online protest would likely start after a triggering event, which may consist in severe punishment on a user which the community would deem as harsh and unfair (however, the triggering event would have to occur within the context described above)• The news of unfairness and harshness of the triggering event would spread through social media and other users would join.• Punitive actions on behalf of the administrators would be characterized by being: visible, of immediate effect, being both, perceived unfair and severe while the protesting actions would be characterized by being: low risk, low effort, of immediate effect and having a defiant tone.• Interactions would likely occur in what we call a catch me if you can game, in which more punitive actions will engender more protests.• The online protest would probably end when either the administration capitulates and allow the protests to continue or by eliminating protesters access to resources through the shutting down of the site.</td></tr></table>
