# Exploring social network interactions in enterprise systems: the role of virtual co-presence

Niran Subramaniam\*, Joe Nandhakumar<sup>†</sup> & João Baptista (John)<sup>{</sup>

\*Saïd Business School, University of Oxford, Oxford, OX1 1HP, UK, email: niran. subramaniam@sbs.ox.ac.uk, and Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry, CV4 7AL, UK, <sup>†</sup>email: joe.nandhakumar@wbs.ac.uk, and <sup>{</sup>email: j.baptista@wbs.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper investigates the impact of social media interactions on the use context of enterprise systems (ES) at a global telecommunications company based in Europe. We conducted a detailed <sup>fi</sup>eld study of users in three countries who execute tasks collectively using social media capabilities within their ES. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide rich insights into these social media interactions, their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on ES users’ sense of ‘presence’ and their impact on the completion of tasks. Our key research contribution is to introduce the idea of ‘virtual co-presence’ as a means of understanding the digitally mediated presence of ES users. In addition, we demonstrate how virtual co-presence and the relationships afforded by social media shape ES users’ interactions and collective completion of tasks in a dispersed work context. Drawing on the insights gained from the effect of virtual co-presence on ES users, the paper outlines some implications for the theory and practice of collective work in ES use.

Keywords: social media, enterprise systems, interpretive approach, co-presence, interaction, collective activity

## INTRODUCTION

The growing use of social media within organizations enables new forms of digital interaction among employees often challenging the process-centric nature of traditional enterprise systems (ES). ES are software packages designed to integrate business processes across the functional divisions of an organization (Davenport, 1998; Robey et al., 2002; Staehr, 2010). They support the ‘seamless integration of all the information <sup>fl</sup>owing through a company – <sup>fi</sup>nancial and accounting information, human resource information, supply chain information, customer informa tion’ (Davenport, 1998, p.121). ES typically include enterprise resource planning, customer relationship management, supply chain management, product life-cycle management and eProcurement software (Ramdani et al., 2009).

Much of the academic research on ES has sought to understand problems with the acquisi tion, adoption, implementation and use of these systems (Esteves & Pastor, 2001; Esteves & Bohorquez, 2007). This body of work tends to emphasize the way in which ES model complex business processes of organizations, and as such describe them as process and data-centric systems (Cardoso et al., 2004). The process-centric perspective of most of the existing research emphasizes improvements in ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of business processes but adds relatively little to ou understanding of user interactions and communication within the context of ES use (Oja & Galliers, 2011). The challenges of incorporating ES into everyday work practices of employees remains and is an increasingly important issue in Information Systems research (Sia & Soh, 2007; Strong & Volkoff, 2010; Wagner et al., 2010). One of the underlying issues is that ES ‘impose their own logic on a company’s strategy, organization and culture’ (Davenport 1998, p.122), which may not accurately re<sup>fl</sup>ect users’ actual work practices. This is because work practices vary and may not be fully aligned with the way the ES is con<sup>fi</sup>gured. Therefore, users often devise workarounds to overcome structural constraints of ES (Ignatiadis & Nandhakumar, 2009).

Studies suggest that to align ES processes with their diverse work practices, users need to interact with each other in completing tasks together (e.g. Volkoff et al., 2005). Some organizations have responded to this requirement by adopting and/or integrating social media tools to provide a user-centric front-end layer to their ES (Hofmann, 2008; p84). These social media tools provide ‘interactive platforms via which individuals and communities share, co-create, discuss, and modify user-generated content’ (Kietzmann et al., 2011, p.241). They also allow ES users to connect and keep in touch with colleagues within a digital network regardless of their physical location. In this way, they have the potential to enable users’ availability for interactions (presence availability), which is an important aspect of online social relationships (Kietzmann et al., 2011). Despite the relevance of social media in the use of ES (use contexts), the academic literature has so far been fairly mute about this phenomenon and offers very limited theoretical support for researchers. We focus on this important issue in this study, which is driven by the following research question: how do the social media interactions of ES users shape collective completion of tasks in a dispersed organizational context? For the purposes of this paper, we refer to the collective completion of tasks pertaining to a business process as ‘collective activity’.

Drawing on Goffman’s concept of co-presence encounters, we introduce the idea of virtual co-presence as a means of developing a richer theoretical understanding of social media interactions in the day-to-day work practices (use contexts) of ES users. The paper also seeks to demonstrate how virtual co-presence and relationships afforded by social media shape ES users’ interactions and their collective activity in dispersed organizational contexts.

The paper is structured as follows. First, we review the literature on collective activity and social media interaction in the ES use context. We then describe our conceptual foundations by brie<sup>fl</sup>y reviewing Goffman’s (1959) notion of physical co-presence. This is followed by an overview of our research setting and methodology. Next, we describe our case study and conceptualize virtual co-presence within the context of use of an ES in an organization selected for our <sup>fi</sup>eldwork. Finally, drawing on key themes emerging from our analysis, the discussion section elaborates the theoretical understanding gained and outlines some of its implications for theory and practice.

## Collective work in ES

At the core of ES is the notion of ‘collective work’, which is often shaped by the business processes embedded in the ES platform (Ellis et al., 2005). Collective work emerges, for example, when users belonging to a certain functional group, such as Accounting, need to interact and share knowledge among themselves, and with other parts of the organization, in the completion of tasks. Collective work in an ES use context also occurs when users (co-located or dispersed) work to complete tasks within shared timeframes. Some of these connections will be embedded within the ES, whereas others will be based on informal connections and networks of users. Ellis et al. (2005) suggest that collective work is ‘characterised by its <sup>fl</sup>uidity and complex weav ing of organisational, social, political, cultural and emotional aspects’ (p.38). They argue that in physically co-located work settings, collective work of ‘interactants’ is triggered by a combina tion of the physical awareness of each other and the perceived dependences of their own tasks with others. In terms of the perceived dependences of interactants’ own tasks, Thompson (1967) describes three types of task interdependence: ‘sequential’ interdependence, where an employee starts work once another employee has completed his or her part of the task, ‘reciprocal’ interdependence, where an employee works in tandem with others, and ‘pooled interdependence, where users complete tasks for review by each other. He argues that in situations of task interdependence, collective action requires coordination, and coordination is often achieved through standardization of tasks in distributed work settings. The role of digita media in distributed work settings has been studied in the context of computer-supported co operative work research (Olson & Olson, 1997). This body of work has offered interesting insights on the role of technology to support collective work in organizations but has offered very limited views on the context of use of ES, and in challenging the established view that ES ‘are designed in such a way that control over information resides in the system and with the system designers and analysts, rather than in the workforce’ (Coakes et al., 2008, p 15). However, as mentioned previously, although ES packages are often designed around standardized business processes, studies suggest that such processes may not align with the day-to-day work practices of users (Sia & Soh, 2007). Further, ES packages tend to provide very limited support for such collective, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c activities (Wagner & Newell, 2004). Instead, they are usually designed around a set of work processes and business rationales deemed suitable by the vendo (Davenport, 1998, Gable, 1998, Holsapple et al., 2006). Although this is seen as providing much needed ‘discipline’ (Newman & Westrup, 2005) and process formality, it forces organizations to align their <sup>fl</sup>ow of work and business logic to that of the technology solution (Volkoff et al., 2007). This can cause tension and potential misalignments between ES package functionality and organizational requirements (Sia & Soh, 2007; Strong & Volkoff, 2010), ultimately distancing the use of systems from the way the organization operates (Lindley et al., 2008).

Alignment and accommodation of ES within the functioning of organizations requires the mutual adaptation of technology and social systems (Wagner et al., 2010). Yamauchi & Swanson (2010, p.200) claim that users ‘assimilate a system not in isolation but in the collective action of using it’. This interaction between the technology and its organizational context of use is crucial to the embedding of that technology into the workplace (Baptista, 2009). The embedding of digitally mediated social networks in ES work practices has the potential to enable more meaningful user interaction and collective action by forming a user-centric layer of activity on top of the process-centric tools used to support ES. The role of connections and social ties based on mutual knowledge and understanding (Yang & Chen, 2008) plays a key part in coor dination and collaboration within organizations (Coleman, 1988). Furthermore Fowler et al. (2004) suggest that virtually embedded ties in networks provide the basis for collective work. Despite the growing importance of social media and digital networks in this context, existing lit erature has not yet explored fully the effects of social media on user interaction and collective activity in the use context of ES.

## Social media interactions in ES

The use of social media has brought about deep changes in organized human action, empowering individuals to innovate and share their expertise (McAfee, 2006; Parameswaran & Whinston, 2007). However, much of the research in this <sup>fi</sup>eld is concerned with the effects of social media on social networking (Gibson, 2005), informal employee networks for collaboration (Cross et al., 2005), collective action (Segerberg & Bennett, 2011) or the management of customer relationships and organizational image (Gilpin, 2010). Similarly, the ES literature has been relatively mute on the effects of social media on ES and has focused mainly on analysing interaction patterns among groups. For example, Aalst et al. (2005) propose an approach – sociograms – to identifying the interpersonal connections of ES users through an analysis of ES process logs (as depicted in Figure 1).

By analysing events logged within the ES, this approach charts the handover of work or delegation from one user to another. The resulting map of the social network (sociogram) indicates patterns of interactions between ES users based on the <sup>fl</sup>ow of work embedded in a business process. In other words, it depicts the formal user interactions enforced by the system. However, these methods do not capture the informal user interaction patterns that emerge from collaborative work. Therefore, they provide limited insight into how work is carried out in practice and the role played by digital media in supporting dynamic interpersonal connections and col lective completion of tasks within ES use contexts.

Such studies do, however, offer some insights into the nature of relationships within organizations. For example, Yang & Chen (2008) identify two types of relationship ties: ‘knowledge relation ships’ and ‘social relationships’. A knowledge relationship is determined by how well an individual’s knowledge matches the information needs of a fellow employee, whereas social relationships re<sup>fl</sup>ect the extent to which individuals know each other in a personal capacity. Wenger (1998) proposes that people are informally bound together by shared expertise and regards such networks as the best conduits of information-sharing in organizations. He also argues that employees connect at various levels and across departments in such a way as to share their expertise and learn from each other. These studies reveal the importance of understanding the implications and value of social media for interactions and collaborative work in the context of ES use within organizations. They also highlight the need to explore the role that digital media, such as social media, might play in the completion of tasks. One of the key roles played by social media is to enhance the sense of presence within ES use contexts (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010). Kietzmann et al. (2011, p.243) suggest that ‘presence’ – the extent to which users know that other users are available for online interaction – is one of three key features that distinguish social media from other information and communication technologies in an enterprise context. The other two are (1) ‘relationships’, the network of connections that users can tap into if the need arises and (2) ‘sharing’, the extent to which users exchange, distribute and receive content through their social and professional ties.

In summary, our literature review suggests that collective work occurs in the use contexts of ES when users engage in networks of informal connections to complete tasks. We have highlighted the limitations of ES packages in capturing and supporting such interactions and the misalignment between ES functionality and the way work is carried out by users in organi zational settings. We have also discussed how the use of social media technologies has the potential to increase an individual’s sense of presence and therefore support collective work and task completion in the context of ES use.

## CONCEPTUAL FOUNDATIONS

## Presence and interaction

To explore the effects of social media on the completion of tasks in ES use contexts, we draw on Goffman’s (1959) conceptualization of ‘co-presence’ and ‘focused interaction’. Goffman de<sup>fi</sup>nes co-presence as the presentation of self in face-to-face interactions (Goffman 1963, p.17). Focused interaction occurs when individual actors strive to sustain the attention of others, as in conversations. Our analysis is also guided by Goffman’s work on the shared sense of a single mutual activity (1963, p.89) which, among co-located individuals, can result in ‘sustained, intimate, coordination of action, whether in support of collaborative tasks or as a means o accommodating closely adjacent ones’ (Goffman, 1983, p.3).

## Mediated settings and temporality

Goffman restricts his analysis to face-to-face interactions, describing other forms of interaction as ‘marginal and derived forms of social contact’ (1971, p.70). However, more recent work has conceived co-presence as a form of co-location in space–time that allows for instantaneous and reciprocal human interaction (Zhao, 2003). This conceptualization of co-presence is more appropriate for use in mediated settings (situations where technologies serve to connect actors in a digital or virtual space), as it allows us to analyse the role played by ‘sense of presence’ in collaborative work (Fowler et al., 2004) and task completion through the activation of informal connections between employees (Urry, 2004). We draw on Goffman’s theorization of focused interactions in face-to-face situations and extend it to mediated situations of ‘virtual co-present interactions’. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne ‘virtual co-presence’ as co-presence in digitally mediated situations, enabled by the use of social media tools in organizational settings. For example, instant messag ing services such as Skype display the presence of users and make visible their availability to other users. We describe these interactions in virtual co-present mediated situations as ‘virtua encounters’. In sum, we use Goffman’s conceptualization of co-presence to explore how socia media enabled sense of presence may affect collective completion of tasks in ES.

Goffman (1967; 1983) takes physically de<sup>fi</sup>ned, face-to-face social situations as his unit of analysis for the study of interactions. However, as previously discussed, his work has been extended to mediated interactions (Meyrowitz, 1985; Boden & Molotch, 1994), such as copresent interactions in mobile communications (Rettie, 2009). We endorse this extension of Goffman and argue that mediated situations retain roles and mutual expectations of users in interactions, which then in<sup>fl</sup>uence collective task completion. Goffman (1981) argues that in physical co-present situations, ‘interactants’ cooperate to maintain orderly contact with each other. He refers to such co-presence situation as ‘encounters’ or focused interactions, as described previously. Through these focused interactions, each interactant settles for a particular role – such as ‘technical author’ or ‘trainer’ – leading to the formation of an ‘interaction order’. For Goffman (1959), being in society (presence of others) means being on ‘stage’ and constantly performing roles for an audience. In this way, physical presence and location help interactants shape their social experience (Goffman, 1979).

We draw on Goffman’s work on focused interactions and the roles interactants settle into (formation of situated roles) to explore the experiences of ES users working in a shared timeframe but not necessarily in the same place (shared space). Rettie (2009), for example, adopts this approach in her study of mobile communications. She characterizes focused mediated interactions as mediated encounters' and shows that participants in such encounters cooperate to maintain a focused interaction. We build on this work to analyse user interactions afforded by social media in the context of ES use. We also propose extending Goffman’s con ceptualization of situated encounters to virtually mediated encounters ‘in-practice’ (Orlikowski, 2000), focusing on those interactions which enable ES users to share tacit knowledge and expertise in completing tasks together.

## Network ties and interaction orders

Our main focus is on co-presence in digitally mediated encounters as the background to the formation of spontaneous interaction orders that may shape and sustain knowledge sharing and relationship ties within social networks (Yang & Chen, 2008). For Goffman (1979), interaction orders are social norms constituting the ‘script’ for each role an actor plays in his or her interactions with others. In this way, Goffman claims that interaction orders place structural constraints on actors’ day-to-day co-presence interaction. However, other social theorists, for example Giddens (1984), have shown that through their sustained interactions, human actors reproduce and transform these established orders or structural constraints that in turn enable and constrain their subsequent interaction (Giddens, 1984).

We explore how an ES user’s sense of presence of another user in digitally mediated situations evokes focused interactions in shared time. We see the ES integrated with social media technol ogies, as a digital network for voluntary, infrequent and spontaneous online interaction of experts engaged in the collective completion of tasks. ## RESEARCH SETTING AND METHOD

For this study, we follow an interpretive research approach (Walsham, 1993; Klein & Myers, 1999), involving the collection of detailed, qualitative data on ES user practices in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizationa context. Interpretivism assumes a subjective view of the world that exists through human action (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). Interpretive researchers therefore consider social phenomenon as ‘produced and reinforced by humans through their action and interaction’ (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991, p.14). Adopting an interpretive approach in this study implies focusing on the intersubjective experiences (Nandhakumar & Jones, 1997) of the ES users’ sense of presence and mediated encounters (Goffman, 1974; 1981) in practice, which is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in developing an in-depth understanding of this phenomenon. The qualitative data generated from this study there fore places speci<sup>fi</sup>c emphasis on ES users’ experiences and explanations of their interaction with each other in task coordination and collective activity in virtual co-present settings.

The study was conducted at a global telecommunications company, MaxCom (pseudonym). MaxCom is a leader in the telecommunications industry, widely recognized for its innovative solutions and customer service. At the time of our study, the company employed over 30 000 staff in more than 150 countries. Its <sup>fi</sup>nance, logistics and customer relationship management functions are supported by SAP (Systems, Applications & Products), a major enterprise software solution. MaxCom has implemented SAP, to automate its <sup>fi</sup>nance, order ful<sup>fi</sup>lment and customer and supplier relationship management functions. MaxCom was chosen for our <sup>fi</sup>eldwork because of the deep embedding of SAP in its business processes and the normal functioning of the organization.

## Context of the study

In 2006, MaxCom was involved in a merger of two entities focused on supplying large-scale communication eguipment and network configurations. This resulted in a shift in strategy fol the company from being ‘product-focused’ to ‘solution-focused’. It also meant that employees previously tasked solely with marketing and selling speci<sup>fi</sup>c equipment had to work with those responsible for managing customer accounts and delivering complete solutions. For this to happen, MaxCom needed to respond quickly to customer needs and to con<sup>fi</sup>gure solutions for delivery through a geographically dispersed network of teams. This in turn required ES users to work closely together, rather than individually, to speed up task completion and shorten response times for customers. During the transition phase of the merger, there was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant institutional pressure for employees and ES users to interact more often in order to align working practices and develop shared objectives.

Following the merger, specialist divisions were created for customer accounts, sales man agement and solutions delivery. The structure of the collaborative work<sup>fl</sup>ow between divisions, as described to us by a program manager, is reproduced in Figure 2.

## DATA COLLECTION

Our study took place at MaxCom of<sup>fi</sup>ces in three European countries (Finland, Germany and UK). We spent over a week at each of the three sites, engaging intensively with employees in their ES use contexts. During these visits, we conducted a total of 32 semi-structured interviews, lasting approximately 1 h each, across the three sites. Interviews were recorded using a digital voice recorder and transcribed verbatim for our analysis. To gain an understanding of how social media capabilities and ES functionality in<sup>fl</sup>uenced user interactions, our interviews focused on ES users’ accounts of collective task completion ‘in-practice (Orlikowski, 2000). The questions centred on gaining their personal insights into the historical context of ES at MaxCom, the <sup>fl</sup>ow of activities managed by the ES, the key challenges experienced by users and the impact of social media on user interactions and communication practices.

We interviewed a selection of users, analysts (both business and technical) and managers at each of the three of<sup>fi</sup>ces. Interviewees were chosen based on their direct knowledge and experience of working with the ES. Their diverse positions within MaxCom allowed us to gain an understanding of differing roles and expectations of employees within ES use contexts. Interviews were conducted in phases between April and July 2010. Each phase focused on users from a particular functional group – i.e. sales, order management, product con<sup>fi</sup>gura tion, order ful<sup>fi</sup>lment and customer relations. A list of each interviewee’s role and location is provided in the Appendix.

In addition to the semi-structured interviews we took detailed field notes of observations during our site visits. We also examined a variety of documents (c.f. Orlikowski, 1996) to verify the key themes that emerged from the interview data, concentrating particularly on business process descriptions and documents relating to collaboration strategy. Together, these interview transcripts, <sup>fi</sup>eld notes and documents represented ‘thick descriptions’ (Geertz, 1973), providing deep insights into the inter-subjective experience of ES users’ day to day work.

## Data analysis

We followed established qualitative methods to analyse our data (Walsham, 1993; Miles & Huberman, 1994; Orlikowski, 1996; Klein & Myers, 1999). This involved reading and re-reading <sup>fi</sup>eld notes, interview transcripts and documents to identify recurring patterns and themes (Orlikowski, 1996). Transcribed text was imported into the N-Vivo analysis tool as source data for coding and categorization. We created an initial set of codes based on the conceptual foundations of our study. However, additional codes emerged as our analysis progressed (Miles & Huberman, 1994), to which segments of data were assigned. Through this initial coding process, we developed <sup>fi</sup>rst-order categories, which were informed by our sensitizing concepts (Walsham, 1993; Klein & Myers, 1999): temporality, presence, network ties, interaction and interaction orders (Table 1). Our goal was to ensure that data not only re<sup>fl</sup>ected theoretical concepts but was also rooted empirically in the actions and behaviours of the people and the organization under study. A list of categories and codes used for the analysis of the data is provided in Table 2.

These categories emerged during the coding by clustering core concepts around each other. For instance, those relating to the concepts presence and temporality appeared related, suggesting that co-presence was an important concept to explore. Interactions and tasks are also related to suggest task-based interactions occurring over a digital medium such as the dig itally enabled ES. The cluster of emerging concepts and relationships taken together suggest co-present user interactions occurring in a digitally enabled ES.

We then performed pattern coding to identify relationships between and among these categories and assembled them into higher-order themes (Miles & Huberman, 1994). In this way, we were able to gain an understanding of user interactions in the ES (digitally mediated encounters) and insights into how these interactions affected the collective completion of tasks among ES users As our analysis progressed, we were also able to recognize different aspects of mediated interaction in the completion of tasks and the motives for collaborative work within the ES. For example, we were able to identify the interactive order between certain ES users as dynamic patterns of interaction that may sustain over time. ## FINDINGS AND CASE ANALYSIS

In this section, we focus on presenting and analysing our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the MaxCom study. First, we provide an overview of the use of social media for interaction and collective completion of tasks, as perceived by ES users. We then focus on the main analytical themes that emerged from our inductive examination of ES use within this organization.

Enterprise systems users at MaxCom recalled their frustration with a previous ES platform, which had been con<sup>fi</sup>gured using standardized business processes, and how they strove to create a <sup>fl</sup>exible system that could accommodate their everyday work practices. At our <sup>fi</sup>rs meeting, the Head of Sell Process Excellence summed up the challenge users faced:

Do we stick with the traditional standard SAP platform, which is very process driven and task oriented, or should we try and implement a system which allows us to be more <sup>fl</sup>exible?

As social media capabilities (instant messaging, wiki and blogs) were implemented within the ES, many users saw the emerging platform as providing a sense of awareness of others and facilitating knowledge exchange. They perceived the SAP ES integrated with the social media, as a digital network for user interactions, as noted by one of the Sales Process Analysts:

There’s the instant messaging type of interaction that they’re all the time online so you don’t need to call them. You can just type in a message and if they happen to be at their station and available . . . they can quickly reply. When they’re constantly online and there are constantly messages being passed back and forth via this [it is like] the social network, the rea people network . . ..

. . . we already see that the adoption of some wikis and blog type of functionalities is very rapid inside the Enterprise System, we are very dynamic and the knowledge is sort of key to execution, I think social media will have a large role to play.

We found that when providing solutions to customers’ speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs, design engineers from different locations also used the social media capabilities integrated into their ES, such as tags and blogs, to interact and discuss ways of con<sup>fi</sup>guring products. The Head of Capability Design explained:

It’s kind of a platform [ES integrated with social media] where expertise in the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of solutions for customers is shared; people can put their cases in and engage standard knowledge sharing through the so-called INS folder [in ES], where we have a repository of data structures that everyone can access with certain access rights. We call it a ‘uni<sup>fi</sup>ed communication platform’ that consists of WebEx, IP based Soft-Phone, Skype etc.

Thus, from identifying prospective customers to con<sup>fi</sup>guring product portfolios to meet customer needs, ES users interacted with one another using a combination of interactive functionality. This included social media features implemented within the ES, such as messaging, tagging and blogging, and commercial social media tools for teleconferencing, such as NetMeeting, Skype and WebEx, which resided outside the ES.

Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on the rapid growth of social media ES, many users felt that the ES environment at MaxCom was increasingly moving from being transaction and process-focused to being more interaction-oriented and people-centred. They also commented that social media capabilities such as messaging, Skype and NetMeeting, brought them closer together. This shift was noted by the Manager of Capability Improvement:

So this 360 view is enabled by this platform [ES integrated with social media] where the various ES users are interacting together in achieving our goals of really becoming a solution- driven company.

The Head of Sell Process Excellence summed up collective completion of tasks within the division as follows:

The target is only to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a task - the nature of process is to do that, to work together and collaborate with each other.

## Emerging themes

Three main themes emerged from our analysis of ES user interactions at MaxCom. The <sup>fi</sup>rst, ‘virtual co-presence’. relates to the availability of presence in interactions between geographically dispersed ES users, supported by social media. The second, ‘focused interactions in ES’, re<sup>fl</sup>ects the way in which ES users engage with each other using wikis, tags, blogs, messages, Skype and NetMeeting in collective task completion. The third, ‘interaction order in ES’, is concerned with how users’ repeated interactions in the collective completion of tasks form distinct patterns of interaction. For analytical purposes, we will deal with each theme separately; however, in practice, they operate together to shape users’ interactions and the collective completion of tasks pertaining to the various business processes in ES. A summary of the themes is presented in Table 3.

## VIRTUAL CO-PRESENCE IN ES

Users interviewed for this study were based in three different locations of MaxCom (in Finland, Germany and UK) but worked together to coordinate work and devise solutions for their customers. Knowing of each other’s presence while working in the ES was a key factor in these interactions. Meeting and messaging capability, integrated with ES, allowed users to <sup>fi</sup>nd out who was available for interaction and to make themselves available online (which we term virtual co-presence). The role of digital media in supporting interactions was made clear by one of the analysts we interviewed, who used the sales work<sup>fl</sup>ow application in the completion of sales contracts:

Well of course we have face-to-face meetings but because we are working as geographicallydispersed teams it’s the NetMeeting plus phone, where we work on the same documents and use chat capabilities to discuss and exchange information.

It is clear that messaging, NetMeeting and Skype functionalities emerged to <sup>fi</sup>ll gaps in MaxCom’s standard ES for facilitating user interaction. The importance of the fast feedback and response times enabled by these tools was highlighted by the Head of Capability Design, who commented:

This informal network . . . this connection is strong. The people who know it know that it’s fast and have supported each other in the past. You think [expect] to get the answers pretty fast.

Our analysis also indicates that the online relationships formed from repeated interactions that go beyond the speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts of those interactions and that users formed mental notes of who was in their network and their areas of expertise. For example, a number of expert users formed their own networks or clusters within the ES through continuous use of digital media. The Head of Capability Design re<sup>fl</sup>ected on the value of tapping into such emerging online networks:

Our analysis suggests that interactions in digitally mediated situations for completing tasks in ES helped to form relationships between the users, characterized by routine contact and shared expectations of each other (Konrad, 2006). As Goffman (1981) notes, through these recursive interactions, users positioned themselves in co-present interactions so as to be available for subsequent interactions. For instance, a customer relations specialist noted:

This comment suggests that users were taking on roles and <sup>fi</sup>nding their place in co-present interactions or settling into an ‘interaction order’ in the completion of tasks. Our analysis suggests that these interactions allowed users to develop a sense of ‘virtual co-presence’, which formed a unifying concept for user interactions. A process analyst highlighted how they saw themselves as part of a digital-mediated environment and as ‘present’ in this shared digital space:

These interactions where you communicate within your network make everybody in that network more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient because they can always rely on the expertise of each other.

The importance of users’ sense of presence for interaction in the coordination of tasks was one of the key issues to emerge from our study. Our analysis suggests that virtual co-presence not only provided users at MaxCom with an opportunity to connect with one another in real (shared) time – for example, to complete the order ful<sup>fi</sup>lment process – but also allowed them to tap into the expertise of others who were not physically present in the same location in order to complete tasks. One of the key <sup>fi</sup>ndings was therefore that virtual co-presence provided the conditions for user interaction in the ES.

## FOCUSED INTERACTIONS IN ES

Another key <sup>fi</sup>nding was the way in which digitally mediated situations created roles for users and raised mutual expectations in interactions. This evidences, but also extends, Goffman’s conceptualization of physically present situations to digitally enabled contexts for user interaction. For example, a senior specialist in Business Excellence commented on the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of solutions at MaxCom:

Together we specify a document or create the <sup>fi</sup>rst concept drawing of what our ideas look like or whatever. So I just think this sharing of information via Intranet, via enterprise documen management system or sending documents and working together is collaboration.

These roles con<sup>fi</sup>gured focused interactions in the collective completion of tasks not only in terms of day-to-day activities, where ES users might have been in physical proximity to one another but also in ad hoc, quick response, exceptional situations where presence was strictly virtual. The Head of Business Quality Excellence describes his approach to form task-oriented networks of people connected through digital media:

Who do I need to get online to get somewhere with this topic? So then I think okay, this guy, this guy, not those, but this guy and that guy and then we create this kind of an ad hoc group [on line] and discuss it.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support Meyrowitz’s extension of Goffman and provide evidence that the medi ated situation described earlier is a ‘pattern of access to information’ (Meyrowitz, 1985, p.37). They also support our claim that an ‘interaction order’ or an ‘interaction pattern’ develops tem porally and spatially in the use context of ES (Gibson, 2005, p.1561).

In summary, our analysis seems to indicate that virtual co-presence of ES users invoked focused user interactions at MaxCom, which in turn created the necessary conditions for collec tive activity.

## INTERACTION PATTERN IN ES

Our analysis reveals how patterns of focused interactions form an interaction order. Through focused interactions, users settle into roles, such as that of subject matter expert and are often then perceived as being available for future requests and interactions. Goffman (1983) refers to this as the ‘available and willing expert in the network’. This form of interaction order often emerges spontaneously to complete tasks and ad hoc activity as suggested by the Head of Sell Process Excellence, who noted:

If you have a quick issue to <sup>fi</sup>x, then you don’t have too much to share but, on the other hand, it keeps that network up and running in [the] longer term. So next time you might have a planned thing, you have more time to discuss it with your expert counterpart

The ‘willing expert’, referred to by Goffman, was a recurring theme in our analysis, as illustrated by the following quotation from the Head of Capability Design:

I see senior experts taking time away from their burning tasks, being so open and receptive to questions from anybody in the network that they are willing to discuss something which is basically trivial for them.

User interaction played an important role in accomplishing planned activities where the requisite data or knowledge was dispersed among users. These users come together, both by chance and by choice, as required by the tasks or projects and according to their role, objectives and targets. However, as the following comment from the Manager of Process Excellence suggests, the interaction order that emerged in these networks dissolved once the project was completed, leaving a ‘memory trace’ for future interactions.

So in this kind of network people may come and go, but the network is more or less there. Another [example] is when we need to work on some speci<sup>fi</sup>c topics, I just quickly call the guys and agree on tasks, and then there is this more intensive period of work and the network is for that purpose only, and then it dissolves.

We can infer from this that individuals’ focused interactions result in ‘coordination of action, whether in support of collaborative tasks or as a means of accommodating closely adjacent ones’ (Goffman, 1983, p.3). We further suggest that individuals connected through process chains in use contexts (e.g. sales order ful<sup>fi</sup>lment) can be thought of as engaging in a collective activity (Ellis et al., 2005), and that this kind of mediated encounter (Rettie, 2009) is sustainable over time through the formation of interaction orders between ES users. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is illustrated by a quotation from a sales analyst at MaxCom who discusses the emergence of collab orative structures and how they were maintained over time within the organization:

What I see is people supporting each other, an unspoken agreement that we help each other. That is a thrust. It’s probably coming from the fact that you have worked at some point together. [. . .] These connections can lay dormant for years, and then there are people I know in totally different contexts, then we all happen to commit to work together. So I see that, okay, the network is alive, and I guess this sort of ad hoc dynamic networks are spun off from this most stable overall network. In fact, you have the overall stable network of people who know each other.

Our data suggests that implicit commitments in focused user interactions foster the formation of relationships that in turn enable collective activity. These relationships and connections may ‘lay dormant for years’ suggesting an underlying potential for ‘ongoing collective activity’, on the basis of the activation of informal connections through intermittent co-presence when needed (Urry, 2004).

As discussed previously, the three interconnected themes of virtual co-presence, focused interactions and interaction order help provide an explanation of the effect of the emerging digitally mediated sense of presence on ES users’ interactions and their collective task comple tion. The use of social media technologies can therefore be seen as allowing an intertwining of human and material agency (Pickering, 1995), giving rise to situations of virtual co-presence of ES users and the establishment of social ties with physically absent others.

## Conceptualizing virtual co-present interactions

Our analysis also suggests three different types of virtual co-present interactions in ES: impromptu or informal, planned and ongoing. Impromptu or informal refers to the spontaneous and dynamic gathering of actors based on prior knowledge of their respective roles. This expertise network spins off from the more formal structures for ongoing collective activity. Individuals will disperse and reassemble for ad hoc problem-solving in shared time, as needed. Planned refers to the completion of planned activities where ES user interactions are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to a particular project. In this context, task owners who control processes based on their own priorities assimilate information from different modules of the ES. This type of interaction order lasts only for the duration of the planned event. Ongoing refers to focused and repetitive interactions of ES users in shared time to complete business processes. In this situation, agents (interactants) retain certain roles for themselves through recursive interactions. This is a collaborative structure that endures over time. ## DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

Our study offers two key contributions: (a) to offer ‘virtual co-presence’ as a means of under standing digitally mediated sense of presence of ES users and (b) to demonstrate how the virtual co-presence and relationships afforded by social media technologies shape ES users interactions and their collective task completion in dispersed work contexts.

Virtual co-present encounters, as shown in our analysis, occur in three speci<sup>fi</sup>c use contexts of ES. The <sup>fi</sup>rst involves ES users sharing their knowledge with one another to solve immediate problems in shared (real) time. Such ad hoc, focused virtual interactions in ES re<sup>fl</sup>ect the situation-like (Goffman, 1979) and ‘immediate response’ scenarios described for physical workplace encounters. The second use context of collective activity occurs on a more frequent basis. ES users engage in focused interactions with one another to share information and coordinate the completion of interdependent tasks (Thompson, 1967) within their use contexts. Prior research supports this <sup>fi</sup>nding, for example, by showing how users cooperate to maintain focused interactions in situations mediated by mobile technologies (Rettie, 2009). The third use context of ongoing collective activity arises from mediated interactions in ES use contexts that sustain over time through implicit commitments and relationships.

We found that virtual co-presence of ES users provides important conditions for user interaction in all three use contexts and that the sharing of information and knowledge relating to mutual activities enables focused interactions. For example, at MaxCom, these interactions affected collective task completion in con<sup>fi</sup>guring solutions and managing sales processes. We believe that the emergence of new social media communication capabilities and the need for faster processing and shorter response times moved the process-centred ES into a more digitally mediated and people-centred digital platform.

By analysing the three themes in an integrated way (virtual co-presence, focused interaction and interaction order), we see the connections that allow us to conceptualize and propose a model explaining the role of virtual co-presence in user interactions and collective activity. ## Implications for theory and practice

Our conceptualization of co-presence-enabled collective activity extends Goffman’s theory of co-present encounters and collaboration to digitally mediated network interactions. In this respect, our study supports Rettie’s (2009) application of Goffman’s ideas to mediated encounters in mobile phone conversations. Further, our conceptualization of collective activity in the use contexts of ES highlights the role of actors and their knowledge relationships in interactions. Although previous studies have shed light on the extension of connections and interactions among ES users in daily work practices (e.g. Aalst et al., 2005), our research offers a more detailed account of the emergence of interpersonal connections through social media interactions and how these can enable collective completion of tasks within the use contexts of ES. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings offer a rich understanding of social media interactions and in<sup>fl</sup>uence on ES users sense of presence and their contribution to task completion and organizational responsiveness.

Our conceptualization of how virtual co-presence afforded by social media in ES use contexts shapes user interactions and collective task completion in a dispersed context provides a theoretical basis for further studies analysing the effects of social media interactions in ES use contexts.

Our analysis of the adoption of new social media communication capabilities as a ‘front-end to ES functionality also sheds some light on the mutual adaptation of IT and social systems (Wagner et al., 2010) and the workarounds users devise to deal with the challenges of incorporating ES packages into everyday work routines (c.f. Sia & Soh, 2007; Ignatiadis & Nandhakumar, 2009; Strong & Volkoff, 2010). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our research also suggest that virtual co-presence interactions help to form and reinforce an interaction order. Such routinization (Feldman & Pentland, 2003) of interactions serves to create a more sedimented interaction pat tern or structure (Giddens, 1984) in ES use contexts. Previous studies have claimed that the use of new ES leads to the development of new routines as ‘users learn to interact in new ways not only with the system, but with each other’ (Yamauchi & Swanson, 2010, p.190). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings offer some insight into the development of such interaction patterns in ES use contexts. They also show how recursive user interactions in digitally enabled ES networks have the potential to be re-enacted in the event of a similar use case, forming a new and, in some cases, identica interaction order.

We argue that these ES user interactions are motivated by the sharing of process-speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge, which can sustain interactions and enable collective activity. These interaction pat terns provide a context for future user interactions but are also malleable depending on user availability and speci<sup>fi</sup>c task privileges. We suggest that recursive ES user interactions give rise to sedimented interaction patterns that can be re-enacted in future.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also have several implications for practitioners and developers of ES software. First, by highlighting the role of ES user interactions in the collective completion of tasks, we make clear the need for a more user-centric model of ES task-based functionality. This would have the potential not only to improve the day-to-day ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of organizational units but also to engage knowledgeable participants across organizational boundaries in more meaningfu and effective collective work (Wenger, 1998; Robert et al., 2008; Yang & Chen, 2008). Second, we found that interaction afforded by social media provides the conditions for effective alterna tives to on-site working. This is a useful <sup>fi</sup>nding for managers and team members of geographically dispersed teams. Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings offer impetus for ES to incorporate presence capabilities for focused user interactions to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness of collective activity in an ES use context.

Our study may have few limitations. First, the empirical study was conducted over a period of 3 months. Although in this time we were able to gain an in-depth understanding of the role of virtual co-presence on collective activity in an ES environment, conducting the study over a lon ger period would have provided better insights into how the routine activities shape longer-term structures for ongoing collaboration at MaxCom. Second, our theorization is based on the <sup>fi</sup>nd ings from a single case study. The concern remains as to how well our theorization is general izable across other situations and cases of geographically dispersed interactions in an ES use context. That is, Walsham (2006, p.322) suggests that ‘generalizations can take the form of concepts, theories, speci<sup>fi</sup>c implications or rich insights’ from a single case study. Given the unique nature of dispersed user interactions, our conceptualization of these and the rich insights we gained at our case study organization, we argue that the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study provide useful theoretical concepts and understandings for related work in other organizational settings (Walsham, 1995; Klein & Myers, 1999; Lee & Baskerville, 2003). Third, although certain negative aspects of social media are generally acknowledged, a systematic analysis of these was not feasible within the scope of this study. Future research in this area would help improve our understanding of the drawbacks of social media use in corporate settings.

## CONCLUSION

This study investigated the impact of social media interactions on ES use contexts, in particular, their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on users’ sense of presence and on task completion. Its main research contributions are to introduce the idea of virtual co-presence as a means of understanding the digitally mediated presence of ES users, and to demonstrate how virtual co-presence and relationships afforded by social media shape user interactions and collective task completion in a dispersed context.

The paper is based on an in-depth <sup>fi</sup>eld study conducted at a large, global telecommunications organization, in which ES users from three different countries collectively execute tasks by integrating various social media capabilities into their ES. Drawing on its <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we argue that social and knowledge relationships and the virtual co-presence of ES users provided the necessary conditions for user interaction. We also found that focused user interactions helped to embed relationship ties between virtually co-present actors, forming an interaction order, and that the dynamic interplay of actors and interaction orders played a part in shaping subsequent focused interactions between actors. We also suggest that the sedimented interaction orders serve as a memory trace for recursive interactions of actors in virtual co-presence, with implications for collective task completion in the digitally mediated use contexts of ES.

We present our conceptualization of co-presence-enabled collective activity as an exten sion of Goffman's theory of co-present encounters and collaboration which we found to be applicable to digitally mediated network interactions in ES use contexts. The paper also offers some insights into the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of interactions and knowledge relationships on collective activity in ES to illustrate how digitally enabled social networks can be harnessed in organi zational settings.
