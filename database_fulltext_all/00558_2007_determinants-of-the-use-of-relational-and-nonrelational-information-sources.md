# Determinants of the Use of Relational and Nonrelational Information Sources

J. CHRISTOPHER ZIMMER, RAYMOND M. HENRY, AND BRIAN S. BUTLER

J. CHRISTOPHER ZIMMER is a Ph.D. candidate in the Department of Management at Clemson University. His research interests include information seeking, knowledge management, and human–computer interaction. His work has been presented at national and international conferences such as the Academy of Management and the Hawaiian International Conference on System Sciences.

RAYMOND M. HENRY is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Management at Clemson University. He holds a Ph.D. in Information Systems from the Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business at the University of Pittsburgh. His research interests include IT governance, project management, knowledge management, and human–computer interaction. His research has appeared or is forthcoming in leading academic journals, including Information Systems Research, Journal of the AIS, Communications of the ACM, and others. His work has also been presented at many national and international conferences.

BRIAN S. BUTLER is an Associate Professor of Business Administration in the Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business at the University of Pittsburgh. He received his Ph.D. in Information Systems from Carnegie Mellon University. Recent work by Dr. Butler has appeared in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, and Organization Science. His research interests include the development and modeling of online communities, social computing and Web 2.0, the interplay of power and information technology in organizations, health care and information systems, and techniques for managing and developing complex information systems to support reliable organizational and individual performance.

ABSTRACT: Although it has been argued that knowledge is an important organizational resource, little research has investigated where individuals go to search for information or knowledge. Prior work has investigated sources in isolation, but in an organizational setting, sources are encountered as an open portfolio instead of in isolation. It is important to understand how individuals perceive the wide array of sources available to them and how those perceptions affect their use of different types of sources. Building on prior work, this research looks at factors underlying the selection of sources that require direct interpersonal contact (relational sources) and those that do not (nonrelational sources) and explores factors that differentially affect the use of these types of sources. A sample of 204 working professionals recruited from graduate business studies was used to test hypotheses regarding the effects of accessibility and quality, as well as comparisons and trade-offs between relational and nonrelational sources. Consistent with prior work, source accessibility and quality significantly affect usage of a source. This relationship, however, is moderated by the type of source with accessibility having less effect on the use of relational sources. Furthermore, use of each type of source was also affected by the perceived accessibility and quality of alternative types of sources. Together these results highlight the importance of simultaneously considering the relational and nonrelational sources available to individuals. These results also have implications for the design and implementation of systems for managing information and knowledge assets.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information accessibility, information quality, information seeking, information sources, knowledge management.

IN MODERN ORGANIZATIONS EFFECTIVE USE OF information and knowledge resources is increasingly important. More and more often these resources are considered central to organizational success [58]. Investments in knowledge management are based on the assumption that an organization’s intellectual capital is valuable and that the ability to use these resources has beneficial outcomes for the organization [42]. One of the major challenges of managing information or knowledge within organizations is getting these resources where they are needed when they are needed [52]. Even the best information is of limited value if it is not accessible to those who need it and information resources are not assets if no one chooses to use them. How and where individuals choose to look for information and knowledge becomes an important consideration in the design and management of a firm’s systems and infrastructure. Although researchers have extensively examined the use of information, especially in decision making (e.g., [86]), information acquisition and the selection of information sources has received less attention.

The knowledge-based view of the firm postulates that the services provided by an organization are dependent upon how knowledge resources are combined and applied. Knowledge resides in any number of locations within an organization, including culturally embedded practices, documents, policies, and with individual employees [3, 39, 40, 70]. With a wide array of possible sources, understanding what leads to selection of one source over another must be considered in designing tools and technologies for managing, disseminating, and sharing these resources. While there is a long history of implementing and studying digital information sources, there is increasing recognition among both information systems (IS) scholars and practitioners that individu als are in fact selecting from among both relational and nonrelational sources [80]. When searching, an individual can choose to go to a person or they can choose to go to artifacts such as documents or Web sites. Relational sources are those where the information comes directly from a person, whereas nonrelational sources are those where there is no direct interpersonal contact [80]. As firms grow their investments in systems designed to support knowledge work, differences between these categories of sources have increasingly important implications for organizational strategies for managing information and knowledge resources.

An example of this issue can be found in the utilization of knowledge management systems. One of the goals of these systems is to codify knowledge so that it can be easily stored and retrieved. In the technical support domain, knowledge management systems have become commonplace [35], but despite the availability of such systems, their usage remains quite low [41]. Technical support centers are often contacted to give solutions to the same problems over and over again. A particular solution could easily be reused potentially hundreds of times if it were made available through a system, providing a huge cost savings to the organization. In order to achieve this benefit, however, individuals must choose to access the material available within the system.

Knowledge management systems are but one of a wide variety of information sources that include printed materials, coworkers, and a variety of electronic documents. The introduction of information technologies has changed the way that individuals look for information in several ways. Aside from the effect of knowledge management systems, the implementation of technologies such as the Internet, electronic mail, and other communication technologies has affected the ability of individuals to interact with one another, which in many situations is an important source of information. However, the presence of supporting technologies does not ensure the flow of information or knowledge throughout an organization [8]. How individuals evaluate and choose among the various sources that are available to them has important implications for the design of systems that deliver the information.

A great deal of attention has been paid to how individuals search for information on the Internet over the past several years [45, 89], but significantly less attention has been paid to what drives individuals to seek information from one source over another. While the focus has been squarely on technology-mediated information sources, other research shows that, in some contexts, information seekers prefer to consult other individuals [5, 46, 56, 75]. With a broad array of potential sources available to today’s knowledge worker, what drives source selection?

This paper explores the effects of two factors—accessibility and quality—on the use relational and nonrelational sources. It also looks at how individuals’ evaluation of the set of source types affects their choices about which sources to use.

## Background and Hypotheses

THERE ARE TWO BROAD CATEGORIES OF LEARNING BEHAVIORS—learning directly from the environment and learning from the experiences of others [60]. When individuals choose to access an external source, they are attempting to learn through the experiences of others. Regardless of what type of source is sought, the decision to draw on others experience has been made. It is important to consider how and why individuals in organizations search for knowledge or information.

## Why Do Individuals Search?

An organization is a complex information-processing entity driven by human interactions. Inherent in human interactions is a certain level of ambiguity, and the information-processing systems need to cope with this ambiguity [22]. The nature of this ambiguity has been looked at in terms of uncertainty and equivocality [20, 21, 88].

Uncertainty is the absence of information [51, 67, 82]. When an individual realizes there is a discrepancy between the information needed and the amount of information available, this triggers the learning cycle. The decision to learn via others’ experience is made and the individual then attempts to access a source to eliminate the difference. Within organizations, multiple views about a situation can exist, which leads to disagreements and a lack of understanding. As a result, individuals may realize they need information, but they are unsure about the nature of the problem domain or what questions actually need to be answered. This is an issue of equivocality, where there are multiple interpretations of available information. Before information can be gathered, the proper framing of the issue has to be determined. A certain level of agreement must be reached about the nature of the problem. This is the matter of equivocality, which has been shown to drive the richness of the media used [22].

Therefore, uncertainty leads to the gathering of information and equivocality leads to interactions to define the issue and resolve the disagreements about the issue [22]. This distinction is important because uncertainty affects what information is sought (source selection) and equivocality affects how that information is sought (media selection). This research focuses on uncertainty and not equivocality. Although the two are often intermingled, this research looks at the assessment of the source and not the media used to access that source. In uncertain situations, what drives the selection of information or knowledge sources?

## Types of Sources

There are many potential sources of information available for individuals to use. A study of sources used by chief executives for environmental scanning showed the use of a wide variety of sources ranging from customers to electronic information sources [12]. Similarly, Fulk [33] outlines many information sources used for competitive intelligence, including library, electronic, and interpersonal sources. An information source can be defined as any repository where information or knowledge is stored [13]. Some studies have specifically differentiated between knowledge and information seeking [42], but for this study, the two will be used somewhat interchangeably with the difference being characteristics of what is being sought.

While many categorizations of sources have been used in the literature [9, 42, 69], an important distinction can be made between relational and nonrelational sources [80]. Relational sources are those that require some form of contact with an individual, whereas nonrelational sources require no interpersonal contact. At the most basic level, the difference between these two categories is the difference between going to a person opposed to going to something else. In some instances of relational sources, there is an existing relationship between the seeker and source, but this is not a requirement (e.g., when a technical support line is called). Relational sources include coworkers, supervisors, or other individuals in or out of the organization. Nonrelational sources are those that do not require direct interaction with a specific individual. Although the differences between these two types have often been alluded to in prior work [14, 81], these two types of sources have not been examined relative to each other.

While IS researchers and practitioners have tended to focus on the artifacts of nonrelational information sources, individuals within organizations also make extensive use of relational sources [55]. Allen [5] found that professionals tended to contact other people for information rather than use printed materials. Other studies have found that people become critical sources of information because they can explain and argue about the importance of specific information and explain from where their knowledge originates [46]. A common concept in the knowledge management literature is that knowledge exists in the minds of organizational participants, is the cumulative result of experience, and is highly personal [23]. The acquisition of some material, therefore, may best be supported by the use of relational information sources [80]. If one of the goals of knowledge management is to replace higher-cost relational sources with lower-cost nonrelational sources [2, 3], understanding the use of each type of source is important in attaining that goal. This study addresses this question by exploring differences between relational and nonrelational sources and how various factors affect source selection when both types are available.

## Source Selection

Prior work has identified two basic factors that are important in source selection—accessibility and quality [5, 17, 72]. Accessibility has consistently been found to be the most important driver for source selection, with people consistently choosing lower-quality sources that are more accessible over higher-quality sources that are less accessible [17, 36, 72]. Quality is important, however, because when sources are equally accessible, individuals will consistently choose sources that are perceived to have higher-quality content [49, 72].

## Accessibility

Accessibility is the extent to which an individual perceives that any particular source is available for use. Accessibility has been shown to be the primary factor in the determination of source use, with research in organizational communication finding a positive relationship between perceived accessibility and the selection of a particular information source [17, 36, 72]. Culnan [17] found that accessibility was especially important in the formation of opinions about sources. Studies of interpersonal communication and information exchange have found that individuals will tend to seek information more frequently from those people who are closest to them in their social network [5]. In studies focusing on engineers’ information seeking, colleagues were viewed as easily accessible [16, 46, 59] so they tended to be the first sources used [48, 49].

The accessibility of a source can be viewed from several different perspectives. Most germane to IS research is the distinction between having accessible content and having access to a system that provides the content [64]. Early studies exploring accessibility focused largely on physical access to sources or the technology to use those sources [17, 36]. Physical access to technology was once a significant obstacle because of limited availability of these resources. While physical access to sources is not the barrier it once was because of the widespread availability of Internet access and the ubiquity of the PC, the broader view of accessibility not limited to physical accessibility is still important [93]. An important distinction that must be made is that access to technology is not the same as access to information. Accessible sources are not only those that can be reached but also those that are dependable and convenient. In this regard accessibility is perceptual and not physical.

Regardless of whether barriers to accessibility are physical or perceptual, the concept of source accessibility is important because of what is known about typical information-seeking behaviors. Instead of maximizing the potential gain from using information, individuals tend to minimize the cost, and potential loss, associated with using information, which has been characterized by the effort expended to gain access to a piece of information [36]. In other words, individuals tend to use what they perceive to be the “cheapest” sources among the entire set of possible sources. Therefore, we expect:

Hypothesis 1: The perceived accessibility of an information source is positively related to use of that source.

## Quality

Information quality represents another of the underlying factors of information source selection, and is typically comprised of several dimensions [61, 65, 92]. Quality can refer to either the information retrieved or the system providing the information [24]. For the purposes of this work, we focus solely on the quality of the information retrieved. High quality suggests that information is directly useful for the specific task without the need for clarification or refinement. Sources that provide information that is understandable, reliable, and useful are expected to be used more often than sources that provide lower-quality information [72]. Further, in the decision-making process, managers place more trust in high-quality information [66, 68]. Even though this relationship makes intuitive sense, it has not always proven to be the case in previous research. In his seminal study of information use in organizations, Allen [5] found that individuals did not necessarily choose sources with the highest-quality information, because of accessibility concerns. However, quality did play a role in the amount of trust placed in the source once it was used [5]. Given equal accessibility, individuals, however, will choose the source with higher perceived quality.

Information quality has also been shown to be important for IS use [37, 91]. Poston and Speier [78] demonstrate that credibility clues in a knowledge management system differentially affect the use of and decisions resulting from use of a knowledge management system. Source quality affects the persuasive effect the knowledge has on decision making. This finding holds regardless of whether using a knowledge management system or accessing a knowledge broker such as a consultant [57].

Because individuals seek information to reduce uncertainty, high-quality information best reduces that uncertainty. Also, because high-quality information requires minimal clarification, it has lower costs associated with it. Therefore, we expect:

Hypothesis 2: The perceived quality of an information source is positively related to the use of that source.

## Effect of Source Type

Prior work has not investigated the effect of source type on use, and, in particular, how the type of source affects the relationship between quality, accessibility, and use. Individuals attempt to minimize the effort or cost of accessing information [6, 36]. Studies of information-seeking patterns suggest within organizations there is a preference for going to other people [6, 36, 55]. This finding has been borne out in subsequent studies across a variety of organizational contexts [46, 49, 56, 75]. These preferences for relational sources suggest lower perceived costs for accessing these sources [1, 6, 25, 50]. With prior work indicating preference for relational sources [5, 53, 55], the mechanism driving this preference may be based on differences in perceptions of accessibility. This suggests that the relationship between accessibility and use is affected by the type of source. This leads to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: The relationship between perceived source accessibility and source use will be weaker for relational sources than for nonrelational sources.

Because high-quality information is useful for the specific task an individual needs to perform, both relational and nonrelational sources can potentially qualify as highquality sources. Individuals who have worked in a particular milieu develop a “feel” for the information they need and, to a certain degree, compensate for information shortcomings when dealing with relational sources. When this information is stored in a nonrelational system, the indicators that allow individuals to “read between the lines” is not preserved [29]. Further, relational sources can selectively present their content and tailor the information provided to meet the seeker’s needs. If the provider is considered an authority [90], then the seeker is more likely to view the information as high quality [79]. When getting information from others, individuals pay more attention to quality than when they access nonrelational sources [47]. While quality is important for either type of source, we expect that the relationship between quality and use will be stronger for relational sources, which suggests the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4: The relationship between perceived source quality and source use will be weaker for nonrelational sources than for relational sources.

## Comparisons Between Relational and Nonrelational Sources

The above hypotheses suggest that individuals evaluate an information source based on the perceived accessibility and quality of that source. However, sources do not exist in isolation and prior research has found that individuals seek to minimize the cognitive costs of searching for information, often using the first piece of information that begins to satisfy their needs. For example, in experimental search conditions, participants only asked for a second price quote when searching for online goods about one-third of the time [10]. This is consistent with other work that found that individuals satisfice in their information gathering [15, 26, 43, 83].

Individuals make trade-offs in accessing information along time dimensions. The tendency is to minimize search costs, and even with modern technology lowering the search costs for both relational and nonrelational sources, these cannot be eliminated. Experimental data have demonstrated that people are sensitive to increased search times even when delays are measured in seconds [31, 32, 34]. While these studies were conducted by varying search times on the same type of source, they suggest that costs have a significant effect on an individual’s source selection choices. Evaluation of different types of sources is not likely to be independent. While the perceived quality and accessibility of each source is expected to be important, the perceived characteristics of competing types of sources are also likely to be an important consideration. Hence, it may not be enough to look at relational and nonrelational sources by themselves. There may be trade-offs being made based on the relative evaluation of the source types. People using relational sources may do so not only because the relational sources available are perceived as being highly accessible or high quality but also because the nonrelational sources available to them are seen as substantially less accessible or lower quality. This suggests the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 5: The assessment of the competing type of information source (relational or nonrelational) is negatively associated with the use of that type of information source.

Hypothesis 5(a): The perceived quality of nonrelational sources will be negatively related to relational source use.

Hypothesis 5(b): The perceived accessibility of nonrelational sources will be negatively related to relational source use.

Hypothesis 5(c): The perceived quality of relational sources will be negatively related to nonrelational source use.

Hypothesis 5(d): The perceived accessibility of relational sources will be negatively related to nonrelational source use.

Task complexity was selected because prior research suggests that it can affect individuals’ choice of sources [72].

## Methodology

IN THIS SECTION, WE DISCUSS THE MEASUREMENT of accessibility, quality, source type and use; detail how the data was collected; and examine the reliability and validity of the measures.

## Measures

The items for all three constructs and the control variable are shown in the Appendix. The items for accessibility, quality, and task complexity were drawn from prior work; the measures of source use were developed for this study.

## Accessibility

Accessibility has been found to be a multidimensional construct [18, 19, 93]. Culnan [18] dealt with two types of accessibility—terminal and informational. Terminal accessibility deals with the physical ability to use a particular source. Informational access refers to the accessibility of the needed information once physical access to the system holding the information has been gained. While measures exist to specifically measure terminal accessibility [17], because of changes in the workplace such as the commoditization of the PC and the ubiquity of the Internet, these measures of accessibility were considered less appropriate for this study. Items for accessibility were adapted from the measure used by Zmud et al. [93] and included five adjective pairs on a seven-point semantic differential scale [74]. This scale captures a broad view of accessibility. These items are included in the Appendix.

## Quality

Like accessibility, quality has been described as a multidimensional construct [65, 72]. Quality can apply to both the system delivering the information and the information itself [24]. For this study, system quality was not assessed, and the focus was solely on informational quality. Traditionally, quality at the conceptual level has to do with truthfulness or excellence of the labeling of the information and its content [79]. The absence of a concise definition of quality is taken as evidence to the multidimensional nature of information quality. Prior work has identified the dimensions of understandability, reliability, and usefulness as the most relevant to measure information quality in relation to the source of the information [65, 84]. Items to measure quality were adapted from all three subscales (understandability, reliability, and usefulness) of the information quality inventory developed by McKinney et al. [65]. Originally there were three items per subscale, but only two were used in this study for two reasons. First, the item dropped was a general overarching question that was judged to be redundant in light of the other items retained. Second, because the six items used (two per subscale) were going to be used across all eight sources, dropping the redundant item reduced the total number of items (6 items × 8 sources versus 9 items × 8 sources). According to Dillman [27] aesthetics factor into the likelihood of completion of the survey. Like accessibility, quality was measured for each type of source. The items are included in the Appendix.

## Information Source Use

Eight different sources were chosen to represent a range of different sources individuals commonly use to access information in organizational settings. Four sources were identified as nonrelational—printed media, dynamic Internet, static Internet, and company intranets/knowledge bases—because these sources require no interaction with individuals. Four relational sources were also identified—supervisor, departmental coworkers, other coworkers, and others outside one’s organization. Definitions and examples of the sources are shown in Table 1. For each source, two measures for information use were developed. The first was a relative measure where respondents rated their use of each source on a seven-point Likert scale. The other scale of usage was a percentage of use measure where respondents filled in the percentage of time they used each of the eight sources. Use measures are provided in the Appendix.

## Sample Characteristics

A purposive sample of 204 working professionals was recruited. All participants were enrolled in an MBA program and were approached in class to complete the survey. Only individuals who were currently working full time in industry were asked to complete the survey. Respondents completed the survey during class time, but no additional incentives were given. None of the eligible respondents declined to participate. Respondents were instructed to answer the questions based on how they felt or behaved while at work. The survey included the items to measure source accessibility, quality, and use, as well as demographic items. Completion time for the survey averaged 15 minutes.

Survey respondents ranged in age from 21 to 53 with an average age of 30. The average work experience was 4.41 years (standard deviation [SD] = 4.48 years, N = 201). Some respondents had been in their current position for as little as one month, whereas others had up to 30 years of experience. Eighty participants (40.6 percent) self-reported their positions as technically oriented and 99 participants (50.3 percent) reported a managerial orientation. Last, 123 participants (60.3 percent) were male and 79 (39.1 percent) were female.

## Scale Validity and Reliability

To validate the measures, a factor analysis was conducted on all quality and accessibility measures. With eight distinct types of sources, eight distinct factors resulted, and all six quality items loaded cleanly onto each type of source. No cross-loading was evident, and all items loaded on expected factors with loadings in excess of 0.60.

To further validate the quality measures, a factor analysis of the quality scale was conducted for each source. When each source was analyzed separately, the multidimensional nature of information quality became apparent and three factors corresponding to understandability, reliability, and usefulness resulted. This suggests that the effect of source type on quality is stronger than the effects of the dimensions of quality, supporting the use of a unidimensional conceptualization of source quality in this study.

The factor analysis for the accessibility items also yielded eight factors, one for each source type. The factor analysis largely supported the hypothesized factor structure. Items loaded as expected except for one item for static Internet accessibility. This item did not load with the other four items (item loading of 0.0076). All other items loaded greater than 0.60 on their respective factors. Because only one item out of 40 did not load as expected, this item was retained and used despite the low loading for one source. All hypothesis tests that used accessibility were conducted twice—once where the low loading item was included and once where it was not included. These two analyses were virtually identical and did not affect the resulting conclusions.

The scales were also checked for reliability. For each type of source, the reliabilities (Cronbach’s alphas) of the quality measures ranged from 0.901 to 0.959. Accessibility measures for all eight information sources demonstrated acceptable reliabilities with scores ranging from 0.725 to 0.935 despite retaining the low loading item for static Internet. Table 2 contains each source type’s reliability for both quality and accessibility. All observed reliabilities exceed the recommended 0.70 level [71].

Last, the use measures were tested for reliability. The correlations between the perceived and percentage measures are shown in Table 3. The diagonal shows the correlation of each type of source to itself across the perceptual and percentage measures. The diagonal is higher than the off-diagonal elements, and suggests that each scale is measuring similar types of use. There are two reasons why these correlations range between 0.32 and 0.48. First, scale differences between the seven-point Likert and the percentage measure exist and account for some of the less than perfect correlation. Second, each set of items was trying to capture similar, but slightly different, perspectives on use. These results are borne out by the strong, but not perfect, correlations between perceptual and percentage use. Because the diagonal elements had the strongest correlation, each measure was then standardized and combined for a general use measure. The Cronbach’s alpha for this combined measure was 0.62, which is below the recommended 0.70 value, a result that may be attributable to the small number of (i.e., two) items. Because Cronbach’s alpha is sensitive to the number of items, we calculated two other measures of reliability that are not unduly influenced by the number of items. We calculated the average variance extracted (AVE) and the internal composite reliability (ICR), which are shown in Table 4. According to generally accepted guidelines, scales with AVEs in excess of 0.50 and ICRs over 0.70 can be considered reliable for research, and as shown in Table 4, all sources demonstrate adequate reliability when using these two measures across all study constructs [11, 30].

## Relational Versus Nonrelational Sources

The use, quality, and accessibility measures were factor analyzed to statistically confirm the categorization of sources as either relational or nonrelational. Two distinct factors were expected, one for nonrelational sources and the other for relational sources. To determine the number of factors, the Kaiser criterion, where factors are identified by having an eigenvalue greater than one [54], was used. Contrary to expectations, three factors emerged for all three constructs using the Kaiser criterion. For use and accessibility, printed media, static Internet pages, and dynamic Internet pages all loaded together; company intranets/knowledge bases, supervisors, and departmental coworkers loaded together; and the third factor was comprised of other coworkers not in one’s department and other people outside the organization. Roughly the same pattern appeared for quality, the only difference being knowledge bases loaded with other coworkers and others outside the organization instead of with supervisors and coworkers within the department. ## Hypothesis Testing

For H1–H4, each source was considered separately, which resulted in an N of 1,632 $( 2 0 4 \times 8 )$ . The means, standard deviations, and correlations between the variables analyzed for these hypotheses are shown in Table 8. These variables were created from the items by averaging the appropriate items for each participant on each source. For example, to create the quality measure for printed media, the six quality items for printed media were averaged for each participant. Creating the variables for this analysis resulted in eight quality, accessibility, and use measures for each participant.

To test the four hypotheses, the issue of treating within subjects as between subjects has to be addressed. Because each participant provided assessment for each of the eight types of sources, these responses may not be truly independent. To determine the effect of violating the regression independence assumption, we conducted a mixedlevel analysis computing the intraclass correlation (ICC1) [7]. The ICC1 measures the proportion of total variance accounted for by individual differences. For this data, the ICC1 was 0.02, indicating that only 2 percent of the variance is attributable to the clustering of individuals, a proportion that was deemed low enough that these observations could be treated as independent.<sup>2</sup>

To test H1 and H2, accessibility and quality were regressed against use. This yielded a significant regression model $( F _ { _ { ( 2 , 1 , 6 2 9 ) } } = 4 5 4 . 7 6 ,$ mean square error $( \mathrm { M S E } ) = 0 . 4 7 $ 8 $p < 0 . 0 0 0 1 \rangle$ ). These two variables accounted for 35.7 percent of the variance in use. Thus H1 and H2 are supported with quality and accessibility being positively related to use. These results are shown in Table 9 under the main effects model.

To test H3 and H4, another regression was run. This time in addition to quality and accessibility, the dichotomous source type (nonrelational versus relational) was included as well as two interaction terms (nonrelational × accessibility and nonrelational × quality). The interaction between source type and accessibility was significant, providing support for H3. The interaction between quality and source type was not significant; hence, H4 is not supported. These results are shown in Table 9 under the interaction model.

## Discussion and Implications

THIS STUDY EXAMINES PREDICTORS OF SOURCE USE. Although accessibility has long been shown to be the prominent driver of source selection, many of the studies dealing with accessibility were conducted prior to the explosion in information technologies that make nonrelational sources far more accessible [17, 18, 19, 72, 73]. Furthermore, while prior work has demonstrated the overall effect of accessibility and quality on source selection [72], this study was motivated by the increasing awareness among IS practitioners and scholars that the information and knowledge ecologies of organizations contain both relational and nonrelational sources—creating a need to understand the differences and trade-offs that occur between the two.

Even though nonrelational sources have become more accessible due to the ubiquity of the Internet, respondents to our survey continue to perceive relational sources as being more accessible $( t = 4 . 2 5 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ . This implies that efforts to justify the deployment of nonrelational information sources (such as corporate portals and document repositories) on the grounds that they “make information more accessible” must also take into account that, even with the increased technical accessibility, there can still exist a perception that relational sources are more accessible. This difference may arise from the simultaneous investment in technologies, such as e-mail and instant messaging, which also make relational sources more accessible. Alternatively, it may arise from underlying assumptions (justified or not) that individuals make about the nature of interpersonal and nonpersonal interaction.

One unexpected finding was that in terms of use, accessibility, and quality, knowledge bases factored as a relational source. One of the most widely accepted classifications of knowledge is Polanyi’s [76, 77] distinction between tacit and explicit knowledge where tacit is the less codified, experiential, difficult to transfer type of knowledge, which some refer to as “sticky” knowledge, whereas explicit knowledge is easily codified and transferred. Tacit knowledge is typically associated with relational sources whereas nonrelational sources are seen as appropriate for transferring explicit knowledge. Organizational knowledge repositories are one of the tools that many organizations are implementing in order to manage information and knowledge resources. The results of this research suggest that these sources may be seen more as relational than nonrelational sources. According to Grover and Davenport [44], organizations manage knowledge, information, and data while referring to the management of all three types as knowledge management. If the purpose of these systems is to provide tools for accessing the experiential knowledge often associated with relation sources, then these sources may be delivering on those expectations. In this way, knowledge-based systems may be able to act as “hybrid” sources that combine some aspects of both relational and nonrelational. Of course, there are many approaches to the development and implementation of these systems. While this research looked at this category of system as one homogeneous group, it may be that there are differences dependent on specific implementations. Future research should investigate the nature of hybrid sources—specifically, knowledge repositories—and how the different aspects of relational and nonrelational can be combined to enable more effective source use.

As has been shown in the past, the accessibility and quality of a source is related to use of that source [72, 73]. This study contributes to the literature by theorizing and empirically examining differential effects in these relationships based on source type. The lack of significance of the interaction between quality and source type indicates that quality has a similar effect on the source selection regardless of source type. Although prior work has found differences in quality based on electronic versus handwritten media [62], comparisons of relational to nonrelational sources has not previously been explored. The lack of an interaction is perhaps not surprising, because given equal access, individuals prefer high-quality sources. It could be that most of the respondents would rather do without information than knowingly use low-quality information. Further, a single source type can have both high- and lowquality information. For example, there are many reputable Web sites that provide accurate information while others provide information of questionable value. Future work can experimentally manipulate the quality of the information provided to see if individuals will use it or just do without.

On the other hand, the interaction of source type and accessibility was significant. Analysis of this interaction indicates that accessibility matters more for nonrelational sources than it does for relational sources. This difference may be especially important for organizations implementing tools for managing their information and knowledge resources. These results suggest that individuals may be more sensitive to the accessibility of nonrelational sources than they are to relational sources. As organizations invest in system-based tools, the accessibility of these nonrelational sources is likely to be a major factor in their selection and use. Conversely, for infrastructures designed to support use of relational sources, it may be the case that accessibility arguments will be less compelling. In either case, issues of accessibility and its prominence should be addressed in the design stages to help ensure system usage.

Based on finding the differential effects between relational and nonrelational sources, we also conducted a supplemental analysis looking at the differences across job types. These results can only be considered very preliminary because of the low N for each group (technical N = 80, managerial N = 99), but they indicate some potentially important differences. For nonrelational sources, quality and accessibility demonstrated a positive relationship with use for both managerially and technically oriented individuals. Interesting differences appeared for the competing type of quality and accessibility though. For the technically oriented individuals, quality of relational sources had a negative relationship with nonrelational source use. This suggests that, for these individuals, not only do nonrelational sources need to be high quality but the perception of the quality of the available relational sources available also has an effect. No such pattern was seen for the managerially oriented individuals. The competing accessibility did not display a significant relationship for either group.

For the relational sources, the results were the same for each job orientation. In this case, only quality demonstrated a relationship with use. Both the complementary and competing type of quality were significantly related to use. Accessibility, on the other hand, showed no relationship with use. Although the signs on the regression coefficients were in the expected direction, accessibility was not related to use. While accessibility matters less for relational sources, as evidenced by the results for the interaction presented in H3, accessibility was still positively related to use.

These post hoc analyses highlight some interesting distinctions between the two types of sources. In terms of relational sources, there does not appear to be a difference across job orientation. An individual’s use of relational sources is driven by perceptions of quality (of the source and the lack of quality in the competing source). The analysis of nonrelational demonstrates that there might be some differences due to career orientation in determining source use. The big difference is that a negative relationship with relational quality exists for the technical individuals while no such relationship exists for the managerially oriented individuals. These relationships need further investigation, but these post hoc analyses demonstrate that career orientation might affect the use of different types of sources. We expect that some of these results are due to lower power due to the smaller N for these subgroup analyses, but they suggest possible differences that should be more thoroughly explored in future research.

One of the most interesting findings of this research is the apparent trade-offs between different source types. While it is well established that people satisfice when accessing information [15, 26, 43, 83], and use suboptimal decision rules in selecting what they use [32], this study contributes by showing that comparisons between sources are important in understanding the sources that are used. Our analysis implies that people tend to take into account the characteristics of a range of relational and nonrelational sources when choosing sources. Hence, rather than focus on either social/relational information sources or nonrelational sources as has been the case in most prior studies of social networks and knowledge management systems use, scholars interested in individuals’ information/knowledge behaviors and the success (or failure) of systems to support them must consider and examine both the relational and nonrelational sources that are available.

An interesting extension for future research would be to investigate how the differential effects and trade-offs identified in this work affect individuals who choose to use multiple sources. Recent work has proposed that individuals use sources in a monophasic (single source at a time) or polyphasic (multiple sources at once) fashion [63]. The results of this study raise questions about how individuals might select and manage interaction with multiple sources. For example, when an individual uses multiple sources, do they tend to use one type of source or do they combine different types? When individuals use multiple types of sources, are they used simultaneously or do individuals work through them in sequence, perhaps in order of decreasing accessibility? While the current study takes a small step toward a better understanding of how individuals engage the full portfolio of information sources available within organizations, future research can build on it to better understand how the nature of source use unfolds in information-rich environments.

## Limitations

As with all studies, this work is subject to limitations. First, although some researchers have recommended against using students for business school research [38], in the case of this research, only students currently working in industry were included. By recruiting working professionals who were enrolled in graduate classes, a broad array of knowledge workers was sampled, increasing the generalizability of these findings beyond what would be possible with data collected from a single organization. Further, the verbal instructions they received, the instructions on the instrument, and the item wordings all referred to how they used information in the course of doing their job. The effects of memory lapses in recalling information in the past was minimized by limiting participants to those who were currently employed. This way people reporting how they were currently using information sources minimized memory effects. A possible selection bias may arise, however, from using information workers who have elected to seek out additional education. Future research should consider whether these people use information sources differently than individuals who do not seek out advanced degrees.

A second limitation is that the study only considers the effect of quality and accessibility. These two factors have been shown to play an important role in source selection, but they are certainly not the only factors that can be considered. Other factors leading to source use were beyond the scope of this study and are left for future research to investigate.

One such factor should be the nature of the task. As implemented in this work, we control for task complexity. More complex tasks require more, and often different, information. Information can serve to reduce complexity as the more an individual knows about a task, the easier it is to accomplish that task [87]. Complex tasks can act as a trigger to initiate the learning process in closing the gap between what is known and what needs to be known in order to perform effectively. While individuals satisfice in their information gathering and act in other rationalizing ways, in instances where individuals seek additional information, the task they are trying address could be an important determinant in the type of source used [82].

Finally, the argument can be made that the distinction between relational and nonrelational sources is more nuanced than the definitions put forth above. For example, online forums are categorized as dynamic Internet sources, but often individuals post messages to the entire forum looking for a certain individual to respond and not just anyone who participates in that forum. As another example, knowledge bases are technological attempts at codifying tacit knowledge. This is a marriage again of relational and nonrelational as put forth above. Hopefully future research can expand on these findings to look at how hybrid types of sources are perceived, selected, and used.

## Implications for Practice

Managers should be aware that individuals are more likely to view relational sources as more accessible, and that they are less affected by the relative accessibility of these sources. One response to this might be to make special effort to make nonrelational sources more accessible, or at least to change perceptions of the accessibility of nonrelational sources. However, despite the penetration of computerized nonrelational sources into the modern workplace and the increase in accessibility of nonrelational sources that came with it, people still are more sensitive to the effort it takes to access a nonrelational source. This is exactly where the idea of hybrid sources can have their greatest effect.

On the other hand, the differences in individuals’ perceptions of relational and nonrelational sources can also be leveraged by focusing investments on relational sources. For example, when individuals work together, communities of practice develop [8]. While these communities may not be easily managed, and can even be destroyed when the attempt is made to directly manage them [85], it is possible to invest in technologies and programs that facilitate the development of these communities. Developing these communities increases the amount of intellectual capital [4] that is available to individuals through relational and hybrid sources, allowing organizations to leverage their knowledge assets more effectively.

## Conclusion

TO BE SUCCESSFUL, ORGANIZATIONS MUST BE ABLE TO realize the value of corporate information and knowledge assets. Whether in the form of traditional documents stored in corporate libraries, digital repositories available over a network, or knowledgeable experts accessible through interpersonal contact, knowledge and information assets are valuable only when they are brought to bear on the problems and opportunities that arise. Toward this end, firms invest in technologies to facilitate individuals’ access to information and knowledge sources. Although these systems are often implemented and managed in isolation, individuals encounter them not as independent systems but as a portfolio of options they can select from. Hence, for managers and scholars interested in the use and effect of these systems, it is necessary to understand how individuals perceive different types of sources, and how those perceptions shape use. In spite of disciplinary and professional biases that tend to focus scholars and practitioners on either relational (organizational theorist and frontline managers) or nonrelational (writers and IS developers) sources, properly understanding individual perceptions, selection, and use of sources requires simultaneous consideration of these common alternatives. The results of this study show that, although they may treat them in subtly different ways, individuals’ perceptions and selection of relational and nonrelational sources are interrelated. In this way, the current study builds on prior work and moves in a direction that is increasingly important as we seek to understand and facilitate the interaction of technology investments and management of individual behavior in the information-rich context of modern organizations.
