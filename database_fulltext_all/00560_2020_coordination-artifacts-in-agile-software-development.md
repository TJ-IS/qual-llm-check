# Coordination artifacts in Agile Software Development

A R T I C L E I N F O

## A B S T R A C T

Agile Software Development is characterized by collaborative social interactions and fast-paced and iterative changes in project requirements. Much of the extant Agile development literature focuses on team and organizational aspects of Agile development projects. However, coordination mechanisms used within Agile projects have received less attention. Particularly, existing research lacks a discussion on how artifacts are applied in Agile software development to support project coordination, and how their potential for information richness and mutability impact their ability to support coordination.

To address these issues, we conducted a case study of an Agile development project that involved customer and vendor organizations. Our case follows the interactions between the two organizations, identifies the diferent types of artifacts that they utilized to coordinate their joint activities throughout the various phases of the project, and traces the enactment of these artifacts' informational potential.

Our study reveals that the organizations applied four diferent types of coordination artifacts - Foundational, Projective, Exposition and Indicative artifacts – to address diferent coordination challenges that arose throughout the project. The four types of artifacts were characterized by varying levels of information richness and mutability, which were shaped to support the Agile activities within which they were used.

Our study enriches the understanding of the role of coordination artifacts in Agile projects, provides insights on the informational potential of the artifacts, and highlights some of the pit falls than can result from misunderstanding object use.

## 1. Introduction

Agile Software Development refers to a collection of software development methods and practices based on the values of the Agile Manifesto (Beck et al., 2001). It has become the de-facto method of choice among developers and project managers, with a 2017 survey indicating that approximately 71% of organizations have adopted Agile methodologies to varying degrees (Project Management Institute, 2017). Against the backdrop of the dynamic and uncertain nature of the modern software development environment (Strode, Huf, Hope, & Link, 2012), Agile Software Development departs from the more traditional, plan-driven software development approaches (e.g., the Waterfall method - see Rovce. 1970) in several ways.

First, in Agile projects, the emphasis of development shifts from tools to humans, and from control to collaboration. Second, change is seen a positive force and excessive documentation is discouraged as a bureaucratic mechanism that stands in the way of a fast-paced and iterative development process (Cohn & Ford, 2003; Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001). Third, Agile values highlight the skills of individual developers and celebrate the craftsmanship of the software developer over managerial control (Cockburn & Highsmith, 2001). Fourth, Agile work requires extensive input from various stakeholders, including customers and system end-users (Cohn, 2004).

Taken together, these characteristics require greater involvement and coordination among the participating stakeholders (Cohn, 2004). For instance, the development team needs to engage with the client to collect system requirements. The client is required to coordinate its internal stakeholders (Schwaber, 2004) so that the information they provide to the developers is sufficiently detailed and clear (Cohn, 2004; Sutherland, Viktorov, Blount, & Puntikov, 2007). The need for coordination is particularly important in the absence of traditional top-down control processes and documentation (Kudaravalli, Faraj, & Johnson, 2017).

Coordination is defined as eforts to manage resources, relationships and task interdependencies among stakeholders (Malone & Crowston, 1990). Existing studies have shown that informal relationships (Ring & Van de Ven, 1994) and mutual adjustments promote coordination, which is especially relevant in the highly uncertain environments that characterize Agile projects (Sabherwal, 2003). While various elements of coordination in the context of Agile Software Development have been the subject of previous studies (e.g., see Scheerer, Hildenbrand, & Kude, 2014; Espinosa, Slaughter, Kraut, & Herbsleb, 2007; Kotlarsky, Van Fenema, & Willcocks, 2008; Kudaravalli et al., 2017), the tools of coordination - the artifacts that facilitate coordination and their informational properties - have received little attention in the literature (see Sharp, Robinson, & Petre, 2008; Strode et al., 2012).

We conceptualize artifacts as tangible objects that are created and used in practice to facilitate work activities (Gal, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2008). Coordination artifacts can be applied to bridge boundaries and convey mutual understandings between diferent parties (Bass, 2016; Carlile, 2002). Here we focus on coordination artifacts that are used by the project stakeholders to support their joint work (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009; Strode et al., 2012). Contracts, schedules, system models, and design blueprints are examples of coordination artifacts.

Coordination artifacts can be characterized by their informational potential - their ability to accommodate and convey information of diferent types, quantities, and quality (Sharp et al., 2008). While partly couched in the technological features of coordination artifacts, this potential is realized in situated practice and may therefore vary across organizational contexts and uses (Lee, 1994; Markus, 1994). To date, there have been only a handful of articles that discuss the use of coordination artifacts in an Agile development environment (e.g. Baskerville, Pries-Heje, & Madsen, 2011; Winkler, Huber, & Dibbern, 2014). However, these studies do not address the coordination challenges faced by parties that work within an Agile environment (Kudaravalli et al., 2017; Strode et al., 2012).

In this paper, we aim to contribute to existing knowledge by examining how coordination artifacts, with varying informationa potential, support coordination among stakeholders throughout the course of an Agile project. Our focus on coordination artifacts in Agile projects is important for two reasons. First, due to their volatility (Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001), Agile projects can present particularly acute coordination challenges. Therefore, an in-depth understanding of how artifacts can be used to support coordination in such settings can increase the likelihood of project success (Sabherwal, 2003). Second, against the backdrop of ongoing change during Agile development projects (Beck, 1999), artifacts are likely to be used flexibly (Lee, 2007). Examining how their use evolves over time can enhance the ability of practitioners to manage and deploy them (Winkler et al., 2014). These two reasons are all the more relevant given that Agile methods are becoming more prevalent in the contemporary software development landscape and applied in increasingly complex project environments (Conboy, 2009; Rizvi, Bagheri, & Gasevic, 2015).

To facilitate our inquiry, we aim to address the following research questions: (1) How are coordination artifacts used to support coordination throughout the lifespan of Agile software development projects? (2) How does their informational potential influence their capacity to facilitate coordination in Agile software development projects?

To address these questions, we conducted a qualitative, longitudinal case study of an Agile Software Development project. The project involved a customer organization that contracted a software development vendor to operate in a virtual and distributed project environment. Both organizations had a firm grasp of Agile development and familiarity with its methods. The customervendor relationship that unfolded during the project posed significant coordination challenges for both organizations. Our analysis revealed diferent types of artifacts that the organizations used throughout the project and traced how they facilitated coordination over time. Our findings contribute to a better understanding of the use of artifacts to support coordination during Agile Software Development and of the role of the artifacts' informational potential in this process. They also inform practice on how to establish and sustain efective coordination amidst the volatility of Agile projects.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Agile software development

The existing literature on Agile Software Development can be generally classified into two streams. The first consists of practitioner-oriented guidelines that prescribe how Agile principles should be implemented in organizations (e.g. Highsmith, 2002; Schwaber, 2004; Poppendieck & Poppendieck, 2003). Primarily centered on the behavioral aspects of Agile implementation, this literature is heavily based on the original Agile manifesto and its four core values: (1) individuals and interactions over processes and tools, (2) working software over comprehensive documentation, (3) customer collaboration over contract negotiations, and (4) responding to change over following a plan (Beck et al., 2001).

Within this stream, diferent proponents of the Agile approach have developed their own variants such as Scrum (Schwaber, 2004) or eXtreme Programming (Beck, 1999; Beck, 2000); each consists of practical guidelines on managing software development project, with an emphasis on the actions of the development team. Most variants assert that the project team should focus on the social aspects of the project to enable better collaboration with customers in order to produce flexible, working software (Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001).

The second stream of literature consists of academic studies that can be further classified into two categories. The first focuses on the adoption of Agile methods and their strategic implications and organizational value (Dingsøyr, Nerur, Balijepally, & Moe, 2012). Studies in this category typically seek to identify antecedents of Agile adoption by project teams and other organizational stakeholders (e.g. Boehm, 2002). These antecedents include support from the development team, customers, and senior management (Cohn & Ford, 2003; (Cao, Kannan, Xu, & Ramesh, 2009) as well as awareness of the diferences with traditional methods (Boehm, 2002). Research in this category also examines the relationship between the adoption of Agile methods and high-level organizationa outcomes such as enterprise or system agility (e.g. Lyytinen & Rose, 2006).

The second category centers on the efectiveness of Agile project teams. Factors that have been identified as contributing to team efectiveness include level of autonomy (e.g. Sharp & Robinson, 2004) and member diversity (e.g. MacCormack, Verganti, & Iansiti, 2001; Nerur & Balijepally, 2007). Other studies have looked at ways of enhancing communication and team within globally dis tributed teams (e.g. Korkala & Abrahamsson. 2007: Ramesh. Cao. Mohan, & Xu. 2006), and extended the concept of development teams to include external stakeholders, such as customers and project owners (e.g. Hoda, Noble, & Marshall, 2011). In addition, studies in this category have identified key elements that contribute to team collaboration, both in collocated and distributed settings. These include cultural compatibility, organizational support, customer involvement, team skills, technical skills of the team, Agile ideology, and trust (e.g. Alzoubi, Gill, & Al-Ani, 2016; Chow & Cao, 2008; Lee & Xia, 2010; Misra, Kumar, & Kumar, 2009; Wagner, Newell, Ramiller, & Enders, 2018). Trust, in particular, is one of the most frequently cited enablers of efective collaboration, especially in globally distributed development projects (McHugh, Conboy, & Lang, 2012)

The notion of collaboration bears a conceptual resemblance to the notion of coordination, which refers to “the act of working together” (Malone & Crowston, 1991, pp. 3). Although coordination of work across stakeholders is crucial to ensure project success (Cohn & Ford, 2003), the literature on coordination in Agile Software Development is scant (Hummel, 2014). Existing studies on coordination in software development tend to emphasize the behavioral aspects of software development (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009; Shim et al., 2002). However, the use of artifacts in facilitating coordination in Agile projects has received far less attention (Chow & Cao, 2008). This is surprising given the importance of artifacts in Agile System Development in general, and in ensuring efective coordination in particular (Misra et al., 2009).

To address the scarcity of research on coordination in Agile settings, we next discuss coordination theory (Malone & Crowston, 1994) and how it has been applied in the literature on Agile Software Development.

## 2.2. Coordination theory

Coordination can be understood as a process of managing interdependencies between stakeholders that work jointly (Malone & Crowston, 1990). It is conceptually similar to the notions of cooperation and collaboration, which are often referred to in the Agile literature (e.g. Sharp & Robinson, 2010). In this study, we follow Malone and Crowston's (1994) conceptualization of coordination, which distinguishes between cooperation, collaboration and coordination in the following way:

“cooperation usually implies shared goals among diferent actors…and collaboration often connotes peers working together on an intellectual endeavor. However, it is sometimes useful to consider all these terms as describing diferent approaches to managing dependencies among activities, that is, as diferent forms of coordination.”

(Malone & Crowston, 1994, pp.90).

Malone and Crowston (1994) distinguish among four types of coordination challenges. The first is the coordination of shared resources, which involves resource-allocation processes to regulate the use of limited resources by multiple actors. The second is the coordination of producer/consumer relationships, which is particularly important when the output of one actor's activity is used as an input to another actor's activity. The third is the coordination of simultaneity constraints, which involves managing divergent schedules, milestones, and process cycles that pertain to diferent actors. And the fourth is the coordination of tasks and subtasks. This entails breaking down large-scale processes into smaller tasks and their constituent subtasks, ensuring they are aligned, and allocating the required resources to them to help organizations achieve their strategic goals.

Several coordination mechanisms have been identified in the literature to help organizations address these challenges. One mechanism that can help address coordination challenges is establishing standards (Sabherwal, 2003). For example, project management contracts detail how, when, and to whom resources should be distributed to facilitate project coordination. Other standards, such as change control mechanisms and follow-up protocols, ensure resource availability (Kotlarsky et al., 2008; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). A second mechanism identified in the literature is formulating plans (Sabherwal, 2003) for the purpose of establishing consensus (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). For instance, artifacts such as project management plans, design specifications, or delivery schedules can alleviate coordination challenges by increasing inter-party transparency and predictability, and allowing the parties to increase their awareness of external fluctuations and requirements when planning and structuring their own processes (Strode et al., 2012). A third mechanism is formal mutual adjustments (Sabherwal. 2003). Periodic reporting meetings and established liaison roles. such as Product Owner role, introduce structure and transparency into the development process (Schwaber, 2004). Stakeholders in these roles can enhance coordination and translate information into forms that can clarify issues for all parties (Okhuysen & Bechky. 2009; Wagner et al., 2018). A fourth mechanism is informal mutual adjustments (Sabherwal, 2003). Informal relationships are especially important in knowledge work, such as software development, because they promote the successful sharing of tacit knowledge (Kudaravalli et al., 2017). In additional, informal relationships enable impromptu coordination between parties and reduce need for formal coordination mechanisms (Kotlarsky et al., 2008).

Notwithstanding their theoretical and practical implications, a number of scholars (see Gkeredakis, 2014) have noted that the coordination challenges and the mechanisms for ameliorating them may be too abstract and blunt to be applied in dynamic contemporary Agile projects. Consequently, several authors have sought to further develop them and proposed more refined coordination frameworks. For example, Doherty, Karamanis, and Luz (2012) took an activity-based view of coordination, which encompasses coordination mechanisms, awareness mechanisms, and communication patterns. Other accounts of activity-based coordination discussed coordination through prototypes and team formation (Seidel & O'Mahony, 2014). Coordination in prototype development, in particular, can be achieved through a cycle of the collective scrutiny of representations, which leads to linking representations to design constraints, and active editing of representations (Seidel & O'Mahony, 2014). These mechanisms are said to enhance cohesion in the understanding of goals and mitigate disunity in product development where teams use stories, metaphors and prototypes. Coordination can be also achieved by establishing team scafolds, which allow people to act collectively and establish boundedness, roles, and shared responsibility (Valentine & Edmondson, 2014). Others have observed that coordination in software development can be attained by introducing conventions and routines, which balance stability and flexibility (Dönmez. Grote. & Brusoni, 2016). In Agile development, both the process and the outcomes fluctuate between stability and flexibility, depending on the context and project phase. The Agile process enables flexibility and reinforces Agile practices by translating the ideology of Agile Manifesto into local practices (Wagner et al., 2018). Agile routines, stand-ups, and estimations can provide a means to address the need for both flexibility and stability (Dönmez et al., 2016). In some circumstances, resolving coordination challenges requires a consideration of the structure of the organization engaging in project work as diferent structures may require diferent coordination mechanisms (Strode et al., 2012). For instance, globally-distributed projects require diferent coordination mechanisms than those required in collocated development projects (see Espinosa et al., 2007; Strode et al., 2012). Hence, accounting for organizationa structure may help address coordination challenges more efectively and promote concerted inter-organizational action (Kotlarsk et al., 2008; Kudaravalli et al., 2017).

Several studies have examined coordination processes in software development projects settings. While they have discussed several coordination mechanisms, only a handful of them have considered the role of artifacts in facilitating coordination. For instance, Sabherwal (2003) described how coordination mechanisms may change over time and outlined the factors that cause the change during system development projects. Kudaravalli et al. (2017) examined how centralized and de-centralized coordination difer in Agile development and design work, and how tacit knowledge and team conflict impact work coordination. Cataldo, Wagstrom, Herbsleb, and Carley (2006) examined how a modification request tool and a chat tool are internally applied by a development team during a large software development project. They focused on the tools' fit and impact on eficiency, but did not discuss their features or informational potential or how they may evolve over time. Similarly, Espinosa et al. (2007) studied a globally-distributed software development project and described the tools that were applied to support coordination. However, the physical properties of the tools or how they might change during the development process were not discussed.

Doherty et al. (2012), Seidel and O'Mahony (2014) and Valentine and Edmondson (2014) discussed coordination mechanisms, but not in a software development context. While Dönmez et al. (2016) did examine coordination in Agile development projects through routines, the artifacts used to support these routines were left outside of their analysis. Strode et al. (2012) studied coordination practices and how they contributed to an overall coordination strategy in Agile development projects. However, their research did not examine the coordination mechanisms used by stakeholders. Finally, Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) looked at coordination artifacts but their analysis focused on the rationale for various applications of the artifacts rather than on the artifacts themselves.

In sum, past studies of coordination artifacts have centered on their application and implications, but did not focus on their characteristics and informational potential. A more detailed investigation of the characteristics of the artifacts is important because coordination artifacts are neither homogenous nor static: an understanding of their characteristics can help shed light on the ad: vantages and disadvantages of the diferent types of artifacts when used in various stages of software development (Mathiassen & Stage, 1992). It can also help explain the possible erosion of their usability in volatile environments (Subrahmanian et al., 2003).

As discussed earlier, Agile software development projects tend to be dynamic and uncertain because they involve multiple actors whose work unfolds through quick turnarounds and multiple non-linear design iterations (Beck, 1999). To support coordination in such settings, artifacts must be mutable and evolve with the project over its lifecycle. Moreover, the quantity and quality of information conveyed through the artifacts must be rich enough to support collective tasks as the project unfolds (Sharp et al., 2008). Consequently, in our study we focus on coordination artifacts' mutability and richness. As noted above, we treat mutability and richness as forms of informational potential that partly derives from the artifacts' technological features but is enacted in situated practice. We elaborate on this in the next section.

## 2.3. Richness and mutability as forms of informational potential

Teams in Agile software development can apply a variety of artifacts to coordinate diferent aspects of their work. These may include contracts and release plans (Bass, 2016), product backlog tools and user stories (Bass, 2016; Strode et al., 2012), technical documentation, testing documents, working software code (Bass, 2016), communication tools (Cataldo et al., 2006; Espinosa et al., 2007) and prototypes (Winkler et al., 2014)

These artifacts vary in their potential for information richness and mutability. The ability of an artifact to hold high-fidelity and detailed information is captured by the notion of information richness. This refers to the quantity, quality, and variability of information (Sharp et al., 2008) that can be contained in an artifact, which contribute to its ability to reduce ambiguity and bridge various frames of reference. For example, three-dimensional construction models can be said to be information rich because they can hold multiple layers of variable and accurate information to enable understanding and reduce communication barriers across groups of designers, engineers, and constructors (Gal, Blegind, & Lyytinen, 2014). Coordination artifacts such as decision support systems (Shim et al., 2002), prototypes (Gal et al., 2008), or enterprise social media (e.g. Von Krogh, 2012) have varving degrees of information richness because they incorporate diferent visual schemas from text, to graphs, to 3D images and videos.

While an artifact's potential for information richness is couched in its technological features (and the quantity, quality, and variability of information it can hold), this potential is realized when the artifact is used in practice within particular organizationa settings (Lee, 1994; Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997) or to support communicative actions of diferent genres (Yates & Orlikowski, 1992). For instance, a potentially information rich artifact, such as a video conferencing tool, would only support lean communication if its users did not have the time, skills, willingness, or opportunity to enable its video function and consequently only used the voice function (Lee, 1994). Conversely, a seemingly information lean artifact such as numeric documents can evoke layered and subjective realities when they are used by people with relevant background and experience, thereby supporting rich communication (Boland 1991).

An artifact's capacity for change is captured by the concept of information mutability, which denotes the extent of informationa variation that an artifact can accommodate after its initial creation. In design theory, mutability is seen as the evolutionary capability of an artifact, or its flexibility and adaptability, which allows for the incorporation of new feedback (Gregor & Jones, 2007). Immutable artifacts are static and cannot reflect variations in information that can occur as coordination needs change. Conversely, highly mutable artifacts are dynamic and can evolve quickly in response to changing coordination needs (Cataldo et al., 2006). For example, an email is mutable only until it is sent and loses mutability afterwards, but a wiki-page is highly mutable over time, allowing any changes by any users at any time. The mutability of artifacts is especially relevant for our investigation because the volatile environment of Agile projects might influence how efectively less mutable artifacts can be used to enhance coordination (Subrahmanian et al., 2003).

Like with information richness, while the potential for an artifact's information mutability can be traced back to its technologica features, this potential is enacted in situated practice. For example, a potentially mutable artifact, such as a Wikipedia article, can prove to be highly immutable if users refrain from editing it due to organizational or political pressures. Similarly, a potentially immutable artifact, such as a sent email, can be rendered mutable when its contents are recombined or incorporated into other texts in order to produce new information.

To examine the use of coordination artifacts and the realization of their informational potential in Agile projects, we conducted a case study that traced the coordination processes between two organizations that were involved in a software development project. In the next section, we elaborate on the context of our case study and describe our research methodology.

## 3. Research method

To carry out our research, we chose to apply the case research method for several reasons. First, case research is particularly appropriate for answer “how” questions (Walsham, 2006) and in our study, we are asking how coordination artifacts are used to support coordination and how their use evolves over the course of an Agile development project. Second, to examine how coordination mechanisms are applied to foster coordination between diferent stakeholders, an in-depth investigation of the underlying interactions between them is required (Eisenhardt, 1989). However, as Agile projects tend to unfold over an extended period of time and often in multiple locations, real-time data collection is not always feasible (Pan & Tan. 2011). Consequently, relving on the interpretation of our informants provides us with the means of accessing the phenomenon of interest (Klein & Myers, 1999).

## 3.1. Case background

The case selected for the study was an Agile Software Development project that involved a customer, AlphaInc (pseudonym) and a vendor, BravoTech (pseudonym). Both organizations are small to medium-sized Australian businesses. AlphaInc, located in Australia, is a mix of a systems development company and online retailer, while BravoTech is a technology-consulting firm with no physical ofice space. At the time of our research, AlphaInc had close to 90 employees of whom 20 were involved with our case project as internal stakeholders and user-testers. BravoTech assigned five people to work on the project: one manager and four software de velopment experts.

The primary aim of the project was to replace AlphaInc's legacy system for managing its online retail operation with an updated more flexible and technologically-advanced system. The legacy system was internally developed by AlphaInc and used within the organization. Every member of AlphaInc was considered an end-user of the system. In addition to being used internally, the system was marketed and ofered as a product to AlphaInc's clients. These clients provided AlphaInc with feedback on the system over the years. One of AlphaInc's objectives for the project was to ofer the new system to their corporate clients as an improved product and increase their market share.

The case project was selected because it constitutes a “crucial case" (Gerring, 2009, p.17) of Agile software development and coordination between diferent parties, defined as a case that is most likely to exhibit a given outcome. We considered the project a crucial case because both the organizations involved had extensive prior experience in Agile software development, and were therefore likely to be able to resolve coordination challenges successfully: AlphaInc's internal software development team - which consisted of team leads, software developers and operations managers - had transitioned to Agile methods several years prior to the project. The internal application of Agile methods had made them familiar throughout the organization. Moreover, BravoTech, a pure software consultancy, had a solid track record of project implementation and extensive experience in Agile Software Development.

Whereas most of AlphaInc's development team was located in AlphaInc's ofices, BravoTech was a fully virtual organization, distributed across Australia and Canada. The company had no ofice spaces; all its employees were remote workers. The ability to work from anywhere was one of BravoTech's key attractions, enticing for experienced software developers who took pride in their capabilities to handle complex Agile projects in an unconventional working environment.

The lack of physical offices meant that the Agile methods BravoTech teams applied were adapted to fit distributed, and virtuallycollaborative projects. These adaptations included the introduction of project managers and coaches as extra project personnel to address the heightened need for coordination, as well as the utilization of an extensive array of coordination artifacts

On BravoTech's side, the development team working on this project consisted of two programmers, user interface designers, user experience designers, and project management. AlphaInc's stakeholders included a Product Owner (a role defined according to the Scrum method (Schwaber, 2004), who was responsible for communicating with BravoTech, monitoring the overall progress of the project, and reporting on the development status to AlphaInc's internal stakeholders. These included the company's CTO, project sponsors who were responsible for the branding and marketing of the product, developers, administrative users of the old system, and system users and testers selected to participate in the project. However, AlphaInc's internal technical team, the team that conducted most of AlphaInc's internal software development, was only marginally involved with this project. Their role was limited to occasional support with testing and development environment setup.

Due to the distributed and virtual nature of BravoTech's team, the project heavily relied on communication tools and Agile meetings to achieve and sustain efective coordination. This unique setting makes the case especially appropriate for the intended purpose of our study.

## 3.2. Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were the main source of data (Mvers & Newman, 2007). In the interviews, we asked our informants about their role in their respective organizations and about their personal experience with software development and Agile methods We also asked them about the project, how it had unfolded over time, and the coordination challenges it had presented.

The interview data were supplemented by an extensive analysis of the artifacts applied by the stakeholders over the duration of the project (a similar approach to studying artifacts was taken in previous studies, e.g., Barrett & Oborn, 2010). We were allowed to view wireframes of the system during the early stages of the project and the actual system later on. We were also shown the various communication tools used by the stakeholders, their functionalities, and how they were used to support coordination.

We conducted two rounds of interviews. The first took place between April and June 2014, during the second half of the project's first phase, where the focus was the internal launch of the system. The second round took place during April and May 2015, at the beginning of the project's second phase, when the developed system was trialed by a few of AlphaInc's corporate clients

We had originally made contact with AlphaInc's product owner during the late project initiation phase, when the organizations were working on product specification and agreed that we could conduct interviews as soon as the project was past the initial, very busy, times and proceeding with a steady pace, to minimize disruption.

We conducted a total of 18 interviews across the two data-collection rounds. In the first round we conducted 12 interviews with seven AlphaInc informants and three from BravoTech. In the second round we conducted three additional interviews with AlphaInc informants. The informants were identified by chain referral sampling (see Biernacki & Waldorf, 1981) that started with our initia gatekeeper (AlphaInc's Product Owner). We adopted this sampling strategy because previous researchers have noted that it was “probably the only feasible means of identifying informants in case research” (Pan & Tan, 2011, pp. 167), and we did not have enough inside information in relation to the project to identify informants independently. The informants represented a mix of customer product-users and testers, project sponsors, developers and project managers (see \*indicates an informant who was interviewed twice in an interview round Table 1). Since the informants that we could access came primarily from AlphaInc, we ensured that our interviews with the BravoTech informants were particularly in-depth (i.e., interviews with BravoTech informants were on average 1.5 times longer). When we started conducting our interviews. BravoTech had just completed the first beta version of the system, which was released for internal testing in AlphaInc. This version was still lacking in functionality but the user interfaces had mostly been developed. During this time, the testing and development were ongoing and the parties were actively engaging with the system. This allowed us to gain insights from a wide variety of stakeholders. We finished the first round of interviews by presenting our initial findings to AlphaInc's Product Owner and CTO and re-interviewed these two key project stakeholders to understand where the project was headed next.

During the first round of interviews, some of the informants alluded to the possibility that some changes might occur after the first clients were brought on-board. Hence, we decided to follow up on the project and conducted three additional interviews with stakeholders from AlphaInc a few months after the initial release of the new system. We interviewed AlphaInc's product owner for the third time and he referred us to two additional interviewees, a development operations manager, responsible for technical aspects of the product and the development lead from AlphaInc's development team. Both had become more involved in the later stages of the project and were familiar with the events that took place during the corporate client release (see \*indicates an informant who was interviewed twice in an interview round Table 1 for a list of interviewees).

## 3.3. Data analysis

Data analysis was performed in tandem with data collection (Eisenhardt, 1989). From our review of the literature on Agile software development and coordination theory, we developed a theoretical lens consisting of a preliminary set of aggregate dimensions and second-order themes (see Gioia. Corley, & Hamilton. 2013) to serve as a “sensitizing device” (Klein & Mvers. 1999, p. 75) that would guide our data collection. The aggregate dimensions include the coordination challenges and mechanisms identified previously in the literature (e.g., Malone & Crowston, 1994; Sabherwal, 2003), while the second-order themes consist of specific instances of each dimension (e.g., coordinating shared resources, producer/consumer relationship, simultaneity constraints, and tasks and sub-tasks – the four coordination challenges identified by Malone and Crowston (1994). – are second-order themes of the “Coordination Challenge” dimension).

After conducting the first few interviews, we adopted a narrative strategy (see Langley, 1999) to help us make sense of the vast amount of data that we were collecting. The narrative strategy involved creating a coherent account (Tan, Pan, Lu, & Huang, 2015) of how the project was executed, documenting the major project events, stages and the transition between them, as well as the use of artifacts in facilitating coordination. The narrative strategy was especially useful in helping us understand the sequence of project events, identify the coordination mechanisms that were used throughout the project, and the Agile practices they were used to support. In addition, we adopted the visual mapping strategy (see Langley, 1999) to capture the project's stages and key stakeholders at each stage. The visual maps included timelines and Gantt charts that outlined project events and milestones as well as the coordination tools that were used during the project (See Appendix A). Both the narrative and the visual maps were verified repeatedly with multiple informants (and modified if needed) as data collection unfolded to ensure the validity of our interpretive understanding (Lee, 1991) of the project we were studying.

The data collected was then coded according to the dimensions and second-order themes of our theoretical lens. More specifically, if an emergent piece of data was closely aligned with an existing dimension or theme (e.g., data related to the four coordination challenges as identified by Malone & Crowston, 1994), the data was associated directly with the conceptual category (Gioia et al., 2013). On the other hand, if the emergent piece of data challenged an existing dimension or theme, or represented one that was not as yet captured by our theoretical lens, a conceptual label would be assigned to the data before the concept was incorporated into our theoretical lens to refine it (Pan & Tan. 2011). For example, we were able to identify four distinct types of coordination artifacts from our data (which we will elaborate upon in the subsequent section), but they have not been discussed previously in the existing literature. Consequently, we came up with a label for each type of coordination artifact and tried to relate them to the known coordination challenges and the typical development phases of an Agile proiect. The artifact types were then incorporated into our coding scheme (i.e., our theoretical lens) and coding was restarted (Gioia et al., 2013). The final version of our coding scheme after the various rounds of modifications is presented in Table 2. This process of iterating between data, analysis, and theory development continued until incremental learning was minimal because the additional data began to overlap and no longer provided us with new insights (Eisenhardt, 1989).

The first Initiation phase entailed negotiations between the parties prior to the commencement of product design and system development. The Discovery phase (the name is adopted from BravoTech's interviewees) centered on scope definition and initial requirements elicitation. During the Development phase, activities focused on system implementation across a number of iterations. In line with the objectives of our study, we describe the coordination challenges that were encountered during the lifespan of the project, the artifacts used to mitigate those challenges, and the outcomes of using those artifacts in each of the project phases.

## 4.1. Phase 1: initiation

The project began with an Initiation phase during which the organizations established the rules of their engagement. First, AlphaInc internally agreed that traditional approaches to software development, such as the waterfall method, were not suitable for this project and decided to take an Agile approach. This decision reflected AlphaInc's experience and expertise; the company had an in-house software development department, which had had extensive experience with Agile methods. A Project Sponsor from

## AlphaInc commented:

“Agile, we had already started using the methods as a business, probably two or three years ago. So it was a continuation of Agile projects we already had done. We didn't want to sit there, waiting for nine months for them to deliver an end product, we wanted something now.”

In addition to utilizing its own expertise, AlphaInc decided to engage an external vendor that was well practiced in Agile methods The chosen vendor, BravoTech, practiced the Scrum method (Schwaber, 2004), utilizing Scrum roles, such as “Scrum Master” and “Project Owner”, as well as holding daily Scrum meetings, weekly prioritization meetings, and retrospective meetings in their projects.

In the Initiation phase, AlphaInc and BravoTech settled on the aims of the project and negotiated an agreement to facilitate their attainment. With this agreement, the parties had to reconcile their difering priorities for the project, which posed a significant coordination challenge. Both organizations had their own goals for the project, and they had to incorporate them in a written format. A project sponsor from AlphaInc described her company's objective:

“So the end goal for the project is to create a globally accepted product and something that was going to really work for us at the moment and allow us to achieve our short-term goals for our existing clients but also something that could be expanded and moved into diferent clients”

Diferent to AlphaInc, BravoTech's focus was on delivering a quality product and ensuring customer satisfaction. A BravoTech project manager explained his team's mission:

“The number one goal of our organization is always client satisfaction. We live and breathe via our reputation. If the customer is happy with what we have delivered, then we live to fight another day. We will be able to continue to work on the product. And get them as referrals to other clients. So that's the primary goal is to make them happy”.

While the parties' priorities were not inherently contradictory or mutually exclusive, the tension between them became apparent due to their diferent resourcing needs and capabilities. For its part, AlphaInc had to consider the amount of resources it was willing to allocate to the project. Its limited resources had informed its decision to contract an external vendor. One of the AlphaInc's development team lead reflected on this decision:

“In all honesty, developing it outside was a very good choice. We wouldn't have had the resources to do it internally or in-house.”

BravoTech, on the other hand, was seeking to meet the needs of AlphaInc while minimizing its own costs. Its resourcing pressures intensified due to the fact that it was working on multiple projects simultaneously. BravoTech's project manager explained:

“I oversee all the diferent projects that we have on the go at one time. We typically have small teams of up to four developers on any project at a time. So we have two of our four projects running concurrently. AlphaInc is only one of our projects."

Adding to the pressure was the fact that BravoTech was operating under a four-day workweek with their customers, leaving one day per week for their employees' personal improvement projects.

These pressures challenged each party to adjust their allocated resources to a level that corresponded to the resources allocated by the other party (Malone & Crowston, 1994). That is, each party aimed to allocate just enough resources to sustain the project, which was dependent on the resources allocated by the other party. Both firms had to estimate their and each other's level of resourcing for the project, assess if the expected returns made the project worthwhile, and accordingly agree on the project's timeframe and deliverables. However, the future-focused outlook that this efort required was at odds with the Agile methods the parties applied, which discourage traditional project planning. The organizations therefore had to balance the flexibility of the Agile project with their resourcing pressures.

To manage the two coordination challenges of reconciling difering priorities and adjusting allocated resources two artifacts were created and emploved during the Initiation phase. The first was a text-based business case, which was devised by AlphaInc and detailed the project's functional and resourcing requirements, as well as a rough outline of the project's timeline and main milestones. AlphaInc's project sponsor explained:

“We had already a huge of list of things that we knew that people wanted in our current technology that we just couldn't deliver. We also had a dream, what does the world-class system look like and how do you make it. We spent a lot of time thinking about those things and the product owner and I just wrote a massive spreadsheet of eyerything, our dream features list... And based on this list we created the business case… We just had these ideas in a list somewhere. It that was either on Google Docs or somewhere in an Excel … In this business case we did put in a timeline and what we were going to take to the market. So first of all there would be a beta release, then we would go to some selected few private clients, and then release a minimum viable product to the public.”

The business case served two purposes: first, within AlphaInc it facilitated an internal assessment process of the project's benefits and costs. AlphaInc's product owner, CTO and project sponsors mentioned how the business case was used to justify project spending to the company's board of directors. Second, the requirements detailed in the document were used by BravoTech to develop their proposed solutions and price estimates. BravoTech's developer recalled the meeting where the business case was discussed:

“Initial requirements? I think the clients did handle most of that before we started, they wrote down the main functionalities… Loosely organized and prioritized it into phases in the project… We did get a presentation on the business case but it was pretty short. It might have been half an hour. maybe, I mean. [AlphaInc] had done so much thinking before we started.

A second artifact that was used to mitigate the two coordination challenges was a text-based contract that was signed by the parties during the first phase of the project. The purpose of the contract was to set broad parameters for the project's scope, delineate areas of responsibilities between the parties, define the product and service standards, and outline the terms of exchange to formalize the relationship between the two organizations. AlphaInc's CTO described how the contract was created:

“I wanted to keep it simple, one pager, so BravoTech's CEO and I had a little bit of a laugh about it. We thought that we should and try and get this contract onto one page. We got to three. But that's ok. I mean, you need the standard liability, indemnity clauses and other things like that, but the actual implementation part is simple: ‘this is how much we charge, this is the based on the scope of work that you wanted.’”

As the quote above illustrates, the contract was kept deliberately vague to allow for the flexibility inherent in Agile processes and the ambiguity of the project's goals. AlphaInc's product owner explained:

“In terms of the Service Level Agreement [in the contract], the only thing that we told [BravoTech], is to ensure that they do work the full four days. We have a certain amount of budget, which brings it to a certain timeframe with them, and they are fully aware of what tha timeframe is.”

Because of their broad scope and the fact that they set basic parameters to guide consequent activities between the parties, the business case and contract laid the foundations for the project. The use of the two coordination artifacts, which were both low in detail. required mutual trust from AlphaInc and BravoTech. Members of both organizations emphasized the importance of trust in enabling their joint work. The following example, provided by BravoTech's project manager, describes the trust involved in managing the financial relationships between the two parties:

“We like to say that the client can walk away anytime: They can walk away when they've got the software delivered because we are delivering on a day to day basis…. But in practice, they do pay for time in blocks. We will typically be paid upfront. It is a tricky business, it's one of the trickiest things as negotiating a new job with Agile means that the client needs to be very trusting in order to accept that you are not going to rip them of by getting paid upfront.”

The Initiation phase concluded after both parties signed the contract. This paved the way for the second phase, where the firms focus shifted to clarifying and refining the requirements of the system, which had only been loosely defined in the initial phase.

## 4.2. Phase 2: discovery

Having laid the contractual and general project scoping foundations in the first phase, AlphaInc and BravoTech's focus in the second phase turned to designing the product and fleshing out its visual and functional features. The main coordination challenge in this phase stemmed from the firms' diferent expectations of the design of the system, which came to the fore as discussions within and across both companies became more concrete and centered on specific design issues and outcomes. AlphaInc and BravoTech worked together to ensure that the designed product aligned with their expectations as well as with AlphaInc's overall vision of the project.

AlphaInc's design expectations stressed the business and financial aspects of the system and were expressed by various interna stakeholders. For instance, the company's product owner emphasized the need to design a scalable system for diferent users and markets:

“We want to bring the product to a global market. That is ultimately our endgame. We do believe that this product is not destined just for Australia. We do believe that our product fits into a really specific niche. It is a positive challenge.”

AlphaInc's development operations manager and development lead's design expectations focused on the future maintenance of the system:

“Most of the project has been turned over to BravoTech, they write the code. We have double-checked how it has been done, have they used the right technology, to make sure that it is going in a direction that we are happy with, and that we are able to manage and maintain it down the road.”

On the other hand, BravoTech's efort was rooted in the technical features of the design and quality of the code as drivers of system usability. BravoTech's project manager explained:

“We aim for functionality, and the most important functionality is the functionality people can see. Software is a bit like an iceberg where a lot of things happen in the background. Then the cap is visible to the user. The key is keeping things simple in the background to ensure usability.”

BravoTech's UX designer added:

“Our goal was to design it to be really easy and intuitive to use. To make it so that the target would use it, not necessarily what I would use. That's why I'm doing all the testing. I don't just go ‘okay I think this would be awesome’ and just go on a tangent.”

BravoTech's developer elaborated on the significance of good design as a driver of functionality:

“Well, just the general goal of writing good code and outperforming the expectations of the clients, while keeping the code simple… As technologists, we derive satisfaction from seeing our code proving useful. This happens much auicker with well-engineered code. Writing good code is both gratifying and eficient. You know what they say - build it right, build it once.”

The diferences in framing the nature and aims of the system created a tension between the parties' expectations of what the system would be and which work activities should be prioritized. AlphaInc's product owner reflected on the diferent perspectives that existed across the two organizations:

“How do you determine what to focus on? Everyone has an opinion about it. For a salesperson, their expectation is that the system can do everything and anything and I don't have to sell anything… From a developer's perspective it would be diferent, because they are thinking ‘I've got this code, the system is working and that's great.’ All these ideas, how do you mush them all into one so you've got this one deliverable project?”

Two artifacts were developed and used by the parties to mitigate the coordination challenge that stemmed from their diferent expectations. The first was a set of black-and-white prototype wireframes (visual skeletal representations of the website's layout) (see Fig. 1), that provided a high-level description of the system's structure, but did not contain a detailed representation of the end product. The visual and partially-functional representation of the system was meant to project a vision of the product in a more comprehensive way as compared to the mere list of requirements which were documented in the business case used in the Initiation phase.

The wireframes were developed by BravoTech's UX designer in consultation with AlphaInc, to capture the system's main features and functionalities, and present them in an easy-to-understand form to AlphaInc in order to solicit feedback prior to actual development. A product owner at AlphaInc described an initial meeting that was held prior to the creation of the wireframes:

“Basically, before we went of and started designing the wireframes, I sat down with the UX designer [at an initial meeting] and said ‘ok, these are the key features that we are looking at, this is what the platform is all about’… I gave her a vision presentation as well. What our future vision is.”

After this initial meeting, BravoTech's UX designer created the first version of the wireframes and tested it with a small group of AlphaInc testers. The feedback from this testing was then used by the UX designer to rework and improve the usability of the wireframes. One of AlphaInc's testers described one of the feedback-gathering sessions:

“I went out and sat with our product owner and [the UX designer]. It was a more basic system at that stage. My role was to pretend that I was a user and go through the system and they would ask me how I would do something… So it was really just me sitting there, using the program and saving. 'this is what I think is good. this is bad. this is. you know, this doesn't make sense. how do I do this."

The UX designer discussed the process of coordinating the work between herself, the visual designer and AlphaInc's testers to develop the wireframes:

“AlphaInc is using the old product internally, so we could use this as an advantage to get access to the user. We had a bunch of interviews with the users, and with my colleague [visual designer], we came up with this site structure, a wireframe that helped us to structure the whole thing and organize it. It started out as not so interactive, basically just a wireframe with the elements, but later on we added interaction to it, so it turned out something you call a medium fidelity prototype”.

Upon receiving the feedback from AlphaInc, BravoTech's developers conducted two iterative sprints to finalize the design of the wireframes. After each sprint, more feedback from AlphaInc was gathered and applied. By the completion of these two iterations, the wireframes were deemed to be clear enough by both parties, efectively mitigating the coordination challenge of this stage by serving as a diagrammatic plan (Sabherwal. 2003). BravoTech's UX designer elaborated on this process:

“We created the detailed version of the wireframe because we wanted to test it. So we took it to AlphaInc's users. So we had first-round interviews, where we listened to what they wanted and then we brought this mock-up back and said ‘ok, so this is what we have so far, let me know what you think’… We came up with some recommendations based on the tests, and AlphaInc decided which ones were going in.”

The second artifact that was developed and employed in the Discovery phase was visual designs that were created by BravoTech's visual designer and UX Designer (see Fig. 1). While the wireframes outlined the functional features of the end product, the visua designs projected a more realistic sense of the look and feel of the end product by presenting various combinations of fonts, colors and other visual elements. After BravoTech created the initial designs based on the first interviews with AlphaInc's testers, the testers and project sponsors had an opportunity to comment on the visual designs and request updates.

The designs were initially subject to some critique, which was addressed through inter-party feedback. For example, AlphaInc's project sponsors had very particular expectations from the system's visual look and feel and were adamant that this vision had to be captured in the visual designs. AlphaInc's project sponsor A explained:

“I had some real concerns about the design in the early days and I felt that it didn't have a personality, didn't have warmth. It felt really two-dimensional. I gave this feedback to our product owner and he gave that feedback to [BravoTech's] visual designer… in the end, we brought the visual designer back for another revision.”

AlphaInc's stakeholders had the final say on what they thought had to be included in the product and when the wireframes and visual designs represented the product well enough for the project to proceed. After the two main iterations, AlphaInc's product owner and the two project sponsors were satisfied with the state of the two artifacts. Their finalization also marked the beginning of the project's third phase – the Development phase.

## 4.3. Phase 3: Development

In the beginning of the Development phase, AlphaInc and BravoTech's emphasis shifted from the design to the implementation of the system. This phase also saw more stakeholders introduced to the project: BravoTech's team grew to include two additiona developers, both senior and skilled technical experts, while AlphaInc's team expanded to include close to twenty internal testers who had an active role in shaping the product during this phase's testing activities. The changed focus of the Development phase and the increased number of stakeholders introduced new coordination challenges for the parties.

One involved managing the producer/consumer relationship (Malone & Crowston. 1994). This challenge stemmed from having a diversity of stakeholders that had to work with each other to complete their own tasks while in a client-vendor relationship. Based on an initial design, BravoTech developed the system, which was then shared with AlphaInc. A strong working relationship characterized by efective and eficient interactions between the two parties was required subsequently: BravoTech needed to provide suficient materials to AlphaInc's testers and product owner to conduct testing and provide feedback on, while AlphaInc had to coordinate their testing eforts and ensure that the results were relayed back to BravoTech's development team. In addition, the coordination of tasks and sub-tasks (Malone & Crowston, 1994) became a prevalent issue as new information afecting both parties tasks was regularly introduced during the development process from users' changing needs and testing.

The parties utilized two artifacts to address these coordination challenges (see Fig. 2). The first was a backlog tool called Pivotal Tracker.<sup>1</sup> The backlog tool stored comprehensive descriptions of development tasks. Those included tasks originally outlined in the business case and tasks that emerged out of the testing of the wireframes. The stored tasks could have been completed, outstanding, or still attended to. As is typical in Agile projects, each task was framed as a user story in the backlog tool that described how a user would use a particular functionality and its consequent business benefits. The main users of the backlog tool were BravoTech's developers and designers and AlphaInc's product owner. BravoTech's developer discussed how he used the backlog tool in his work “I would mark the user story as ‘started’ in [the backlog tool], indicating that I am working on it… I would write the code and test for that…I would usually ping the other developer via [an instant messaging system] and say ‘hey check out this new code’ and then if he gives the thumbs up, or more likely will have some comments for that, ways to improve it, so I would go back and address those comments”.

![](/api/attachments/YGS3WV2W/fulltext/images/4ab4acce6b199def52159617320fbf53f5c38977181bc8462aff23258ed494d4.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Example screenshots of the instant messaging system, Flowdock (left), and the backlog tool, Pivotal Tracker (right)

The backlog tool allowed the developers to allocate the stories they were working on to themselves or to each other, thereby informing other team members that a requirement was being addressed. The user stories were also allocated to diferent sprints, ensuring that both BravoTech's development team and AlphaInc's managers were aware of the work that was planned for each week. Similarly, the results of the testing conducted by AlphaInc were conveyed to the developers via the backlog tool. BravoTech's project manager provided an illustration of how the backlog tool was utilized:

“If [AlphaInc] finds a bug, they will create a ticket, a bug ticket, in [the backlog tool]. And then that ticket will get assigned to somebody. Our Scrum Master will assign it, usually based on who was responsible for that piece of functionality. Then that developer will work through that bug. [AlphaInc] will get notified when the fix or functionality will be ready for acceptance testing. [AlphaInc] can log in and check the fix or feature, and they can also involve other [testers]. This communication process is not just for bugs but also feature changes [AlphaInc] can request new features in [the] backlog tool. We would develop that feature and then the customer can have a quick look at it and say if we hit the mark or not.”

While both firms were generally satisfied with the backlog tool, the tool could not accommodate all the information needs of BravoTech's developers and AlphaInc's stakeholders. At times, some information was unclear or the user stories failed to cover al aspects of a specific functionality. To fill this gap and enable ongoing communication, the parties used a second artifact, an instant messaging (IM) system called Flowdock.<sup>2</sup> A developer from BravoTech explained:

“Quite often, if I look up something in [the backlog tool], there will be some functionality or something but it does not have enough information in the description for me to understand what it is about… so I mean, I can just [try to work] with the information in there [but] it's just quicker to ask in… a direct message in [an instant messaging system]. Pretty much the only time when I use the commenting stuf in [the backlog tool] is when the client is ofline. Outside business hours”

The IM system was not only used for synchronous chatting, but it also served as an asynchronous repository of discussions that was accessible for the development team. In addition, the IM system was integrated with the backlog tool, and users could reference specific requirements in backlog tool via direct links in the IM system.

Supported by the backlog tool and IM system, joint work and development iterations continued in weekly cycles, each concluding with the release of a new version of the system by BravoTech, which captured customer feedback increasingly faithfully and accurately. Gradually, the emerging system surpassed the wireframes and visual designs developed in the Design phase in terms of the level of detail and sophistication, and became the main point of reference between the two parties. At the same time, the wireframes and designs receded into the background of project activities and were ultimately rendered obsolete over time. Project sponsor A from AlphaInc described this transition:

“So it went from the wireframes [and visual designs] to a sort of mock up [initial version of the system]. Then the guys were just building features and then from there, as soon as there was something to show us, we could dib in and dib out and test and have a look and see what is being developed.”

The high rate of iterations, however, introduced an additional coordination challenge for both parties, which stemmed from the tension between the dynamism of changing user requirements and the need for regular product releases. As stakeholders from both firms had to coordinate the concurrent activities of product development, testing, requirements elicitation, and product redesign across a number of iterative cycles, they were confronted with the challenge of balancing their respective simultaneity constraints (Malone & Crowston, 1994).

The primary artifact that served as a common reference point toward the mitigation of this challenge was the emergent system itself. The system, which was to be the end product of the project, was iteratively developed and launched in weekly releases throughout the Development phase. AlphaInc's project sponsor B described how testing was coordinated between the two organizations;

“The testing went on for couple of weeks, maybe a month…The product owner had set up a process where our testers gave their feedback on features they were testing or bugs they found... The product owner was in touch with [BrayoTech] and kept me aware of when the sprints were happening to keep the testing team updated. The product owner knew when new features were going to be launched [by BravoTech] so the communication between [BravoTech], the product owner, and the testing team was very open and regular.”

In particular, AlphaInc's testing results were communicated to BravoTech via the backlog tool and the IM system. But as BravoTech was working on the requested changes, testing continued on AlphaInc's end so that more bug fixes and required modifications may be uncovered. In other words, the emergent system had to provide indications of the work that is completed or stil

required (thereby facilitating the coordination of tasks and sub-tasks). But because development and testing are not enacted sequentially as with most Agile projects, and a new version of the system was being released every week, AlphaInc and BravoTech had to find a way to develop and test simultaneously without impeding each other's work. As such, the emergent system became a powerful synchronization tool because it was the focal point for the work of both parties: BravoTech was working to improve the system over its previous version each week, while AlphaInc's testers were conducting tests based on the emergent system. AlphaInc's tester C provided an illustration of how development and testing were unfolding concurrently, and the progression of the system over time:

“We launched the system to [our] business last Tuesday. Prior to the launch, I was there testing, testing, testing. Just making sure everything was ok. And I probably logged a hundred bugs and features in [the backlog tool]! So, the next steps are to launch to our new clients as of July so we will have our Beta version [referring to the following version from the one being tested at the time of the interview] ready for an external launch… Well, not all the bells and whistles but most the key features and things that a program needs in order to operate.”

## 5.1. How coordination artifacts alleviate coordination challenges

In the Initiation phase of the project, the primary coordination challenges involved reconciling difering priorities as both parties worked to set broad parameters for their engagement as well as for the system that was to be designed and developed. Such divergence in priorities is common to many software projects because the client is typically seeking to maximize system functionality and value, while the developer is aiming to minimize costs (Boehm, Grunbacher, & Briggs, 2001). In our case, this divergence also brought to the fore the challenge of adjusting allocated resources. Both AlphaInc and BravoTech had to ensure that the limited resources they were able to commit to the project were suficient to realize their priorities (see Westerveld, 2003).

To address these challenges, two artifacts – the business case and contract – were applied, We conceptualize them as Foundationgl artifacts, defined as objects that provide the basis for coordination in Agile Software Development upon which the project activities across the various phases are built. Both the business case and contract mitigated the challenge of managing difering priorities and shared resources because they facilitated the establishment of standards for mutual work and an environment of accountability and shared understanding (Okhuysen & Bechky. 2009). As such. the application of foundational artifacts allowed the two parties to negotiate a consensus around the framing of their priorities and resource commitments.

In other words, the foundational artifacts were used by AlphaInc and BravoTech to facilitate the alignment of priorities and resource commitments and arrive at a consensus on high-level requirements, project milestones, the assignment of responsibilities, as well as the allocation of resources. Although these coordination issues were most common during the Initiation phase, references to foundational documents could occur during later phases, One such case was when developers from BravoTech were invited to provide training for AlphaInc's testers during the Development phase. This spurred the parties to refer to the contract to ensure the invitation was consistent with the contract's provisions. And yet, once they have served their initial purpose of setting the broad parameters for the project, the foundational artifacts were not extensively referred to by the parties. Other artifacts. created later during the project, would supersede the foundational artifacts as the primary coordination aids in the subsequent phases, even as the foundational artifacts were retained to provide guidance on the overall direction of the project.

Following the Initiation phase, the project progressed to the Discovery phase. During this phase, the objective of developing an initial specification of the system's functionalities and interface took center stage. The negotiations surrounding what those should be highlighted the difering expectations (Gkeredakis, 2014) that both parties held in relation to the design of the system. This disparity formed the primary coordination challenge of this phase. On the one hand, AlphaInc's expectations centered on the business and financial outcomes of the system - i.e., its scalability and ease of maintenance. On the other hand, BravoTech's design expectations were based on their desire to bring to bear their knowledge of technology. Specifically, they sought to emphasize the simplicity and functionality of the code to ensure the usability of the system and good user experience.

To address this disparity and consolidate their varying expectations, AlphaInc and BravoTech used wireframes and visual designs. We conceptualize the wireframes and visual designs as Projective artifacts, which we define as objects that project a visual and functional sense of the end product before the commencement of software development. In particular, the projective artifacts allowed AlphaInc to flesh out their expectations in a clear, detailed, and visual fashion. While these artifacts were highly detailed, they also represented an abstraction and reduced unnecessary complexity by hiding layers of underlying technical requirements from the stakeholders (Mathiassen & Stage, 1992). BravoTech used the artifacts as a focal point to test AlphaInc's ideas, and discuss, clarify, and negotiate changes in order to define the scope of the project and derive a high-level system design (Gkeredakis, 2014). In other words. the process of negotiation that is required for the formalization of the design of the end product was enabled by the projective artifacts (see Balogun & Johnson, 2005), which facilitated the alignment of expectations between client and developer.

Finally, in the Development phase, three new coordination challenges arose as the work on the system to be delivered commenced. The first was the need for managing the producer/consumer relationship (Malone & Crowston, 1994) between AlphaInc and BravoTech, which was required in order to ensure the efectiveness of their joint work. A major source of tension in this relationship stemmed from AlphaInc's changing business requirements as the project progressed. As the system's development unfolded, the need for managing this tension intensified because a failure to meet AlphaInc's changing requirements could be deemed a service failure, while changes to the scope of the project at that point would typically demand additional resources on the part of BravoTech (Ibert, 2004). Consequently, to address this challenge, there was a need to establish and maintain efective communication channels throughout the multiple iterations of development and testing.

The communication channels used by both parties took the form of the backlog tool and the IM system to specify the required tasks, when they should be performed, and in what order. We refer to these obiects collectively as Exposition artifacts, which we define as artifacts that document and present detailed information on the tasks and progress of the system development process. By capturing this information, the exposition Artifacts enabled the optimization of resource allocation. Because by knowing precisely the tasks at hand and their completion statuses, both parties were able to make better decisions in relation to the manpower, time and costs to allocate to the tasks and ensure their timely completion (Kloppenborg, 2012).

A second challenge that emerged in this phase was centered on coordinating tasks and sub-tasks (Malone & Crowston, 1994), which became particularly pronounced during the third phase because of the continuous and iterative nature of Agile software development. In particular, the tensions between the project's volatility and the need for a stable product, as well as between the changing requirements and existing work breakdown, reinforced the need for the parties to coordinate their tasks.

Our case study revealed that the exposition artifacts can be used to alleviate these tensions. More specifically, they were used to specify tasks and clarify system features that were visually represented in the wireframes and visual designs used during the Discovery phase. Although on several occasions early in the development phase, the BravoTech developers did refer back to the projective artifacts for clarification of emerging requirements, the exposition artifacts were utilized as a formal, consultable record of all development tasks, and provided a means for users to elaborate on existing requirements and articulate new ones that have been omitted from the earlier phases. In addition, the exposition artifacts allowed developers to adapt their priorities and allocate their resources accordingly.

In addition, our case study suggests that Indicative artifacts can be applied in coniunction with the exposition artifacts to mitigate the coordination challenge of tasks and sub-tasks (Malone & Crowston, 1994). We define indicative artifacts visual, interactive, and evolved representations of the actual product, and they are termed as such because they consist of multiple layers of information that provide an indication of the evolving state of the ultimate deliverables of a project. The indicative artifact that manifested in our case study was in the form of the emergent system. More specifically, as a more elaborate and functional version of the previous visua designs and wireframes, the system contained visual elements reflecting AlphaInc's branding, multiple functionalities, and test data which represented the most comprehensive and up-to-date information on the status of the end product. Accordingly, this meant that the system was able to take the place of the projective artifacts of the previous phase because it could provide the clearest indication of what the end product would become in a way that no other artifact did. In relation to this specific coordination challenge, indicative artifacts can be applied to elicit discussion on which tasks and sub-tasks were required in order to complete the product.

With the exposition artifacts. AlphaInc had a straightforward means of conveving their requirements and desired changes, while BravoTech had access to a persistent log of all of AlphaInc's requests. In addition, the indicative artifact enabled the two parties to incorporate an image of the entire project in a joint scafold, and create a representation of their work, which was available for scrutiny to each organization (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009).

A third coordination challenge of the Development phase was related to balancing simultaneity constraints (Malone & Crowston, 1994), which stemmed from the tension between the Agile method's inherent dynamism and the requirement for regular and stable product releases (Dönmez et al., 2016). In our case study, this tension afected the sequencing of tasks and demanded a carefu examination of dependencies between them to allow both parties to manage the successive iterations of development and testing that characterize Agile Software Development (Cohn, 2004).

Our findings suggest that indicative artifacts may also hold the key to addressing this challenge. For instance, in our case study, the emergent system was simultaneously modified by BravoTech while AlphaInc's testers were working on each release to identify bugs and new requirements. This demonstrates that because indicative artifacts embody up-to-date and accurate information on the status of each product release, they can facilitate the alignment of development dynamics, allowing both parties to carry out the work associated with their distinct project roles without hindering the work of the other party.

## 5.2. The four types of coordination artifacts and their informational potential

Our preceding discussion has identified four types of coordination artifacts - foundational, projective exposition, and indicative - described when they were applied across the lifecycle of the project, and outlined the various coordination challenges that they were used to address. A closer examination of our findings shows that the features of the artifacts (i.e., the information media that they could support), combined with their situated use by project participants, resulted in them exhibiting varying levels of informationa potential, i.e., information richness and mutability (see Table 4), which influenced their capacity to facilitate coordination across the phases of the project. We discuss each type of coordination artifact in turn.

The use of Foundational artifacts in the first phase of the project was characterized by low information richness and mutability. Commensurate with the agile method, the contents of the text-based business case and contract were general and detail-poor to accommodate the inherent flexibility of project activities (Turk. France. & Rumpe. 2002: Conboy. Covle. Wang, & Pikkarainen. 2011). Such low level of detail is suficient because the vision of the project is intended to be vague at this early juncture, and if the information contained within the artifacts is too elaborate or specific, it may in fact curtail the extent to which the project can be modified subsequently. Also consistent with many Agile projects, the parties rarely referred to the foundational artifacts during the project or used them to underpin any substantive discussions around the progression of their design activities. Hence, the use of both documents was characterized by low information richness. The use of the documents was also characterized by low information mutability. The foundational artifacts served their initial purpose early in the project; they were mostly retired and remained unchanged subsequently.

The foundational artifacts' low information richness is appropriate because their primary purpose is to serve as a formal and rudimentary agreement between the client and the developer in relation to the objectives of the project, and yet account for the flexibility of the Agile method (Turk et al., 2002). In other words, richness is not only unneeded, it is in fact undesirable as it may cause the parties to get bogged down in premature discussions of project plans and system specifications, which is inconsistent with the Agile method. Similarly, the low information mutability of the foundational artifacts is appropriate in this context because they are meant to form the basis for the subsequent phases of Agile software development. Without a firm basis, tedious and costly change management processes may be constantly required and the project may be rendered unfeasible as a consequence (Conboy, 2009).

Both Projective artifacts – the wireframes and visual designs - were representational, image-based artifacts that elaborated on the textual information contained in the foundational artifacts to capture the initial design of the system to be developed. Both of them were interactive and contained varied information that pertained to the system's features, functionality, and user-interface. Furthermore, they were the focus on discussions within AlphaInc and BravoTech that lasted throughout the Discovery phase. These discussions revolved around the clarification of expectations and feature requests within AlphaInc, and around testing activities within BravoTech. Therefore, the use of the projective artifacts can be characterized by high information richness. Both the wireframes and visual designs were created, modified, and developed through several iterations between AlphaInc and BravoTech during which AlphaInc's vision and requirements were captured with increasing fidelity (Balogun & Johnson, 2005). This process of interorganizational negotiation facilitated the consolidation of expectations between the parties. However, once they were finalized and approved by the stakeholders, the projective artifacts remained static for the remainder of the project. Therefore, their use was characterized by low information mutability.

As the projective artifacts were meant to elaborate on the high-level project objectives specified within the foundational artifacts, it is desirable for their use to embody high information richness. In particular, their visual and interactive nature is required to provide a preview of the design of the end product, elicit early feedback from the client, and align the expectations of the project stakeholders in relation to the system's planned features (Espinosa et al., 2007). However, like foundational artifacts, it is suficient for the use projective artifacts to manifest low information mutability because they are meant as the blueprints for the end product and there is a need for an extent of constancy (i.e., requirements that do not change suddenly) to facilitate the efectiveness and eficiency of its initial development (see Ferreira, Sharp, & Robinson, 2011). Moreover, as was evident in our case, it is common for the design of the actual system in Agile projects to start relatively early on and develop gradually through multiple and frequent design iterations. Therefore, beyond a certain point, projective artifacts do not need to be modified to reflect ongoing design changes as these are captured in the emerging system.

The Exposition artifacts contained text-based information that was used to communicate changing requirements and track the status of the project tasks in a straightforward manner. Despite their one-dimensionality, the backlog tool and IM system were used in tandem to inform real-time and substantive discussions among participants to seek clarifications about system features, solicit feedback about feature releases, and drive the development process. Therefore, their use can be characterized by high information richness. Due to the highly dynamic and iterative nature of Agile development, both exposition artifacts were subject to constant modifications from the moment they came into use until the end of the development process. Therefore, their use can be characterized by high information mutability.

When used in the context of an Agile development project, exposition artifacts should exhibit high information richness because their purpose is to support the technical and temporal aspects of the project (Espinosa et al., 2007). In the absence of other conventional coordination mechanism (e.g., detailed plans, progression charts, and linear timelines), exposition artifacts can serve to synchronize the allocation of resources and facilitate the development of a shared understanding between project stakeholders, thereby facilitating the progression of the project. It is also appropriate for exposition artifacts to exhibit high information mutability because software development under Agile principles unfolds iteratively, and the information contained within these artifacts must be constantly modified to support the coordination of development work across the various iterations (Strode et al., 2012).

Finally, we discuss the use of the Indicative artifact in our case. The emergent system was a visual, interactive, and an increasingly elaborate and detailed artifact which contained multiple layers of graphical and functional information. Moreover, the system was the focal point for development discussions within and between the parties throughout the Development phase. These included requests for features and bug fixes on the one hand, and testing and feedback elicitation on the other. Due to its elaborate and multilayered nature, and because it served as the backbone for substantive discussions during the development phase, the use of the system can be characterized by high information richness. Additionally, given that the new versions of the system were being released weekly based on ongoing development and feedback, its use by the two parties can be characterized by high information mutability.

We maintain it is advisable for indicative artifacts to exhibit high information richness. Such richness can allow client stakeholders (i.e., AlphaInc in our case study), and even business users with non-technical backgrounds, to understand and directly interact with these artifacts to contribute valuable feedback that would enhance the shared understanding between project stakeholders. This will also enhance the eficiency of the development process and ensure that the development dynamics of an Agile software project (comprising successive iterations of development and testing) can be efectively managed (Strode et al., 2012). Indicative artifacts should also exhibit high information mutability because they are meant to provide comprehensive and up-to-date information on the project deliverables' status, and the information contained therein would naturally be modified with each iteration of Agile development (Beck et al., 2001).

## 6. Conclusions and future research

In this paper, we set out to examine the aspects of coordination in Agile development projects and answer the following questions: (1) How are coordination artifacts used to support coordination throughout the lifespan of Agile software development projects? (2) How does their informational potential impact their capacity to facilitate coordination in Agile software development projects?

In relation to the first question, we developed a framework (Fig. 4 above) based on the case study we conducted which specifies the primary coordination challenges that can arise over the course of Agile development projects. These include the challenges of (1) reconciling difering priorities and (2) adjusting allocated resources in the Initiation phase, the challenge of (3) consolidating differing expectations in the Discovery phase, and the challenges of (4) managing the producer/consumer relationship, (5) coordinating tasks and sub-tasks, and (6) balancing simultaneity constraints in the Development phase. We have also identified and conceptually developed four coordination artifacts that were used in our case study to address these challenges, which include Foundational Artifacts for the first two challenges, Projective artifacts for the third challenge, as well as Exposition and Indicative artifacts for the three

remaining challenges.

In relation to the second question, we organized the four types of coordination artifacts in Table 4 based on the informationa potential that they manifested (i.e., the varying levels of information mutability and richness of each artifact) when they were used during the various phases of the project. More specifically, we found that the use of foundational artifacts manifested in low information richness and mutability, the use of projective artifacts manifested in high information richness and low mutability, the use of exposition artifacts manifested in high information richness and mutability, and the use of indicative artifacts manifested in high information richness and mutability. More importantly, we have also included a discussion of how the informational potential of each artifact appears to be appropriate to the role that they play over the course of Agile software development.

It is interesting to note that, in our case study, project participants employed coordination artifacts that manifested increasingly higher levels of information richness and mutability when they were used throughout the lifespan of the project. It is likely that this observed pattern is due to the specific nature of Agile development projects which deemphasizes extensive planning activities and encourages high degrees of equivocality and dynamism well into the advanced stages of development. Without many other formal structures or processes to rely on, participants in Agile projects can resort to elaborating and deploying such coordination artifacts to support their activities.

## 6.1. Limitations and future research

Our study is not without its limitations. First, although studies based on the single case research method are a “typical and legitimate endeavor” (Lee & Baskerville, 2003, pp. 231) in qualitative research, a common criticism of the approach is the problem of generalizability (Walsham, 2006). Although we readily acknowledge that statistical generalization is impossible from a single case study, we nevertheless contend that our study is generalizable beyond its singular context as our findings resonate with some of the more established works in the literatures on Agile software development (e.g. Cataldo et al., 2006) and coordination (e.g. Malone & Crowston, 1994; Sabherwal, 2003). As such, this study invokes the principle that some refer to as “generalizing from description to theory” (Lee & Baskerville, 2003, pp. 235). Nevertheless, future research can be directed at statistically validating our theoretica insights, so that the boundary conditions of our study can be better defined.

A second limitation of our study is due to the context of the studied proiect and their implementation of Agile software development methods. The virtual adaptation of the methods does not represent Agile development in its most rigorous form, as would be advocated by the original proponents of Agile development (e.g. Beck. 1999. 2000). Future research can seek to ascertain our findings in the context of other variants of Agile development, but regardless, we contend that distributed and virtually conducted projects that have adopted and adapted Agile methods (Alzoubi et al., 2016; Scheerer et al., 2014) can benefit from a better understanding of how to utilize coordination artifacts.

The third limitation is that our study focuses on interview data and information derived on coordination activities is entirely based on informant accounts. We did not have access to the chat tool logs or backlog tools. We based our discussion of the mutability of these tools on our informants' descriptions of their usage rather than observing directly how their content was modified. However. these tools are not unique to our case study. The specific tools we studied or similar tools in Agile project settings share similar features and afordances (e.g., Alyahya, Alqahtani, & Maddeh, 2016; Kalliamvakou, Damian, Blincoe, Singer, & German, 2015). In addition, the coordination between the project stakeholders of our case study was primarily virtual. and they did not interact extensively via face-to-face conversations and phone/Skype calls. But our informants emphasized that in partially or fully virtual projects, the coordination artifacts were all the more important in keeping the stakeholders informed because they were unable to rely on meetings and calls. In any case, future research can seek to explore if the coordination challenges and the artifacts that may be used to overcome them would remain the same for projects that comprises a greater extent of tangible/document-based coordination.

A fourth limitation is that that the focus of our study was restricted to the coordination between a client and a developer (i.e., AlphaInc and BravoTech) because this is one of the most common relationships in Agile software development that requires efective coordination (see Hoda et al., 2011). However, it should be acknowledged that other forms of coordination can occur within this setting that can impact the nature and outcomes of Agile Software Development (e.g. relationships between internal stakeholders within the client or the developer – see Strode et al., 2012). While it is impossible to exhaustively examine all possible coordination artifacts for all the various coordination relationships that can manifest in an Agile software development Project within a single case study, examining the coordination relationships that fall beyond the scope of our study could be a fruitful avenue for future inquiry. Not only could this line of inquiry yield potentially important insights, but it could also provide a more complete picture of the role of coordination artifacts in Agile software development.

## 6.2. Implications for theory

By addressing the research questions set forth at the beginning of this paper, we have made a number of theoretical contributions. First, this study is one of the first to examine the application and utility of coordination artifacts in the context of Agile Software Development projects. In doing so, it complements the existing perspectives of coordination in general, and in Agile software development in particular (Chow & Cao, 2008; Misra et al., 2009). Specifically, while some of the challenges we observed (i.e., shared resources, simultaneity constraints, tasks and sub-task, and producer/consumer relationship) have been previously identified in the existing literature on coordination (e.g., Malone & Crowston, 1990), there were a number of other challenges (i.e., difering priorities and difering expectations), as well as the four coordination artifacts we identified and conceptually developed that are all conceptua innovations. Moreover, we have extended the previous work on coordination mechanisms (e.g., Sabherwal, 2003) by elaborating on how the coordination artifacts can be used by project stakeholders to address their coordination needs.

Second, our study extends coordination theory (see Malone & Crowston, 1990) by contextualizing its constructs and mechanisms in Agile software development. As discussed previously, Agile software development has unique implications for coordination due to the uncertainty and dynamism it entails (Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001). Consequently, by mapping the various coordination challenges that we have observed in our case to the phases of Agile software development (see Balaji & Murugaiyan, 2012; Stoica, Mircea & Ghilic-Micu, 2013), and by tracing how coordination artifacts support their resolution, our study presents a more nuanced view of the tenets of the coordination theory in this unique setting.

Third, our examination of the informational potential of each of the artifacts extends prior studies on the use of artifacts to support Agile software development (e.g. Cataldo et al., 2006; Espinosa et al., 2007). In particular, by focusing on the artifacts' information richness and mutability, we were able to explain how well they were suited to support diferent coordination needs during Agile software development. In fact, our study suggests that the various coordination artifacts may have been consciously shaped to embody the appropriate levels of information richness and mutability so that they are able to best support the Agile activities for which they were used. These findings contribute toward a more general explanation of the role of artifacts in facilitating coordination, and a more refined understanding of how coordination artifacts work.

## 6.3. Implications for practice

Our findings can be useful for Agile practitioners as they seek to better understand the function of diferent project artifacts and how they may be used to facilitate efective coordination in Agile settings. In particular, our study suggests that artifacts should be deliberately crafted to ensure that they possess the appropriate informational potential that can be actualized in the various phases of software development. In this regard, the framework presented in our study (i.e., Fig. 4) can be used as a diagnostic blueprint to assess what types of artifacts have been applied and which are still missing in an Agile software development environment. In particular, practitioners can analyze their utilization of artifacts and decide if new artifacts should be introduced or if the existing artifacts can be leveraged more efectively. For example, foundational and projective artifacts can be powerful tools in ensuring common understanding, and should be applied in the earlier stages of a project (e.g. Gal et al., 2008; Subrahmanian et al., 2003; Winkler et al., 2014).

As one of the most novel aspects of Agile software development lies in the sharing of the early versions of the final product in the form of prototypes (Cohn, 2010), our study also suggests that Agile practitioners should collaborate with the help of the information contained within these early system versions. This notion also encourages the practice of continuous delivery whereby project developers can elicit feedback from users who might be less familiar with how to use and how to comment on unfinished or crude prototypes (Da Silva, Martin, Maurer, & Silveira, 2011).

Finally, Agile practitioners can also benefit from an understanding of the precise mechanisms through which artifacts can facilitate the coordination efort. In our study, we have contributed to this understanding by elaborating on the diferent ways in which various artifacts can be deployed to facilitate coordination, and discussed the main coordination challenges that are specifically related to Agile development. Artifacts that are not designed for flexibility may quickly become obstacles for coordination as they are unable to adequately capture frequently changing project goal, scope or product vision (Lee, 2007). Understanding these dynamics would help practitioners identify potential coordination pitfalls, and consequently, circumvent them to improve the efectiveness of coordination.
