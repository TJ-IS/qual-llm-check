# Research Perspectives: Using a Process Philosophy Perspective in Information Systems Research: Principles of Creative Evolution

David Kreps,<sup>1</sup> Frantz Rowe<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Galway, Ireland, david.kreps@universityofgalway.ie <sup>2</sup>Nantes University, France, frantz.rowe@univ-nantes.fr

## Abstract

What if reality fundamentally consists in events and processes rather than things? All actions are situated within processes, influenced by a broader set of preceding and concomitant flows of digital information and other actions. This paper offers a process philosophy perspective that sees things as merely constellations of processes. Decisions within these processes, moreover, must usually be made within time constraints. Rather than a view of time, such as in process theories, as being tied to notions of entities, variance, or—at best—the flow of things, process philosophy sees time as duration: tied to consciousness, sensemaking, and free will. While principles for conducting positivist, interpretive, and critical research have been widely discussed in the IS literature, criteria and principles for a process philosophy perspective are lacking. In the context of Bergson and Whitehead’s creative evolution process philosophy, this paper proposes a set of five principles for the conduct of process philosophy research in information systems: (1) heterogeneous multiplicity, (2) immanence in a process, (3) experience over abstraction, (4) consciousness in duration, and (5) a relational ontology. We describe these in detail and discuss some potential applications, methodological issues, and related difficulties in the principles’ implementation for IS research. We highlight their value for requirements engineering in large-scale projects.

Keywords: Process Philosophy, Ontology, Duration, Consciousness, Free Will, Requirements Engineering

Dirk Hovorka was the accepting senior editor. This research perspectives article was submitted on March 17, 2020 and underwent five revisions.

## 1 Introduction

Sometimes, a fresh perspective can shed significant light on problems that elude easy solutions. In some cases, it may be that fundamental worldviews of scholars within a field are acting as impediments to better understanding and that viewing such problems from very different angles may present crucial insights. This paper offers a fresh perspective that we call creative evolution process philosophy. This philosophy may lead to a better understanding of why particular problems in IS related to the unfolding of processes in time have been enduring and how viewing them from this perspective may present new solutions.

Process philosophy is not new to IS (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2016). It is, in fact, of growing interest in IS literature (e.g. Mousavi Baygi et al., 2021; Pentland et al., 2021) and is of established and increasing interest in reference disciplines such as organization science (Chia, 1999; Hernes & Maitliss, 2010; Helin et al., 2014; Hernes, 2014; Langley & Tsoukas 2010; 2017). While the central focus of process philosophy on processes rather than entities is readily grasped (e.g. Burton-Jones et al., 2015; Niederman, 2021), its use in IS research requires deeper understanding and anchoring in theoretical principles and seminal foundations.

Similar to other papers that lay foundational principles for more established research approaches founded in philosophy (e.g. Klein & Myers, 1999) the aim of this paper is to present a set of principles for using what we have called a creative evolution process philosophy perspective, founded in the work of Henri Bergson and Alfred North Whitehead in information systems research. Creative Evolution was the title of Bergson’s Nobel Prize-winning 1907 magnum opus in which much of his processual philosophy was laid out. <sup>1</sup> Whitehead acknowledged his debt to Bergson, and the form of process philosophy we lay out in this paper is more Bergsonian than Whiteheadian, although founded in both; hence a creative evolution process philosophy.

We believe that embracing a creative evolution process philosophy perspective more anchored on Bergson and more explicit on Whitehead would strengthen the diversity of process theories and related phenomena. It might also increase cross-pollination in their various traditions (Mendling et al., 2021). This perspective offers an ontological approach that makes it distinctive from other process philosophies (e.g. Dewey, James, Mead) and process theories in IS (e.g. as opposed to variance theories—Burton-Jones et al., 2015). Such theories have been characterized as “weak” process approaches (Van de Ven & Poole, 2005). Our set of principles for a creative evolution process philosophy perspective constitutes a particularly useful and “strong” process lens through which to identify and understand conditions of performance in a range of contemporary problems—such as sufficient inner time needed for creativity (Jarvenpaa & Välikangas, 2020; Ryden & El Sawy, 2019) or large-scale project management (Bergman et al., 2002). With these principles, this perspective goes beyond the depiction of an event-based understanding of reality (Chia, 1999; Mousavi Baygi et al., 2021) to the key role of consciousness in what Bergson describes as the durée réelle. It also contrasts sharply with process theory approaches in which “process may be ‘first,’” but “processes and objects always co-exist” (vom Brocke et al., 2021, p. 4).

The paper seeks to address IS researchers who have heard of process approaches but are not yet sufficiently conversant with the depths and complexities of such a philosophical grounding to begin such work and who would benefit from a set of principles to follow. They will be able to decide whether their process theorizing is consistent with certain principles of creative evolution process philosophy and potentially present their work in a more precise and better-justified way.

One area of IS where a fresh perspective can shed new light is requirements engineering—particularly for large-scale projects—where problems of project failure have not found satisfactory solutions to date (Nelson, 2007). This may be because the perspectives on which they are founded are problematic. We make this our primary illustrative example, in Section 4 of the paper. The paper is organized as follows: The next section provides the theoretical background of the study, including a brief definition and overview of the philosophical, organizational, and other general literature on process philosophy, briefly mentioning some of the relevant IS research to date. Section 3 proposes a set of principles for using a creative evolution process philosophy perspective in IS research. In Section 4, these principles are then used to examine, as an illustrative example, how a study of requirements engineering might be undertaken from this creative evolution process philosophy perspective. The last section discusses its value and indicates some directions for implementing the principles and directions.

## 2 Background to Process Philosophy and Creative Evolution

## 2.1 Introduction to Basic Terms

The distinction between what Van de Ven and Poole (2005) referred to as a sequence involving entities and continuous and emergent change has been discussed since the earliest days of Western philosophy. Rescher (1996)—frequently referenced in processual organization studies texts (e.g. Chia, 1999; Helin at. al. 2014; Hernes & Maitliss, 2010; Hernes, 2014; Langley & Tsoukas, 2017)—synthesizes the historical background since ancient Greek philosopher Heraclitus as a dispute between the processists and the substantialists (1996, p. 5) whom he represents in four competing contentions about the nature of reality, where priority represents a lead of one over the other, and primacy denotes one almost to the exclusion of the other:

1. “Process has primacy over things. … Things are simply constellations of processes.

2. Process has priority over substance. Things are always subordinate to processes because processes inwardly engender, determine, and characterize the things there are. Processes as such transcend the realm of things since there are also substance-detached processes.

3. Substance has priority over process. The only sort of processes there are, are those involved in the doings and comportments of things.

4. Substance has primacy over process. Indeed, substance is all there is; all processes and changes are simply a matter of how things appear to certain (mind-equipped) substances.” (Rescher, 1996, p. 2)

## 2.1.1 Process Philosophy in Philosophy and Organization Studies

A simple focus on process—Rescher’s #2 above— acknowledges that processes should be regarded as the more important, while still regarding the world as populated by entities, albeit entities that are subject to processes. A good example of this is how “knowing” processes, as ongoing social accomplishments, enable things to get done and enact a collective sustainable identity in distributed organizing (Orlikowski, 2002).

Our focus in this paper is on Rescher’s #1 process, most famously described by Heraclitus as “The river where you set your foot just now is gone—those waters giving way to this, now this” (Heraclitus, 2003, Fragment 41, p. 27) and “Just as the river where I step is not the same, and is, so I am as I am not.” (Heraclitus, 2003, Fragment 81, p. 51). These two fragments are most often quoted as “No man ever steps in the same river twice, for it's not the same river and he’s not the same man.” This approach could be characterized as an “event ontology” rather than a “thing ontology” (Lowe, 2009). There are many subvariations of this approach, including in the organizational literature on process (Chia, 1999; Hernes & Maitliss, 2010; Hernes, 2014; Langley & Tsoukas, 2017), social anthropological literature on fluidity (Ingold, 2011), and agential realism (Barad, 2007). We wish to present, in this paper, a very Bergsonian version of this Heraclitean form of process that rarely appears in the literature but which we believe may be of interest to scholars and of use in IS research.

## 2.1.2 Distinctive Ideas of Bergson/Whitehead Process Philosophy

The subvariation of Heraclitean process peculiar to Bergson and, to a lesser extent, Whitehead, is distinguished by two distinctive aspects foregrounding consciousness and free will. The first is shared by both these founding thinkers; the second is more peculiarly Bergsonian. We regard both as key elements of a creative evolution process philosophy approach for IS.

The first is the significance of consciousness in duration. The core concept of Bergson’s philosophy is durée réelle (real duration), an understanding of the nature of time that locates it through personal experience and thus (re)connects consciousness to reality. An early form—and precursor—of what is described by some today as a New or Neo-Materialism (Barad, 2007; DeLanda, 2016; Braidotti, 2013, 2019), Bergson’s multipolar understanding, however, of consciousness, the self, the material, and the social, neither privileges the self, nor erases it in the mangle of its imbrication within the material (Pickering, 2010; Leonardi, 2011; Ingold, 2011). Bergson’s focus is to locate both the self and the material within a processual medium, a duration—where duration has primacy— allowing both a coherent inner consciousness and situating it within material flows. He describes this in Matter and Memory by discussing the materiality of perception and the distinctive nature of the perceiver. That which is perceived, and the perceptions themselves, are all material and subject to scientific investigation. Experiencing this process, the conscious perceiver is, however, not material but temporal. Consciousness remains, to this day, contested and unresolved in both science and philosophy (Lenharo, 2023), and Bergson’s insights here continue to offer both a radical and compelling explanation. Perception, for Bergson, is a physical act indistinguishable from what is being perceived; i.e., the activity of perception is as physical as the object being perceived, and the two are conjoined in physical processes to such an extent that they are almost indistinguishable in the flow and flux of reality. Separately, there is a subjective someone who experiences affects in reaction to physical sensations—the perceiver. Perception and the perceiver, moreover, are continuously subject to duration and the relation between past and present mediated by memory. For Bergson, “Memory is not— cannot be—physical” (Kreps, 2015, p. 35). Memory is an act by which the perceiver reenacts the past within the present; it is an act of consciousness, located within a distinct self-awareness. This is the “self” understanding the act of perception through duration. This process, by which a knowing self recollects the past within the present, acts to co-create the future, with material realities impacting and impacted by choices informed by memory. This durational understanding of consciousness as a process of past, present, and future, situated within materiality while being distinct from it, is a uniquely Bergsonian view.

Bergson argues that “The duration lived by our consciousness is a duration with its own determined rhythm, a duration very different from the time of the physicist” (1896/2004, p.272). There are two concepts here: (1) durational time, to be distinguished from scientific time, and (2) durational multiplicity, by which each of us heterogeneously experiences our own duration. In the first of these Bergson is arguing that the (scientific) idea of a discrete and measurable time is an artificial concept formed by the intrusion of the idea of space into the realm of duration. The distinction between this process approach and the substantialist/entitative approach is thus here represented as a distinction between time and space. Substance occupies space, but process is experienced as duration. Time, therefore, can be understood from either a “processist” (durational time) or a “substantialist” (scientific time) view, and the notion of duration is both processist and bound up with the nature of living consciousness. Scientific time, then, represents an inert reality as substances in space for which we may observe sequences (Boland, 2001). But in Bergson’s durée réelle things are simply constellations of processes of which, as experiencers, we are a part.

The second concept is more subtle, arguing that each of our individual durations is different. Time and movement are not merely a series of discrete positions. Bergson uses the image of the tolling of a distant church bell to illustrate this durational multiplicity (Bergson, 1889/2005, p. 87). We hear the sounds and imagine the movement of the bell, toing and froing, that they represent. Yet because, here, we are dealing with time, we either simply grasp them as a group— church bells—and hear a continuous sound, or we separate them in our thoughts, and hear discontinuities instead. Such states of consciousness, where the intervals between the sounds of the church bells remain to be counted—the sounds themselves having already passed—turn out to be a multiplicity that is not so very different from the multiplicity of objects counted in space: a spatiotemporal multiplicity (Boland, 2001). But a durational multiplicity—where we grasp the tolling of the bells as a group and hear a continuous sound—depicts a kind of time that is a consciously experienced duration in which we play a part. It is a time that endures, a duration—a durée réelle. Crucially, this is not the same for each of us. This durational multiplicity is heterogeneous. Today I hear the sounds of the bells as I have heard them over and over again outside my window, and the sounds barely register: it seems over almost as soon as it has started. But my guest hears them for the first time, and the tune reminds her of another time and place, of an experience in her memory; the sounds of the bells occupy her thoughts and seem to go on for a long time. Our experience of the duration of the same tolling bells has been very different.

As with Bergson, Whitehead incorporates consciousness into his processual universe. In his organic philosophy, “everything experiences” (Skrbina, 2007, p. 21). In Whitehead’s view, “every singular actuality” as it takes part in the coming together of new events “must be related to … a world of antecedent actualities. In this regard, the actuality is a subject whose object is a world of antecedent subjects” (Mays, 1959, pp. 11-12). This is a form of what philosophers term “panpsychism,” which deems “all objects, or systems of objects, [to] possess a singular inner experience of the world around them” (Skrbina, 2007, p. 16), a world in which “experience is impossible without an experiencer” (Strawson, 2006, p. 23). This is a world no longer centered on “man,”

but on an understanding of consciousness that admits its presence in everything around us (Braidotti, 2013, 2019). Such a view of reality, of its immanence, is founded on an assumption that “experiential phenomena cannot be emergent from wholly nonexperiential phenomena” (Strawson, 2006, p. 21). In other words, what is—and what it is like—is for Whitehead as it is for Bergson, fundamentally interwoven with consciousness. There is no reality without a consciousness of it; this is a world built on a model of consciousness.

Western philosophy’s chief errors, according to Whitehead, result from “overstatement,” principally, “The fallacy of misplaced concreteness” (Whitehead, 1925/1967, p. 51) in which we reify our abstractions— confusing the map for the territory. This “consists in considering experience in terms of such notions as space, time, matter and mind, and then by supposing [such notions] to have an independent existence [which] gives them a concreteness they do not possess” (Mays, 1959, p. 45). For example, in IS research, the tendency to follow an implicit approach of scientific idealization without necessarily realizing that this is the case (Iivari, 2023).

The second distinctive aspect is free will. For Bergson, this incorporation of consciousness into his processual ontology has profound metaphysical implications for the notion of free will. In contemporary terms, Bergson argues that free will and determinism are compatible (Kreps & Rowe, 2021). The durée réelle hinges on what he terms “indetermination”: the moments of possibility within consciously experienced processes when human choices can be made, and the universe can take a different course. Listening to the sounds and experiencing the memories evoked by the bells, my guest decides to share a part of that memory with me, and both our lives are changed by it. This active durational universe, thereby, is making itself up as it goes along from multiple heterogeneous foci, a “creative evolution” of which we are a part.

Crucially, in perhaps the simplest illustrative contrast by which one may grasp the durée réelle, we must acknowledge that scientific time, like all equations, can run forward or backward. Yet the nature of succession in conscious duration is that it flows only forward, building experience as it goes. At each moment, I am renewed, no longer the person who experienced the previous moment but the person, armed with and changed by that moment, now experiencing the present. In this flow of change, free will is exercised. Our memory assists us with weighing up different potential routes and then we choose and action unfolds. We do this, of course, as social beings within systems of mutual “social obligation” (Bergson, 1935/2006) that coconstitute the stories we tell. Thus, Bergson acknowledges that choice—the free will and autonomy so key to the durée réelle—is, of course, overlaid by all the necessary coordination required of social grouping: It is relational. His accent on the reality of free will is thus a form of individualism neither privileging agency nor denying the structural influence of social and material constraints.<sup>2</sup> It is a balance and, crucially, one that is played out through time, through the durée réelle.

## 2.2 IS Research Relevant to the Heraclitean Process Approach

In this section, we examine key texts of IS researchers that are relevant to the Heraclitean approach. These texts are specific to IS in that they discuss processual approaches rather than merely making use of one. These texts are indicative of the potential of the Heraclitean approach, but they are not representative of IS research in general, which is largely dominated by a substantialist/entitative view of the world. Following Leidner (2018), our theorizing review of these texts seeks to cover the phenomenon of Heraclitean process philosophy in IS while focusing on a comparison with the particular flavor of process philosophy more peculiar to Bergson. The first two papers devote themselves to process philosophy, and the others to process theory. We included the latter and notably the last two because, while they are not specifically Heraclitean, the theoretical discussion they offer still highlights their value. Process theory was introduced into IS by Markus and Robey (1988), via a “simple distinction between process and variance theory” (Markus & Rowe, 2018, p. 1264). Today process theory might be given a different theorization such as a “causal mechanism” (Rowe & Markus, 2018), or historical explanation (Van de Ven & Poole, 2005). The approach of process theory as a form of logical structure of theories continues to be found in that “simple distinction” to variance approaches (e.g. Niedermann, 2021). With Rescher, however, we would categorize process theory as giving priority to process rather than primacy. Recent acknowledgment of the diversification of process theory includes constitutive causality (Rowe & Markus, 2021), which could be considered closer to creative evolutionary process philosophy (e.g. Ngwenyama et al., 2023).

Boland’s paper, “The Tyranny of Space in Organizational Analysis” (2001) focuses on a Bergsonian understanding of duration and introduces perhaps the earliest and most distinguished mention of Bergson’s work to the IS community. Boland brings to our attention that “our temporal experience as conscious agents” is predominantly understood through spatial imagery and metaphor (2001, p. 4). Boland also gives support to a narrative understanding of cognition in this paper involving “actors with causal powers” (2001, p. 12) and quotes Bergson’s own critique of the “spatial journey like description of cognition” (2001, p. 19) so prevalent in the literature.

Cecez-Kecmanovic published the first paper in an IS outlet with the overt intention of introducing the IS community to a deeper process metaphysics. In her brief review for IS readers of the history of process philosophy, Cecez-Kecmanovic (2016) strives to indicate how process philosophy informs the sociomaterial paradigm in IS and relates to Barad’s work on agential realism (2007), the philosophical source of inspiration for Scott and Orlikowski (2014). It is possible that the focus on the performative here may be interpreted as granting more power to the structural than to the agential: Perhaps it is merely a matter of emphasis. It is important—to our mind—that an understanding of the imbrication of the self in the mangle of new materialism does not erase or subsume the importance of free will, rather than simply, as Bergson does, place it within context. Giddens, notably, rejects the “death of the subject” leanings in poststructuralism, complaining of its “fallacious assumption that if there is no pre-existing or transcendental subject there can be no subject at all” (1979, p. 47). With Bergson, we see room for a self situated as well as imbricated in the flow.

Mousavi Baygi et. al.’s (2021) paper, “Everything Flows” (2021) enjoins IS researchers to undertake a shift “from actor-centric orientations towards a flow-oriented approach and vocabulary” (2021, p. 2). Turning to anthropologist Tim Ingold, whose approach critiques the notion that “bounded actors are deemed as the significant and original causes of transformation,” the notion that change is planned is supplanted with a notion of the “temporal flow of action” (2021, p. 2). Mousavi Baygi et al. make reference to both Bergson and Whitehead, but “more importantly” base their theorization of flux on Ingold’s work. Ingold’s notion of “wayfaring,” in particular, represents a call for us to live and act responsively and ethically.

Pentland’s paper, “Building Process Theory with Narrative: From Description to Explanation,” (1999) explains that the structure of narratives can be understood as process theories. In narratives, stories are made of processes, themselves manifesting through events. “Given some telling of a story, what was the true underlying pattern of events?” And what is “the underlying process that gave rise to those events?” (Pentland, p. 712) The author builds a story among a huge potential sequential variety <sup>3</sup> of events (the sequence in which events appear in a process). In doing so he selects events as more important than others as well as focal actors. From multiple subjective voices and by silencing those that are not selected when considered as of minor importance, he constructs its deeper and more “objective” structure. There is a strong structuralist thread through this paper, suggesting that deep narratives tell people, rather than the other way around. Situating the subjective voice within a deeper event ontology, such as we have described above, could ground Pentland’s work philosophically in a manner consistent with our creative evolution approach and present a more nuanced balance between the narratives and people who tell and are told by them. The storytelling of this paper in 1999 came closer to a creative evolution process philosophy perspective than subsequent papers authored by Pentland. This is because even digital trace data cannot measure personal experience (Pentland et al., 2020); as a result, depth of conscious duration is left behind.

Niederman’s monograph (2021) promises the most complete work on process theory in IS to date yet does not mention Bergson and only briefly treats Whitehead’s ideas. For Niedermann, “notions of starting, ending, and duration, also based on discrete representation of time add to our ability to use time as if it were steady, predictable and linear” (2021, p. 47). Niederman asks: “Where the time horizon is large enough that no change is observable, what is the harm in treating entities as if they were permanent?” (2021, p. 16) and concludes that there is “potential value in studies that consider only substance, only process, or the two combined in various ways” (2021, p. 17). In certain respects, we agree with this view— and indeed our example of requirements engineering, later in this paper, suggests that while an entitative ontology is often sufficient for small-scale projects, our creative evolution process philosophy principles would help IS scholars understand why it consistently ends in failure in larger scale and multipolar projects—a substance view for one scale and a process view to enhance another.

## 3 A Set of Principles for a Creative Evolution Process Philosophy Perspective

In this section, we propose a set of principles for using creative evolution process philosophy in research in information systems. One of the authors of this paper has undertaken a study of the works of these thinkers (see Appendix A) over the past 25 years and, in a series of conversations with the other, the two authors attempted to distill and describe the essential points of Bergson’s and Whitehead’s philosophy that might best inform IS research. The result is perhaps more how Bergson’s oeuvre can be improved as a result of reading Whitehead than the other way around or any kind of synthesis—which is probably not possible. Each of these five principles comes from both Bergson and Whitehead, although Principle 4 resonates more with Bergson. Nonetheless, we found that it is possible to find descriptions in the literature of IS phenomena that are a good fit for one or several of the principles.<sup>5</sup>

Principle 1 (P1), heterogeneous multiplicity, offers an ontological foundation: that what exists is fundamentally multiple and diverse, which is continually transforming. This idea of the heterogeneous composition of a rather more “fuzzy” (Boncella, 1995) than Boolean reality (Tsoukas & Chia, 2002) is particularly appealing to the sociomaterial scholars (Introna, 2013; Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2016) and aligns with work in data mining and distributed information systems. P1 states that not only what exists is never fully completely known but existence is in permanent beta and never completed (Introna, 2013). Developments in keeping with this principle include the rise of the “permanent beta” in IS development, which should broadly be understood as normative, within reasonable constraints (where safety critical systems are involved); continuous “patching,” which should be understood as ongoing development; and continuous “versioning,” which should be conceived of as stages in a narrative. Further personalization through machine learning SaaS would also be in keeping with this principle.

Heterogeneous multiplicity can also be illustrated with big data issues. The real can be approached through all sorts of digital traces (Osterlund et al., 2020; Pentland et al., 2017; Pentland et al., 2020; Pentland et al., 2021; Whelan et al., 2016), although knowledge of the multiplicity is never complete. In well-known terms, the “whole” is greater than the “sum of the constituting parts” because it is affected by the nature of their complex interactions. Because of this, entity-based representations (Shanks et al., 2008) may miss the multiplicity resulting from the intricacy of relational representations. A key aspect of this principle is that heterogeneity and multiplicity require highly sensitive and adaptive modeling to avoid misunderstanding. To try to capture meaning from such heterogeneous and multiple contexts as big data, two principles are applied: invariance and symmetry. The principle of invariance seeks to ensure consistency by capturing measurement equivalence (ensuring the same thing is being measured in different contexts) across multiple data streams. The principle of symmetry requires that the representation offered by the measurement-equivalence model should be a representation of the real—a good mirror. Data mining models that attempt to make sense of the “flow intensity” of big data rely on invariance discovery to try to make some sense of the massive volume of data in distributed information systems. But to avoid breaking the all-important symmetry, such models must ensure that their invariant search is as broad as possible (Ge et. al., 2013; Giacomoni, 2023). The point of the principle (P1) in relation to the other principles is that this heterogeneous multiplicity is subject to changes wrought by consciousness, as well as intra- and intersystemic interactions producing changes in kind.

Principle 2 (P2), immanence in (a) process, stresses how “the past is immanent in the present and this fact implies that each outcome, each situation or state, always necessarily incorporates and absorbs the events of its past” (Chia, 1999, p. 220). This understanding of an immanent causality has deep roots. As Johnson puts it: “In immanent causality cause occurrence and effect occurrence are attributed to the same continuant [thing or process]” (Johnson, 1924, p. 128). This is what Whitehead terms concrescence and is reflected in Bergson in terms of how reality is becoming in a continuous flow of experience. Crucially, this immanence includes consciousness—the “experiencer” without whom there is no experience—and foregrounds the panpsychic nature of this variation of process philosophy. The experiencer is at the same time the agent, engaging with affordances as they emerge.

P2 emphasizes that the real is created from the past (Chia, 1999). It does not mean it can only reproduce the past. There is the possibility for path creation, but it is out of a past situation. Path creation theory emphasizes this point (Garud & Karnoe, 2001) and is a good fit for digital innovation (Boland et al., 2007). Understanding how the present and the past influence the ambiguities, demands, and dilemmas inherent in IT reinvention undertaken by actors situated in the flow of time could be improved and deepened with this process philosophy principle, better enabling researchers to move beyond a simple “gap analysis” toward a broader focus “on the intention of the user” (Nevo et. al., 2016). P2 is in line with the early orientation of design science in this respect (Simon, 1996, p. 5).

Principle 3 (P3), experience over abstraction, reminds us that it is from the patterns that our “intellection” (a term of Bergson’s) discerns in what has past that we construct the abstract models that we use to explain the world in the present. Such abstractions can both mislead us as to the true nature of what we are trying to describe and institute changes in it that shift it from what it was becoming. Both Bergson and Whitehead enjoin us to recognize these models as merely useful tools, rather than reify them as the territory itself. Experience over abstraction valorizes experience alongside rather than instead of abstraction.

P3 stresses that belief in abstractions may be misplaced and alienating. This is exemplified by pandemics, where we can design tracing systems for a reality that is not well understood (Rowe et al., 2020). Even if widely adopted, such systems cannot control or curb the disease because their specifications are predicated on only one transmission path (big droplets) and are too narrow (Rowe et. al., 2023). P3 is an important principle because it applies to all those in IS who are interested in designing systems (Klein, 1981). Early design science in particular reminds us that imitation (simulation) of the world is just that: “Artificial things may imitate appearances in natural things while lacking, in one or many respects, the reality of the latter” (Simon, 1996, pp. 5, 13).

Principle 4 (P4), consciousness in duration, highlights the understanding of time in Bergson’s process philosophy that enriches his event-ontology with the role of the experiencer/agent. Thus, Bergson incorporates the narratives we tell and by which we give meaning to and understand the world (Pentland, 1999; Boland, 2001) into a universe he shows is built on the model of consciousness.

P4 is one of the key—and least appreciated—insights of Bergson’s (Boland, 2001). The constitutive power of narrative inherent in the notion of durée should not be underestimated. Research capturing the richness of the narratives we use to understand processes (Pentland, 1999; Webb & Mallon, 2007) needs to capture how we make the world as we tell those stories to one another and as they play through us. This narrative process brings our perceptions, our consciousness, and our sensemaking activities (Jarvenpaa & Välikangas, 2020) into the stories we tell ourselves and each other. The process sits at the nexus of many heterogeneous durations that require the stabilization of the intellect—the means by which we interpret processes as things—for us to be able to make sense of them. We see a blur rather than the individual beats of a butterfly’s wings, and our lives are too short to mark the great upheavals and long sleeps of mountains.

Each of us dances to our own tune, and the contrasts between such heterogeneous time scales—and how the subjective measure of our internal narrative (its “temporal thickness”) differs from the objective length of scientific time—are the precise milieu of consciousness (Hirai, 2019, p. 45). Importantly, this process of navigating temporal contrasts requires sufficient time for us to ponder, ruminate, and cogitate, before making sound narrative choices.

That conscious narrative is more a collection of short stories than a novel (Boje, 2017) is perhaps clearer in light of the notion of durée: each of us is a storyteller, and the principle of heterogeneous multiplicity underscores the multiplicity of durations. Understanding business process narratives (Kuechler and Vaishnavi, 2006), arguably, could benefit greatly from an appreciation of the significance of order in the ongoing development of lived experience. Path-dependent analysis relies on the reality of P4, especially in the design of living systems (Swanson & Miller, 2009).

Principle 5 (P5) is a relational ontology: everything is related. P5 captures the relationality of the process philosophy approach: every “thing” is related to every “thing” else, not as an aggregation of distinct entities that all affect one another but as foundationally interdependent and mutually constitutive continuities (Introna, 2013). Relations, in this ontology, are existential: Through any point or moment in the flow, we may glimpse the totality, as nothing exists independent of everything around and before it. We, too, as conscious beings, are relational in Bergson’s picture, while never erased: “While his consciousness, delving downwards, reveals to him, the deeper he goes, an ever more original personality, incommensurable with the others … , on the surface of life we are in continuous contact with other men whom we resemble, and united to them by a discipline which creates between them and us a relation of interdependence” (Bergson, 1935/2006, p. 14).

P5 thus underlines the inherent contingency of every choice, the interdependency of all processes, and the intersubjectivity of all positions. Observer-dependent observations (in space and time) implicitly acknowledge this fact. Moreover, this understanding of relationality is redolent of a more feminist appreciation of the social world (Adam & Richardson, 2001; Leidner & Tona, 2021), close, as it is, to Gilligan’s “ethic of care” (1982). Noddings’s (2013) philosophical foundations for this “ethic of care” make it clear that “the relational [is] more fundamental than the individual” (2013, p. xiii), and that “the caring relation is ethically basic” (2013, p. 3).

While the principles are certainly apparent in some IS scholarship and seem to apply, piecemeal, in many situations, their more conscious consideration as a whole is likely to improve the quality of processual research in IS. The principles are summarized in Table 2.

## 4 An Illustrative Example of How a Creative Evolution Process Philosophy Perspective Might Inform IS Research: Questioning Requirements Engineering

In this section, we illustrate how a creative evolution process philosophy perspective can bring valuable insight by changing the way we understand a fundamental IS practice: requirements engineering (RE). The practices of RE—the drawing together of requirements statements derived from requirements capture activities such as interviews, focus groups, JAD workshops, and similar activities—are familiar to practitioners and scholars alike. These practices have long been considered both of primary importance to the eventual success of any information systems project and the primary source of failure when projects do not work out as expected. A process philosophy perspective helps us further understand what many have grappled with over the years: While these practices not only present problems at smaller scales, at larger and more multipolar scales, they are often the source of widescale failure. Although there have been attempts to redress these failings that have included more process theory approaches (e.g. Chakraborty et al., 2010), our process philosophy perspective enables us to see that it is the entitative abstraction underpinning both requirements engineering and the more processual approaches (e.g. Holmström & Sawyer, 2011) trying to fix it that presents a barrier to capturing the required complexity.

Failures of large-scale projects have been well documented (e.g. Nelson, 2007). Bergman et al. (2002) described the cancellation of the London Stock Exchange Taurus project, which had been under development for six years after two decades of deliberations. The National Programme for IT, also in the UK, which began in 2002, described as “the biggest computer programme in the world ever” (Brennan,

2007) was similarly canceled in 2011, as it was deemed unfit to provide the modern IT services the NHS needed (Currie, 2012). Many large-scale computer projects simply fail to achieve their expected outcomes (Ewusi-Mensah & Przasnyski, 1991; Keil & Mann, 2000). While many scholars have sought to understand why this might be so, one such study, by Bergman et al. (2002), offers what we regard as a particularly valuable assessment of such failures, which allows us to elucidate a philosophical underpinning.

The concept of “requirements” itself immediately reifies entities as stable and unchanging. This, we argue, need not necessarily be the case but has become so due to the persistent reliance on an entitative philosophical underpinning for RE. As Wand et al. (1999) ably demonstrated, and as recalled by Burton-Jones and Meso (2006) and Shanks et al. (2008), many of the process diagram approaches of RE—e.g. entity relationship diagrams, functional decomposition diagrams, data flow diagrams—are based within/on an ontological theory originally proposed by Mario Bunge (1977) and brought to object-oriented programming and conceptual modeling by Wand and Weber (1990, 1995) as the Bunge-Wand-Weber (BWW) model. This model seeks to delineate a world of “things” with properties, of which combinations or composites can be made. Shanks et al. (2008, p. 569) use the ontology in their assertion that “composites should be modeled as an entity … and not as a relationship.” Bunge’s ontology posits “a set of rigorously defined constructs to describe all types of phenomena in the world” (Shanks et al., 2008, p. 555). Bunge’s ontology is entirely entitative/substantialist in Rescher’s (1996) #4 sense. Of course, this approach is fundamental to the kinds of process-mapping activities that result in the possibility of much basic systems engineering.

Yet the representation models of entity relationship diagrams and functional decomposition diagrams present huge problems when mapped onto reality. Representation is never perfectly “correct,” and only ever remains an abstraction that misrepresents the ever-changing complexity of the real. That our systems of representation require trade-offs, then, seems inevitable, and constant misalignment seems to be a feature of representation itself. Our point—and the thrust of both Bergson’s and Whitehead’s approach—is not that we should not use such systems but that we should remain mindful of their fallibility while we do and strive to continually remake them, rather than relying on them and thus distorting the real through the lens of the imaginary.

At small and manageable scales, of course, the representational entitative approach has proven itself of value (with new languages every few years trying to keep pace) and clearly will continue to be of use, despite its problems. At large and complex scales, however, we argue that it is perhaps one of the primary drivers of the failure of so many large IT projects. The rest of this section lays out why we believe the many multiorganizational, mixed, and complex multi-systems of today need a processual perspective.

For scholars engaged in entity modeling in the 1990s (e.g. Chan et. al., 1993), the view was that while processes may change significantly over time, the data (entities) involved remain stable and are thus a better foundation for analysis. Attempts have been made to improve requirements engineering through the use of more processual approaches, including complexity theory, actor-network theory (ANT), distributed cognition theory (DCog), social construction of technology (SCOT), and science and technology studies (STS) (e.g. Truex et al., 1999; Mathiassen et al., 2007; Chakraborty et al., 2010). Howard et al. (2004), for example, envisioned a complexity-informed process engineering, whereas Xiao et al. (2018) offered a distributed cognitive approach. The ANT/ DCog/complexity approaches tend, however, to treat human agents as equivalent to machine agents in actor networks open to an algorithmic determination of intention: Cognition is described in machine terms as “the human ability to acquire, transform, store, and apply information” (Xiao et al., 2018, p. 1220). Abstracting the rich lived experience of consciousness in duration into such simplistic models promises solutions only to the simplest of intentions. Holmström and Sawyer (2011), building on the work of Bergman et al. (2002), write, “requirements are not things that exist, or have been clearly articulated, ready to gather, in an organization. Rather, requirements are generated through negotiation and conflict resolution among the different actors involved in the project” (2011, p. 44). But the social construction of technology (SCOT) and science and technology studies (STS) analytical lense by which the authors then explain the “processes of interpretation, negotiation, and closure” are both ones that—in Rescher’s (1996) terms—give priority (#2), rather than primacy (#1) to process.

In keeping with this, as Holmström and Sawyer describe it, these approaches hold that “technological artifacts are open to sociological analysis” (2011, p. 37) and that these processes are additionally governed by the realms of possibility of the constraining technological frame (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Davidson, 2002). Actors, in other words, in their interactions between themselves and technological things, shape how those things are engendered, developed, and deployed. Thus, the entitative world exists alongside the processual, with technological objects conditioned by the power of social processes. The Bergsonian philosophical approach that we have outlined in this paper goes further, giving primacy to process and incorporating consciousness into our understanding of reality, rather than merely taking the opinions of engaged actors, as separate entities, into account as additional factors alongside more “concrete” technological realities.

As can be seen from Table 3, there are aspects of the ontology of Bunge that are quite contrary to a process philosophy perspective (e.g., 1, 2) and aspects that seem to translate rather better (e.g., 5). The use of Bunge’s entitative ontology in the BWW model has produced abstractions such as functional decomposition diagrams and the process maps of business process engineering and other UML depictions of systems and these are of course frequently useful and needed— albeit the understanding of “process,” as something that happens to “things” in these modeling diagrams is at odds with a more Heraclitean process philosophy. However, significantly, these diagrams miss a crucial factor: the human consciousness of the observer/user or commissioner/designer of the system (Allen & March, 2007). By human consciousness, of course, we mean not just the particular talents and skills of the human being who happens to occupy a particular job role—or function—at a particular time. Rather, it is the integral part of the system that an individual plays through their experience of its unfolding that is absent from Bunge’s ontology.

As shown in Table 4, in studies of requirements engineering, this comes to the fore in consideration of the problems inherent to large-scale projects where shifting priorities guide multiple stakeholders to adjust their requirements as projects unfold. The solutions that specialists are competent in implementing may determine which problems are to be addressed. Thus, “finding a workable solution-problem space pair” (Bergman et al., 2002 p. 169) in large-scale projects is unavoidably both technical and social. Software development—particularly in large-scale projects— has been hindered by the inscription of specific values in the requirements themselves, such as efficiency, fast decisions, and a focus on financial concerns and the pecuniary requirements of the sponsor (Clarke et. al., 2020). In recent years, there has been a growing focus on process in the systems development community. The more recent approaches of agile methodologies in software development, including narratives and the embracing of change, and the ideas around tailorable technologies (Germonprez et al., 2007; Germonprez et al., 2011) rather than narratives of workarounds and unfaithful adoption all offer alternative approaches that processual thinking can help underpin. With notions of built-in iterative feedback mechanisms, change over time, and incorporating the agency of the user to tailor the technology in the context of use, coupled with a clearer awareness of the persistence of systems in peoples’ lives even as those lives change, agile approaches and tailorable technologies represent changes in software development practice that are already underway, as exemplars—we would argue— of a more processual approach. What we offer in this paper, then, constitutes an opportunity for researchers who take this shift seriously to approach empirical studies of ongoing phenomena from a new perspective: a creative evolutionary process perspective.

This perspective then offers an opportunity from which to rethink agile, A/B testing, and component architectures. It may be that this perspective can provide an answer to the issues surrounding the application of agile methodologies (just like their Bungean forebears) to larger-scale projects, using commercial methodologies such as SAFe, LeSS, Scrum-at-Scale, DAD, and the Spotify Model (e.g. Berger & Beynon-Davies, 2009; Read & Briggs, 2012; Goh et. al., 2013), or indeed custom-built approaches scaled up from tried and tested agile methodologies used within organizations (see Edison et. al., 2021). Agile methods were fundamentally designed based on (1) reduced goals to accomplish and (2) short periods of time, so that any discrepancy can be identified as early as possible. But in large-scale projects, both the problem and solution spaces are additionally subject to constant change, and agile was designed for small but not large projects (Edison et. al., 2021). As Edison et al., (2021) suggest in their comprehensive review of the literature in this area, agile methodologies may prove tenaciously appropriate only at the small scale.

The traditional (pre-agile) means of achieving the coordination of multiple teams through “control from the top down by appointing a set of controllers responsible for ensuring procedures are adhered to using extensive standardized reporting over long periods of time” is antithetical to agile methodologies (Edison et. al., 2021 p. 20). Because of the multipolar nature of large-scale projects, as shown by Bergman et. al. (2002), such top-down control is itself frequently impossible to impose. Is the solution, perhaps, that large-scale IT projects should simply not be attempted, that only modular and distributed/connected systems with considered interoperability have a realistic chance of succeeding at scale (Kreps & Richardson, 2007)? Certainly, the problem of interteam coordination, as Edison et. al., (2021) conclude, is “a much more complex one in an agile era, and one that requires different solutions to those of the past” (p. 20). The design of an information system (particularly at large scale) must incorporate the flow of conscious time if it is to accurately and successfully reflect its true requirements as they evolve. Indeed, the term “requirements” itself may no longer be fit for purpose. It may be time for a new focus on the goals of those commissioning and building systems and a shift away from artifact creation toward a practice that orients itself towards engagement in unfolding processes pointed at the future. Such a practice would adopt the use, generally, of more processual language. For this, Bunge’s ontology is a barrier preventing relevance and an appreciation of an alternative ontology is essential. Adopting such a perspective overtly in a range of other contexts in IS may, we argue, generate insightful ways forward out of problems whose origin, we suggest, may also lie in the over-reliance on methodologies built exclusively on an entitative ontology.

## 5 Discussion

## 5.1 Value of a Creative Evolutionary Philosophy Perspective

While the principles of our perspective are certainly apparent in some IS scholarship and seem to apply, piecemeal, in many situations, we believe their more conscious consideration as a whole is likely to improve the quality of processual research more widely in IS by adding to its diversity (Mendling et al., 2021).

The essence of Creative Evolution (Bergson, 1907) is that we—as conscious beings—take part in the evolution of a universe that is, at every moment, new, and incomplete. In part, it is our capacity to choose that makes the future indeterminate, uncertain, and novel. It is a universe neither confined to our subjectivities nor determined wholly by objective realities; instead, it joins these perspectives in an ongoing motion in which they co-shape each other—neither a dualistic nor a causally closed monistic perspective. Bergmann et. al.’s (2002) analysis of RE acknowledges this. Fundamentally, a shift from entitative to creative evolution process thinking enables us to encounter the world from different grounds. This allows us to see things in new ways and gives us new language for speaking about process (e.g. our principles).

Other less-developed examples written by IS authors also implicitly display some of these principles. For example, we would contend that this is the case for the wakes of innovation (Boland et al., 2007). However, in all cases, we found P4 to be missing: the presence of our conscious agency and free will within the durational flow of change is both taken for granted and, at the same time, designed out of so many of the models that we use (Kreps & Rowe, 2021). Even some of the most interesting attention to process philosophy in IS tends more toward a Whiteheadian and less toward this more Bergsonian focus on the lived experience of consciousness. Ryden and El Sawy (2019) highlight the importance of “a broader ‘ambitemporal’ lens” for what they call “sense time.” Jarvenpaa and Välikangas, in presenting “a futuristic doomsday in which advanced technology has extinguished” what they call “inner time” (2020, p. 2), warn of the possibility of technology using humans, rather than the other way around. Fears around generative AI conjure similar nightmare scenarios. Retaining flexibility, therefore, for a creative evolution approach in the systems that we design and for the input and quality of human experience is very important if our systems are not to contribute to the closing off of opportunity for those who use them— with potentially apocalyptic results (Jarvenpaa & Välikangas, 2020; Kane et al., 2021). A certain technophilia (Coulthard & Keller, 2012) in IS circles needs to be addressed—and we should be mindful not to “fix” problems by making things worse. “Fixing,” indeed, from a process perspective, isn’t always the best approach. The concept of the permanent beta is indeed both timely and appropriate to many systems, and sometimes abstention and discontinuance are the right way forward (Page et al., 2018).

To follow our principles for a creative evolution process philosophy approach, IS researchers and practitioners need primarily to always remember the key significance of human consciousness, agency, sense time, and inner time—P4—regardless of how many of the other principles they may choose to adopt. Large-scale multipolar projects, as we have seen from the requirements engineering example, are a particular IS context where our principles—especially P4—would help practitioners both reach success rather than failure and ensure that humans can continue to use advanced technology “as their creative and collaborative artifact” (Jarvenpaa & Välikangas, 2020, p. 32) (our emphasis). In light of our principles, mobile app development designed for large-scale data harvesting is becoming even more of an ethical pariah than the implementation of GDPR has already made it. A blinkered focus on monetizing “user data” with no appreciation for the real human beings behind the data is something that is far easier when those human beings are only represented in function diagrams—where their “function” is to provide monetizable data: In such scenarios, we really are becoming “slaves to the algorithm” (Edwards & Veale, 2017; Ngwenyama et al., 2023).

The narratives in organizational change, moreover, once a more holistic balance between individual narratives and deeper structural narratives has been struck, offers IS scholars the opportunity to include stories in systems contexts that give faces and names to study situations rather than effacing them as mere “users.” Personalization takes on a capacity for much more effective reach in a range of organizational settings when flexible interactions are possible. Creative evolution process philosophy in action can enable scholars to approach phenomena with a novel combination of assumptions that as a cohesive set have been ignored until now. Defending inner time for free will and creativity is key for individuals, organizations, and society (Jarvenpaa & Välikangas, 2020). This focus on lived experience rather than abstract models or intellection offers a critical interpretivism counter to the managerialist instrumentalism of user acceptance of unquestioned innovation.

## 5.2 Implementation

What would be the methods that would be amenable to the principles we have outlined? This is for a different paper. In Table 5, we offer our understanding of some of the consequences of our principles for the implementation of process philosophy research in IS. The underpinning ethos is one of individuality in collectivity and agency in structure that is interpenetrating and mutually constitutive.

Building on this ethos, Table 5 offers orientations for IS researchers implied by each of the principles. Based on these orientations, research objects—be they the implementation of a new system, the development of a

new platform, or the adoption of new techniques or devices—can be studied using creative evolution process philosophy with methods developed for each context. Thus, P1 entails an acceptance that snapshots of the research object will likely be partial and diverse and that gaining a whole picture is neither the goal of the research nor, in truth, an attainable one. P2 entails approaching each snapshot with a perspective that seeks to understand the threads that brought it about, how the moment of the snapshot arose, and what implications for potential outcomes it carries. P3 enjoins researchers to engage with the motivations, values, and narratives of lived experience through the methods of their study rather than relying on abstract models. P4 stresses the significance of human timescale in the processes of autonomy, enjoining researchers to study new systems and their implementation for their effects on the delicate mechanisms of cogitation and deliberation. In this sense, creative evolution process philosophy offers an ethical stance in favor of human freedom from automation and datafication. Finally, P5 stresses the contingency of all elements of the research object, both in relation to each other and to the contexts in which they are severally located. Just as research should look back at where such elements arise from, so also should research look around at how other evolving processes interlock and co-determine the evolution of the processes in focus.

## 5.3 Possible Difficulties

Quite apart from the difficulty of moving from a “thing-ontology” approach to understanding reality— so habitual among not just IS but vast swathes of the scientific community—toward an “event-ontology” approach, there are possibly other difficulties that IS scholars may find with a process philosophy approach.

The event-ontology approach runs counter to the necessary intellection that enables us to engage with objects. The doublethink required—facing forward into unfolding duration with an intuitive understanding while simultaneously examining the ground behind us through the intellectual lens of object analysis (e.g. theorizing delegation mechanisms to autonomous systems)—is a skillset little appreciated, let alone taught, for its efficacy in dealing with the real problems managers face (Mingers, 2015).

The notion that depicting heterogeneous and evolving parts of phenomena is not only acceptable but sound may be quite shocking to a positivist sensibility (Kreps, 2018).

## 6 Conclusion

We have presented a perspective on reality as process that in some respects “contradicts earlier knowledge in the field providing opportunities for changing the path of knowledge or knowledge reconciliation,” (Grover & Niederman, 2021, p. 1766), at the same time offering an alternative to prior IS work that adopted a substantialist view or a view where process has priority. This is evidenced particularly in the example on requirements engineering. Thus, our creative evolution process philosophy perspective contributes to “theoretical diversity,” as set out in Rowe and Markus (2021) with an argument that process has primacy (Rescher, 1996) that can also be very relevant and hopefully convincing, in certain contexts, as a rival (Rowe & Markus, 2023) to prevailing perspectives in IS. It is likely that this approach will not be appropriate for some scholars, for some topics, and for some problems. However, the approach may help to inform several others, as we believe it encompasses a good deal of several other theoretical approaches while offering a slightly different perspective on each. It may be highly appropriate for particular research projects, such as studies of digital traces, user intention studies, tracing systems, and interpretation for a reality that is not yet well understood (e.g. interpretation of artificial intelligence outputs and work and organizational adaptation to AI), and can potentially offer a deeper understanding of business process narratives, relational studies concerned with an “ethic of care,” and doubtless many more. It is an approach, moreover, that will assist IS scholars and practitioners alike in ensuring that we avoid the kind of “endtime” (Jarvenpaa & Välikangas, 2020) and dystopian future that many authors in IS are beginning to warn of.

As we have seen in our Requirements Engineering illustration, processual approaches, in many cases, have deepened the understanding of change in information systems scholarship from a previous exclusive focus on entitative variance models through providing social and contextual views of technological change and allowing for subjectivity in the analysis of technical and organizational phenomena. The creative evolution process philosophy outlined in this paper furthers this causal explanation for technological phenomena to include universal, immanent, and everchanging material processes that incorporate pervasive and unfolding consciousness, and thus the substrate in which both subjectivities and their evolving technological and social structures are embedded in all their multiple and nonlinear complexity. Sociomaterial analyses are thus deepened and broadened by this ontological perspective.

Our creative evolution process philosophy principles can both (1) help us, as IS scholars and researchers, to be more critical about current aspects of digitalization that are suppressing consciousness and free will and (2) help us realize that reality is not only not fixed but that everything is related and continually ongoing—in particular, with respect to current developments in AI. Perhaps the key lesson from these insights, then, is that placing consciousness in duration at the heart of analyses of technological phenomena and change promises to underpin, ontologically and ethically, the much-needed shift away from “digital entrapment” (Ngwenyama et. al., 2023). If individuals and natural resources are seen as valued conscious beings rather than merely nodes in a program by which variance from investment to wealth might be achieved, the requirements of new technologies may begin to include “none,” “doing no harm,” and “for the public good” (Stahl, 2012) rather than simply “for profit.”

• Deriving common properties, themes, or patterns from a wider set of data. By definition, this leaves out much that does not quite fit the pattern.

## • Abstract theoretical model

• Models drawn up as an output of the activity of abstraction to represent theories about reality. As with abstraction, such models inevitably leave out many aspects of reality that do not conform to the model.

## • Consciousness

• Writing before Freud, Bergson did not mention the “unconscious”; consciousness in this paper, then, refers to both self-awareness and the (sub)conscious processes that underpin or shadow selfawareness. There is plenty of support for the experience of consciousness in nonhumans.

## • Duration

• The conscious experience of an event (over time), of related processes, rather than any particular “length”—which is itself a specifically spatial measure of time.

## • Imbrication

• A word used by new materialists and sociomaterialists to capture the overlapping, interpenetrating, and mutually co-constituting relationship between the social and the material

## • Scientific time

• Time that is measured in spatial terms—a “length” of time.
