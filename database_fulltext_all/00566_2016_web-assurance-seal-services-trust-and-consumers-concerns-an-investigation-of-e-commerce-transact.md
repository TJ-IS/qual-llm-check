# Web assurance seal services, trust and consumers’ concerns: an investigation of e-commerce transaction intentions across two nations

Dan J. Kim<sup>1</sup>, Myung-Seong Yim<sup>2</sup>, Vijayan Sugumaran<sup>3</sup> and H. Raghav Rao<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Information Technology and Decision Sciences, University of North Texas, Denton, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Department of Business Administration, Sahmyook University, Seoul, South Korea; <sup>3</sup>Department of Decision and Information Sciences, School of Business Administration, Oakland University, Rochester, U.S.A.; <sup>4</sup>Management Science and Systems, School of Management, University at Buffalo, Buffalo, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Vijayan Sugumaran, Department of Decision and Information Sciences, School of Business Administration, Oakland University, Rochester, MI 48309 U.S.A. Tel: +248-370-4649; Fax: +248-370-4275; E-mail: sugumara@oakland.edu

## Abstract

Trust is considered as a critical enabler in reducing consumer concerns regarding e-commerce transactions. Another enabler that helps reduce consumers’ concerns is Web Assurance Seal Services (WASS). We suggest that both factors help in the reduction of a critical hindrance to e-commerce, namely consumer concerns, and foster e-commerce transactions. Prior research has focused on trust in e-commerce, and separately, on the effectiveness of WASS within certain nations or cultures. However, given that e-commerce is now a global phenomenon we contend that the national or cultural characteristics of consumers are important to understand. This comparative national research attempts to fill this gap. This study makes the following contributions: It identifies a hindrance (i.e., consumers’ concerns), and two enablers (i.e., effectiveness of WASS and trust) in e-commerce technologies as a shopping channel (i.e., trust in e-Channel). It proposes consumers’ concerns for e-commerce as a second-order three-dimensional construct (i.e., security, privacy, and business integrity concern) and compares the effects of trust in e-Channel and WASS on consumers’ e-commerce transaction intention in two different national/ cultural contexts (i.e., the U.S.A. and South Korea). The results of the study indicate that the perceived effectiveness of WASS of the U.S. consumers has a strong positive impact on their transaction intention and has a strong negative influence on their concerns for e-commerce. In contrast, Korean consumers’ perceived effectiveness of WASS does not significantly influence their transaction intention and their concerns for e-commerce. The results of group comparison analysis confirm that the strength of perceived effectiveness of WASS of the U.S. consumers is significantly stronger than that of Korean consumers. Interpretations from a cross-national perspective, theoretical and practical implications as well as limitations are discussed.

## Introduction

With electronic commerce (hereafter e-commerce) becoming a mainstay in the global service management economy, companies are trying to maximize their revenue by providing not only quality products but also making it easy to buy them online. Potential consumers visiting an e-commerce website should feel safe in that their personal information will be protected and their business transactions will be executed correctly and they will get their goods in a timely manner. However, consumers are often reluctant to purchase on the web because their perceived concerns regarding e-commerce transactions are relatively higher as compared with the traditional mode of shopping. Because of this reason, previous literature in e-commerce has reported online consumers’ concerns as a major hindrance for making electronic commerce transactions (hereafter e-transactions) (Fram & Grady, 1997; Khazanchi & Sutton, 2001; Hoy & Phelps, 2003; Moores, 2005; Kim et al, 2008a).

When online consumers have concerns regarding e-transactions, trust plays an important role in diminishing their concerns (Arceneaux, 1994; Gefen, 2000; Kim et al, 2008a). As an enabler of successful e-transactions, trust has received considerable attention in the e-commerce context (Zhou, 2011). In addition, there is another enabler to reduce consumers’ concerns and increase e-commerce transaction activities when they have little knowledge of sellers (Sharma & Lijuan, 2014),Web Assurance Seal Services (WASS). WASS have been used as one of the main tools to convey to consumers the legitimacy of a business and gain their con<sup>fi</sup>dence by decreasing their concerns regarding e-commerce. The basic function of web seals is to provide a third party view of the trustworthiness of a company so that growth in e-commerce activities is not hampered by consumer fears (Stephens, 2004).Web seal services such as Verisign, WebTrust, TRUSTe, BBBOnline, and so on help consumers feel more comfortable with the e-transaction process and ensure that the company follows through with its promises (Kim et al, 2004). Previous studies (e.g., (Sivasailam et al, 2002; Kim et al, 2004) categorized assurance seal services into three types: security (e.g., Verisign, WebTrust), privacy (e.g., WebTrust, TRUSTe, BBB Online Privacy), and business integrity (e.g., BBB Online Reliability, BizRate.com certi<sup>fi</sup>ed). Appendix A summarizes three assurance types of WASS, the issues, and potential solutions related to the issues. These three assurance types are generally the concerns of online consumers when they make an e-commerce transaction with selling parties on the web (Kim et al, 2004). Following the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the previous studies, this study proposes consumers’ concern for e-commerce as a second order construct, which consists of three concerns for e-commerce transactions (i.e., security, privacy and business integrity).

Several studies have been reported in the literature regarding the impact of web assurance seals and the results are mixed (e.g., Cranor et al, 1999; Houston & Taylor, 1999; Kovar et al, 2000; Kimery & McCord, 2002; Kim et al, 2004; Zhang, 2004). While some studies have found value in using web assurance seals, others indicate that they may not have much effect on consumer trust for e-commerce related activities (e.g., McKnight et al, 2004; Sinason & Webber, 2011). How people perceive WASS depends on a number of factors. The effect of online consumers’ perception of the effectiveness of WASS on their general concerns in e-commerce and their e-commerce transaction intention is thus a topic that needs further investigation.

Prior research has focused on web assurance dimensions (e.g., Kim et al, 2008b) and the impact of WASS (e.g., Zhang, 2004). However, to the best of our knowledge, the national or cultural characteristics of consumers have not been considered in this context. As e-commerce becomes more global, it is more important to understand the existence and nature of cultural differences on web assurance in e-commerce. We argue that the national/cultural perspective is necessary in understanding online consumers and how they perceive WASS within the market structure and its entities. This research attempts to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap.

In two different national culture contexts (i.e., the U.S.A. and South Korea), this study investigates the competing relationship between hindrance (i.e., concerns for e-commerce) and enablers (i.e., trust and WASS service) of e-transactions and how trust and WASS service help in decreasing the hindrance of successful e-transactions. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this study tackles the following research question: how online consumers in two culturally distinctive countries perceive the roles of trust in e-Channel and the effectiveness of WASS in alleviating the concerns about e-transactions and transaction intentions?

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The section ‘Theoretical background’ provides the theoretical background. In particular, it discusses the four entities of e-commerce and the role of culture, the cultural characteristics of the United States and Korea, as well as WASS as a third-party assurance mechanism to reduce concerns and increase online purchasing. The section ‘Research model and hypotheses’ discusses the research model of the study and the hypotheses developed. The section ‘Methodology and data collection’ presents the methodology used and the data collection procedure. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it discusses the measures and the survey procedure employed. The section ‘Data analysis and results’ provides data analysis and the results that include measurement model testing, structural model testing, and group comparison analysis. Finally, the paper ends with the discussion and conclusion section. This section also includes the limitations of this work as well as future research.

## Theoretical background

## Four entities of e-commerce and culture

The ongoing rapid growth in the popularity of the Internet is having a revolutionary impact on the way companies do business (Runyan et al, 2008). However, the rapid diffusion of e-commerce continues to be hamstrung by persistent concerns of consumers about the safety of transacting over the Internet (Fisher & Chu, 2009). Evidence suggests that consumers often hesitate to transact with Web-based vendors because of uncertainty about vendor behavior or the perceived risk of having personal information stolen by hackers (McKnight et al, 2002).Other studies have suggested that consumer online purchasing is hampered by fears about the security of information transmitted over the Internet, legitimacy of the business behind the website and the privacy of personal information (Mauldin & Arunachalam, 2002). Furthermore, consumers are reluctant to purchase online because of various risks speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the Internet as a distribution channel (Nikitkov, 2006). Lack of trust in Web vendors can deter consumer adoption of e-commerce (McKnight et al, 2002). Of vital importance in all e-commerce research is the role of consumer concerns and perceived risks in relation to the likelihood of purchase (Noteberg et al, 2003).

Business-to-Consumer (B2C) e-commerce is dependent on: the consumer (trustor), the seller (trustee) and the environment (technology and third party). In addition to examining these four, in this study, we argue that a <sup>fi</sup>fth in<sup>fl</sup>uence – national culture is a critical element that would have an impact on e-commerce transaction intention (See Figure 1). Cross-cultural impacts on consumer behavior in online shopping have not been generally considered in the MIS literature, though there have been studies related to traditional shopping experiences (Lee et al, 2007a).

A major factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the successful proliferation of e-commerce is people’s trust in Internet vendors (Jarvenpaa et al, 1999; Gefen, 2000; Kim et al, 2008a). Trust is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in terms of reliance on the integrity, veracity, or reliability of a person (or thing) to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a promise or complete a task (Moores, 2005). The presence of trust is an effective factor for reducing or simplifying the complexity of consumer decision making (Ho & Oh, 2009). Increased trust toward a seller has a positive impact on future interaction, loyalty, purchasing, and commitment intentions (Noteberg et al, 2003; Kassim & Asiah Abdullah, 2010). Thus, the need for consumer trust has been emphasized heavily, especially in the area of electronic commerce (Noteberg et al, 2003).

An electronic market structure supported by information technology facilitates the communication and exchange between buyers, sellers, and third parties. In B2C e-commerce, buyers are individual consumers and sellers could be online retailers, intermediaries or suppliers. Third parties are impartial organizations (Lekkas et al, 1999). Many trusted third parties such as banks, credit card authorization companies facilitate online transactions for e-commerce. To be successful, B2C vendors should disclose their information gathering and dissemination practices, and their disclosure should be backed by credible third-party assurance (Benassi, 1999). As the reach of e-commerce is becoming truly worldwide, the B2C space is no longer limited to a particular country or location, but is open to the global market. This brings in the varying cultural background of the consumers into play (Liu et al, 2004; Gefen et al, 2005; Greenberg et al, 2008). Thus, along with the four entities of the e-commerce markets mentioned above. there is a need to consider culture as another environmental element that affects the global e-commerce market and its impact on the other entities. Cultural Dimensions and Two Distinctive Cultures of the U.S.A. and Korea

Hofstede (1980a) de<sup>fi</sup>ned culture as ‘the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one human group from another’ (p. 125). Hofstede distinguished countries from each other in terms of four clusters. These became the Hofstede dimensions of national culture: power distance, individualism vs collectivism, masculinity vs femininity, and uncertainty avoidance (Choi & Geistfeld, 2004).

Prior studies support the idea that behavioral models do not always hold across cultures (Hovav & D’Arcy, 2012; Warkentin et al, 2015). Culture in<sup>fl</sup>uences individual perceptions and forms shared beliefs (Park, 2008). In addition, culture provides people with sets of acceptable behaviors and norms within a society (Park, 2008). For example, Park & Jun (2003) found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the Internet usage and perceived risks of online shopping in a comparative analysis of the U.S. and Korean consumers; the Korean sample showed longer internet usage per week and higher perceived risks of online shopping than the U.S. sample (Hwang et al, 2006). However, typically, research has not focused on whether differences in cultures are manifested in the effectiveness of WASS; we use the phrase ‘perceived Effectiveness of WASS’ and ‘Effectiveness of WASS’ interchangeably to avoid monotony. We argue that while e-commerce activities in the B2C domain are similar, often countries have vastly different cultures and this is important to consider while conducting research in the e-commerce area.

In this paper, we chose to compare the U.S. and Korean consumers for several reasons. First, the United States and Korea are leading countries in terms of Internet usage and e-commerce (Lee et al, 2007b). According to an OECD report, the Internet subscribers per 100 inhabitants of the countries are well above the OECD average (Park & Jun, 2003). Second, the two countries are culturally very different from one another and thus could provide revealing evidence about cultural effect on user attitudes towards e-transactions. The United States and Korea represent nearly opposite positions on the dimensions of culture as discussed by Geert Hofstede (http://geert-hofstede.com/). It should be noted that Hofstede’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings were derived from research conducted nearly three decades ago. However, more recent work using different international population supports the stability of Hofstede’s scores (Hovav & D’Arcy, 2012). Especially, based on Hofstede’s (1980a, 1991, 2001) dimensions of culture scales, where countries were given scores from 1–100, the U.S. culture had an extremely high individualism score (91 out of a 100) while the Korean society was found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly less individualistic and more of a collectivist society (Bang et al, 2005). In other words, Korea was included in the ‘low individualism’ cluster, while the United States was in the ‘high individualism’ cluster. Individualism pertains to societies in which the ties between individuals are loose (Hofstede, 1991, p. 51). Everyone is expected to look after himself or herself and his or her immediate family (Hofstede, 1991, p. 51). Collectivism pertains to societies in which people from birth onwards are integrated into strong, cohesive groups, which throughout people’s lifetime continue to protect them in exchange for unquestioning loyalty (Hofstede, 1991, p. 51). In addition, the fact that the authors have access to potential research subjects in both countries and have a good understanding of the two cultures and languages also made such comparative research feasible.

Along with individualism-collectivism, low-context vs high-context culture is another distinctive cultural dimension between the United States and Korea. Context culture refers to the tendency of how people and their society seek information and knowledge (Hall, 1976; Hall & Hall, 1990). High- vs low-context leads to communication differences among cultures (Lee et al, 2007b). A high-context culture is one in which people are deeply involved with each other (Lee et al, 2007b). On the other hand, a low-context culture is one in which people are highly individualized, somewhat alienated, and fragmented, and there is relatively little involvement with others (Lee et al, 2007b). Korea belongs to the high-context culture, while the United States has a lowcontext culture (Jin et al, 2008).

Given this understanding, it is reasonable to suggest that cultural differences between the United States and Korea may in<sup>fl</sup>uence how online consumers evaluate the effectiveness of WASS. Therefore, the effect of the national cultural dimensions of individualism-collectivism and low context and high context are considered in this paper.

## Web assurance seal services (WASS) as a third-party institutional mechanism

E-commerce vendors have to act purposefully to overcome consumer perceptions of uncertainty and risk by building trust – both in their own websites and in the broader

Internet environment (McKnight et al, 2002). Web assurance seeks to reduce these information risks and increase online purchasing by improving the reliability of certain information on the website (Mauldin & Arunachalam, 2002). Web assurance is related to online consumers’ perceptions of e-commerce sites, that is, how a website assures its consumers to make transactions using that site. Web assurance includes that the site discloses and follows through with its operating practices; it handles payments in a secure and reliable way; it has certain return policies; it complies with a privacy policy that outlines what it can and cannot to do with the collected personal data (Koreto, 1997; Castelfranchi & Tan, 2001).

Assurance services can be described as activities conducted by trusted, independent organizations (private or not for pro<sup>fi</sup>t) to certify and/or validate business transactions between trading partners and/or trading partners and consumers (Khazanchi & Sutton, 2001). Especially, WASS for online vendor’s website are offered by third party certifying bodies (e.g., banks, accountants, consumer unions, and computer companies), which are designed to assure that the e-retailer’s behavior will be consistent with accepted standards in e-commerce. WASS help instill trust in the e-commerce vendor by verifying that the vendor’s website has a policy about its collection and use of personally identi<sup>fi</sup>- able information (Mauldin & Arunachalam, 2002; Moores & Dhillon, 2003; Wu et al, 2010; Bansal & Fatemeh‘Mariam’Zahedi, 2015). Thus, the presence of web assurance seals can be considered a means by which consumer trust in an Internet store is increased through a reduced sense of uncertainty and risk (Noteberg et al, 2003).

Lesser known e-vendors have used various strategies to signal their trustworthiness and to ease shoppers’ concerns over online shopping (Wu et al, 2010). One such strategy involves Web assurance seals that are provided by a third party and act as a means to guarantee consumers’ private information (Park et al, 2010).The independent third party or assuror attests to an online merchant’s compliance with speci<sup>fi</sup>ed criteria, with the aim of providing the consumer with a degree of assurance regarding aspects of a vendor’s online business practices (Fisher & Chu, 2009). Such third parties are impartial organizations that incorporate mechanisms delivering business con<sup>fi</sup>dence through an electronic transaction and using commercial and technical security features (Kim et al, 2005).Vendors who meet the assuror’s criteria are entitled to have a clickable ‘seal’ displayed on their website for a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed period of time (Fisher & Chu, 2009). Consumers will be able to access the website owners’ business policies, trust-assuring arguments, and the auditor’s opinion regarding business practice disclosures, transaction integrity, and information protection by clicking on the seal of assurance on the web page (Pacini & Sinason, 1999; Kim & Benbasat, 2009).

This study proposes consumer’s concerns regarding e-commerce as a second-order construct that consists of three <sup>fi</sup>rst-order sub-dimensional constructs (i.e., security, privacy, business integrity). Compared with prior studies covering trust and concerns in e-commerce in crosscultural context, the present study is novel in terms of <sup>fi</sup>ve perspectives: consumer trust, WASSs, consumer’s concerns, dimensions of concerns, and cross-cultural context. The study by Jarvenpaa et al, (1999) dealt with trust in store and risk perception from a cross-cultural context. Their study, however, did not cover any assurance mechanisms including WASSs nor any sub-dimensions of concerns regarding e-commerce. Grazioli and Jarvenpaa (2000) covered trust in e-commerce site, perceived risk, and assurance mechanisms including assurance seals, news clips, warranties, and physical presence, but this is not a cross-cultural study nor does it cover subdimensional elements of risk or concern. The study conducted by Kassim and Asiah Abdullah (2010) is limited in scope. The study covers the assurance construct but actually what they measured is security concern; they did not cover any deterrent elements of e-commerce.

## The relationship between perceived concerns and e-commerce transaction intention

Online consumers’ reluctance to make e-transactions is partly because of the fact that their concerns for shopping on the Internet are relatively high (Kim et al, 2008b). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, security, privacy, and business integrity concerns are the main reasons online consumers give up purchasing over the Internet (Gefen, 2000; Kim et al, 2008a).

Security is one of the key assurance dimensions in e-commerce transaction because without an appropriate level of security, consumers are reluctant to use e-commerce channels (Kim, 2008). How a consumer perceives security protection when making online transactions depends on how clearly he or she understands the level of security measures implemented by the seller (Kim et al, 2008a). When an ordinary consumer <sup>fi</sup>nds security features and protection mechanisms in the seller's Website, he or she can recognize the seller’s intention to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the security requirements during online transactions (Kim et al, 2008a). This helps the buyer to make a purchase decision since all the above artifacts will emphasize that the vendor is making efforts to earn the consumer’s con<sup>fi</sup>dence and diminish the amount of risk that the consumer perceives (Kim et al, 2008a).

Privacy, along with security, is another critical dimension of information assurance on the web. Privacy concern in online transactions refers to consumers’ fear about the lack of protection of individually identi<sup>fi</sup>able information on the Internet or consumers’ worry to share information over the Internet (Limbu et al, 2011). While e-commerce sales are expected to grow rapidly, privacy concerns are a prominent public policy challenge (Hoy & Phelps, 2003; Moores, 2005). This is because consumers share personal and <sup>fi</sup>nancial data with the retailer on nearly all interactions and expect con<sup>fi</sup>dential treatment of their information (Limbu et al, 2011). In the online arena, the core principles of privacy include: disclosure of information collection, and dissemination practices; providing the consumer with a choice with respect to how their information may be used; measures to protect the integrity of personal information and preclude their unauthorized disclosure, and protecting children (Kim et al, 2004). Invasion of privacy is usually interpreted as the unauthorized collection, disclosure, or other use of personal information as a direct result of e-commerce transactions (Wang et al, 1998).

Business integrity is another essential component of information assurance, which relates to how businesses use the collected information (Hoy & Phelps, 2003). With the exponential proliferation of online scams and fake e-commerce websites, online consumers are always worried about getting cheated or providing sensitive information to crooks that perpetrate identity theft. The integrity of the online businesses is a major factor in determining whether a consumer engages in commerce activity with the business. Thus, consumers worried about the legitimacy of an online business might demand some explicit demonstration of the integrity of the business through web assurance seal services.

Considering these three concerns in information assurance literature discussed above, we conceptualize online consumers’ concerns for e-transactions as a higher second-order formative construct, which includes three <sup>fi</sup>rstorder elements: concern of security, privacy, and business integrity. We contend that when online consumers across different countries have concerns on security, privacy, and business integrity of e-transactions, they are reluctant to make e-transactions. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Consumers’ concerns for e-commerce have a negative effect on e-commerce transaction intention across the U.S. A. and Korean consumers.

## The effectiveness of WASS on concerns for e-commerce and e-commerce transaction intention

The emergence of e-commerce has encouraged various groups to provide an atmosphere of safety and reliability to users of the Internet (Runyan et al, 2008). When a user approaches an e-commerce website, he or she wants to get an idea about how good the online business is (Hoffman et al, 1999). On the basis of the information provided by the online business on the website, the user tries to develop an opinion about the legitimacy of the business and ultimately to form an intention to make an e-commerce transaction (Kim et al, 2008a). Without the presence of visible trustworthiness standards for consumers to distinguish the legitimacy and credibility of the e-vendors, consumers will be concerned (Ho & Oh, 2009). For this reason, WASS are used to enable consumers to distinguish credible e-vendors from others (Ho & Oh, 2009; Özkan et al, 2010).

To mitigate consumers’ concerns on security breaches, privacy violations for exposing consumer records, and poor business integrity for unful<sup>fi</sup>lled orders coupled with lax consumer service, a variety of WASS are offered by associations and/or corporations (Kim et al, 2004). The fundamental objectives of WASS are to reduce consumer’s perceived risks, assess internal controls, and increase buyer’s con<sup>fi</sup>dence or trust in e-transactions. These goals are achieved by providing businesses, trading partners, consumers, and the public at large with the assurance that commercial transactions conducted electronically using the Internet, private networks, and web-based systems are safe, secure, and backed by appropriate internal controls (Khazanchi & Sutton, 2001). Thus, online consumers’ perception about the effectiveness of WASS can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence their intentions to make e-transactions; their perceived effectiveness of WASS signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduces their concerns for e-commerce. Therefore, we posit that:

Perceived effectiveness of WASS has a positive effect on <sup>:</sup>e-commerce transaction intention across the U.S.A. and Korean consumers.

Perceived effectiveness of WASS has a negative effect on <sup>:</sup>consumers’ concerns for e-commerce across the U.S.A. and Korean consumers.

Regarding the impact of national culture on e-commerce behavior, several studies (e.g., Kim, 2008; Yoon, 2009) showed how culture plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the way people perceive and approach a multilingual e-commerce website; how national culture in<sup>fl</sup>uences information-seeking behavior (Zaheer & Zaheer, 1997). As discussed earlier, the two distinctive national level cultural variables are individualism-low context culture and collectivism-high context culture.

Individualism-collectivism refers to the extent to which members of a culture tend to have an independent vs interdependent construal of the self (Park & Jun, 2003). These cultural values in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumption related behaviors (Park & Jun, 2003). In collective and high-context cultures, group bonds and harmony are viewed as important (Park & Jun, 2003). Thus, people from collectivistic countries prefer not to stand out and are more discreet. Collectivists also tend to emphasize feelings or emotion, and social acceptance when making a decision (Choi & Geistfeld, 2004). They are more likely to be conformists, adopt other’s opinions, and be involved in others’ lives. On the contrary, in individual and low-context cultures, explicit communication and clear procedures are preferred (Park & Jun, 2003). Thus, people from individualistic countries are more independent and strive to be unique individuals. Individualists also seek to satisfy personal goals when considering the attributes, alternatives, and resources associated with a decision (Choi & Geistfeld, 2004). Furthermore, collectivists might be relationship-oriented and content-oriented, whereas individualists might be action-oriented or tool-oriented.

Individualism is very often connected to low-context communication, whereas collectivism very often is connected to high-context communication (Hermeking, 2005). Thus, members of low-context cultures have less personal contact with each other, while members of highcontext cultures have closer and more familiar contact with each other (Hermeking, 2005). When people seek information and knowledge, members in a high-context culture obtain information from personal networks such as friends and relatives while members in a low-context culture <sup>fi</sup>nd information from direct information sources such as consumer reports, articles and databases.

Individuals may have varying degrees of informationseeking behavior because of their different cultural backgrounds (Zaheer & Zaheer, 1997). People who have similar cultural background may have similar perceptions on assurance dimensions and the effect of perceptions on the effectiveness of WASS compared with those who have different cultural background. More individualistic and low-context cultures tend to show a certain independence of thought and action, and a lower level of propensity to share information compared with more collectivistic and high context cultures (Earley, 1989; Zaheer & Zaheer, 1997). Owing to the global nature of e-transactions and cultural in<sup>fl</sup>uence on informationseeking behavior, consumers’ perceptions of privacy, security, and business integrity vary across cultures (Milberg et al, 1995; Milberg et al, 2000; Kobsa, 2002). For example, consumers in individualistic and low social context-culture view an individual as an independent and autonomous entity from groups and seek information about decisions from direct and formal information sources while those in collectivistic and high social context culture consider an individual as a part of interdependent social unit and their decisions are strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the group norm and other members’ opinions.

Security, privacy and business integrity are consumers’ perceptions on the online transactions (Roman, 2007). Thus, in order to reduce concerns on security, privacy, and business integrity in e-commerce websites, online consumers in an individualistic and low social context culture are more likely to seek information from formal information sources such as WASS to reduce their concerns. In contrast, online consumers in collectivistic and high social contexts are more likely to obtain information from informal sources such as personal networks. With respect to Hofstede’s cultural score (Hofstede, 1980b; Hofstede, 1994; Hofstede, 2001), the United States has higher level of individualism score than Korea. The United States is considered to have low social context while Korea has high social context culture (Kim, 2008). Thus, it is expected that the effectiveness of WASS will be stronger in the United States than Korea as an information assurance mechanism. Thus, we hypothesize that:

Perceived effectiveness of WASS on e-commerce trans-<sup>:</sup>action intention is stronger for the U.S.A. consumers than for Korean consumers.

Perceived effectiveness of WASS on consumers’ con-<sup>:</sup>cerns regarding e-commerce is stronger for the U.S.A. consumers than for Korean consumers.

## The effects of trust on concerns for e-commerce and transaction intention

Many studies on trust in e-Channel <sup>fi</sup>nd that lack of consumers’ trust is a major impediment in the adoption of e-commerce because they are unlikely to make an e-commerce transaction using the Internet as a shopping channel that fails to convey a sense of its trustworthiness (e.g., Jarvenpaa et al, 2000; Gefen, 2002; McKnight & Chervany, 2002; Li et al, 2006; Kim et al, 2009; Fang et al, 2011; Kim, 2014). According to Sharma & Lijuan (2014), high levels of trust lead to more consumer participation in e-transactions, since it makes consumers comfortable sharing personal information and providing the payment information such as credit card numbers over the Internet in advance. Because of this reason, online vendors consider trust as an important determinant and an enabler of success of e-commerce transaction through creating a positive attitude toward the purchase behavior in e-commerce, which in turn leads to transaction intentions (Jarvenpaa et al, 2000; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). With higher degree of trust, online consumers would be thinking about forming an intention to participate in e-transactions (Kim et al, 2008a).

Online consumers often <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves in uncertain risky situations in e-commerce including sale of fake or defective online products, fake photos and misleading descriptions on websites, failure of e-vendors to deliver the right merchandise that they ordered in a timely manner, sending something of lesser value than advertised, and high handling and shipping costs (Fang et al, 2011). For them to make an e-commerce transaction, they expect web technology to be a reliable means as a shopping channel and believe e-vendors should behave in a manner similar to brick-and-mortar business and ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their purchase request. In these risky situations, trust alleviates consumers’ concerns on e-commerce uncertainty, vulnerability, dependence, and potential opportunism of information asymmetry by the selling parties (Fang et al, 2011). Therefore, once online consumers are assured of a reasonable level of trust, it decreases their concerns for e-commerce and they won’t hesitate to make e-transactions (Koreto, 1998; Kim et al, 2008a). Thus, we posit that:

Online consumer’s trust in e-Channel has a positive effect on e-commerce transaction intention across the U.S.A. and Korean consumers.

Online consumer’s trust in e-Channel has a negative effect on consumers’ concerns for e-commerce across the U.S.A. and Korean consumers.

## Methodology and data collection

To test the proposed hypotheses and to validate the research model, we collected data using Web-based surveys across two culturally different countries (i.e., the U.S.A. and Korea). Web-based surveys have many advantages over traditional methods: they are convenient, cheap, fast, more accurate, and can survey hard-to-reach respondents. On the other hand, there are some limitations: unequal opportunity and self-selection for participation (McCullough, 1998; David, 2000). Even though only respondents who were able to access the Internet could participate in this survey, this bias is exactly what is desired of the data for respondents of this study, since it provides data about the actual online consumers who are already familiar with the Internet and e-transactions. That is why a Web-based survey was chosen.

## Measures

To <sup>fi</sup>nd appropriate measures of the constructs of the study, we comprehensively reviewed the literature. A series of survey questions were adapted from previous literature and revised to provide insight on online consumers’ perceptions regarding effectiveness of WASS, concerns for e-commerce assurance dimensions (i.e., privacy, security, and business integrity), general trust in e-commerce, and e-commerce transaction intention. Following the recommendation by Anderson & Gerbing (1984), we measured each construct by multiple observable indicators – four or <sup>fi</sup>ve items per construct. The items were written as English statements or questions. Most of the items used a 7-point Likert scale system ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). After the measures were developed, a panel of four experts reviewed them to ensure their validity and to identify any ambiguous items. A small pilot test was also conducted before collecting data for the <sup>fi</sup>eld test. The <sup>fi</sup>nal measurement items are presented in Appendix B. The English questionnaire was translated by a bilingual professor at a university in the United States into Korean and then translated back into English by another bilingual professor to cross check.

## Survey procedure

The approval by a university level human subject review board was given to collect data and standard humansubjects protocols were followed. Participants were recruited at several public and private universities in the United States and Korea. From those universities, 437 and 762 students in the United States and Korea respectively participated in the survey, which took place outside of regular class time. They were offered extra credit. College students were used as participants because the largest demographic block of the e-commerce users is those young ages (e.g., Pedersen, 2005; Rainie, 2010; Xu, 2010; Lowry et al, 2011).

For validity check purposes, we added a series of questions to check the validity of response. Since this study mainly focuses on online consumers’ general e-commerce transaction experience, their perceptions on web assurance dimensions and the effects of assurance dimensions on the importance of WASS across national cultures, respondents must have e-commerce transaction experiences. The survey participants had to report whether they had made any e-transactions earlier. They also provided information about the name of online shopping company that they used for their very last e-commerce transaction, the items purchased from the shopping site, and how much they spent. In order to <sup>fi</sup>lter out some random and false reporting errors, we also added questions such as ‘please do not answer this question’. Respondents who answered these questions were excluded. After eliminating invalid and incomplete responses, a total of 383 and 659 U.S. and Korean samples respectively remained. Since the focus of this study is on web assurance dimensions, the effectiveness of WASS and its effect on e-commerce transaction, the participants were also asked to report their awareness of WASS. If they were unaware of any WASS, their survey responses were <sup>fi</sup>ltered out. About 26 and 19% of the U.S. and Korean participants are aware of WASS, respectively. After <sup>fi</sup>ltering out respondents who were not aware of WASS, a total of 104 U.S. and 124 Korean samples were used for data analysis.

Regarding the minimum requirement for sample size, it is recommended to be ten times either (a) the latent construct with the largest number of predictors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing it or (b) the block with the largest number of formative indicators (Temme et al, 2006). The research model has one formative construct, ‘Concerns for e-commerce’ having three formative indicators. The construct having the largest number of independent variables is ‘ecommerce Transaction Intention’, which has three input constructs. Thus, a total of 104 and 124 samples for the U. S. and Korean models respectively are higher than the required minimum sample size. To con<sup>fi</sup>rm the required sample size, we also conducted a power analysis using the G\*Power 3.1.6 tool. With P-value = 0.01 and f <sup>2</sup> = 0.13 – medium effect based on Cohen’s (1988) criteria, the required sample sizes for the case of three different powers – 0.70, 0.80 and 0.90 are 58, 70 and 90, respectively. Therefore, the sample size of the study more than adequately meets the minimum requirements.

## Data analysis and results

## Measurement model testing

To ensure the appropriateness of the research instrument, extensive analyses were performed to examine the convergent validity, reliability, and discriminant validity of the scales, to ensure that multi-collinearity was not an issue, and to check for common-method bias using the latest techniques appropriate for re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs.

An acceptable level of convergent validity is when all item loadings are greater than 0.50 (Wixom & Watson, 2001) and the items for each construct load onto only one factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1.0. Even though all survey questions were adapted from previous research and revised to <sup>fi</sup>t the context of this study, we conducted two Exploratory Factor Analyses (EFAs) of both data sets (the U.S. and Korean samples) to verify discriminant validity of the scales. The results of EFAs are summarized in Table 1, and reveal the same six distinct factors in both data sets. All of the items loaded highly (above 0.60) onto a factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1.51.

The descriptive statistics (mean and standard error), reliability, correlation, and covariance of constructs of the U.S. and Korean data are summarized in Table 2. Please note that although we propose Concerns for e-commerce as a secondorder construct combing three formative indicators (i.e., security, privacy and business integrity concerns), we report descriptive statistics, reliability and validity of each formative construct separately to show each concern is independent. In terms of the reliability of the constructs, we applied the two most conservative criteria: the Cronbach’s α and the composite reliability. The Cronbach’s α and composite reliability coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of all constructs are higher than the recommended threshold (0.70) (Nunnally, 1978; Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994; Kock, 2010). These values indicate strong reliabilities. We therefore conclude that our data exhibits adequate reliabilities overall.

To test discriminant validity, we also used two approaches: examining the factor loadings of measures and the square roots of the AVEs described in (Gefen & Straub, 2005; Lowry et al, 2009). First, along with convergent validity test, we examined the factor loadings of all components across two data sets (see Table 2). We found that there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor overloading across components, which is an evidence of discriminant validity because it ensures that no cross-loading exists between the constructs. Second, we also examined the square roots of the AVEs. As suggested in (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Staples et al, 1999), the square root of the AVE of constructs should be higher than any of the correlations involving the construct. The bolded numbers shown in the diagonal are the square roots of the AVEs, which are higher than any of the correlations in the row (see Table 2). Strong discriminant validity was shown for all constructs across the two data sets. On the basis of these results, we can be sure that our data exhibits strong discriminant validity.

SEM methodologies recently have addressed the potential deleterious effects of multi-collinearity and common method bias (Podsakoff et al, 2003; Cenfetelli & Bassellier, 2009). In order to address the multi-collinearity issue, which is not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in our data sets, we assessed the possibility of multi-collinearity among all the indicators in the model across the data sets. Bagozzi et al (1991) proposed that if the correlations among constructs are all below 0.7, it indicates that multi-collinearity is not a potential problem in the model. According to the results of correlations presented in Table 2, the highest correlation is 0.673, which is lower than the suggested value. Common method bias or common method variance represents the amount of spurious shared variance in the data that occurs because a single method is used for data collection (Buckley et al, 1990; Podsakoff et al, 2003). To check common method bias (CMB), we used two approaches: checking whether any of the correlations were above 0.9 and testing Harman’s single-factor suggested by Podsakoff et al (2003). As presented in Table 2, all correlations among constructs are below this threshold. Harman’s single-factor test shows the amount of spurious covariance shared among variables. The EFA of both the U.S. and Korea data revealed six factors with the eigenvalue higher than 1 (please see Table 1). The second and most rigorous approach to testing CMB was conducted by following Podsakoff et al (2003) using Smart PLS.

We checked the ratio of two models both with and without a latent common method variance factor. As summarized in Appendix C, most method factor loadings are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The average substantive factor loading and average variance explained for the substantive factor loading are 0.8553 and 73% for the U. S. model and 0.8454 and 72% for the Korean model,− respectively. In contrast, the average method-based fac-− tor loading and average variance explained for the method factor loading are 0.0083 and 0.8% for the U.S. model and 0.0239 and 0.6% for the Korean model respectively. The ratios of substantive variance to method variance for the U.S. and Korean models are approximately 93:1 and 112:1 respectively. Given that the data sets passed both tests of common method bias and multi-collinearity, we conclude that multi-collinearity and common method bias have little in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the research model of the study.

## Structural model testing

After testing the measurement model, we analysed the hypothesized paths in the structural model with SmartPLS 2.0.M3 using the two cross-cultural data sets. All measurement items are treated as re<sup>fl</sup>ective manifest variables except concerns for e-commerce, which is a second order formative construct including three <sup>fi</sup>rstorder elements: security, privacy, and business integrity concern. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the path estimates was calculated using the bootstrap technique with 500 resamples. The explanatory power of the model is assessed through the $R ^ { 2 }$ scores. We also explored the effects of three control variables on the model across the countries. Figures 3a and b summarize the testing results of the model using the U.S. and Korean data sets. As shown in Figures 3a and b, the results are to some extent consistent and contrasting across two country data. As expected, consumers’ concerns for e-commerce have a very strong negative effect on their intention to make e-transactions across two countries, supporting H1.

Interestingly, the effect of consumers’ perceived effectiveness of WASS on e-commerce transaction intention is not consistent across the U.S. and Korean samples; the effectiveness of WASS shows statistically very strong positive effect on e-commerce transaction intention in the U.S. sample, but it is not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the Korean sample. Similarly, the effect of consumers’ perceived effectiveness of WASS on concerns for e-commerce shows a very strong negative effect on concerns for e-commerce in the U.S. model only; it is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the Korean sample. These results partially support H2a and H3a and we can understand that WASS does not play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role for the Korean consumers. We conducted a group comparison on these relationships and a possible interpretation of these results from cross-cultural perspective is presented in the discussion section.

Consistent with the results of trust in e-commerce in previous literature (e.g., Kim, et al, 2005), consumers’ trust shows a very strong positive effect on transaction intention and a very strong negative effect on perceived concerns for e-commerce across two country data, supporting H4 and H5, respectively. Approximately 36 and 48% of the variances in e-commerce transaction intention are explained by concerns for e-transactions, effectiveness of WASS, and trust in e-Channel; 44 and 54% of the variances in concerns for e-commerce are described by effectiveness of WASS and trust in e-Channel in the U.S. and Korea models, respectively. These are reasonably well-explained variances.

Among the control variables in the U.S. model, gender (i.e., 1-male, 2-female) has a negative effective on both e-commerce transaction intention and trust in e-Channel while age has a negative effect on e-commerce transaction intention only. Web experience does not show any in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the model. In contrast, in the Korean sample, only web experience has a strong positive effect on e-commerce transaction intention and trust in e-Channel. These results show that younger and male consumers show higher intention to engage in e-transactions in the U.S. model; female consumers have lower degree of general trust in e-Channel in the U.S. model. In the Korean model, consumers who have more web experience show higher degree of trust in e-Channel and are more likely to engage in e-transactions.

## Group comparison analysis

In order to test the moderating effects of culture, a set of multiple group analysis was conducted using SmartPLS, which allows an interpretation of the differences in effects between discrete groups such as gender and national culture. The different levels of effectiveness of WASS and trust in e-Channel on e-commerce transaction intention and concerns for e-commerce can be tested by comparing models’ resulting path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients across groups. Following the recommendations by Chin & Dibbern (2010), the differences between the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of two groups are tested for signi<sup>fi</sup>cance with pair-wise t-tests. We use the resampling estimates for the standard errors of the structure paths in two sample groups under consideration gained from the bootstrapping procedure used for model evaluation in general (Eberl, 2010).

Regarding the effect of concerns for e-commerce on transaction intention, there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant statistical difference between the two counties. The results show that the effect of perceived effectiveness of WASS on both transaction intention and concerns for e-commerce are different in two counties; the effectiveness of WASS is much stronger in the United States than in Korea, supporting H2b and H3b.Concerns about e-commerce show no statistical difference on transaction intention across the two countries. Although we do not explicitly propose the difference between the United States and Korea in H1, H4 and H5, we also conducted group comparison analysis on these hypotheses. As seen in the results, trust in e-Channel neither shows any statistical difference regarding transaction intentions nor concerns for e-commerce between the two countries. ## Discussion and conclusion

The main focus of this study is an examination of the effectiveness of WASS from a cross-cultural perspective. To this end, we use the competing relationships between hindrance (i.e., concerns of e-commerce) and enablers of e-commerce (i.e., effectiveness of WASS and trust in e-Channel) on e-commerce transaction intentions and compare the relationships in a cross-national/cultural context (i.e., the U.S.A. vs Korea). In addition, we propose consumers’ concern on e-commerce as a second-order construct that consists of three <sup>fi</sup>rst-order sub-dimensional constructs (i.e., security, privacy, business integrity).

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to investigate the effectiveness of WASS in crosscultural setting and provide some important theoretical and practical contributions with respect to cross-national context. The results indicate that both impacts of consumers’ concerns and trust in e-Channel on e-commerce transaction intention are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the two countries. We can interpret these results as consumers’ concerns for e-commerce are still a major obstacle while trust is a critical determinant of the success of e-commerce across the countries. Interestingly, there are no differences identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for the impacts of consumers’ concerns and trust between two countries, suggesting that the moderating effect of national culture on these two constructs is not identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with previous studies (e.g., Jarvenpaa et al, 1999; Kim, 2008). Although consumers in different cultures may have different expectations of what makes e-vendors trustworthy, there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in the strength of the effect of trust in e-Channel.

While most of the hypotheses are sustained as expected, some of them are not supported across the countries. The results show that the perceived effectiveness of WASS for the U.S. consumers has a strong positive impact on their transaction intention and has a strong negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on their concerns for e-commerce. In contrast, Korean consumers’ perceived effectiveness of WASS does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence their e-commerce transaction intention and their concerns for e-commerce. The results of group comparison analysis con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the strength of perceived effectiveness of WASS for the U.S. consumers is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly stronger than that of Korean consumers. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings seem very interesting but not directly obvious. A possible interpretation of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings is that online consumers in the United States seriously consider WASS as an institutional mechanism to mitigate their concerns of e-commerce in terms of security, privacy and business integrity. In turn, they are more willing to engage in e-transactions when they <sup>fi</sup>nd the third party assurance seals in e-vendors’ websites. However, Korean online consumers are not well accustomed to WASS as a meaningful institutional mechanism to help their e-commerce decisions through alleviating their concerns for e-commerce. The insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant impact of the effectiveness of WASS in Korea is partially because of the immature state of WASS in that country. Compared with the United States, Korea has a limited number of WASS services of its own.

Another possible interpretation of this result from national/cultural aspects is that consumers in individualistic and low context culture (e.g., the U.S.A.) are seeking information about decisions from direct and formal information sources whereas consumers in collectivistic and high context culture (e.g., Korea) are searching for information from indirect and informal sources. Therefore, we see that the effectiveness of WASS in the United States is much stronger than in Korea. From the results above, we can conclude that national/cultural aspects do possibly affect how people perceive WASS and their importance in B2C e-commerce space. Multinational companies need to take into account these cultural differences when creating e-commerce websites and use WASS for a worldwide audience. Many managers have resisted WASS because of doubts about the positive impact that displaying the seal would have on their organization’s performance (Gendron & Barrett, 2004).While we agree that it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to demonstrate whether perceived effectiveness of WASS by itself primarily increases online consumers’ purchase intentions, we can argue that at least WASS is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in reducing the concerns for security, privacy and business integrity, which in turn increases e-commerce transaction intentions in the United States.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study provide interesting insights for WASS in B2C websites for information goods/services from a cross-national/cultural perspective, yet this study has several limitations. Like many national cultural studies, in this study, we assume that samples from two different countries represent two different national cultures; people belong to a single cultural group only; and national culture groups are static, homogeneous, and mutually exclusive. Although national cultural comparison studies are popular, there are many concerns about the concept of national culture and the usage of individual level data as samples of national culture. Because of the reasons mentioned above, several studies (e.g., Leidner & Kayworth, 2006) delineate new research challenges for culture related research in IS. One of the greatest challenges is that culture related research in IS needs to consider individual cultural values as an individual factor when studying the impact of culture on certain IT outcomes and include it as an independent construct (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). Thus, a future study may focus on this challenge.

A second limitation is that this study focused on college students as respondents. Though college students are the largest demographic block of the young e-commerce users, they do not fully represent the online users and their cultural variation may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient enough to represent the general e-commerce user group. Future studies need to consider participation of various age groups for generalizability of the study. Third, since WASS are third-party safeguarding mechanisms to protect online consumers against potential risks of e-commerce transitions in terms of security, privacy and business integrity, different WASS emphasize different dimensional elements of consumers’ concerns for e-commerce. However, in this study, we did not consider the separated effect of WASS on consumers’ concerns, because the purpose of this study is mainly to compare perceptions of effectiveness of WASS between two different nations. Since security, privacy, and business integrity are considered distinct factors, future studies should consider the separated effect of WASS on consumers’ concerns for e-commerce.

Finally, we used post hoc statistical techniques for detection of common method variance such as Harman’s singlefactor test. When self-reported questionnaires are used to collect data at the same time from the same participants, common method variance (CMV) may be a concern. This concern is strong when both the dependent and focal explanatory variables are perceptual measures derived from the same respondent (Chang et al, 2010). A post hoc Harman one-factor analysis is often used to check whether variance in the data can be largely attributed to a single factor (Chang et al, 2010).
