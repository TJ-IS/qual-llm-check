## a b s t r a c t

The study offers a decision support tool for stepwise benchmarking. Within a benchmarking exercise, we propose a method for selecting a preferable development path that consists of a sequence of intermediate benchmarks. First, an outranking multi-criteria approach is used to establish the hierarchical position of benchmarking units. For each unit, we evaluate possible development paths and distinguish those that are characterised by balanced improvement steps along the whole path. The proposed method can help decision makers to 1) detect disparities among benchmarking units and 2) identify intermediate benchmarks for less successful ones in each stage of their progress toward the ultimate benchmark target. The method is applied to benchmark European Union Member countries against Digital Agenda key performance targets.

## 1. Introduction

Benchmarking is a management tool de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a systematic process of measuring products, services or business practices against those recognised as business leaders [1]. The idea behind benchmarking is to evaluate one's own performance relative to the others to select the best practice exemplars and to consider performance improvements by learning from more successful competitors. All of these considerations require speci<sup>fi</sup>c decision-making activities that range from how to gauge pertinent statistics on performance and pinpoint areas that need improvement to setting goals and <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to meet them. Hence, there are a number of DSS (decision support systems) that exploit benchmarking as an underlying method, whereby prevails its use as a tool to compare and/or to set target values. A recent example is the study of Dong et al. [2], who presented the model that identi<sup>fi</sup>es the managerial benchmark (for the purpose of comparison). Similarly, Tremblay et al. [3] de<sup>fi</sup>ne three types of benchmarks to use for comparisons.

There are two essential elements of benchmarking. The <sup>fi</sup>rst involves comparing with other practice exemplars, which allows for a more objective evaluation of one's own performance. The second involves evaluating against other benchmarks—the term refers to a point of reference, a target, or the best in class standard, that is, someone or something to revere.

Originally, benchmarking was used to measure and improve processes and performances of various types of business entities, but over time its scope and level of implementation have been extended. At the international level, benchmarking serves as a tool for comparing national successes in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>eld of interest. Benchmarking involves determining how well a country is doing in reference to other countries and/or in reference to the benchmark/reference point and identifying ways to improve. Thereby decision makers are provided with objective insights on performance and on improved user understanding [4]. The added value of international benchmarking is in transforming performance indicators into a framework for decision making. This is an important feature when complex and multidimensional phenomena are assessed and evaluated, such as that of public policy [5,6]. For those sectors where countries share the same policy goals, benchmarking evolves from a management tool to a policy instrument in the sense that it supports policy makers as they engage in learning from their counterparts in other parts of the world [7–9].

International benchmarking found widespread implementation in the <sup>fi</sup>elds of electronic communications and ICT (information and communication technology) policy where it was promoted as an ICT policy tool both globally (through WSIS<sup>1</sup> and activities of ITU<sup>2</sup>) and in the EU. One of the seven <sup>fl</sup>agships of the new Lisbon Strategy (Europe2020) – the Digital Agenda for Europe (DAE), with its seven pillars, 101 actions and 13 speci<sup>fi</sup>c targets – is intended to achieve social and economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a digital single market founded on fast and ultra-fast Internet access.

The prevalent impetus behind the popularity of benchmarking was provided by the European Union's (EU) promotion of a new model of governance called the open method of coordination (OMC). At the core of the OMC are non-binding policy instruments and collective monitoring tactics aimed at encouraging exchange of knowledge and experience among countries.

While the OMC led to the mass production of international benchmarking studies, it also invoked debates on the merits and pitfalls of benchmarking as a decision aid tool [10–17]. Among principal concerns are those related to progress toward a desired performance. Where the speci<sup>fi</sup>c targets are long term and/or require signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvements, as is the case with Digital Agenda goals, gradual progress toward benchmark targets is gaining importance. One way to adjust benchmarking to address successive performance improvements is found in the literature under the term stepwise benchmarking.

This study addresses the concept of stepwise benchmarking and is grounded on our previous work that has promoted a new ELECTRE (ELimination Et Choix Traduisant la REalité; Elimination and Choice Corresponding to Reality) based benchmarking tool—ELECTRE Multi-level Outranking, abbreviated as EMLO [6]. We have enriched the EMLO with development paths and analyses of development dynamics and incorporated it in a user friendly software solution. To demonstrate the utility of our model, we have applied it to benchmark EU member countries against DAE key performance targets.

This paper is organised as follows. Section 2 addresses issues and concerns on benchmarking as a decision aid tool for policy makers and explains the stepwise benchmarking approach. Section 3 refers to the methodology for stepwise benchmarking as devised in this study, which is subsequently applied to a particular benchmarking problem within Section 4. Section 5 summarises our work and points to limitations and future research.

## 2. Background of the study

As indicated in the previous section, benchmarks are related to the best-in-class achievement and can be used as an etalon for conducting comparisons [18,19]. In cross-national comparisons, benchmarks are often associated with successful countries and their policies—policy benchmarks [15]. This is a practical implication of international benchmarking. The benchmark countries are those whose development policies should be emulated. One of the principal concerns with policy benchmarks, however, is related to the socio-economic, cultural and other differences that may impede countries as they attempt to learn from each other [10,14,20–23]. Rose [20] notes that although each country thinks that its problems are unique, within a given policy area, there is much in common across state and national boundaries, and accordingly, policy makers can learn from how their counterparts elsewhere responded to speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations. Therefore, regardless of national differences, countries can act as partners in the benchmarking process. This is especially the case when countries share the same development policy framework and institutional support for cooperation, such as in the case of EU member countries.

With respect to the problem that this paper addresses, we should emphasise that ranking countries according to their performance and tracking best practice exemplars prevail within international benchmarking studies. Much less attention, on the other hand, has been given to the questions from whom to learn and how to improve, which are the focus of this study. Namely, the traditional learning from the best approach raises a number of concerns related to setting goals that are particularly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve immediately or within a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed timeframe. Moore [24] studied this question and found that it is often the case that only 50% of the targets are achieved. Therefore, the search for benchmarks should not focus on the pursuit of the ideal values of performance indicators, but on the values that exist in practice, and as such, can serve as something to strive for. To our knowledge, the idea of learning from the similar, i.e., tracking relevant practice exemplars or corresponding benchmarks has appeared in several studies [6,15,16,24,25].

## 2.1. Underlying concept

The purpose of the pursuit for relevant practice exemplars and corresponding benchmarks is to avoid the uncertainty of tracking towering policy targets. As such, it is an important issue for improving the practical side of benchmarking, as well as the essence of stepwise benchmarking. Although the term stepwise can be used to interpret multi-stage analysis process in benchmarking [26], the majority of authors associate this concept with gradual improvement toward performance targets [6,27–30].

The logic of our approach is that a benchmarking unit (country) should follow a sequence of relevant benchmarks along its improvement path toward target performance as doing so allows the decision maker to opt for successive improvement and thus set pragmatic aspirations.

In our study, units under observation are countries having common policy frameworks and the same target values of performance. This speci<sup>fi</sup>city affects the choice of benchmarks, i.e., the best practice exemplar is drawn from legal requirements. These requirements, achieved by considering better performing units/countries drawn from practice exemplars, are termed intermediate benchmarks. Conceptually, this work develops a decision support tool, which seeks an optimal sequence of intermediate benchmarks out of a multiple of possibilities. The optimal sequence of intermediate benchmarks constitutes a recommended development path, and it is characterised by balanced improvements through all steps of the progress and on all criteria/performance targets.

## 2.2. Operational basis

In general, benchmarking involves a quantitative analysis for performance evaluation, and depending on the scope and type of problem, various analytical support/benchmarking tools are used. For example, in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of policy making and public communications, composite indicators (CIs) have been widely used for ranking and comparing countries' performances within international benchmarking exercises [16]. In the presence of multiple criteria that are different by nature and that have different units of measurement, MCDM (multi-criteria decision making) techniques are used to judge the performance of the benchmark units. On the other hand, when the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and/or productivity of units is evaluated, a linear programming-based method <sup>fi</sup>rst proposed by Charnes, Cooper and Rhodes [31] – Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) – is the first choice. A variety of different DEA models, adaptations and modi<sup>fi</sup>cations have been introduced to address benchmarking issues, and among them, several approaches address the concept of stepwise benchmarking [27–30,32]. An important feature of DEA in the context of stepwise benchmarking is its capability to set the criteria for the selection of benchmarks from multiple corresponding benchmarks.

As an operational basis for our solution, we selected the MCDM approach, based on the multi-level outranking ELECTRE method, as it was developed specially for the purpose of benchmarking and it was inspired by the DEA procedure and its concept of similarity, i.e., corresponding benchmarks. The suitability of the outranking multi-criteria approach has been presented in recent benchmarking studies, such as

Iazzolino et al. [33] and Petrović et al. [6]. There are two important advantages of the EMLO over DEA-based solutions that are important to mention. The <sup>fi</sup>rst advantage is related to the scope of application with respect to the typology of performance indicators. Because it is intended primarily for measuring ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, DEA requires input–output typology of performance indicators. This type of constraint does not exist with the EMLO solution as EMLO treats benchmarking evaluation problems expressed by various criteria and thus has a broader range of application. Furthermore, DEA can tell you how well you perform against your competitors, but it cannot assess your performance relative to a theoretical maximum [34], thus DEA imposes constraints on its application in the event of prede<sup>fi</sup>ned performance targets, while the EMLO is suitable for application when ultimate performance targets are <sup>fi</sup>xed (such as quantitative values set by a policy agenda).

## 3. Methodology

In this section, we present the architecture and procedures of the proposed stepwise benchmarking model. As shown in Fig. 1, the inputs provided by the decision maker are the units under observation and the performance indicators (in the format of scores), along with the decision maker's preferences for establishing dominance relations. These inputs are then used to establish the hierarchical positioning of the units (EMLO module, Fig. 1) and to determine the evolutionary development paths—EDPs (EDP module, Fig. 1). To explain the procedures for building modules, we <sup>fi</sup>rst recall the EMLO concept introduced in [6]. Next, we develop a new method for routing the benchmarking units that will be targeted. Additionally, the decision maker can opt for the sample or unit output level, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The sample level solution may be of interest to representatives of higher level decision-making entities (for example at the level of the EU), while the unit level may assist policy makers from particular regions/countries to (re)de<sup>fi</sup>ne policy targets.

## 3.1. EMLO general description

The ELECTRE based multi-level outranking method, EMLO, produces the hierarchical structure of the alternatives (e.g., countries) according to their level of performance in the observed <sup>fi</sup>eld. Accordingly, EMLO accounts for the comparability among countries and reveals more achievable practice exemplars, termed corresponding benchmarks, as explained in the previous section. Due to this feature, EMLO may be considered as a tool that offers a methodological framework for crossnational learning and decision making.

The EMLO uses the conceptual framework of the ELECTRE I method. Although the <sup>fi</sup>rst developed method of the ELECTRE family [35], ELECTRE I, is, due to its simplicity, still active today even in its original form. The method is based on pairwise comparisons of alternatives for each criterion and the computation of concordance and discordance indices. The concordance index $C _ { i j }$ denotes the degree of dominance of one alternative over another. The EMLO method creates partial preorder, thus making hierarchical levels similar to other ranking methods of the ELECTRE family [36,37]. To construct hierarchical levels of performance, the criteria where the alternatives perform equally should be excluded. Hence, the concordance index is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the modi<sup>fi</sup>ed form as [38,39]:

$$
C _ {i j} = \frac {\sum_ {k \in K _ {i j} ^ {+}} \omega_ {k}}{\sum_ {k \in K _ {i j} ^ {+} \cup K _ {i j} ^ {-}} \omega_ {k}}.\tag{1}
$$

In Eq. ((1), ω is the weight of criteria k, while the sets $K _ { i j } ^ { + }$ and $K _ { i j } ^ { - }$ are those where the performance score of the alternative $A _ { i }$ is higher $( K _ { i j } ^ { + } )$ and lower $\left( K _ { i j } ^ { - } \right)$ than the score of the alternative $A _ { j } .$

The discordance index $d _ { i j }$ prevents the declaration of dominance if an alternative is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly bad on some criterion. It is expressed $\mathsf { a s } d _ { i j } = \operatorname* { m a x } _ { k \in K _ { i i } ^ { - } } \big ( \big ( a _ { j k } { - } a _ { i k } \big ) / | I _ { k } | \big )$ : where $a _ { j k }$ and $a _ { i k }$ are the scores for alternatives $A _ { j }$ and $A _ { i }$ with respect to criterion $k ,$ and $\left. I _ { k } \right.$ is the scaled-score range of criterion k.

The assertion “alternative $A _ { i }$ outperforms alternative $A _ { j } "$ (denoted as $A _ { i } \succ A _ { j } )$ is valid if the values of the concordance and discordance indices $C _ { i j }$ and $d _ { i j }$ are above and below prede<sup>fi</sup>ned threshold values p and $q ,$ respectively.

The outcome of the ELECTRE I is a set of promising alternatives as well as outranking relations. If neither $A _ { i } \succ A _ { j }$ nor $A _ { j } \succ A _ { i } ,$ meaning that either the concordance or discordance test failed, the alternatives are incomparable.

To obtain qualitative levels of performance visualised in the form of a relation tree [6], the iterative procedure with repeated elimination of preferable alternatives is provided by EMLO. The alternative $A _ { i }$ appears at a certain performance level $L _ { n }$ when the following equation holds:

$$
\max \left\{t \mid \left(\exists A _ {j} \in L _ {t}\right) A _ {i} \prec A _ {j} \right\} = n - 1.\tag{2}
$$

This means that $A _ { i }$ is at the level of performance $L _ { n }$ if there is at least one alternative located at the level $L _ { n - 1 }$ that outperforms $A _ { i } .$ The <sup>fi</sup>rst level of performance is comprised of alternatives from the core subset.

In the context of benchmarking, an alternative that dominates another alternative from the adjacent level below can act as its corresponding benchmark. For the outperformed alternatives, corresponding benchmarks are those with more achievable performance because a less quantitative gap to bridge is required. On the other hand, the incomparability relations between alternatives at the adjacent level of performance indicate less argument when declaring dominance and, hence, suitable benchmarks as well (as discussed in [6]).

The outcome of the EMLO model, in the form of hierarchical levels of performance and outranking relations raises a natural question—how to move up and advance toward the established target. This may also be interpreted as how to choose the development path on the route to the desired performance level. The paths are represented by the alternatives at the adjacent level of performance to which they are comparable, i.e., connected by outranking relations. These development paths are evolutionary by nature as movement to the top goes through all hierarchical levels, level by level in a stepwise manner. Therefore, they will be referred to hereafter as the evolutionary development path— EDP. The EDPs are represented by the sequence of alternative benchmarking units (BM units) at the higher levels, intermediate benchmarks (IBMs), which is consistent with the interpretation of the benchmarking path in [29]. Taking into account a potentially large number of possible EDPs for a single alternative to follow it is of importance to provide an appropriate decision aid tool. For this purpose, we go a step further with the EMLO approach and propose procedures for the selection of the optimal EDP, which is described in the next section.

## 3.2. Selecting the optimal EDP

Moving from one level to another requires improvements in a number of criteria (performance indicator scores). The question then is in what way and on what grounds should the selection of the most suit able path be made.

One possible solution for establishing a benchmarking path, (e.g., proposed by Lim et al. [29]) is the successive selection of preferable paths between adjacent levels using the DEA approach. The benchmarking path consists of the most preferable paths at each level of performance. However, regardless of the criteria for selection, it is more reliable to consider the overall best path than the best path at each level.<sup>3</sup> This leaves the possibility that a benchmark unit does not necessarily follow the path of its intermediate benchmarks.<sup>4</sup> The performance of the benchmark unit is more respected without its conversion to the performance of the better ranked ones.

Following the premise of gradual improvement, we further consider the issue of performance increments along the development path. To overcome a performance gap of a criterion (and achieve the target), we propose a balanced distribution of performance improvements. This implies that the ideal EDP for an alternative (BM unit) would be the one with equal incremental steps in each stage of improvement. To explain this concept, consider an alternative moving up through four levels to reach the top. The EDP consists of three intermediate benchmarks plus the ultimate benchmark (top level alternative). The <sup>fi</sup>rst IBM on the ideal EDP would be the one whose performance is improved by one-fourth of the overall performance gap for each criterion; the second IBM would be as much improved compared to the <sup>fi</sup>rst, etc. In contrast, the worst EDP is the one where the entire gap must be overcome in a single step at some point in the hierarchical progress. Both the ideal and the worst EDPs are theoretical as intermediate benchmarks are virtually established and are not practically applicable. Consequently, the search for an optimal EDP is the search for the EDP that is closest to the ideal EDP.

An analytical support for evaluation of all possible EDPs is given below. We <sup>fi</sup>rst denote π as one possible EDP from an alternative $A _ { i }$ to the top level, and the set of all possible EDPs for an alternative A as $\Pi _ { i \cdot }$ . Assume that the path π contains alternatives $A _ { i } = A _ { i _ { 1 } } , A _ { i _ { 2 } } , . . . , A _ { i _ { m } }$ where $A _ { i _ { m } }$ is an alternative from the top level (core alternative).

Because we consider only the criteria by which improvements are needed, we equalise the scores for the criterion where a higher level alternative performs worse. The modi<sup>fi</sup>ed performance indicator scores $a _ { i _ { s } k } ^ { * }$ are:

$$
a _ {i _ {s} k} ^ {*} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} a _ {i _ {s - 1} k}, & \text { if } a _ {i _ {s - 1} k} > a _ {i _ {s} k} \\ a _ {i _ {s} k}, & \text { elsewhere } \end{array} \right.,\tag{3}
$$

Subsequently, for each criterion k, the difference between scores of the alternatives from the adjacent levels of performance is calculated as:

$$
R _ {s k} ^ {\pi} = a _ {i _ {s + 1} k} ^ {*} - a _ {i _ {s} k} ^ {*}, \quad s \in \{1, \dots , m - 1 \},\tag{4}

## 4. Application—stepwise benchmarking against DAE targets

This section demonstrates an application of the proposed method as a decision aid tool in the EU digital development policy. We apply it for benchmarking 27 EU member countries against DAE targets.

## 4.1. Performance indicators and targets

There are 13 key performance targets de<sup>fi</sup>ned within Annex 2 of the Digital Agenda for Europe [40]. A quantitative value for desired performance at the EU level is assigned to each target, along with the deadline to achieve it. The question is what makes these targets suitable for stepwise benchmarking exercise. First, de<sup>fi</sup>ned target values can be regarded as benchmarks—something to strive for and a reference point for comparison purposes. Second, there is a harmonised statistical support on performance indicators—the DAE Scoreboard. Most importantly, EU countries share the same policy and regulatory framework and can act as benchmarking partners, i.e., there is a background for cross-national learning. As discussed in Section 2, the very essence of the EU model of governance is the exchange of knowledge and experiences through international benchmarking.

In our study, we focus on 11 indicators re<sup>fl</sup>ecting 9 key performance targets (Table 1). All indicators and values are taken from the Digital Agenda Scoreboard. With the exception of total broadband coverage (2010 data), data refer to 2011, the latest available at the time of this writing (Table 2).

Due to inadequate indicators or the lack of data, some targets were excluded from the study. For example, currently there is a lack of adequate data at the country level regarding differences between national and roaming tariffs. The BEREC (Body of European Regulators for Electronic Communications) reports and the DAE Scoreboard Report [41] provide only charts without the underlying data, thus limiting individual studies and analyses (a similar observation was made in [42]). Some targets are yet to be measured in the future (key performance target 4b related to online availability of all the key cross-border public services contained in the agreed list and target 6 associated with overall reduction in energy use on lighting [41]), while target 5, regarding public investments in ICT R&D was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to treat in terms of target value for each country.

In addition to the indicator set (criteria), the EMLO benchmarking approach and its stepwise upgrade require de<sup>fi</sup>ning scores, weights and thresholds. In this study, we relied on a 9 score scale as it allows more dominant relationships to be detected [39]. The higher the score, the more progress that has been achieved toward the target value. All indicators have been equally weighted (Table 3).

Thresholds re<sup>fl</sup>ect the preferences of the decision maker, i.e., his position on whether a particular dominance relation holds.

In this study, the value of the concordance threshold (p) has been set at 0.80, meaning that one country outperforms another only if the sum of the criteria weight in favour of its dominance is greater than 80% of the total weight of all of the criteria where the scores of alternatives differ. The discordance threshold has been set at 0.40, which means that a country will not be perceived as more successful if there is at least one criterion where the difference in performance scores is four or more in favour of the less successful country.

The relation tree illustrates the relative position of a country and the distance from the desired performance. It also reveals more successful countries—potential sources for cross-national (policy) learning. On the way to the DAE targets, which are virtual goals, a country may follow the roadmap that includes not only improved performance but also the performance that other countries have managed to achieve. These countries may serve as policy benchmarks for the country under observation.

As observed from the resulting hierarchical arrangement illustrated by the relation tree, a country may follow different development paths toward the top. These paths are represented by the sequencing of countries from the higher level of performance, which are intermediate benchmarks for a country under observation. Consistent with the stepwise benchmarking approach, a country should follow the evolutionary development path (EDP), which implies that intermediate benchmarks appear at every performance level without skipping. Following the methodology that we described in the previous section, each EDP can be valorised by the criterion of balanced progress—the variation from evenly distributed improvements across all levels/steps of advancement. The path with minimal relative variation is the optimal path— the path closest to the ideal.

Obviously, a country may have more or fewer EDPs. The Czech Republic, for example, has four possible evolutionary paths to the target. By calculating the relative path variation for each (Eqs. ((5)–((9)), we obtain the corresponding values for the development paths (ρ<sup>π</sup>) (Fig. 3).

A path with the least value of relative variation includes the United Kingdom, the Netherlands and Sweden as intermediate benchmarks, thus from a stepwise benchmarking perspective, the path may be considered optimal (red, dashed line in Fig. 2). Note that the optimal path for the Czech Republic has a relative path variation of 0.53, thus suggesting that the solution is between the worst and the ideal.<sup>7</sup> Based on the results that have been achieved in practice, this path highlights the indicators and the extent to which each step of the progress can be improved. In the initial development phase, the Czech Republic should strive to achieve a performance similar to that of its sole corresponding benchmark—the United Kingdom (the rest of the countries from the upper adjacent level are not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently better to be the ones to look up to). This suggests that the Czech Republic should increase the proportion of the population ordering goods or services online both from domestic sellers and sellers from other EU countries and increase the penetration of regular Internet users to a level approximately equivalent to that of the UK. It must be remembered that the model uses scores (not raw indicator values) to evaluate development paths. This implies that a country should endeavour to reach the (higher) score of its benchmark, not strictly the value of its penetration. This is important as there are some targets where a number of EU countries (especially those from the top levels) have achieved beyond that which was set by the Agenda, as is the case with ordering goods and services online. Namely, the situation of the Czech Republic striving to achieve the performance of the UK does not mean increasing the percentage of the population ordering goods or services online to that of the UK's, which is above the DEA target, but raising it to the target value.

In the next stage, when these goals are achieved, the Czech Republic should concentrate on improving its fast broadband (above 30 Mbps), its online cross-border purchasing and its use of e-government in an attempt to match the Netherlands' performance scores.

The difference from the second best solution (third path in Fig. 3) is in the <sup>fi</sup>nal step. The method favours Sweden rather than Denmark as the last intermediate benchmark due to the smaller gap that must be overcome in the <sup>fi</sup>nal stage of the progress toward the goal, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in the domain of ultra-fast Internet access and crossborder online trade with other EU countries. It is worth noting that if the benchmarking paths were to be formed from most of the preferable paths at each level of performance, this solution would be selected as the best choice.

The obtained status and suggested improvement path present a framework for further decision-making activities, which can be related to redesigning national policy agendas and action plans to adjust benchmark targets to national performance potentials and/or broader policy plans (investment plans, other policies interacting with ICT policy, etc.). Consistent with this, we note that the Czech Republic's policy agenda – the National Policy in Electronic Communications – Digital Czech Republic contains intermediate target values for 2015 related to broadband above 30 Mbps [43]. Additionally, because the intermediate benchmarks along the EDP are more successful EU countries, their agendas can serve as sources of potential solutions (policy instruments, actions, plans or projects) that can be emulated or be an aspiration for the Czech policy makers.

An optimal EDP can be determined for each country (whereby it can happen that there are two paths of equal value, e.g., Slovenia) as illustrated in Fig. 4.

It is important to note that the optimal paths of lower ranked countries are not automatically mapped to the better ranked ones (as indicated in Subsection 3.2). This is because the variation is measured along the entire path, and therefore, the initial performance of a country matters. For example, the development path for Hungary differs from that which is recommended for its <sup>fi</sup>rst intermediate benchmark, France. In this particular example, the divergence arises from the fact that Hungary currently has a better performance in broadband above 30 Mbps than any of its intermediate benchmark countries.

After all, it can be argued that an optimal development path highlights feasible performance improvements needed to advance in the hierarchy and move toward the ultimate target. From the decisionmaking perspective, the optimal path may be the initial trace that, however, requires further consideration. An inevitable question that decision makers should be aware of is the potential of different performance indicators to rise and expand swiftly.

As the results depend on the input data, the accuracy of the results would be improved if data on all key performance targets had been included.

## 5. Conclusions

This study promotes stepwise benchmarking by developing a new decision support tool that identi<sup>fi</sup>es the optimal development path. The topology consists of a sequence of intermediate benchmarks drawn from practice that serve as transitional performance targets toward the ultimate benchmark. Unlike the previous research on stepwise benchmarking paths in which linear programming-based methods (DEA) have been used predominantly, we propose relying on multi-criteria analysis, namely the multi-level outranking ELECTRE method. The method enables the deployment of hierarchical levels of performance and outranking relations between BM units. These relations are subsequently used to establish EDPs for less successful units/alternatives. The main contribution of the study is the procedure for selecting the optimal development path among all possible evolutionary paths. By optimal we denote the development path composed from improvement steps that are gradual and balanced along the entire progression in the hierarchy. By de<sup>fi</sup>ning the theoretical best and worst EDPs, we can determine not only if our solution is better than other solutions but also how much it matches the ideally balanced distribution of performance improvement.

The applied logic of improvement path construction can be considered quite consistent with the concept of stepwise benchmarking, but beyond that, there are practical implications as well. These are related to international benchmarking analysis, where the improvements of a sector performance at the country level usually involve more efforts as well as massive investments and resources. This reinforces the need to track for more successful practice exemplars and to learn from those whose performance scores are more likely to be achievable.

The proposed method has bridged some restrictions of existing (DEA based) stepwise benchmarking methods. These are related to:

• The broader scope of application. Unlike DEA, the EMLO is able to include various typologies of performance indicators. The EMLO, for example, is suitable for application when ultimate performance targets are theoretically prede<sup>fi</sup>ned, while DEA relies on empirically established benchmarks.

• The possibility to search for the overall best path. There is yet no evidence that the DEA based approach offers this type of solution because it seeks for the best path at each level.

To illustrate the utility of the proposed ELECTRE based stepwise benchmarking, we have applied it to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of digital development across EU member countries. The obtained results provide additional support for evidence-based policy that complements the existing Digital Agenda Scoreboard.

There are several concerns and limitations related to the proposed benchmarking model. The <sup>fi</sup>rst drawback is due to the relative nature of comparison and the dynamic nature of performance indicators. Namely, the change of a single performance indicator of the BM unit may affect not only its position in the hierarchy but also the development paths of all BM units in the sample. The tendency for rapid and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes is especially present in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of ICT where values of some indicators may rise swiftly, while others may oscillate over time. That is why some additional criteria should be included in the <sup>fi</sup>nal choice of intermediate benchmarks. One direction, which may be the subject of future research, is to add the criterion of stability in performance within the dynamic framework of evaluation.

Secondly, there is a restriction caused by the characteristics of the sample. If the majority of alternative/BM units are incomparable due to discordance test, they will appear at the same hierarchical level, which means that the development paths cannot be established.

Furthermore, there is the issue related to the parameters of the model. Namely, the extent to which the relation tree is affected by decision makers' preferences expressed by concordance and discordance threshold values. The sensitivity of the results to threshold changes is a subject for future study.
