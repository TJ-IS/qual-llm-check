Abs tra ct: Application service providers (ASP), which host and maintain information technology (IT) applications across the Internet, offer an alternative to traditional models of IT service for user firms. We build on prior literature in transaction cost economics (TCE) to argue that the contract design should address ex post transaction costs that result due to contractual incompleteness and opportunism. We argue that contract design is multidimensional, and that it is necessary to design governance structures that can protect user firms from shirking and monitoring costs, as well as provide for efficient adaptation when requirements are incompletely specified at the start of the initiative. Our empirical analysis suggests that factors such as uncertainty in specifying service requirements, interdependence between the ASP application and IT systems in the client organization, and the need for specific investments favor time and materials contracts, whereas fixed prices are desirable when strong incentives are needed for cost reduction. We also find that contracts that are aligned with transaction attributes in a transaction cost–economizing manner are significantly less likely to experience budget overruns and realize better ex post performance than those that are not. These results hold normative implications for both user and provider firms to assess the performance implications of choosing contracts in line with prescriptions of TCE.

Key words a nd phras es : application service providers, contract choice, logit models, transaction cost economics.

There is a cons iderab le a mount of litera ture on the outsourcing of information technology (IT) functions [5, 21, 33, 35] that has examined boundary choices and performance implications. It is estimated that the global IT outsourcing market in 2004 was approximately \$316 billion [65]. Apart from cost considerations, concerns about quality, leveraging innovation potential of business partners [50], service quality improvement [33], as well as faster time to market [37] have been suggested as motivations for IT outsourcing. The emergence of application service providers (ASP), which deliver IT services to organizations over the Internet or a wide area network (WAN), offers yet another area for exploration.

In other types of outsourcing such as offshore software project development, the end-user organization works with the developer to determine software requirements that are customized to its internal routines, and the IT department has the final responsibility to ensure successful rollout. By contrast, the ASP model can transform the role of the IT group in the user firm from deploying and maintaining IT applications to managing a stream of IT resources [56]. The service model of ASPs necessitates a dependence on the public Internet infrastructure, creating an additional dimension of service uncertainty. The ASP business model is also different in that there are no sunk costs for the user firm, making it easier to switch service providers. Firms can now rent software that stays on someone else’s computers and is delivered online through a Web browser, thus avoiding the substantial licensing fees and installation services required up front in outsourced software development or in the acquisition of packaged software.

The shorter time period required to install and implement new software applications [43] could be a major advantage to small and medium-sized enterprises (SME) that lack the organizational resources incurred in installing and configuring complex business applications, while larger firms can benefit from reduced capital investments and improved purchasing power. In fact, many large IT vendors such as IBM are betting on the future of the “software as a service” model [58]. Some analysts have prophesied that the ASP model represents the most important development in the software industry to date and some have suggested that all software will be delivered in the ASP model in the future [17]. However, despite the optimistic assessment of the potential transformation of the way IT services are delivered to firms, the performance record of ASPs to date is mixed at best. Industry reports suggest that many of the initial adopters of ASPs have been highly dissatisfied with the service [44]. Many early observers highlighted the use of fixed price contracts for ASP services as an advantage of the ASP business model [19]. However, evidence from the business press involving the early years of the ASP model shows that fixed price contracts did not allow ASPs to recover costs of providing client-specific services [31]. ASPs deploy the Internet as a low-cost delivery mechanism and can spread the costs of fixed assets such as network infrastructure and hardware costs; thus, although production costs may matter to transaction performance, such explanations may be relatively less important to understand failure in this setting [56],<sup>1</sup> suggesting that transaction costs may play an important role in the success or failure of the ASP phenomenon.

We focus on the choice of contracts in ASP markets and establish the appropriateness of different contractual forms to deal with ex post transaction costs that result from potential opportunism by contracting parties. We focus on how the exchange between the user firm and the ASP needs to be structured to deal with factors such as interoperability with the user firm’s internal IT applications, specific investments by the ASP, uncertainty in specifying the stream of requirements, and the potential for opportunistic behavior. We also examine whether the presence of guarantees in contracts can serve as a substitute for specifying contingent terms. Our study also adds to the literature that has investigated implications of contract choices on performance outcomes [48]. We assess whether a discriminating choice of contract [62] achieves superior performance outcomes, and find that the contracts that are not aligned with transaction attributes are more likely to have budget overruns and achieve lower performance compared to contracts that are transaction cost economizing. Further, we demonstrate that costs of opportunism are less severe than costs of inflexibility in the context of ASP services. This study holds normative implications for managers by analyzing how performance would vary if the best possible contract were not chosen [39], which is especially critical given the context of managed services provision, where a discriminating alignment between transaction attributes and contracting structures is vital to achieving success.

## Transaction Costs and Opportunism in the ASP Context

Pricing for ASP s ervices is either fixed price or time and materials. In fixed price contracts, users agree to pay a prespecified fixed price per month for the agreed length of the contract, while in time and materials contracts, ASPs use a cost-based pricing approach and bill clients for monthly usage. The latter form takes into account extra costs incurred by the ASP to suit the needs of individual clients, such as extra hardware, software, and implementation. In deploying applications over the Internet (and charging clients a fixed rental), an ASP can utilize shared hardware and software resources to keep operating costs low [19], allowing clients to benefit from economies of scale. This arrangement also has the advantage that the ASP does not have any incentive to overcharge the client, thereby reducing potential costs of haggling. Industry watchers also recommend fixed price contracts as a way of getting visibility into total IT costs.

Literature in transaction cost economics (TCE) suggests that information asymmetry between service providers and clients gives rise to ex post opportunism, which is defined as a violation over the course of a relationship [62].<sup>2</sup> The consequent difficulty in predicting the actions of relevant actors is referred to as behavioral uncertainty [62]. We highlight two potential costs of opportunism. When the contract does not compensate the ASP for services not defined up front (i.e., the factors that create ex post uncertainty in the stream of services required from the ASP), the threat of bargaining during renegotiation carries the potential for opportunism in the form of inflexibility, or refusal to adapt to circumstances not specified in the contract [61, 62]. At the same time, a concern with time and materials contracts is that they may trigger opportunism in the form of shirking, where the ASP is not concerned about reducing costs to the user firms—that is, the ASP refrains from taking actions that will benefit the user. Fixed price contracts are efficient in lowering costs to the user, since the ASP is the residual claimant from cost-savings effort [63].

A few examples from the ASP context can illustrate the problems involved in crafting the right contractual relationship. Consider a scenario where a client has outsourced an online procurement system to an ASP. At the start of the outsourcing initiative, the client and the ASP agree on an overview of the service to be delivered, and only after the ASP starts providing service are the complete details ironed out. For instance, the ASP-hosted software can automate quotes or purchase requests, but these, in turn, need to be tied to the back-end enterprise systems of the client by porting the client’s software to ASP’s hardware [11] so that the client can share with business partners various types of inventory data, such as availability of items, delivery times, availableto-promise reports, and so forth. To populate the data in other functional areas in a client organization, the ASP also needs to provide interfaces with systems in the client organization to avoid redundant data entry [19]. Such requirements may be difficult to define up front. Once the contract begins, the ASP’s role could be redefined from that of hosting to that of a consultant involved in redesigning the client’s business processes, providing systems integration and user training.

With growth of business, the number of transactions performed by the ASP as well as the service description can change, and these changes in requirements may necessitate architectural changes, the costs of which are not easy to foresee at the start of an initiative.<sup>3</sup> It is infeasible to write contracts specifying the volume of transactions and type of throughput the ASP will have to handle, and all future functionality changes that the ASP application may have to cope with. The difficulty in describing requirements up front creates uncertainty in the stream of services to be delivered by the ASP. In TCE literature, such uncertainty is referred to as primary uncertainty in the transaction environment [57, 62].<sup>4</sup>

Since ASPs deliver IT services over the Internet, a critical aspect of the ASP service is to ensure reliability, guaranteed availability, and application response. However, ASPs have no control over the application code, the peering points of a network, third-party service performance, and the performance of the Internet. Further, an ASP may not invest sufficiently in reliability or quality of service, and blame the Internet infrastructure for what is essentially nonperformance by the ASP. Thus, there is also a risk of shirking in that the ASP may undersupply quality of service [61]. In both cases, an organization outsourcing to an ASP may be unable to access critical applications. ASPs offer a service-level agreement (SLA) with a predefined minimum performance; for instance, offering 99 percent reliability and guaranteeing service delivery that is considered acceptable. When the ASP cannot deliver the service agreed upon, the SLA carries with it a built-in penalty such as a payment to compensate the end user for downtime and loss of business [51] that can address some of the concerns regarding shirking. When it is difficult to anticipate ex ante the stream of services required from the ASP, time and materials contracts make it easier to compensate the ASP for business needs that are not foreseen initially. However, concerns about ASPs’ ability to manage the pay per usage billing and overcharging customers impeded the adoption of time and materials contracts. Anecdotal evidence indicates that poorly designed contracts led to considerable dissatisfaction among early users of ASP services [31].

## Transactions Cost Determinants of Contracts: Theory and Hypotheses

It has b een pos ited tha t whenever there is tra de, there are limits to reaching and enforcing agreements, the costs of which differ across different modes of organizations [13]. TCE recognizes that economizing on transaction costs, which are defined as the costs of planning, adapting, and monitoring task completion, is the unifying logic behind various contractual arrangements of production [62]. Writing and enforcing contracts is expensive when parties lack knowledge of all future contingencies [8]; it is costly for contracting parties to negotiate and agree upon mutual obligations that are not specified in the contract. The difficulty in drafting complete contracts implies that transactors need to plan, monitor, and continually adjust behavior to realize benefits from exchange [62].

We specifically concentrate on two types of ex post transaction costs that are the result of potential opportunism and contractual incompleteness—monitoring costs and maladaptation costs. Since contracts are incomplete, transacting parties need to spend substantial effort to ensure that there is no shirking of agreed-upon contractual obligations [41]. It is argued that the problem of shirking results from information asymmetry [61]. In the ASP context, the ASP may not exert sufficient effort to keep costs low to end-user firms, and therefore the firm needs to incur monitoring costs to ensure that an ASP is complying with the conditions of the contract and does not shirk contractual responsibilities (e.g., [16, 61, 62]). Another form of ex post transaction cost is that of maladaptation [1], where one party exhibits unwillingness or inflexibility in adapting to changing circumstances or takes advantage of the need for renegotiation to extract concessions from the other [61]. Changes to an ongoing contract also result in costly haggling, dispute resolution, and bargaining [62], which are maladaptation costs caused by the refusal of the supplier to adapt to unfolding contingencies.

Wathne and Heide [61] build upon Masten’s argument [38] to posit that opportunism takes specific forms and that different circumstances facilitate different types of opportunism. Which type of opportunism arises, and the impact on ex post transaction costs, depends on which form of vulnerability characterizes the transaction. The type of transaction cost that results depends on whether the source of the opportunism is information related or not [61]. It is said that there is an “endogenous incompleteness” in contracts when it is difficult to define the scope of service definition at the outset, and when parties need to incorporate ex post changes to service scope [8]. We therefore suggest motives for contract design corresponding to the potential for transaction costs. One of the motives for contract design is to reduce the scope for provider opportunism that increases monitoring costs for the firm. Another motive in designing contracts is that when contingent performance is costly to stipulate, and when ex ante requirements are incomplete, governance forms should facilitate “adaptive, sequential adjustment” [62] by lowering potential maladaptation costs.

Specific investments create the threat of bilateral holdup where parties bargain over appropriable quasi-rents, increasing the costs of contracting [14, 30]. When specific investments are important to the service being contracted, inflexible contracts increase the hazard that parties are bound to an inappropriate contractual form or technological regime [14]. Greater renegotiation costs should lead to the adoption of more flexible contracts, and when there is greater potential for shirking, parties should adopt more complete contracts [15]; thus, contract forms should minimize the expected costs of governing transactions over the life of the relationship. ## Ex Post Transaction Costs and Contract Design

Because contracts are necessarily incomplete, the twin challenges that need to be resolved are that of balancing adaptation with monitoring costs. Building on prior literature, we suggest that there are two contractual mechanisms to solve the twin problems of monitoring and adaptation—the incentive structure as well as performance guarantees. The choice of incentives may be used to address the problems of monitoring costs and adaptation costs [8], while performance guarantees can mitigate the problem of monitoring costs [55] but not maladaptation costs. A fixed price contract is a compensation scheme for specific performance, and minimizes monitoring costs by offering high-powered incentives ex ante, whereas cost-based compensation is well suited for initial specification (i.e., the set of services to be provided by the ASP defined up front) as well as modifications required, and thus minimizes maladaptation costs [8].

TCE literature argues that contracts should specify a rule for calculating “damages” if the agreed upon performance has not been delivered [52]. The presence of performance guarantees ensures that when nonperformance by the ASP impedes the business of the client or when the performance expectations are not met, the ASP can compensate its clients by paying a prespecified damage measure. Prior literature has suggested that such contractual provisions safeguard against potential opportunism [55]. Performance guarantees are specific to each end user; in defining SLAs, the ASP and the end-user organization need to carefully assess potential loss of business in setting expectations and establish transactions that could be lost in setting reliance damages [52]. Because it is costly to fully enumerate contractual contingencies ex ante, the presence of performance guarantees can serve as a substitute for specifying contingent terms in detail [10, 55]. Because performance guarantees serve as a substitute for specifying contingent terms, they are associated with fixed price contracts. Thus,

Hypothesis 1: Performance guarantees are associated with a higher likelihood of fixed price contracts.

Service Uncertainty, Ex Post Contracting Costs, and Contract Design

Fixed price contracts offer strong incentives for the ASP to lower the cost of service, since the ASP is the residual claimant for its cost-saving effort, and thus protect against potential shirking [8]. However, exchange relationships are characterized by considerable uncertainty, defined as the “unanticipated changes in circumstances surrounding an exchange” [46, p. 82], which increases the extent of maladaptation costs and the extent to which shirking can occur undetected. The uncertainty resulting from the transactional environment has been characterized as primary uncertainty [62], which is distinct from uncertainty resulting from the strategic misrepresentations of information by economic agents. Primary uncertainty reflects a lack of knowledge about the state of events, as opposed to a lack of knowledge about the actions of other economic actors [57].

When outsourcing a service provision to an ASP, organizations may find it difficult to estimate in advance the stream of future services required, transaction volumes that need to be supported by the ASP, and the extent to which the ASP service should be interoperable with their future application needs [11]. Such uncertainty in specifying service requirements makes it difficult to estimate future costs, increasing contracting incompleteness, making time and materials contracts desirable as they can handle renegotiation arising from changes to the initial specifications [8]. Thus,

Hypothesis 2a: The higher the service uncertainty, the higher the likelihood of time and materials contracts.

Performance guarantees in a contract ensure that when performance expectations are not met, the user organization is paid a penalty for nonperformance by the ASP, thus providing incentives for the ASP to carry out the contractual obligations. Performance guarantees are thus posited to offer “insurance” against the threat of shirking [52, 55]. Because service uncertainty increases the degree of ex ante contractual incompleteness, increasing ex post monitoring costs, performance guarantees can protect against the threat of ex post shirking by the ASP. Thus,

Hypothesis 2b: The higher the service uncertainty, the higher the likelihood of performance guarantees.

With a long-term contract, there is a need for intensity of interaction between the firm and the ASP (as opposed to a spot-market type of arrangement in a shorter contract), which makes it difficult for the ASP to hide shirking behavior [12]. ## Interdependence

Contracting for ASP services is characterized by interdependence with the user’s internal technology environment. The data residing in the ASP’s system needs to be combined with data from functional areas within a client’s organization such as accounting.<sup>6</sup> Users of ASP services may also demand that the data from the ASP-hosted application be made available in the organization for analysis. When an IT activity cannot be easily separated from other business processes and technical systems, there is greater risk in the outsourcing initiative. To design user interfaces, the ASP needs to adopt a holistic approach in understanding the technical architecture as well as the business needs of the client organization rather than adopting a narrower functional perspective. Some of the business processes as well as technology in the client organization may be unfamiliar to the ASP; thus, future costs and maintenance requirements are difficult to foresee. Because it is difficult to anticipate exact compatibility required with client software and future demands from the ASP’s services, interdependence between the outsourced service and internal applications leads to transaction costs by creating scope for renegotiation [12], necessitating the adoption of governance forms that can facilitate “adaptive, sequential adjustment” [62], by lowering costs associated with renegotiation.

Hypothesis 3: Interdependence is associated with time and materials contracts.

## Bilateral Holdup, Specific Investments, and Contract Design

ASPs need to spend considerable effort understanding how the customer’s processes work, how the IT applications are configured, and how the IT platforms are deployed at the client’s location, and make client-specific investments such as creating databases to consolidate data in the end-user organization. However, such specific investments may lead to two problems. One is the possibility of holdup [22] acting as a maladaptation cost, whereby the client could try to appropriate some of the surplus from specific investments [30] during renegotiation. Our conceptualization of holdup is consistent with that of the arguments in prior literature [28, 29] that posit that holdup as defined in a traditional setting constitutes bilateral, rather than unilateral, holdup. This is due to the fact that the buyers incur switching costs as well as search costs in locating an alternate seller, while the seller makes asset-specific investments that create “ap propriable quasi-rents” for the buyer [30]. A second problem is that buyers may need to make investments in ASP-specific routines and processes in order to maximize the value from the ASP service. Such investments carry opportunity costs, because there may be no value of deploying these investments in an alternative contractual setting.<sup>7</sup> Because the potential gains from investment can be dissipated by ex post bargaining [22], the risk of being bound to an inflexible agreement combined with the fact that specific investments limit the ability to seek alternative trading partners induces parties to underinvest in the relationship [40]. An important aspect of contractual protection for the ASP as well as the client is to have a contracting arrangement that can facilitate adaptation to changing circumstances without relying on detailed specification of contingencies or external verification of contingencies [40]. Contractual flexibility, allowing for adaptation, also smooths out the frictions in renegotiation and thereby protects client-specific investments [30]. Thus,

Hypothesis 4a: The higher the level of client-specific investments, the higher the likelihood of time and materials contracts.

An alternate mechanism to reduce the threat of holdup and underinvestment is the use of performance guarantees. When there are contingencies that are not covered by the contract, the threat of a breach penalty in a specific performance guaranteed by the contract provide investment incentives [52, 55] by essentially compelling the contracting parties to be held to a performance standard (such as an SLA). Bilateral holdup can be similarly solved through guarantees in contracts, as the threat of contractual penalties acts as a holdup tax [18], reducing the incentives for holdup during renegotiation and minimizing maladaptation costs. Thus,

Hypothesis 4b: Client-specific investments are associated with a greater likeli hood of performance guarantees.

## Controls: Relational Norms and Expectations of Continuity

Among exchange partners who interact frequently, such as in an ASP context, the actions of the ASP, including credible commitments and behaving trustworthily, can reduce the hazards accompanying formal contracts. Researchers have suggested that formal contracts and trust are substitutes, as trust alleviates the fear that one’s exchange partner will act opportunistically. By reducing the transaction cost associated with potential shirking by the ASP, relational norms embedded in social processes, such as trust, bilateral action, and information exchange [49], create shared values and a sense of common motivation, reducing the need for tightly defined contracts. Expected payoffs from future interactions can motivate long-term cooperation, and can substitute for tightly defined contracts. Firms learn ways of communication and cooperation [26] from their prior business experience with their trading partners. The expectation in the continuity of the relationship leading to confidence in the future of a relationship makes firms more willing to engage in activities that will enhance the relationship, reducing renegotiation costs and providing protection against opportunism.

Transaction Attributes, Discriminating Alignment, and Ex Post Performance

Although earlier research has argued that organizational planners make performance trade-offs in governance selection [48], with a few exceptions the link between performance and contract choice has not been investigated in the literature. In the hypotheses developed above, we conjecture that different exchange hazards have differential effects on contract choices due to the potential for ex post transaction costs. A guideline to assess the comparative efficacy of governance structures is the discriminating alignment between transaction costs and governance [63]. The discriminating alignment argument posits that “transactions, which differ in their attributes, are aligned with governance structures, which differ in their costs and competencies, in a discriminating (mainly, transaction-cost-economizing) way” [63, p. 277]. This has been interpreted to mean that the contract structure that minimizes transaction costs should be the preferred option [36].

Contract choices affect ex post performance outcomes such as vendor’s profits [20] and satisfaction with outsourced services [48]. When the transaction context is not aligned or unmatched with the governance structure, there will be subsequent transaction problems [3]. A lack of discriminating alignment between transaction costs and governance structures leads to ex post transaction costs that impose higher organizational costs, and therefore lower ex post performance, compared to when governance is aligned with normative predictions from TCE [36, 48, 54]. A discriminating alignment between transaction attributes and governance results in lower management costs as a result of reducing the scope for ex post transaction costs [39].<sup>8</sup>

We build upon the discriminating alignment arguments of TCE to assess the impact of transaction cost–economizing contract choices on subsequent transaction problems. Wathne and Heide [61] posit that different governance mechanisms possess “inherent benefits as well as prerequisites, which must be evaluated with respect to particular manifestations of opportunism” [61, p. 42], which has implications for ex post transaction costs. Comparing across the two contract forms, fixed price contracts economize on the transaction cost of monitoring, whereas time and materials contracts economize on the transaction costs of maladaptation. Therefore, we expect to find that attributes of transactions that increase the need for adaptation, such as uncertainty and interdependence, should lead to time and materials contracts, and the resulting performance will be better than that from adopting fixed price contracts.<sup>9</sup> Thus,

## Hypothesis 5a: Contracts that are chosen in accordance with transaction cost minimization achieve better performance than contracts that are not.

As contracts are adopted based on considerations of adaptation and shirking, the lack of discriminating alignment results in maladaptation or monitoring. When contracting with an ASP, it is difficult for managers to apply rules learned from other settings, since the ASP business model is relatively new for many user firms. Because decision makers exhibit bounded rationality [63], they may overestimate their ability to adapt to conditions that call for adaptation to unfolding circumstances. When managers have limited visibility of unpredictable circumstances, a lack of discriminating alignment can be severe when there is a high need for adaptation [54]. In such circumstances, the expected organizational costs of choosing a fixed price contract (i.e., a governance form with less scope for adaptation) may outweigh the organizational costs of choosing a time and materials contract (which increases the ex post transaction cost of monitoring) when there is potential for shirking, leading to monitoring costs. Therefore, deviations from transaction cost minimization lead to asymmetric costs. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5b: When contracts are not chosen according to transaction cost minimization, contracts that are inflexible achieve lower performance than contracts with excessive monitoring costs.

## Dependent Variables

The contract type is either fixed price or time and materials type. We consider two different measures of ex post performance.

Cost overrun is defined as the difference between actual effort and that estimated by the ASP to provide the service [9]. For fixed price contracts, cost overruns are incurred to satisfy user requirements that were not initially foreseen. Even with time and materials contracts, the user firm does not pay for extra costs that were not initially foreseen [9]. Further, in time and materials settings, the increased need for coordination between the ASP and the user firm [8] could lead to unanticipated costs for both clients and ASPs and thus overruns in schedule. When a firm and an ASP agree on a time and materials pricing scheme, both parties may have expectations about the variable charges that will be billed by the ASP, taking into account facts such as the expected growth in usage. Contrast that with a scenario where there are unexpected spikes in data or users in the organization, and where the ASP may find that the variable pricing scheme may not be enough to cover the overheads incurred in providing service. With a time and materials contract, the contract design takes care of the first, whereas the second creates overruns.<sup>10</sup>

The effects of maladaptation and monitoring costs may not be captured by the impact on overrun alone. Another indicator of ex post performance is the effectiveness or efficiency with which transactions are undertaken, which we refer to as exchange performance of the ASP. Following prior literature that has considered the performance implications of contract choices in IT outsourcing [48], we measure the ASP’s perception of how the client views the exchange, which can “distinguish whether the vendor has realized pre-established performance expectations” [46, p. 863] central to interorganizational exchange, such as the quality of the service provided and the responsiveness to problems and inquiries by the user firm [48]. Capturing the ex post performance of an ASP indicates whether a lack of discriminating alignment between transaction characteristics and the formal control structure can result in ex post transaction problems [3].

## Independent Variables

Service uncertainty is the difficulty in precisely defining the stream of services required from the ASP. IT outsourcing is characterized by uncertainty regarding cost and project specification [9, 48]. We adapted these measures for the ASP context to assess the difficulty in specifying services required from the ASP and estimating costs at the contracting stage. Evidence from the business press<sup>11</sup> suggests that estimating compatibility with client applications and integrating the service with clients’ applications [11], delays in rollout, cost of service, and technical problems [31] are factors that are difficult to foresee. Our conceptualization of service uncertainty is consistent with prior literature where uncertainty is an aspect of the transaction [48].

To assess performance guarantees, we developed measures to assess the importance of service-level agreements. To ensure a stable infrastructure, ASPs have to develop network platforms that are scalable and design systems that reduce delay time. Because most ASPs offer SLAs as part of a contract, an ordinal variable that measures the presence of the SLA may be used as a proxy for the ASP’s capability to provide service [25]. Thus, we measure the importance of SLAs to the client engagement on three dimensions—tools to prove SLA achievement [51], SLAs on network reliability [56], and SLAs on customer response time [6].

We coded a binary variable called interdependence (equal to 1 if there is interdependence between the ASP-hosted software and applications in the user firm). Evidence in the business press indicates that depending on the type of application hosted, the ASP needs to provide interfaces to client applications and to ensure that there is no redundant data entry [6, 7, 59]. Applications that are associated with a higher amount of interdependence with applications in the clients’ organization are harder to describe, while ex ante specification of service attributes becomes more difficult.

Client-specific investments in information systems functions denote the extent to which the vendor needs to acquire company-specific information to adequately perform the outsourced task [48]. Prior literature in TCE has measured specific investments in terms of tailoring service to meet buyer needs. We also note that Poppo and Zenger, in their study of IT outsourcing contracts, measured specific investments in terms of a vendor’s “approach to this function (or set of applications) custom-tailored to the company” [48, p. 866]. We adapted this measure for the ASP setting by drawing upon industry and trade press emphasizing the need for ASP investments in knowledge of the functional area and the client’s business [6] as well as service providers’ acquisition of client-specific knowledge [48, 49].

## Controls

The contract length<sup>12</sup> (LENGTH ) is the length of time the ASP agrees to provide service to the end-user organization. Prior literature has suggested that the length of time that parties agree ex ante to commit themselves to the terms of the contract [28] is an important transaction-specific characteristic that influences choice of contracts. We developed the measures for length as an ordinal scale as observed in actual length of ASP contracts [6, 56].

A company outsourcing its IT services to an ASP may find that different users in its organization, such as sales and marketing managers, need aggregated information about diverse customers in a single view [32]. ASPs have to tailor services to suit clients, such as providing personalized content to suit a client’s business needs that may affect the trade-off between fixed price and time and materials contracts as well as the extent to which ex post performance is perceived. We therefore control for service personalization by the ASP in the contract choice estimation as well as the performance estimation. We coded the qualitative responses from the ASP personnel indicating the following: (1) creating reports specific to clients, (2) providing training and support specific to a client, and (3) whether the application software permits tailoring applications to client needs. Providing such service personalization does not necessarily involve client-specific investments in the software as a service model (e.g., the hosted application software can provide personalized services such as generating a variety of reports for individual customer’s requests) and, hence, will not necessarily lead to holdup problems.

Relational norms have been conceptualized in terms of communication and sharing of information, trust, dependence, and cooperation [2, 48]. In adapting these items to the ASP context, we considered factors highlighted in the industry and trade press such as the cooperation and partnership with clients [6, 56]. The expectation of continued interaction between the parties is the anticipation that the relationship will persist into the future [24].<sup>13</sup>

End users are concerned about potential loss of control in outsourcing initiatives, leading them to prefer tightly defined contracts such as fixed price contracts [33]. Thus, we include a single-item scale, client concern, which indicates clients’ perceived unease with outsourcing to ASPs, as a control variable in the contract choice estimation. We also control for the client contact, which denotes the principal contact or individual that the ASP first contacts when marketing to a new client company, which has a bearing on the type of performance that may eventually result.

Software service providers make firm-level investments in learning and service improvements [20]. Because different providers make different investments in organizational capabilities, if an ASP has demonstrated capabilities that are superior to those of its competitors, then the performance that results from selecting the ASP will be better than choosing another provider. Because performance is determined not only by a lack of discriminating alignment of transactions with contracting hazards, it is important to control for capabilities of the ASP in assessing performance implications of governance. Infrastructure capabilities of the ASP denote whether an ASP has an advantage in service delivery, in line with prior literature that has characterized capabilities of an IT service provider as the “persistent and deliberate” investments made by the provider [20]. We draw upon factors highlighted in the business press such as the ability to manage its network [11], provide best practices [6], and improved technology [19].

We also control for the reputation of the ASP, which could be critical since most of the ASPs are relatively new firms. We used several variables to proxy for reputation of the ASP. The use of industry-wide standards such as .NET or Java may ease the burden of providing compatibility across multiple clients [56]. We have incorporated a measure of ASP’s accreditation, which reflects the market perceptions that an ASP follows good processes and therefore serves as an indicator of ASP reputation [9]. We control for an ASP’s participation in alliances with prominent IT vendors, which may improve its status and therefore its reputation. We also control for whether the ASP is publicly traded.

Argyres and Mayer posit that knowing what to plan for and what not to plan for can be critical in designing a contract [4]. We therefore denote a variable, contracting template, which measures whether ASPs use a contracting template that can take into account the legal and financial contingencies that may affect a transaction [3]. Planning for these types of contingencies is different from the operational and technical aspects of executing outsourcing arrangements [4]. For each ASP, the data were aggregated from public databases that aggregate news and press releases, such as ABI/ INFORM Trade & Industry, Dow Jones Interactive, Reuters, and LexisNexis, as well as the Internet Archive, which is a nonprofit group that captures snapshots of Web pages across the Internet at weekly intervals. ## Data Collection

The initial structured questionnaire was generated based on interviews with managers in ASP firms, academic work based on TCE, and practitioner-oriented literature. The questionnaire was pretested by multiple faculty members, doctoral students, and an initial sample of 30 ASPs identified by a consulting organization specializing in IT. Each item was reviewed for content, scope, and content validity. Data were collected by a professional data collection agency that has extensive expertise and contacts required in large-scale data gathering, through a telephone and mail survey designed by us. Because telephone inquiries allow close interaction between the respondent and the data collection firm, this provides the opportunity to define the terms in the survey and to ensure that the respondents understand the survey questions. We specified that respondents should be individuals who are involved with strategic business development or similar areas in the organization. To enforce accuracy in reporting, the criterion was that all respondents qualify for our definition of an ASP as

any company that provides access to remotely hosted IT applications over a wide area network (WAN), a virtual private network (VPN), or the Internet. These services may include financial and accounting applications, IT networking applications, and customer relationship management.

As part of the screening process, each ASP that was contacted was asked to provide details about a current service agreement undertaken for a client. This approach to data collection regarding current engagements is in line with previous studies [9] and ensures that the respondent provides details about the client engagement they are most familiar with. Prior studies have recommended using key informants in evaluating exchange performance [48] because higher-ranking informants can provide more reliable information than their lower-ranking counterparts. The profile of informants included individuals in executive (chief executive officer, vice president, chief information officer, etc.) or managerial positions in ASP firms who are likely to be well informed. Further, we used the multiple informant technique to get responses from different individuals. If the interviewer determined that the respondent would not be able to answer some of the questions, a referral was sought to someone else who was aware of project-level details. In some cases, multiple individuals were contacted in a firm and asked to complete different aspects of the survey. The ASPs were also contacted for a follow-up telephone interview to clarify any issues in the responses and probed for additional details. If there was any discrepancy in responses, the respondents were probed until consistency was reached.

To minimize potential biases, the respondents were assured that their responses and identity would remain confidential. To minimize the risk of obtaining inaccurate responses from participants who may not know the answers to certain questions, a “don’t know” response category was added to each question. This would enable respondents to avoid feeling pressured to respond to a question that they did not feel confident about. This is especially critical given that some of our measures are self-reported by the respondents in our survey. To ensure data integrity, it is recommended that subjective measures be validated against quantitative performance [45]. We included some unstructured questions in the survey. Responses to the unstructured questions were coded after the data collection was complete. We cross-checked the responses to the structured and unstructured questions for consistency. Whenever possible, we checked for consistency of the survey responses with information available in industry reports, including the types of contracts offered by ASPs, typical duration of each type of contract, and so forth. We also conducted a similar survey across user organizations for a study of satisfaction with ASP services, which also provided validation for some of our measures. Since prior literature posits that buyers and suppliers share consistent perceptions of the performance of exchange relationships and attributes of exchanges [2], responses from end users and ASPs providing services were matched to assess whether there were any significant deviances on many of the qualitative terms as well as on measures such as length of the contract, reported overruns, and so forth. A comparison of sample means and distributions did not reveal significant differences. To check for any systemic bias between early and late respondents, we conducted the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov Z-test and verified that there was no systematic difference between early and late respondents. A total of 600 questionnaires were administered, and resulted in 167 (28 percent) responses. One hundred fifty-three responses proved to be usable in terms of completeness of data.

We performed an exploratory as well as confirmatory factor analysis for all of the items simultaneously. The exploratory factor analysis revealed that the variance explained by the first factor was less than 25 percent of the total variance. The principal components extracted from the confirmatory factor analysis were used in subsequent analyses. The factor loadings and scale development are presented in Table 3. For multi-item indicators, we assessed the composite reliability, which reflects the internal consistency of the indicators [47]. ## Results and Discussion

Our hypothes es rela te contra ct choice as a function of transaction cost drivers enumerated earlier. Because our dependent variable is binary and discrete, an ordinary least squares estimation produces inefficient estimates. We therefore conduct a probit estimation of contract choice. The chi-square value of 24.0537 (9 degrees of freedom [df], $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and the Goodman–Kruskal’s gamma statistic of 0.77, which is a nonparametric measure of correlation between the predicted contract type and the actual contract, indicate a good model fit. The estimation details for the contract design elements are presented in Table 5. Because performance guarantees and choice of incentives are driven by the underlying transaction characteristics, we need at least one instrument in the performance guarantees estimation that is not used to estimate the contract choice. We use the size of the ASP as an instrument in the performance guarantees estimation.

We find that performance guarantees as measured by the presence of SLAs are significantly associated with a higher likelihood of fixed price contracts (H1). The importance of performance guarantees implies that ASPs and end-user organizations need to develop detailed contractual safeguards such as service provisions and disaster recovery that can reduce monitoring costs. Service uncertainty is significantly associated with the likelihood of a time and materials contract (H2a). Because a fixed price contract with uncertainty in service requirements may lock both sides into an unsuitable technology or system design, a time and materials contract is suitable when parties prefer arrangements that can be easily adapted. One of the reasons attributed to the demise of many early ASPs was that their pricing structure did not allow them to recover the costs of software installation [31]. Indeed, industry commentators have reported that the ASP sector has witnessed growth after clients switched to flexible pricing structures that enable renegotiation of the license terms [44].

We also find that service uncertainty is significantly associated with the importance of SLAs (H2b), which attests to the importance of contractual insurance against monitoring costs when contracts are incomplete. It is possible that in a long-term relationship, learning by the ASP can reduce the costs of delivering service. In fact, the TCE literature has found that, over time, contracts may become more complete since the underlying technology may become more stable and processes better known [15]. Recently, some ASPs have used a pricing approach where they bill clients on a time and materials basis for the initial implementation, but where they switch to a fixed price per month as the requirements stabilize [6].

## Test of Discriminating Alignment

To assess the performance of a contractual form, we consider two indicators: (1) the observed cost overruns associated with a contract [9] and (2) the performance of the ASP, which refers to client perceptions that the vendor has realized preestablished performance expectations central to interorganizational exchange [48]. Cost overruns in outsourcing of IT services are driven by misspecification of projects as well as poor understanding of requirements [9]. Therefore, when projects are managed according to the predictions from TCE, we expect to observe no overruns. Similarly, we expect to see that contracts organized according to TCE predictions minimize subsequent transaction problems and achieve better performance [3]. For consistent identification, we used an exogenous covariate, client contact, which assesses whether ASPs market to business decision makers or IT decision makers, in the overrun estimation but not in the performance estimation. We controlled for the contract template used by an ASP, which measures whether ASPs can take into account the legal and financial contingencies that may affect a transaction [4], as an explanatory variable in the performance estimation but not in the overrun estimation.

It has been posited that estimating the performance implications of governance choices should consider the differential unobservable (to the researcher) advantages or disadvantages that an organizational decision maker faces in choosing a particular contract form [39]. Testing the impact of governance choice on performance is complicated by self-selection, which creates an endogeneity problem. This is because of two reasons. First, we only observe performance for the contract form that has been adopted [42]. Second, a direct estimation of performance resulting from a given contract assumes that a contract form is randomly chosen and that the performance of a given contract form is uniform across firms. However, firm-specific heterogeneity implies that the impact of transaction cost drivers is systematically different under different governance regimes and therefore the choice of a contract is a deliberate decision that can systematically affect the resultant performance.

We present the outcome estimations in Table 6. To correct for the systematic effect of self-selection in the estimation of realized performance, we compute the inverse Mills ratio [23]. However, in the overrun estimation, because the outcome variable as well as the endogenous regressor is binary, we cannot deploy the standard correction for endogeneity [23]. We used two methods to address the endogeneity issue: a two-stage conditional maximum likelihood approach [64] and the treatment effects approach used for binary outcome variables [60]. To compute both, we use the standard exclusion restriction where the exogenous covariates used in the first stage (i.e., the contract choice equation) consist of the covariates used in the second stage as well as instruments [64, pp. 567–571] not included in the second stage. We included a continuous covariate in the second stage (i.e., overrun estimation) that does not enter contract choice estimation, satisfying the conditions prescribed by Vytlacil and Yildiz [60].

Column 3 presents the estimation for realized performance, controlling for endogeneity of contract choices. In columns 2 and 3, we find that the inverse Mills ratio is positive and significant (H5a is supported). The sign of the inverse Mills ratio indicates the relative organizational costs of discriminating alignment, and shows that contracts that are organized with TCE predictions are more likely to result in better performance (i.e., no overruns and better exchange performance) compared to contracts that are not. The fact that interdependence is associated with overruns and that service personalization is associated with lowered perceptions of exchange performance implies that since the ASP business model is not mature, tailoring services to suit client needs is still a difficult proposition.

To assess the organizational costs resulting from a lack of discriminating alignment, we consider the impact on contract overruns as they may proxy for costs imposed on organizations. Columns 4 and 5 of Table 6 present the switching regression model for binary outcome implementing the prescription of [60] in a maximum likelihood approach. This requires a continuous covariate that enters the outcome equation but which does not enter the model for the endogenous variable. In the first stage, we estimate contract choice using a probit specification including an instrumental variable. In the second stage, we conduct two separate estimations for overrun, one for time and materials and one for fixed price contracts, reported in columns 4 and 5 of Table 6. Estimating two separate equations rather than one allows parameter estimates to vary by contract mode. The positive sign of the inverse Mills ratio indicates positive selection, and the relative magnitude indicates that the coefficient of the inverse Mills ratio is greater for time and materials contracts (H5b is supported). In other words, the costs to a firm in choosing fixed price contracts when time and materials contracts should be chosen, such as the case when interdependence, uncertainty, and specific investments are high, consequently leading to excessive inflexibility, is more deleterious than choosing time and materials contracts when transaction cost minimization predicts fixed price contracts, leading to the attendant hazard of shirking. The probability of no overruns when the contract chosen is of a time and materials type is greater than that of all projects with equivalent observable characteristics had the projects adopted a fixed price contract. Since ASPs deliver a continuous stream of service, in an environment where firms need to take into account the effect of their actions on future business, the potential loss from shirking could be low.

We also compare across governance structures by examining exogenous factors that affect transaction costs. For instance, relational norms have the potential to lower monitoring costs by lowering the incentive for shirking. In Table $^ { 6 , }$ a comparison across columns 4 and 5 reveals that with greater relational norms, performance significantly worsens when governance structure shifts from time and materials contracts to fixed price contracts. In other words, when monitoring costs are low, we do worse by shifting to fixed price contracts, whose chief advantage in lowering monitoring costs is not very useful when monitoring costs are exogenously lowered through relationships. Another factor that has a bearing on monitoring costs is the type of client contact that the ASP engages in. When the ASPs interact more with business decision makers, there could be greater monitoring costs due to greater difficulties in communicating requirements and verifying performance ex post [16]. A comparison between fixed price and time and materials contracts reveals that ex post performance is better under fixed price contracts, which are effective in lowering monitoring costs.

A third approach adopted in this paper builds upon empirical tests in Sampson [54]. The propensity score, which is the probability of treatment conditional on the observables [53], is functionally independent from the outcome. Since the expected value of choosing a time and materials contract (in other words, the conditional probability of contract choice, which corresponds to the treatment effect), given the observable exchange hazards, is independent from the outcome, the propensity score method provides us a way to compare across governance choices controlling for selection effects. Using the estimates from Table 5, we classify contracts in terms of high and low hazard of maladaptation depending on the expected probability of choosing a time and materials contract, using a 40 percent cutoff; that is, contracts are classified as high hazard if the expected probability of time and materials contracts is in the top 40 percentile (expected probability from 0.6 to 1). We then calculate expected probability of no overruns for firms choosing misaligned governance; that is, expected outcome for the firm that chooses a time and materials contract when it is a low hazard of maladaptation, and expected outcome for a firm that chooses a fixed price contract with a high hazard of maladaptation. The results are presented in Table 7. We find a significant improvement with choosing aligned governance, and find that a lack of discriminating alignment results in excessive cost overruns, in line with previous research that incorrect governance induces asymmetric organizational costs [54].

## Controls

Relational norms between the ASP and the end-user organization are significantly associated with time and materials contracts. Since trust develops over a period of time from “credible commitments” [62] and the structure of interaction between participants, partners to an exchange may find that risk of opportunism is lower in an environment where there is trust. However, we note that expectations of continued interaction lead to a higher likelihood of fixed price contracts. When firms make an effort to continue long-term interactions with trading partners, opportunities for future interaction could increase goal congruence [26] and provide a mechanism to support current exchange. Adopting a longer time horizon may imply that the firm and the ASP resolve unanticipated contingencies through noncontractual mechanisms. Alternately, the ASP could absorb extra costs incurred in specific investments made for a client in the interest of the future stream of revenue from that client. The finding that relational norms are significantly associated with the likelihood of cost overruns when fixed price contracts are used indicates that the strength of the relationship between ASPs and end users can substitute for flexible contract terms, which is consistent with findings in prior research [49].

## Conclusions and Implications for Future Research

In this pa per, we identified a nd va lida ted a set of factors that determine selection of ASP contracts and conducted an analysis of performance implications of contract choices. Our findings contribute to the evolving area of ASP and business process outsourcing, where there are considerable costs in coordinating, adapting, and monitoring contractual arrangements. Many of the early failures in the ASP business model can be attributed to a lack of discriminating alignment between governance and transaction attributes. Our study holds normative implications for managers of user firms as well as ASPs in that the governance of outsourcing arrangements calls for ASPs and firms to adopt considerably different enforcement mechanisms depending on the contract regimes called for in the service interaction. A vendor needs to develop client-facing processes that are appropriate to the structure of incentives offered in a governance mode, and this has widely different implications for allocation of job roles and creation of talent to support incentive structures. For instance, an ASP that uses fixed price contracts has strong incentives to reduce costs (and consequently is focused on creating economies of scale in service delivery), while an ASP that uses time and materials contracts has incentives to deliver analytic capabilities to support business needs specific to individual clients (and consequently is focused on creating a pool of knowledge about clients’ industry and business functions).

Complex contracts may simultaneously incur costs of inflexibility and monitoring. Future research can analyze the role of contracting structures that protect parties by imposing costs on inflexibility as well as shirking, similar to prior literature that has prescribed both a holdup tax and a breach penalty [18]. Given the importance of managing user expectations and the role of satisfaction with vendors [48], it is important to note that, while contracts can be designed to provide incentives for ASPs on measurable dimensions, attributes such as user satisfaction are not always enumerated in a contract. Another area for future research is to consider the role of contract incompleteness and imperfect monitoring in that it affects the design and performance of contracts. Longitudinal research can further analyze discriminating alignment and impact of market competition on the evolution of the ASP business model. By integrating insights from industrial organization literature, future research can analyze whether adjustment costs impede ASPs and firms from realizing discriminating alignment and provide further insight into transaction costs and firm-level adaptation in the ASP business model.
