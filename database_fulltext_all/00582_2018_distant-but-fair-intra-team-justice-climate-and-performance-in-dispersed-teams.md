# Distant but Fair: Intra-Team Justice Climate and Performance in Dispersed Teams

MASSIMO MAGNI, MANJU K. AHUJA, AND LIKOEBE M. MARUPING

His general research interests include technology-enhanced behaviors, information systems development teams in geographically dispersed settings, and technology use in the work-life domain. His current interests revolve around emergent behaviors under pressure both at the individual and team level of analysis, as well as in conducting multilevel research that bridges team context and individual behaviors. His work has been published in Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Research Policy, Journal of Product Innovation Management, and other journals.

Dr. Ahuja served as a Senior Editor at MIS Quarterly and serves as a Senior Editor for Journal of the Association for Information Systems. Previously, she served as Division Chair and Program Chair of the Academy of Management’s OCIS division. His interests in this area pertain to the enabling role of digital collaboration platforms, the mechanisms underlying the collaboration process, and the leadership and governance of collaborative efforts in organizational and open environments. ABSTRACT: In an attempt to take advantage of distributed knowledge, organizations are increasingly relying on dispersed teams–teams of individuals who primarily utilize information and communication technology (ICT) to collaborate to achieve a shared objective. Team context has often been recognized as an important factor in affecting performance in dispersed team settings. Recent research has called for the consideration of intra-team justice climate in dispersed teams; yet, how it shapes performance for teams at different degrees of dispersion remains unresolved. Drawing on and integrating extant justice climate and Information Systems (IS) literature on team dispersion, we develop a model to better understand the precise nature of the role played by intra-team procedural justice climate in the relationship between team member dispersion and team performance. Responses from 468 team members and supervisors belonging to 101 work teams were used to test the research model. We found that team dispersion and procedural justice climate affect team performance, and that procedural justice alleviates the negative effect of dispersion on performance, indicating that intra-team procedural justice climate can be a lever for mitigating the challenges posed by ICT-based interaction among members. These findings make important contributions to the extant IS literature on dispersed teams by outlining how organizations may mitigate some of the challenges of dispersion and improve team performance by creating an intrateam environment based upon fairness.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: dispersed teams, virtual teams, procedural justice climate, team performance, intra-team justice, organizational fairness.

## Introduction

The widespread diffusion of information and communication technologies (ICTs) has enabled organizations to leverage a dispersed work force in order to handle a broad array of complex situations such as developing new products, improving organizational processes, or satisfying increasingly demanding customers [6, 11, 72, 93, 109]. Allured by the potential benefits of leveraging the intellectual resources possessed by indivi duals who are located at different sites, an increasing number of organizations are taking advantage of advances in ICTs to introduce dispersed teams as part of their strategy [107]. Dispersed teams are a collective of individuals who are distributed across different geographic locations and rely primarily on ICTs to communicate and collaborate with each other to achieve joint outcomes for which they are responsible.

The prevalence of dispersed teams (variously called virtual teams, distributed teams, etc.), which involve approximately 80% of employees [42], has resulted in scholarly interest in this area in recent years. To better understand these teams, researchers in IS as well as other disciplines, have examined a variety of factors that affect team outcomes, ranging from frameworks of virtual teams [48, 96] to examinations of team dynamics such as conflict [58, 19, 88], trust [2, 56], team coordination [41], and knowledge management [73]. The results of these studies demonstrate that dispersed teams often fall short in fulfilling performance expectations because organizations tend to replicate the same approaches to managing these teams as those used in traditional co-located team settings [e.g., 80], overlooking the fact that such organizational structures are ICT-enabled and bring with them challenges that are inherently embedded in an ICT-enabled environment [78].

Developing an environment that promotes positive interpersonal interactions is one of the most critical challenges that managers of dispersed teams face [75]. In dispersed contexts, team members must rely on interactions via ICTs, which by their very nature provide limited cues about colleagues working at remote sites, thus making the interaction with others more ambiguous and uncertain [e.g., 86, 107]. Social information exchange relies on communication of tacit cues such as body language and attitudes in addition to explicit information [2], which is much more difficult to transmit using electronic media [101]. Further, a lack of visual cues and social interactions along with limited information exacerbates any tendencies to suspect unfairness and limits understanding of others’ contexts [2, 28].

A major concern that arises when team members’ context and ongoing actions are unobservable is whether or not there is fairness across the team members in terms of rewards, input expectations, resource support, recognition, etc. [e.g., 33]. Perceptions of fairness have proven to be pivotal in enabling individuals to cope with uncertainty regarding decision-making processes within teams [25, 70]. By observing and integrating the information collected from their social environment, team members make sense of events that occur in their workplace and develop perceptions that could be useful to interpret events, develop appropriate attitudes, and understand expectations concerning their behavior and its consequences [2, 3, 95]. These perceptions of fairness are collectively referred to as organizational justice and interest in them has increased significantly over the past couple of decades as evidenced in numerous empirical studies, books, and meta-analyses [e.g., 25, 30]. Perceptions of higher levels of justice have been empirically associated with positive outcomes such as organizational commitment, job satisfaction, job performance, and citizenship behaviors [23, 25]. A review of the literature suggests that much of the prior research on justice and its effects has been conducted in co-located settings and at the individual level, failing to account for their ICT-enabled team context [e.g., 67, 92]. This makes sense because most employees are assessed on an individual basis and traditionally, most work arrangements have focused on individuals rather than teams. However, as organizations have leveraged ICT to shift their work arrangements to focus on dispersed teams of individuals working collaboratively, it makes sense to better understand how justice perceptions operate at the team level when members rely on ICT to manage their interactions [17, 24, 26, 36, 38, 68]. This emergent research stream recognizes that most of the potential information about fairness lies in the collective construction of perceptions, rather than in personal experiences and that team members can also be important sources of justice [90]. This research has therefore developed the concept of team justice climate, which differs from individual justice in that it does not reflect a single individual’s perceptions. Rather it originates as a shared cognition of fairness among team members [26, 91].

Previous studies have called for incorporating contextual factors [25] and suggested that team member dispersion might be an important contextual factor in team justice research [92]. Although the extant research on procedural justice climate has made important advances in understanding its effects on team and individual outcomes [5, 68], several important research gaps remain that warrant examination as dispersed team arrangements have now become the norm. First, to the best of our knowledge, current studies on procedural justice climate have not taken into account the role of team dispersion, and we aim to do just that. Teambased work arrangements have challenges that are exacerbated when team members are dispersed, and developing a shared context becomes more difficult when team members mostly rely on ICT to manage their interactions [52]. For instance, in the case of software development projects with India or China, two common complaints among the developers are related to fairness. They lament that their U.S. counterparts get far more cushy assignments while they get maintenance or socalled grunt work, and that cross-border team meetings are often scheduled during times that are convenient for their U.S. counterparts but inconvenient for the offshore developers [27, 47]. Such a situation can potentially influence procedural justice climate or its effects on team outcomes based on the fact that ICTs support both collective communication and subgroup communication [35, 48, 75]. The implication is that it creates opportunities for subsets of team members to make decisions about team assignments without being observed by other teammates. Clearly, such technology-enabled collaboration dynamics have potential to advance our understanding of procedural justice climate and its role in dispersed teams. Therefore, our first objective is to incorporate the concept of justice climate in the context of teams with different degrees of dispersion.

Second, the literature does not clarify the role that procedural justice climate plays in influencing team outcomes under different contextual conditions—in this case dispersion. Two main pathways emerged from previous research that examined how procedural justice climate operates under different team contextual conditions. On one hand, some studies have suggested that procedural justice climate is influenced by team characteristics, and that this may in turn affect team outcomes [e.g., 5]. Along these lines, Roberson and Colquitt [92] developed a theoretical argument suggesting that team justice may be influenced by team dispersion, based on the assumption that reliance on ICT to manage communication across different sites may constrain the development of a shared perception of justice, with potential negative effects on team outcomes. On the other hand, research also suggests that team justice may play a facilitating role [111], thus implying an interaction effect of procedural justice climate with dispersion in explaining team outcomes. This perspective relies on the argument that the presence of a favorable climate may enhance the advantages of relying on dispersed knowledge, while attenuating the challenges of ICT-mediated communication. By integrating these two perspectives, our study attempts to respond to the call made by Cropanzano et al. [29] that a better understanding of the role of justice climate represents an interesting line of inquiry, and relatively little work has pursued this avenue. Thus, our second objective is twofold: first we examine the direct effect of team dispersion on the development of procedural justice climate; second, we investigate whether a high level of procedural justice climate may temper (or even overcome) the challenges that dispersion poses to achieving team performance.

Developing a deeper theoretical understanding of the nature of the relationship between team dispersion and procedural justice climate contributes to both the dispersed teams and justice streams of research. Research on team dispersion would benefit by enhancing understanding of the relationship between team dispersion and performance. It is broadly understood that ICT enables dispersed teams to be more effective at information sharing and coordination [59]. Less well understood is whether ICTs create conditions for dispersed teams to promote shared perceptions of procedural justice climate and whether the existence of procedural justice climate may facilitate the ICT-based interactions of dispersed members. Our study allows us to highlight whether procedural justice climate may represent a way to achieve better outcomes associated with tapping into dispersed knowledge, and whether team dispersion represents an obstacle for the development of a climate that is conducive for achieving high performance. In order to examine the issues previously detailed, we conducted a study involving 468 individuals from 101 work teams of varying degrees of dispersion.

## Theoretical Development

## Intra-team Procedural Justice Climate

Procedural justice refers to the fairness of the process by which a decision is made [67]. It has primarily been studied using an individual-level lens [29]. In general, this research has found that individuals who perceive that they are treated fairly are more likely to exhibit positive attitudes and behaviors such as job satisfaction [e.g., 103], organizational commitment [67], and citizenship behaviors [77], and they are also less likely to quit [103].

Although these studies represent a stepping stone for understanding employee behaviors, they do not take into account the role of the social context within which justice perceptions are shaped. Individuals are likely to interact and observe each other’s behavior, and engage in collective sensemaking, which leads to shared perceptions of fairness [91]. Such an emergent perspective, in which justice is considered as a socially constructed phenomenon, has paved the way for the development of the concept of procedural justice climate, which refers to the shared cognition held by team members about the fairness of decision-making processes within the team [24].<sup>1</sup> Thus, by relying on this integrated perspective, our conceptualization of justice climate takes into account the perception of fairness within the team (i.e., intra-team justice climate), reflecting how people who are part of a team treat one another [29]. In light of this conceptualization, intra-team procedural justice climate is defined here as the perceived fairness of the procedures and decision-making process within the team [67]. Chen et al. [22] examined procedural justice climate as a potential antecedent of group citizenship behavior, while others investigated the effects of procedural justice climate on workplace aggression [36]. Other studies took a cross-level approach and found that procedural justice climate affects individual commitment, satisfaction and citizenship behavior [68]. The root of these studies on procedural justice climate can be traced back to social information processing theory, which states that climate influences individuals’ behavior by affecting how they think and feel about certain aspects of their proximate environment [95], such as the team to which they belong. Employees rely on cues from their teammates to interpret events, develop appropriate attitudes, and understand expectations concerning their behavior and its consequences [95].

The intra-team conceptualization of justice climate has its roots in the social psychological literature that focuses on teamwork [e.g., 39, 98]. It relies on the assumption that interaction with teammates represents an important source of the climate perception within the team, thus including the perception of whether team members interact in a fair manner [16, 33, 98]. As suggested by recent studies, fair treatment from teammates promotes behavior that serves the needs of coworkers and members’ willingness to exert effort for the benefit of the team [54]. An intra-team view of procedural justice climate suggests that fairness can refer to the interaction among members, and it complements previous literature on justice climate that mainly focused on supervisors and the organization as a whole as agents for providing justice cues [54]. Relying on the critical value of team member interaction, according to Li and Corpanzano [67], the same set of principles regarding justice that refer to the organizational level can be applied within the team. Through the interactions within the team, members develop a set of shared principles and expectations regarding the acceptable way to treat each other and influencing the behaviors of the team members. As such, the existence of perceived fair intra-team climate, in which members encourage participation and suppress biases, may play a pivotal role in supporting the team to achieve its goals. Moreover, intra-team procedural justice climate has been advocated to be especially critical in contexts where team members dominate day-to-day operations [77], and adopting an intra-team perspective of procedural justice climate is particularly suitable in studying dispersed teams since it focuses on the relationship among team members rather than focusing on the relationship with the leader. As highlighted by previous research, as the dispersion of team members increases, team leaders often have no choice but to distribute and delegate leadership functions and responsibilities to team members, decreasing their own influence on team functioning [10, 54] and team members may function as a substitute for many of the leadership functions that are normally executed by a team leader who is physically present and interacting face-to-face with a team [54].

## Dispersion in Teams

The seminal conceptualization of team dispersion primarily focused on a unidimensional representation that depicted dispersion as an alternative to co-located settings [63, 82]. Such representation looked at the extremes of full co-location versus full dispersion and most of the empirical work aimed at comparing team processes and outcomes between the two scenarios [e.g., 21, 55]. While the dichotomous interpretation of dispersion represented an important stepping stone to advance research on the phenomenon of dispersed teams, recent studies developed a more finegrained representation of team dispersion that enables researchers to gain further insight on the role of dispersion in teamwork settings [84]. In departing from the dichotomous view of team dispersion, this stream of research emphasizes different degrees of dispersion by looking at the arrangement of team members across physical sites (e.g., configuration of the team across different locations) [53, 84, 106]. Therefore, in our study, we rely on a more fine-grained and nuanced conceptualization of dispersion by considering different configurations.

In theorizing about the role of team dispersion and its relationship to intra-team procedural justice climate, it is important to keep in mind that this all occurs within an ICT-enabled context. Indeed, dispersed teams are ICT-enabled and, therefore, theorizing about the effects of dispersion necessarily requires consideration of ICT [35, 105].<sup>2</sup> Griffith et al. [48] point out that, although ICT makes dispersed teams viable, it can also result in uneven distribution of knowledge among individuals within an organization, which is particularly relevant in shaping the way team members form and rely on the shared perception of being in a context characterized by fairness. In support of this point, Ahuja et al. [1] found that individuals establish different patterns of communication within their ICT-enabled group, and that being at the center of this pattern of communication has positive effects on performance. This is all possible because ICTs can simultaneously enable shared communication with all team members or networks of communication among a small subset of team members [35]. Research suggests that such ability of ICT to enable parallel communication channels gives dispersed teams flexibility in determining which communications should be shared by all team members and which should be more limited to a subgroup [75]. In sum, because of the inherently ICT-enabled communication that occurs among members across diverse locations, team dispersion represents a pivotal phenomenon to better understand the role of procedural justice climate in shaping team interactions and in facilitating team performance.

## Effect of Members’ Dispersion on Procedural Justice Climate

While there is evidence that procedural justice climate can be affected by contextual variables [38], the role of team dispersion has only been theoretically advocated as an important contextual condition for justice research. To date, there is no empirical research examining the relation between the two. Given this, Roberson and Colquitt [92] called for empirical research in this domain, arguing that a situation characterized by lower proximity, where individuals rely on ICTs to manage their interactions, may limit the opportunity for the development of a shared context between members, thus preventing the emergence of justice climate.

The assumption that dispersion may hamper the development of procedural justice climate is linked to three main arguments. First, when team members are highly dispersed, they mostly rely on ICT to manage their interactions thus limiting the opportunities for spontaneous communication processes, and eroding the establishment of shared context [28, 53, 57]. A shared context is a state in which members of a team have access to the same information about their work, the same understanding of the environment in which aspects of the work are being executed, use the same tools in executing the work and have the same work processes [28, 53]. When employees are working across different settings, they are less likely to be exposed to the same, or even similar, contextual cues, thus limiting their ability to develop a shared interpretation of the environment and hampering the development of a team climate [68]. Even synchronous ICTs that facilitate greater symbol variety in communication are limited in conveying contextual cues [28]. This limitation in ICTs makes it difficult to psychologically experience a team member’s local context. This is likely to lead teams to show greater dissimilarity in members’ perceptions as team members adopt a biased perspective on their distant colleagues [28], which, in turn, affects the ability to develop a shared procedural justice climate.

Second, as underscored by previous studies, dispersion can lead to the weakening of interpersonal bonds, leading individuals to be more focused on their local interest rather than being focused on other team members’ concerns across different locations [6, 53]. Individuals who communicate through ICT can be prone to psychologically dehumanize team members who are not physically present [6]. Therefore, an ICT-based communication process may lead to selective involvement of team members, limiting the opportunity to develop a shared perception of fairness. A higher level of dispersion may reduce the consideration that team members exhibit toward one another, eroding perceptions of fairness regarding the team-decision making process that underlies the development of a procedural justice climate.

Finally, sharing of spontaneous and social cues that promote understanding and bonding is less likely to occur when members are not proximal to each other [2, 28]. Dispersed team members are less able to monitor each other’s progress on tasks, to determine availability for impromptu meetings, and to verify necessary information for completing their own tasks [41]. All of this makes it more problematic to provide timely feedback to other team members during the decisionmaking process. Indeed, in dispersed team settings, it is more likely that information exchange happens on a one-on-one basis without necessarily involving the rest of the team members [53]. In such situations, it can be easier for team members to conclude that they have been excluded from the team’s decision making process [28], thus reducing their perception of a procedural justice climate.

Conversely, as the distance of team members decreases, individuals are more likely to engage in spontaneous and scheduled interactions, which can trigger more frequent e-mail and verbal communication among proximal team members [8], thus contributing to the development of a shared understanding on the expected behaviors that should be embraced in the team’s decision making process. Finally, close proximity among team members enhances their ability to ensure they have a shared representation of the problem at hand and understand each other’s roles and responsibilities in real-time [41, 79], making it easier to provide feedback about the decisions taken and their implementation, and triggering the shared perception that the team’s decision making processes are fair. Based on the aforementioned information, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1: Team members’ dispersion has a negative effect on team procedural justice climate.

## Effect of Members’ Dispersion on Team Performance

Whereas the relationship between team dispersion and team outcomes has been studied in depth and from different angles, we develop and test this hypothesis for completeness of our research model. Developing a hypothesis on the effect of members’ dispersion on team performance allows us to corroborate the fact that our study is built on accreted evidence from previous research. It also allows us to make more evident that the contributions of our study (i.e. the role of procedural justice climate) go above and beyond the current empirical evidence emerging from research on dispersed teams.

Prior research indicates that sustaining interdependent actions among team members becomes more difficult with increasing levels of dispersion [9, 57, 84, 106]. As dispersion increases, the possibility of spontaneous or informal face-to-face communication drops, and the so-called hallway or water cooler discussions are largely unavailable as vehicles of communication. Cummings et al. [31] find that dispersed teams that utilize asynchronous ICTs experience greater coordination delays. Consequently, in dispersed contexts it becomes more difficult for team members to seek information, address differences of opinion, and question problem-solving assumptions with their teammates [41]. As team members become more dispersed, the likelihood that they span temporal boundaries increases, making opportunities for synchronous communication scarcer [48]. By limiting social interaction processes, team member dispersion reduces the knowledge and information immediately available to team members and creates a context in which it is more difficult to recombine the available resources and achieve a high level of performance [52, 72]. Indeed, integrating heterogeneous expertise from different locations becomes particularly challenging because shared context is limited [9, 15, 28, 41] and ICTs may not provide real-time awareness of various team members’ task status [41]. Such a perspective is consistent with previous studies that have shown that dispersion may hamper ongoing coordination and synchronization of team activities [54, 40], thus making it more difficult to converge to a shared course of action to achieve objectives.

Conversely, close proximity of team members allows individuals to be more aware about the status of the team tasks through a real-time observation of other members’ activities. In addition, team members are better able to take advantage of new information that may become available and to restructure work sequences and task assignments as needed [9]. With increased proximity, team members tend to be more aligned in performing team activities and it is less likely that there is duplication of effort in task execution while execution of other tasks falls through the cracks.

In sum, team member dispersion has important implications for the ability of teams to perform. As such, we expect that team dispersion may hamper team performance.

Hypothesis 2: Team members’ dispersion has a negative effect on team performance.

## Moderation Effect of Team Procedural Justice Climate

The evidence that fairness perceptions play a moderating role in shaping the effect of contextual variables has been elucidated in previous studies. For instance, Aquino et al. [7] underscored that procedural justice climate enhances the effect of organizational hierarchical status and likelihood of reconciliation among parties. In a similar vein, Tangirala and Ramanujam [102] found that procedural justice climate enhanced the positive effect of individuals’ perception of the context on individual willingness to speak up as problems occur. The primary logic in these studies is that procedural justice climate constitutes an environment in which individuals belonging to the same group share the feeling that processes are fair and thus were more likely to exhibit favorable behaviors.

By creating an atmosphere in which members share a perception of fairness in decision-making, procedural justice climate may help mitigate the liabilities that characterize dispersed settings, thus fostering a team’s ability to perform. Procedural justice climate facilitates the interaction of dispersed members by taking into consideration other members’ perspectives and concerns, suppressing personal biases, and by providing timely feedback about decisions and their implementation. Three main arguments underlie the moderating role of procedural justice climate.

First, by inducing team members to be more considerate of the perspectives of their teammates when accomplishing tasks, procedural justice climate can help overcome the collaboration pitfalls often associated with dispersed teams [42]. Teams characterized by high procedural justice climate emphasize concerns for the other team members’ points of view when making decisions, thus increasing the likelihood that team members will express their opinion even if they are not physically present. The functionality of ICTs makes it possible for team members to communicate to the team as a whole, ensuring that all are apprised of ongoing decisions and developments [35]. As suggested by previous research, only if team members feel as though they are in an environment in which they are able to share information across contexts will teams be able to leverage local expertise and resources and support team goals [76]. This makes dispersion less of a liability in a team’s attempt to achieve performance.

Second, a procedural justice climate helps to increase communication flow among team members as well as the process of giving and receiving feedback to overcome problems related to misunderstanding and knowledge interpretation that result from dispersion and lack of social cues [101]. A procedural justice climate encourages timely feedback about the decision making process within the team, which is recognized to be a critical element that influences performance because it creates the opportunity to avoid misalignment by facilitating continuous evaluation, crafting, and recombination of team members’ ideas. Synchronous ICT functionalities support this objective [31, 35, 75]. Numerous studies show that a climate that encourages timely and continuous feedback among individuals is likely to build bonds between distant colleagues and enable information to flow more fluidly between sites [e.g., 81], thus facilitating better performance.

Third, procedural justice climate has been identified as a particularly important element that can promote helping behaviors among team members in their attempt to overcome contextual difficulties [68]. The shared perception of fair team decision-making procedures activates a sense of team harmony that leads team members to subsume their local interests in order to support team functioning [70]. This argument is consistent with recent research findings that, to the extent that procedural justice climate conveys fairness, team members could be expected to better contribute their voluntary effort to cooperate in service of goal accomplishment [62]. This reduces the risk of dispersed team members pursuing their local task objectives at the expense of team goals [45]. Such reasoning is also consistent with the findings of previous research which points out that, when a favorable climate exists, individuals are more likely to provide unsolicited information to other members as a way of showing both goodwill and intimacy, thus strengthening relationships in the team [76]. This may support team members in overcoming the weakened relationships caused by dispersed team arrangements [64], consequently increasing the ability to effectively recombine dispersed knowledge.

Finally, we argue that procedural justice climate may mitigate the challenges related to team dispersion by leveraging the development of team values and norms that emphasize expected ways of relating to others, reducing ambiguity regarding those behaviors that are expected and supported within the team. In teams with high procedural justice climate, team members will be less likely to evaluate other team members’ behaviors negatively, prompting them to adopt a cooperative stance (rather than a competitive stance) when problems or miscommunications derived from dispersion arise [53]. As observed by extant research, members of dispersed teams are naturally inclined toward harsh, dispositional attributions about distant team members because they lack situational information to help them interpret the behaviors and activities of their distant colleagues [53, 104]. The existence of high procedural justice climate may work as a de-biasing mechanism by creating a shared understanding of what is considered fair in the decision making process within the team. Such a positive disposition is robust to supporting communication via synchronous and asynchronous ICTs, rendering dispersion less of a barrier to effective collaboration. Through procedural justice climate, members are more likely to reduce in-group/out-group barriers, suspend judgment, and remain open to others’ ideas and perspectives, thus fostering the ability to exploit dispersed knowledge [83].

Hypothesis 3: Procedural justice climate moderates the relationship between team dispersion and team performance such that the relationship will be weaker with increasing levels of procedural justice climate.

## Effect of Team Procedural Justice Climate on Team Performance

The role of procedural justice climate in influencing team outcomes [e.g., 5] can be understood in light of social exchange theory [12]. The theory suggests that socioemotional resources activate mutual support among members of an organization. From such a perspective, the expression of fair behaviors within the team represents a good-will gesture, which is likely to be reciprocated with fair behaviors, thus enhancing team members’ effort toward a common goal within the team [66]. According to this perspective, the existence of a climate where team members treat each other fairly facilitates the emergence of a social exchange relationship within the team.

Three main arguments underlie the positive effects of procedural justice climate on team performance. First, a situation characterized by procedural justice promotes cooperation among team members because reciprocation among members is supported and expected [99]. Team members are more prone to support each other’s ideas and to assist others when needed, thus increasing team members’ ability to leverage team resources to achieve team goals. Conversely, when procedural justice climate is low, team members tend to express competitive behaviors within the team, thus diminishing the effort they put in fulfilling the team activities, requiring coordination and integration. Such a lack of collaborative activity can hamper team performance.

Second, procedural justice climate fosters a sense of respect among team members because individuals feel that they are treated fairly by their teammates [70]. As a consequence, team members are likely to develop a sense of pride in belonging to the team and are likely to demonstrate strong caring for the other members [61], which enhances their willingness to contribute to team outcomes. Specifically, if members believe that fair treatment exists among team members, they will exert more effort in accomplishing the team’s tasks, will better fulfill their individual role requirements, and will present information or knowledge to solve problems and to enhance effectiveness of decisions that lead to better performance [5].

Finally, according to previous studies, procedural justice climate can provide important cues to individuals about whether expressing work-related concerns or opinions will make them vulnerable to adverse actions, thus shaping their willingness to contribute. It is well-understood that team success is influenced by team members’ willingness to take other members’ perspective, question and challenge team assumptions in order to reach a common understanding on ongoing activities [32, 87, 106]. High levels of procedural justice climate allow team members to freely voice their opinion regarding how to conduct team activities without the fear of being vulnerable, thus allowing the team to utilize team members’ resources to achieve their goal.

On the basis of these arguments, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 4: Procedural justice climate has a positive effect on team performance.

## Method

## Participants and procedure

Given our interest in examining the phenomenon under investigation in naturally occurring conditions, we utilized surveys of team supervisors and members from a broad sample of organizations. In particular, our study involved several teams whose supervisors were participating in an executive education program on leadership development in a European business school and who agreed to involve their team members in the study. The involvement of supervisors and team members from a broad sample of organizations is consistent with previous studies and it is said to increase the generalizability of the study findings [110]. Each team involved in the study had a formal supervisor (i.e., the person who participated in the training program) who bore the responsibility of ensuring that the team met its goals.

At the beginning of the training courses, the supervisors were presented with the purpose and procedures of the study by one of the researchers and offered a personalized report (with aggregated data) containing the strengths and the areas for improvement for their team. Participation in the study was not mandatory, and upon consent, team supervisors provided the roster of their team with the necessary information to contact the members. This resulted in a total of 533 individuals who were then invited to participate. Before sending the invitation, the supervisors were asked to provide an advance notice about the upcoming request to fill out a survey related to the team, underscoring that the supervisor would not have access to their individual responses and that their participation was on a voluntary basis. In the invitation letter sent by the research team, participants were assured of the anonymity of their data. They were told that the data collection, along with research purposes, was aimed at supporting their team supervisor in developing his/her team leadership skills and that their candid input would be particularly important for creating a personalized report for their supervisor (containing the average values in aggregated form and not attributable to the individual rater). A total of 468 individuals belonging to 101 teams provided usable responses.

## Measures

Unless otherwise stated, all variables were measured using a five-point Likert-type scale with values ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” In order to ensure content validity and to avoid a possible common source bias, data from different respondents were used to operationalize the variables.

## Team Performance

Team supervisors responded to questions about the overall performance of the team they oversee. A four-item scale from Joshi et al. [57] was utilized. The scale taps into the following dimensions of performance: effectiveness, innovation, adherence to schedule, and efficiency. A sample item is “The team delivers its commitments.”

## Procedural Justice Climate

Team members responded to questions about team procedural justice climate. We measured procedural justice climate by adapting a scale from Cropanzano et al. [29], and we used the team as the referent, following a referent-shift consensus approach [20]. A sample item is “In the team, procedures are designed to hear the concerns of all those affected by the decision.”

## Team Dispersion

Our measure of team dispersion tapped into the spatial and configural dimensions of dispersion discussed elsewhere [see 84, 106]. There was no variability in temporal dispersion. For each participant we obtained country, city, and address location, following previous studies [57]. On the basis of this information we calculated the proportion of team members in each location represented in the team (configural dimension) as well as the number of office building locations represented in a team (spatial dimension). Following previous studies [e.g., 57] we created a single team-dispersion index encompassing spatial and configural dispersion.

## Controls

In order to isolate the impact of dispersion and procedural justice climate, a number of variables that may affect team performance were included as controls. We included team size because larger teams are argued to give team members access to a broader array of resources. However, larger teams also create greater interactional complexity, thereby hindering the ability of individuals to collaborate and recombine resources to achieve performance. We also controlled for task interdependence, members’ average level of education, and members’ average tenure because they may potentially affect team outcomes [50]. Moreover, following Joshi et al. [57] we controlled for the team composition in terms of proportion of women in each team.

## Analytical Approach

Before proceeding to test the hypotheses, we assessed the measurement model by examining the construct distinctiveness through confirmatory factor analysis (CFAs) using Amos 25. Since covariance-based approaches such as Amos achieve good parameter estimation with a suggested minimum threshold of 200 observations [74], we evaluated our measurement model at the individual level of analysis. We performed a CFA on the three exogenous latent variables (procedural justice climate, team performance, interdependence) to establish their discriminant validity. The three-factor model provided a generally good fit to the data: comparative fit index (CFI) = .96; incremental fit index $( \operatorname { I F I } ) = . 9 6 ;$ root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = .05. CFI and IFI values that are closer to 1 reflect better-fitting models [18] and values of .08 or less for RMSEA indicate good fit [37, 49]. We compared the three-factor model against a single-factor one. The onefactor model was a significantly poorer fit than the three-factor model (CFI = .49, IFI = .51, RMSEA = .17). Thus, the CFAs suggested good discriminant validity in our measurement model. In addition to assessing the discriminant validity, we also evaluated the internal consistency of our measures by calculating the Cronbach’s alpha. All the constructs in our model are fairly above the suggested threshold of 0.70, thus demonstrating good internal consistency [43]. ## Results

In light of the robustness of our measurement model, we proceeded to test our hypotheses using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analysis on the 101 teams. Results are presented in Table 2. Hypothesis 1 posited a negative influence of team dispersion on procedural justice climate. Results indicate that team dispersion does not have a statistically significant effect on team procedural justice climate (Model 2; $\beta ~ = ~ . 0 5 , ~ \mathfrak { p } ~ > ~ . 0 5 )$ . Thus, Hypothesis 1 is not supported. Hypothesis 2 predicted a negative effect of team dispersion on team performance. Results show that team performance is negatively affected by team dispersion (Model 4; $\beta = - . 1 7 , \ p < . 0 5 )$ , thus providing support for Hypothesis 2.

Hypothesis 3 posited that procedural justice climate moderates the relationship between team dispersion and team performance, such that this relationship becomes weaker with increasing procedural justice climate. In order to test this hypothesis, we followed a two-step approach [51]. In the first step, the main effect terms were entered (Model 4), in the second step the interaction term was entered into the model (Model 5) [4, 89]. In order to create the interaction term, we relied on the orthogonalization approach, which has been shown to have an accurate prediction of the model especially with small sample sizes [71]. As a first step, the orthogonalization approach requires the creation of all product indicators of the interaction terms (i.e., multiplying each indicator of the independent variable with each indicator of the moderator). As a second step, in order to avoid any collinearity that may remain from the standardization process and to avoid biased estimation of path coefficients, we regress each product indicator obtained from the first step on all indicators of the independent variable and the moderator variable. In this way, it is possible to obtain the standardized residuals of each indicator product term and use them as indicators of the interaction term. As outlined by Little et al. [71] the orthogonalization approach that is based on residual centering, offers several advantages in terms robustness and interpretation. First, the coefficients of the model are stable, such that the coefficients and standard errors of the main effects remain similar when the interaction term is added in the model. Second, because of the elimination of any residual collinearity between the interaction term and the variables under consideration, the effect of the interaction term is not biased and does not suffer from inflated standard errors. Finally, and consistent with the previous arguments, the orthogonalization approach ensures full independence between the moderator and the main effects.

Support for the moderation hypothesis was assessed in several ways. First, we examined the significance of the interaction coefficient, which is positive and significant $( \beta ^ { \mathrm { ~ = ~ } } . 1 7 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } . 0 5 )$ . Second, we examined the additional variance explained when the interaction term was added to the regression model. Results in Table 2 (Model 5) indicate that the two-way interaction between team dispersion and procedural justice climate explained an additional 3% of the variance in team performance over and above that explained by the main effects model (Model 4). In order to assess the magnitude of the moderating effect, we calculated Cohen’s $f ^ { 2 }$ for the hypothesized interaction since it represents the effect size of a moderator. A value of .025 indicates a large effect size of the moderation [60]. The Cohen’s $f ^ { 2 }$ for the interaction model predicting team performance is .033 corroborating the significant effect of the interaction between team dispersion and procedural justice climate and its medium-high magnitude. Finally, in order to understand the form of the moderation, we plotted the interaction effect following the guidelines outlined by Aiken and West [4].<sup>3</sup> Specifically, we plotted the relationship between dispersion and performance at one standard deviation above and below the mean for procedural justice climate. As the interaction plot in Figure 2 depicts, the negative relationship between dispersion and performance is attenuated when procedural justice climate is high. The aforementioned results demonstrate support for Hypothesis 3 in that procedural justice climate attenuates the effect of team dispersion on performance. ## Discussion

## Theoretical Contributions

Our study offers several contributions to the literature on team dispersion and justice climate. First, this research contributes to the IS literature by adding a contextual perspective focused on justice climate to previous studies that investigated factors such as conflict, trust, coordination and knowledge management in dispersed settings [58, 19, 88, 56, 41, 73]. Our consideration of procedural justice climate in dispersed teams adds an important social context that enhances understanding of conditions in which dispersion is or is not a liability for teams. As suggested by research on telecommuting [46] and dispersed teams [85], communicating strictly through ICTs can be socially isolating. Moreover, even as the ICT functionalities are becoming increasingly sophisticated, there are limits to how well they support a sense of shared context for team members who are in different geographical locations. Our research suggests that there is value in promoting a procedural justice climate in which team members feel that they have a hand in participating in decisions that affect the team. Many ICTs already come equipped with such functionality as suggested by research on media capabilities [35].

Second, this study aims to shed more light on the role of procedural justice climate in predicting team outcomes under different contextual conditions. Our model represents a step forward in this field of research by empirically reconciling different views in the research on procedural justice at the team level of analysis. We believe that because the precise role of procedural justice is not yet known, it is particularly appropriate to examine this mechanism and provide a better understanding of the interplay between dispersion and procedural justice climate. In particular, our study contributes to extant literature by showing that both procedural justice climate and team dispersion influence team performance, and that procedural justice attenuates the negative effect of dispersion on team performance. Our results show that team dispersion does not influence procedural justice climate, and the occurrence of a team climate based on fair decision-making processes is not contingent on team members dispersion. In other words, even if team members are highly dispersed, it is possible for the team to experience a climate of procedural fairness. This finding acquires even more importance when coupled with the results that demonstrate the moderating effect of procedural justice climate. Indeed, while our findings show the lack of a direct impact of dispersion on procedural justice climate, we also demonstrate that the existence of procedural justice climate plays a pivotal role in overcoming the challenges posed by team dispersion. From an IS standpoint, our findings on the moderating effect of procedural justice climate suggest that it can sensitize team members to use the necessary functionalities to ensure broader participation in decision making and alleviate the perils of dispersion. Under the condition of a fair climate within the team, contemporary tools that are commonly adopted for managing communications in dispersed settings (e.g., Slack, Yammer, and Wikis), may represent a useful way to support members’ interactions, and they could be helpful to raise awareness about the dispersed knowledge that team members possess [65], and to effectively leverage such knowledge to achieve team goals.

Third, we identify potential mechanisms for mitigating the negative effects of team dispersion. In theorizing about the moderating role of procedural justice climate, we reasoned that increasing levels of procedural justice climate would attenuate the negative effects of dispersion on team performance. The underlying logic was that, while team reliance on ICT-based interaction erects barriers to timely and effective interaction, the existence of a team climate rooted in fairness is likely to encourage timely and continuous feedback among individuals, while also facilitating the development of bonds among distant colleagues. Interestingly, we found a strong interaction effect indicating that, at low levels of procedural justice climate, increasing degrees of dispersion among team members is associated with low performance. Therefore, the lack of a fair climate within the team is likely to exacerbate the challenges that team members face when they heavily rely on ICT to manage their interactions. Conversely, at high levels of procedural justice climate the negative relationship between dispersion and performance is attenuated. This result is particularly significant because it shows that the potential benefits of dispersed knowledge inherent in dispersed teams can be leveraged by the existence of a team environment that is characterized by fairness. Thus, teams that are characterized by high levels of procedural justice climate are more likely to leverage the benefits offered by ICTs to transform individuals’ dispersed knowledge into effective outcomes, while attenuating the pitfalls that are usually associated with reliance on technology-mediated communication. Our results show that the development of a procedural justice climate becomes more critical as the reliance on ICTs becomes more pivotal because of an increase in team member dispersion. This represents an important boundary condition that the simple reliance on dispersed teams in an attempt to leverage dispersed knowledge is not sufficient for managing complex tasks [108] and that it requires managerial interventions in shaping the justice climate in which these members operate. This evidence complements the findings of previous studies that outlined the importance of implementing effective ICT tools to deal with dispersion [44]. Beyond making choices about the technological tools, there is the need to put a significant effort in developing a team environment that fosters the effectiveness of such tools.

Fourth, this research contributes to the justice literature by incorporating the role of team dispersion as an important and increasingly prevalent aspect of team context that can influence team performance. By finding that procedural justice climate moderates the relationship between team dispersion and performance, we extend the current literature by showing the team climate contingency, which may help teams to reap the advantage of dispersed knowledge for reaching higher levels of performance. Much research has argued that reliance on ICT-enabled communication is increasingly becoming common to support teams that operate in dispersed settings. Our findings provide clarity regarding the implications of such emerging structures on justice climate in teams. While previous research advocated the need to incorporate the role of dispersion in justice literature [92], to the best of our knowledge there are no studies that empirically consider the emerging and important role of team member dispersion and its interplay with procedural justice. While we show the effect of procedural justice climate in dispersed settings, our results also demonstrate the potential danger associated with dispersion and a lack of a team climate based on fair decision making. Under the circumstances characterized by high levels of dispersion, the absence of procedural justice climate could further exacerbate the hindrance of dispersed knowledge advantages.

Finally, our research contributes to the emergent debate on the role of justice as a group-level phenomenon. Much of the research on organizational fairness has been conducted at the individual level of analysis. As noted by Li and Cropanzano [67] the focus on the individual level of analysis is potentially problematic because there is sufficient evidence that justice climate plays a pivotal role in influencing team and individual outcomes [94]. By taking into account the important role of justice climate, we focused on an emergent conceptualization of procedural justice, which looks at the intra-team domain (i.e., the perceived fairness among team members). While there is scant research on intra-team procedural justice, extant research underscores its difference from the traditional conceptualization of procedural justice, pointing out that individuals are able to distinguish the different sources of justice perceptions (i.e., the organization as a whole or the team members). Therefore, our results corroborate the importance of studying procedural justice climate as an intra-team concept, and they complement previous findings indicating justice climate as particularly suitable for those contexts in which the interaction among team members is critical for accomplishing team-level goals.

## Managerial Implications

In addition to making the theoretical contributions outlined in the previous section, this research offers several insights for managers. From a managerial perspective, our results underscore the fact that organizations and teams must develop a fertile climate if they want to leverage the advantages of dispersed knowledge. By leveraging the development of a procedural justice climate within the team, organizations may offer support tools for teams to prepare them to face the challenges of a dispersed setting. Indeed, our results suggest that managers should create a procedural justice climate within the team in order to overcome the pitfalls of dispersion and enhance team performance.

We suggest two ways in which the above can be accomplished. First, they could leverage formal training interventions in order to prepare the team to deal with the challenges of dispersion. In particular, the training could be aimed at helping the team members to focus on those processes and behaviors that are likely to enhance the likelihood of developing fair decision processes within the team. Moreover, managers could leverage cross-training interventions by providing team members with practical knowledge regarding the roles and responsibilities of their teammates, enhancing the ability to develop shared mental models among team members and a sense of mutual bonding, thus fostering the development of procedural justice climate. This managerial suggestion also finds empirical justification in previous studies on dispersed teams which reveal that training for digital collaboration environments plays a pivotal role in supporting individuals who work in such teams [63].

Second, managers bear responsibility for encouraging members to avoid enacting behaviors that hamper interaction among individuals and which may erode the climate of procedural justice. In this sense, managers should play the role of facilitators in supporting the team in maintaining fair processes to manage their decisions. In such a sense, managers should facilitate the circulation of information and knowledge among team members, thus acting as a link that supports information flow. In such a role, managers should promote discussion among team members, creating conditions in which members can challenge their teammates’ ideas and decisions and modify them with consensus. All our findings fit together, in that they provide specific “action steps” that managers can take to support teams in the development of a procedural justice climate as dispersion increases. Such actions, besides being worthwhile in their own right, seem to have a positive effect on the organization, since they help to leverage the dispersed knowledge of team members while avoiding the pitfalls of distance.

## Limitations

The results and implications of our research should be interpreted in light of a few limitations. One limitation relates to the fact that empirical data gathered for this research primarily allows generalization of the results obtained to the domain of work teams. Future research should assess whether the present study’s findings could be replicated in different team contexts such as project teams. Indeed, previous research highlighted different types of teams may present different patterns in their interactions. Finally, while there is no indication of a country-specific nature of our conceptual arguments and empirical findings, further research in other countries is encouraged in order to increase our understanding of the possible influences of country context on the relationships investigated here.

There are several methodological strengths of our work that offset these limitations. First, the study design involved data collection from multiple sources within participating teams. In particular, responses to questionnaire items were obtained from a majority of members in each team. Second, different respondents were used to measure the independent and dependent variables in the model. While team members provided responses pertaining to the procedural justice climate variables, team leaders responded to questions pertaining to the dependent variable, while dispersion was collected through objective data. Finally, the research design was strengthened by focusing on teams that operated in their natural environment. This is particularly noteworthy given the difficulty of obtaining data from different sources at different times in a field setting.

## Conclusion

In this study, we sought to understand the relationship between team procedural justice climate and team performance when team members are dispersed and rely on ICTs to manage their interactions. Team dispersion and procedural justice climate have been identified as important boundary conditions affecting team performance. We found that team dispersion and procedural justice climate affect team performance, and that procedural justice alleviates the negative effect of dispersion on performance. These findings make important contributions to the extant IS literature on dispersed teams by outlining how organizations may mitigate some of the challenges of dispersion and improve team performance by creating an intra-team environment based upon fairness.

## NOTES

1. It is important to note that, consistent with previous studies, we refer to the shared perception of justice as a “climate” rather than a “culture” phenomenon. Whereas “climate” and “culture” both refer to the development of a shared social context affecting individuals behavior, the concept of climate is more appropriate when focusing on justice in a teambased setting. Indeed, while culture can be described as a broader concept referring to the profound foundations and the values upon which an organization is built, climate refers to a more specific phenomenon and it is based on the perception and the feelings regarding a particular contextual situation in terms of processes, practices and behaviors (such as procedural justice) [14, 34]. Therefore, the concept of climate is particularly suitable and applicable to phenomena that are more observable and reflect the day-to-day interactions among members [69, 100], such as the degree to which fair treatment is perceived among team members.
