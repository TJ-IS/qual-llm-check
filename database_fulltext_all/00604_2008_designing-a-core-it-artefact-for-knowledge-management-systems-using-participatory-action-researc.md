# Designing a core IT artefact for Knowledge Management Systems using participatory action research in a government and a non-government organisation

Tom Butler <sup>\*</sup>, Joseph Feller, Andrew Pope, Bill Emerson, Ciaran Murphy

University College Cork, Western Road, Cork, Ireland

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 22 September 2006 Accepted 26 October 2007 Available online 29 August 2008

Keywords: Knowledge Management Systems Action research IT artefact

## a b s t r a c t

There is a growing interest in the application of information technologies (IT) for Knowledge Management (KM) in the public sector; there is, however, a dearth of research on the design and development of effective, integrated Knowledge Management Systems (KMS). Consequently, public sector organisations and private sector enterprises are offered little in the way of guidance in designing KMS from the bewildering range of information technologies said to capture, store, retrieve, transfer and apply knowledge. Action research, with its emphasis on praxis and on the importance of ‘situated, practical theory,’ is an ideal vehicle for deepening the IS field’s understanding of the design of IT-enabled Knowledge Management Systems for use in Government and Non-Government Organisations. This paper reports on the outcomes of two related participatory action research studies involving a University R&D team, practitioners from the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), and a Government Department. The first of these studies focused on the design and development, using UNFPA practitioners’ practical theory, of a desktop-based IT artefact for knowledge sharing. The second action research study extends and applies the lessons learned from the first to develop an enterprise-wide core IT artefact for the Government Department’s Knowledge Management System. This paper contributes to both research and practice by: (1) offering a conceptual schema based on practical theory to inform the design and development of a core IT artefact for KMS and (2) describing the functions and features of a typical core IT artefact. The study also differentiates between core and non-core IT artefacts for knowledge sharing in organisations.

## 1. Introduction

Knowledge Management (KM) now plays an integral part of e-Government (Misra, 2007). In highlighting the relationship between e-Government and KM, Friis (2002) draws on Zuboff (1988) to illustrate that IT support for e-Government ‘automates’ public administration processes, while IT-enabled knowledge management goes beyond mere automation to play an ‘informating’ role in government organisations by facilitating knowledge sharing. The importance and relevance of KM to public administration is indicated by the European Commission’s Information Society initiative which argues that ‘‘organisational innovation and the emergence of knowledge-enhanced e-Government will lead to better and more efficient services and the empowerment of the civil servant in his/her task, supporting complex decision making and collaboration”.<sup>1</sup> This is underpinned by the keynote article at the IFIP WG 8.3/8.5 3rd International Workshop on Knowledge Management in e-Government, where Friis (2002, p. 10) argues that ‘‘knowledge management in public administration as an emerging research field”. Leitner (2003, p. 22) states that ‘‘public administrations are knowledge intensive organisations whose officials are ‘‘knowledge workers par excellence. . .Therefore, the prospects for knowledge management in eGovernment are remarkable.” She stresses (Leitner 2003, p. 23) that the use of knowledge portals and IT-supported networks in public sector organisations ensure that ‘‘[k]nowledge derived from previous action or gained through policy evaluation will be fed back into policy making in an effort to improve and better target policies. . .[as] human knowledge and action. . .remains at the heart of good governance.” Accordingly, the United Nations’ Department of Economic and Social Affairs (2003) Ad Hoc Expert Group Meeting on Knowledge Systems for Development places special emphasis on the need for knowledge management in government and non-government organisations. It therefore comes as no surprise to find that the OECD’s (OECD 2003, p. 5) second annual Knowledge Management Survey of 20 countries and 132 departments/ministries/agencies reveals that ‘‘KM ranked high on the management agenda of a good majority of central government organisations across OECD Member countries.” The OECD report highlighted that many government and non-government organisations are beginning to follow those in the private sector in adopting KM, with, for example, government organisations in Finland, Sweden, France, Iceland, and Canada scoring highest on key KM indicators.

Riege and Lindsay (2006) indicate, however, that there is a dearth of research on KM theory and practice in the public sector. One important issue identified by Wimmer and Traunmuller (2003) relates to the design of Knowledge Management Systems (KMS) for use in public sector organisations; they also argue that the transfer of KMS-related concepts from the private sector may be inappropriate for public administrations. These concerns appear to be justified as research indicates that KM concepts appear not to be entirely effective in the private sector, as many KMS projects in business enterprises are unsuccessful (see Schultze and Boland, 2000; Huber, 2001), with Storey and Barnett (2000) reporting failure rates of over 80%, The recent experience of the World Bank, which invested \$280 million on IT-based KM solutions over 6 vears in both regional and global knowledge sharing initiatives, appears to support Wimmer and Traunmuller (2003) argument regarding the public sector adoption of KM concepts from the private sector, as the World Bank failed to achieve its strategic objective of embedding knowledge sharing into its work processes (World Bank, 2003).

In a general context. Chua and Lam (2005, p. 6) argue that “KM failure factors fall into four distinct categories, namely. tech: nology, culture, content, and project management” – these relate to the people, process and technology dimensions of a KMS While such issues are similar to those associated with the general category of IS projects, Bansler and Havn (2003) argue that the development and implementation of KMS presents unique challenges in addition to those associated with traditional IS. For example, recent studies by Jennex and Olfman (2005, 2006) and Butler et al. (2007) on the critical success factors for KMS report the following as being of particular importance: (1) KM strategy; (2) possessing a knowledge structure or taxonomy; (3) having a clear goal and purpose for the KMS; (4) senior management support; (5) possessing an integrated technical KMS infrastructure; (6) security/protection of captured knowledge; (7) KMS search, retrieval, and visualization functions; (8) KMS ease of use; (9) motivation and commitment of users; (10) using incentives and rewards; (11) user training; (12) assessing the effectiveness of the KMS; (13) instituting appropriate work processes; and, finally, (14) having an organisational culture that supports learning. Recent research by Aakhavan and Jafari (2006), on the design and implementation of KMS at a na tional level in Europe, generally confirms these findings. It is clear from the studies cited that IT-based factors exert a significant influence on KMS implementation outcomes in private sector organisations; this also appears to be true of public sector organisations, as Misra (2007) argues that one of the great myths' of KM in government organisations is that information technologies are not important for KM success. Nevertheless, there is broad agreement that, all things being equal, IT concerns have to be balanced with, and placed in the context of, people and process issues surrounding KMS development and use (Sambamurthy and Subramani, 2005). The present paper contributes to the nascent body of research on KMS in public sector organisations. by focusing on the design of the IT artefacts that form the technology dimension of a KMS

## 1.1. Core and non-core it artefacts in KMS

A range of diverse IT artefacts are posited to support the creation, storage, retrieval, transfer, and application of knowledge in organisations. Table 1, which draws on Alavi and Leidner (2001), lists these IT artefacts and indicates their posited relevance to KMS. Additional technologies include, but are not limited to, email, collaboration and communication tools (including telecommunications and videoconferencing technologies), group decision support tools, social network analysis tools, and knowledge codification tools (cf. Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Chua, 2004). Nevertheless, given the diversity of IT artefacts available to practitioners to facilitate knowledge sharing, Sambamurthy and Subramani (2005, p. 2) point out that the ‘‘focus of the deployment of Knowledge Management Systems has been on developing searchable document repositories to support the digital capture, storage, retrieval, and distribution of an organisation’s explicitly documented knowledge.” If Sambamurthy and Subramanil are correct in their assertion that organisations have been focusing on ‘searchable document repositories’ in implementing KMS, the question begs as to whether this is information management, rather than knowledge management. It may also be concluded that such organisations may have fallen into the ‘great trap’ identified by McDermott (1999, p. 104), namely ‘‘using information management tools and concepts to design Knowledge Management Systems”, thereby contributing the aforementioned high rate of KMS failures.

It is clear from Table 1 that the diverse range of information technologies there listed could not meaningfully constitute a KMS. Research by Massey et al. (2002), Gottschalk (2005), Benbya (2006), Jennex and Olfman (2005, 2006) and Butler et al. (2007) indicate that effective, i.e., successful, KMS are constituted by highly accessible and well-integrated web-based Intranet technologies that facilitate knowledge sharing on tasks/processes and/or generic/infrastructures among general and/or specific communities-of-practice. Benbya (2006, p. 4) argues that effective knowledge sharing technologies (i.e., core IT artefacts) are integrative, highly accessible, and searchable, because ‘‘[i]ntegration is a strong predictor of KMS effectiveness, the ability of a system to integrate knowledge from a variety of sources and present it in a manner that enables easy access and reuse is associated with both knowledge quality and knowledge usage.” IT artefacts, such as email, datamining and learning tools, are important, but non-core, as they are generally not well-integrated and do not provide a focal point or node for effective knowledge sharing (cf. Benbya, 2006).

## 1.2. Practitioner perspectives on it artefacts for knowledge sharing

Practitioners at the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), who participated in the first action research study, argue that while the various KM technologies referred to previously are important ingredients in a KMS, they do not help knowledge workers to systematically capture and share their experiential knowledge of key organisational processes and practices. The UNFPA's Knowledge Sharing Branch (KSB) arrived at this conclusion after a comprehensive assessment of KMS-based IT artefacts from major vendors in order to select one to support the organisation’s Knowledge Sharing Strategy. Brendan O'Brien stated that “we were disappointed to find that none of the commercially available [KM Toolsl met our needs. , so we decided to build one that did.” In contrast to the point made by Sambamurthy and Subramani (2005, p. 2), both O’Brien and his colleagues at the KSB were of the opinion that KM Tools such as ‘‘searchable document repositories” constituted non-core KM technologies – the IT artefacts listed above were regarded as important but supplemental to a core KM technology. Indeed, it is interesting to note that an electronic bulletin board/discussion forum, email, and a knowledge directory of sorts were already in use in the organisation. Being highly intimate with the World Bank’s unsuccessful experience in deploying their IT-based KMS, O’Brien is adamant that one of the major ‘‘unresolved issues” and ‘‘challenges” in knowledge management is the design of a core IT artefact for KMS.

Given the foregoing points on: (a) the dearth of research on the design of KMS; (b) the multiplicity of technologies in use to support knowledge sharing; and (c) the need to identify the functions and features of a formal KM tool for use in government and non-government organisations, this action research study’s objectives are: (1) to arrive at a conceptual schema to inform the design and development of a core IT artefact for KMS and (2) to present a list of features for the core IT artefact for KM in public sector organisations. The following section describes the context of the study and the research approach adopted.

## 2. Participatory action research on it artefacts for km in a government and a non-government organisation

Baskerville (1999, p. 9) argues that action research is a ‘‘class of research approaches, rather than a single monolithic research method”; however, Baskerville and Myers (2004) argue that action research is, at its essence, a two-stage process; the first stage is the diagnostic stage where researchers and practitioners collaborate in diagnosing the problem to be solved and theorise about how to solve it; the second stage involves the collaborative application of theory to guide action aimed at remedying the problem. The outcomes of the action are studied and research findings generated to inform theory (McKay and Marshall, 2001). Drawing on John Dewey’s pragmatism, Baskerville and Myers (2004) argue that action research is dominated by an underlying pragmatic orientation. The emphasis on praxis is especially evident in participatory action research, which extends traditional action research approaches (Baskerville, 1999), and which is the approach adopted in the present study.

Action research was also chosen as it is deemed to provide a tight fit with systems development (for example Checkland 1981; Avison et al., 1999; Baskerville, 1999; Burstein and Gregor, 1999; Hevner et al., 2004). Moreover, Avison et al., 2001 suggests that the active role of the action researcher in organisational change versus the passive role of the case study researcher, make action research a more viable approach to information systems research. The requirement to solve a real world problem coupled with the need for iterative approaches to software development provide an excellent fit with action research. Indeed Baskerville (1999, p.12) suggests that action research is ‘‘one of the few research approaches that we can legit imately employ to study the effects of specific alterations in systems development methodologies in human organisations”.

Benbasat and Zmud (1999) posit that in order for IS research to be relevant, researchers must be involved in practica context of IT usage and management. In participatory action research the responsibility for theorising lies with both practitioners and researchers; in this approach, practitioners have the status of ‘‘co-researchers” as they ‘‘bring situated, practical theory into the action research process” (Baskerville, 1999, p. 17). Baskerville, 1999, p. 9 provides a distinction between conventional action research and participatory action research, stating that participatory action research is ‘‘distinguished by the additional characteristic involvement of the practitioners as both subjects and co-researchers”. In an effort to capitalise on the synergy between the university R&D team members’ knowledge of KM theory and software development and the UNFPA practitioners’ practical theory, participatory action research was chosen as the most appropriate form of action research. It must be noted that participatory action research is also cyclical, in that the outcomes of one cycle of research feed into another; however, the second cycle of action research does not necessarily happen in the same ‘place’ (Wadsworth, 1998).

Despite growing acceptance in the IS community, action research has been criticised for providing relatively few guidelines for the would-be action researcher (McKay and Marshall, 2001). In an effort to ensure methodological rigour, this study’s approach is based on a five step action research method (see Table 2), known as canonical action research, proposed by Susman and Evered (1978), and adapted for use in a participatory research context by iterating through two cycles of action research in different organisations. This five stage process is the most prevalent in the IS literature (Baskerville, 1999) and has been applied successfully in a number of IS research projects (for example, Baskerville, 1999; Olesen et al., 1999; Lindgren et al., 2004; Street and Meister, 2004). It is, therefore, significant to have the canonical approach married with participatory action research: Table 2 illustrates the application of the participatory action research approach across the five phases of two cycles.

## 2.1. Applying the participatory action research study design

The design and development of the pKADS application began in September 2003. Developed based on open source<sup>2</sup> technologies and released under an open source license, several thousand copies of version 1.0 of the software were distributed in December 2003 at the World Summit on the Information Society (WSIS) in Geneva, Switzerland; subsequently there have been 2000+ copies downloaded from the pKADS website up to mid-2006. The product of the first action research project is, therefore, available for public evaluation. The participants in the R&D project included 4 academic researchers (including two former IT professionals) from the University, 2 software developers (contracted in by the lead researcher), four members of the UNFPA, and a third-party IT consultant retained by the UNFPA. The researchers began the initial study on the system requirements on September 9th 2003 and the major research and development phase continued to the 12th of December 2003. The second phase of the research lasted until April 2004, with the launch and web-based release of version 1.1 of the software. The software application and its source code are freely available in English, French and Spanish versions for the Windows, Linux and Mac OS platforms.

In March 2004, the University researchers undertook a second knowledge management participatory action research pro ject with a Government Department. The initial three month phase of this project saw the pKADS application successfully deployed on a pilot basis in one division of the Department. However, the Government wished to have an enterprise-wide KMS-based on Web technologies. Thus the e-Government Knowledge Platform (eGovKP) was developed from the ground up using the conceptual design schema from the first action research study and open source technologies. While a working pro totype of eGovKP was successfully deployed in the pilot site in July, a full-featured eGovKP version 1.0 was released in September 2004. The integration of this system into departmental enterprise systems continued with the deployment of eGovKP version 2.0 (which was rechristened as simply the KMS) in the sponsoring Government Department in May 2005. The KMS was initially deployed on an open source-based technology platform incorporating the MySQL database management system and later on Oracle 10g. The participants in this second action research R&D project included 6 academic researchers (including three former IT professionals) and 5 research assistants from the University, 3 experienced software developers (contracted in by the lead researcher to code the system), as well as a team of 5 knowledge workers from the Government Department formed the pilot project team, along with 12 business and IT managers from the department’s HQ, and a business consultant from Cap Gemini. The experiences of knowledge workers in this second action research study confirmed the findings of the first, in that people from a different culture and tradition, domains of knowledge, and areas of expertise, found the conceptual architecture on which the pKADS and eGovKP KM Tools were designed to be intuitive, easy to understand and ‘logical’ – it therefore informed the design of the department’s core IT artefact for Knowledge Sharing.

Both R&D studies were publicly funded to a total of approx. €670,000 (US \$804,000). Academic researchers participated in all project related activities: e.g., requirements gathering, design, detailed design (inc. GUI design), coding, testing, drafting technical and end-user documentation, end-user training, etc. Both projects were viewed by the participating University Department as an opportunity to make a public service contribution to the wider community. For the duration of the projects, team members were in daily contact (informal), while formal project meetings were held on a fortnightly basis and joint meetings with participating institutions occurred on a monthly basis or as required. Four of the researchers attended the WSIS conference with two of the UNFPA KSB team to demonstrate the pKADS KMS and obtain feedback from practitio ners. Field notes of observations and informal conversations (telephone and interpersonal), detailed minutes of the various meetings, memos, email threads, documentation and other project artefacts formed the research database for both studies In the second study of the KM initiative in the Government Department, formal individual post-implementation interviews with 18 practitioners took place in late August to early September 2006. It must be noted, however, that the first participatory action research study with the UNFPA provided much of the 'practical theory' that informed the conceptual architecture of the KM Tool.

## 3. Practical theory and the design of core it artefacts for KM

This section reports on the lessons from the first action research study, the outcomes of which led to the initial conceptual design of a Core IT artefact for KM. The first subsection presents a short case study on the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), the situated practical theory of its practitioners in the shape of their conceptualisation of Knowledge Assets and their approach to knowledge sharing. This forms the necessary context for the description of the first action research study on the portable Knowledge Asset Development System (pKADS). Following this, the technical requirements and features of the pKADS KM Tool is outlined. Together they prepare the reader for the Section 4’s delineation of the conceptual architecture of the core IT artefact for KM which was fully worked out in the second participatory action research study (see Table 2 above).

## 3.1. Knowledge sharing in the UNFPA

The United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) was founded in 1969 to support developing countries through the promotion of reproductive health and the equality of women. In order to meet the challenges of delivering global services more effectively, the UNFPA realized that it had to capture, distil, validate, store, apply, and reuse the know-how of its people for learning and innovation. In early 2002 the Knowledge Sharing Branch recognized that technical and social processes interact in complementarities to shape knowledge management efforts. There is ample evidence in the UNFPA’s documentation and from participant researchers that the organisation recognized such issues; indeed the Knowledge Sharing Branch (KSB) studied the available literature on KM and KMS to inform their strategy. The core of UNFPA’s approach to knowledge sharing is the systematic collection, synthesis and dissemination of UNFPA worker’s know-how and experience. The organisation’s pilot project in knowledge sharing saw its personnel experiment in the knowledge capture and transfer that allowed inexperienced workers to quickly gather the requisite knowledge and competencies required in three specific areas of service delivery viz. Quality of Care, Adolescent Development and Vesico-Vaginal Fistulae.

## 3.1.1. The knowledge asset concept as the situated practical theory of practitioners

Practitioners in the UNFPA's Knowledge Sharing Branch adopted and adapted the Knowledge Asset (KA) concept as the cornerstone of its Knowledge Sharing Strategy. The knowledge asset concept is widely used in business and academia (see, for example, Conway and Sligar, 2002); however, the UNFPA’s conceptualization and its operationalisation have particular strengths. Knowledge at UNFPA is conceptualized as ‘‘how to do things”, ‘‘where to find examples” and ‘‘who to ask for help”: hence, KAs contain the ‘‘distilled experiential knowledge of organisational actors on a well-bounded subject area or topic of interest”. According to Brendan O’Brien, of UNFPA’s Knowledge Sharing Branch, KAs are ‘‘. . .the living repositories of our collective know-how. . .[they] represent the very best and most current knowledge in areas that are critical to UNFPA.” Thus, KAs are typically based on the key work processes of the organisation. In the UNFPA, they are designed and structured to provide an intuitive, empirically grounded, logical structure to systematically capture, store, and share knowledge. Appendix A contains the Knowledge Asset Model that emerged from the initial requirements analysis conducted by the University’s R&D Team in collaboration with UNFPA practitioners. This model provided a basic conceptual schema and knowledge taxonomy to help the R&D team begin the development of the pKADS application. A detailed discussion on the conceptual schema and taxonomy is presented in Section 4, where it is described in the context of the changes made to it during the second action research study on the Government Department.

## 3.1.2. Knowledge networks and communities-of-practice

The UNFPA views two social and organisational groupings as being vital to the success of its knowledge sharing initiative. The first of these is a formal grouping of knowledge workers called a Knowledge Network (KN); these are structured teams of individuals who share a common functional interest in particular domains of knowledge, The roles and responsibilities of KN members are strategically defined and formally delineated by the KSB. In keeping with the participatory nature of the UN-FPA’s approach to KM and the focus of knowledge sharing on key organisational processes, knowledge domains are to be ‘‘be developed through action-oriented collaboration in support of actual work in progress in UNFPA.” The second grouping involves members of informal communities-of-practice, which have shared knowledge interests that may, or may not, have organisational processes as their subject. In addition, the UNFPA recognized that one of its major challenges in managing knowl edge is to provide adequate incentives and rewards for performance, so that its professionals in the field would use the system.

## 3.1.3. Organisational routines for knowledge sharing

Practical research at the UNFPA has illustrated that people are willing to share their experiential knowledge, but are often unable to do so due to the unavailability of suitable procedures and mechanisms. Staff at the KSB concluded that in order for information to become knowledge, it needs to be relevant and provide adequate descriptive context. This requires a knowledge sharing process that stimulates knowledge workers to provide relevant data, insights, ideas, ‘rules of thumb’ and contextual information so that their codified know-how becomes useful to others. According to the UNFPA, such an approach does not require the elimination of existing KM tools; however, it calls for the systematization, standardized design and configuration of knowledge sharing processes, enabled by, and embedded in, IT, and a readily accessible, unambiguous knowledge ‘product.’ The former were codified in the organisation’s Knowledge Sharing Strategy and practices, while the latter was conceptualized as a Knowledge Asset (KA).

## 3.1.4. IT as an enabler of knowledge sharing

The UNFPA had limited experience with the application of IT to support organisational processes. In building a Knowledge Sharing Technical Environment (KSTE), the KSB made sure that the lessons learned by the World Bank in the failure of its ITenabled KM Strategy were not forgotten in formulating the UNFPA’S Knowledge Sharing Strategy and in the design and deployment of the KMS’ core IT artefact. As indicated, the outcome of an extensive evaluation of a KM Tools then available in the marketplace was unsatisfactory viz. Brendan O’Brien, stated that ‘‘we wanted a tool to capture and disseminate data, information, experiences and know-how of people. . .we also wanted the ability to easily share that knowledge with others in the organisation – no matter where or when.” He added that ‘‘search capabilities, data mining, document management, discussion forums and expert directories are all important. . .and we are currently using some of these. . .but unless you can systematically capture the knowledge in people’s heads, and in a format that renders it easy to access and understand, then knowledge sharing is not going to happen.”

The outcome of the KSB's efforts was the prototype Knowledge Asset Development System (KADS), which was implemented on a pilot basis in the organisation. This IT artefact was constructed in 2002 using Lotus Notes/Domino technologies; these incorporated a Web-enabled Intranet-based Knowledge Asset Wizard and a Knowledge Asset Viewer. The KMS was redesigned in 2004 using the experience gained in the pKADS initiative (see Fig. 1 for an example of the new interface) and UNFPA rechristened it as the Knowledge Sharing Network (KSNet); in 2006 it was being actively accessed by all 1500 employees worldwide. Like the original KADS, this system was limited by the underlying technologies; accordingly, the September 2006 the UNFPA decided go to tender for a system based on Java and open-source technologies, including MySQL, and which ran on Sun Solaris and employed Sun Java System middleware. However, the underlying conceptual architecture and general functions and features were to remain as is.

## 3.2.1. pKADS technical requirements and architecture

When the University R&D Team investigated the initial scope and technical architecture of the pKADS IT artefact, they identified the following criteria as critical to the success of the project:

1. Stand-Alone: The pKADS architecture was not to be based on a complex relational database management system (RDBMS) or other server software for its knowledge base.

2. Portable: Due to the wide variety of operating systems and hardware platforms used by UNFPA’s partner organisations and clients, pKADS would need to be platform independent to ensure compatibility.

3. Extensible: Many of UNFPA’s partners and clients operate in geographically disperse locations with differing work processes and working environment. In order for the system to be extensible, users of the system would require the ability to modify the system at the data and source levels.

4. Flexible Output: Knowledge asset would have to be output in a number of different formats. For example: XML for sharing between pKADS applications, HTML for the Web and other formats for print, etc.

5. Create a Community of Practice: pKADS would have to be freely available modifiable and redistributable in order to foster a community of practice. This would ensure an increased uptake in the number of users and developers involving themselves in the project.

Java was chosen for several reasons, including its (relative) platform independence and sophisticated support for localisation, graphical interface elements, event driven execution, etc. pKADS presents the user with a Java Swing interface for data entry (see Fig. 2), and also accepts input in the form of XML data (individual KA files), XML Schemas (used for validation) and XSLT Stylesheets (used to control output). ## 4. Building on pkads: the e-Government Knowledge Platform as a core IT artefact

The e-Government Knowledge Platform (eGovKP) was researched, designed and developed in partnership with a large Government Department using practical theory and experiences gained in the UNFPA pKADS action research study. In its Annual Report of 2004, The Department stated that it had ‘‘embarked on transforming itself into a knowledge organisation.” This move was seen as being central to the Department’s strategic change and modernisation programmes, which positioned it as a leader in the application of knowledge management in the public sector. This is not insignificant, as a recent study by the OECD (2003) indicates, and many aspects of the department’s transition to a ‘knowledge organisation’ provide valuable lessons to other government and non-government organisations.

The department is structured into 6 Sectors (Business Units) and 33 Divisions nationally but some have an international brief also, liaison with the UN being part of this brief. Hence, a diverse range of knowledge interests exist in and across the department, which is due to undergo restructuring in the not too distant future. The department’s relationship with the UN brought it into contact with the UNFPA and its knowledge management activities. Witnessing the success of the UNFPA’s endeavours, it wished to emulate that organisation’s experience with knowledge sharing. It comes as no surprise then that the department’s knowledge management strategy is highly similar to that of the UNFPA’s and has been implemented in like fashion, albeit in a shorter timeframe.

As indicated, the R&D Team undertook a field trial of the pKADS in February 2004 in the department’s Marine Engineering Division. Following the pilot deployment of pKADS, the department’s business and IT managers asked the University’s R&D team to further evolve the UNFPA’s conceptual schema in collaboration with participating users from the pilot team. This led to the development of a Web-based KMS: this participative research process led to the release of eGovKP version 1.0 in September 2004. The next subsection describes the conceptual schema that was developed by the University R&D Team in February 2004 in collaboration with engineers from the Department’s Marine Engineering Division; it was then applied to develop the e-Government Knowledge Platform IT Artefact.

## 4.1. Building a conceptual knowledge asset schema for the eGovKP core IT artefact

Table 3 presents the eGovKP conceptual schema and knowledge taxonomy that underpinned the data model used to develop the e-Government Knowledge Platform. As indicated, it emerged from the researchers’ experiences of the pKADS project, the conceptual schema employed in the design of the pKADS application, and the researchers’ participation in the department’s Marine Engineering Division pilot. It is significant that the Knowledge Asset concept that emerged from the pKADS project was readily adopted by practitioners in the Government Department, albeit certain modifications were suggested by the civil servants participating in the initial pilot project phase and subsequent phases. The various elements of the conceptual schema, taxonomy and model are now delineated with respect to their origins.

In the pKADS schema, the UNFPA groups related Knowledge Assets (KA) into Knowledge Domains. A Knowledge Domain simply describes the context for a collection of related Knowledge Assets. KAs that are grouped by domains describe the various areas of interest, activities, etc., which together constitute a recognizable body of knowledge in the organisation. This was a departure from the original UNFPA Knowledge Asset Development System (KADS) schema, where asset groupings were termed Super Assets. The Government Department wished to change the emphasis from a bottom up Knowledge Asset to Super Asset to a top down Knowledge Domain Hierarchy that mapped onto functional and cross-functional areas of operation, typically in the form of Business Units (Departmental Sectors) and Divisions, which constitute Knowledge Domains and Sub-domains; KAs are then mapped onto function-specific and cross-functional processes.

In pKADS and KADS, the visual representation of a KA is a ‘Knowledge Map.’ Its purpose is to enable users to navigate and graphically explore a KA. Fig. 2 represents the Web-View of a Knowledge Asset (the sample Sexual and Reproductive Health Care Knowledge Asset) within the pKADS system. The tree structure accommodates the nesting of several levels of Domains and Sub-Domains and associated Knowledge Assets. A Category is therefore a sub-topic of a KA. Categories are further divided into, and described by, a set of Questions and Answers. (As users navigate the KA, Categories and associated Questions and Answers details are displayed in the right hand frames.) UNFPA posits that to be effective, a KA should have about eight Categories. In the eGovKP schema (or knowledge taxonomy, as the Government Department refer to it as) Knowledge Assets are structured into Topics. The term Topic was chosen over pKADS Category following consideration and debate by participants in the eGovKP study: As were the other changes to the original schema in order for eGovKP taxonomy to be logically consistent. An example will illustrate the purpose of this schema: Just like a textbook is composed of chapters, a KA is composed of Topics. Please note that the textbook analogy, while useful for explaining the structure of KAs, is limited in its explanatory power, as texts are static repositories, while Knowledge Assets are viewed as being dynamic, living entities that grow over time through use. Chapters in a textbook are subdivided into sections – so too are Topics, except that Topics are composed of groups of Questions and related Answers – these are called Topic Dialogues. According the UNFPA’s KSB, a KA should have about eight Topics to be effective; however, government participants in the eGovKP action research project put no limitation on the number, as indicated.

In order to be as available as possible to end-users, the UNFPA schema viewed KA Categories as consisting of one or more question and answer pairs, depending on the complexity of the Category. UNFPA practitioners argue that questions and answers should be concise and to the point and be designed to elicit essential information. This schema was altered slightly in the second action research study in that Topics were structured to have two or more Dialogues in the form of Question and Answer pairs; the number of such pairings depending on the complexity of the Topic. Hence, as with pKADS, a KA presents information in a Question and Answer (Q and A) format. However, the new schema permits more than one Answer to a Question.

Finally, in pKADS, Questions and Answers are supplemented with informational resources external to the KA; these Related Resources include ‘examples’ of best practice and ‘further reading’. KAs also incorporate links to experts or individuals who can provide additional information and guidance; the conceptual architecture explicitly delineates the role of ‘experts and provides mechanisms for referencing ‘external’ experts. This element of the pKADS schema was altered in the eGovKP project where it was decided that all elements, including Questions and Answers, should be supplemented with Rich Knowledge Links to related Domains, Assets, Topics, Dialogues and external sources of information. Rich Knowledge Links include examples and further reading that can be uploaded or included as linked documents and/or URL (Web) references (to the Intranet or the Internet): Rich Knowledge Links may also contain video and audio links, and so on. Extending beyond the functionality of pKADS’ Related Resources, Rich Knowledge Links also provide a vital mechanism for eGovKP to hook into other systems and link to or import relevant data sets, images, email threads, and so on. In addition, eGovKP also has the capability to link to experts or individuals who can provide additional information or offer guidance both internally and externally. Like KADS and pKADS, it provides a built-in ‘Golden Pages’ of subject matter experts (contributing authors), however, it also incorporates an Instant Messaging tool, to facilitate communication.

Textbooks may have one or more authors; similarly, a Knowledge Asset may have several contributing authors called KA Members. In pKADS, subject matter experts who contribute to the building of an asset by sharing their knowledge are term Knowledge Asset Coordinators, and Primary and Secondary Contributors. The eGovKP schema adopts a top down approach to designating key players of subject matter experts in order for the application to map on to its organisational structure viz. Knowledge Domain Administrator, Domain Coordinator, Knowledge Asset Coordinator, Knowledge Asset Contributors, Domain and Knowledge Asset Members. In pKADS, KA Members consist of contributing authors or readers. In single author sce narios during KA creation, the author/expert adopts all the roles in a Knowledge Network, while other members constitute the target audience for the asset. As knowledge is distributed in organisations and across societies, a dynamic KA will have a network of contributing authors and a ready audience. In such scenarios, a Network Member will be responsible for each Category. The same and/or other Network Members will be responsible for the various questions and answers that constitute a Category; these are called the Primary and Other Contributors. The left-hand bottom panel in Fig. 3 provides a comprehensive list of these subject matter experts, with links to contact details. In the eGovKP IT artefact, a Knowledge Asset Member will, typically, be appointed coordinator for one or more Topics. The same and/or other Members will be responsible for the Topic Dialogues (i.e., Questions and Answers) that constitute a Topic. All contributors, including Knowledge Asset Member will be both visible and contactable in eGovKP.

In conclusion, eGovKP employs a well-defined knowledge taxonomy based on a conceptual hierarchy that includes Knowledge Domains, Assets, Topics, Topic Dialogues, Rich Knowledge Links, Domain and Asset Coordinators and Members. The structure of this conceptual design is illustrated in Fig. 5.

## 4.2. Designing a core IT artefact for implementation in the Government Department KMS

The most successful approach to IT-enabled KM initiatives involves the use of a pilot implementation of the KMS in a carefully selected site where potential problems can be addressed, positive experiences gained, and a series of small wins obtained to support an organisation-wide roll-out (Hackett, 2000). Indeed, pilot implementations have long been associated with IS development (Butler and Fitzgerald, 1999). The KMS implementation approach adopted by the department involved two pilot projects: the first was a KM pilot at a HQ division (completed July 2004) conducted by department staff and whose objective was to promote KM in what the department called its Mobilization Phase; the second involved the University R&D Team and an Engineering Division (completed in early-October 2004) – this was termed the Technology Research and Development Phase.

## 5. Summary and conclusions

Practitioners at the UNFPA concluded from their analysis of the KM Tool marketplace that it would be impractical to de ploy, what could be for users, a bewildering range of IT-based tools, with a multiplicity of conduits and formats for commu nication, to support knowledge sharing systematically in a successful knowledge management initiative. Returning to first principles, so to speak, this public sector organisation placed primary emphasis on capturing and sharing their knowledge workers’ experiential and technical knowledge of the organisation’s core institutional processes. To achieve this, they began with a straightforward conceptual architecture that was designed into their nascent KM tool, the prototype Knowledge Asset Development System (KADS). They subsequently refined and elaborated on their ‘situated, practical theory’ in collaboration with the University R&D Team, who designed the resulting extended conceptual schema and knowledge taxonomy into the pKADS application. By early 2004, the UNFPA was using the Portable Knowledge Asset Development System (pKADS) appli cation to promote knowledge sharing among its 1,500 knowledge workers. The tool was used by UNFPA officers in the field where access to the organisational Intranet is unavailable. The UNFPA also promoted the use of the system in other UN agen cies, NGOs and public and private entities in developing countries. Significantly, the lessons learned by the UNFPA in the first action research study have been integrated into the latest version of their Knowledge Asset Development System (KADS) to bring it beyond the level of a pilot prototype KM Tool, and will be incorporated into its successor, the planned Knowledge Sharing Network (KSNet) application. As of 2006, 15 Knowledge Assets were deployed for global use across the organisation. It is significant that the outcome of the second participatory action research study, the eGovKP application or Government Department KMS, was based on the conceptual schema and knowledge taxonomy that emerged from the UNFPA study. The University R&D team extended this schema in collaboration with civil servants from the Government Department in order to design and develop a core IT artefact for KM; this technology was deployed as the departmental KMS in all 33 divisions of the Government Department in June 2006.

This paper contributes to research and practice on KM in the public sector by (1) offering a conceptual schema based on practical theory to inform the design and development of a core IT artefact for KMS and (2) describing the functions and features of a typical core IT artefact. Table 3 presents a conceptual schema and knowledge taxonomy that informs the general design of a core IT artefact, while Figs. 5 and 6 model the key elements of this schema. The functions and features of a core IT artefact that is a highly accessible, well-integrated web-based Intranet system that facilitate knowledge sharing are presented in Table 4. In order to place the contribution that these research objectives make in context, this paper presented the range of IT artefacts that may be employed in a KMS (see Table 1). Although some of these are in daily use by most knowledge workers (email, calendaring, telecommunications and videoconferencing, bulletin boards on Intranets etc.), experience at the UNFPA and the Government Department would suggest that many are non-core or supplementary tools for knowledge sharing, as they are employed generally in an ad hoc fashion. The findings of the two studies presented herein indicate that for knowledge sharing to be supported by IT, the KMS should provide a uniform, systematic, and shared mechanism in its core IT artefact for social actors to capture and share their knowledge. However, this core IT artefact requires an effective knowledge taxonomy and related data model.

Pfeffer and Sutton (2000) argue that one of the reasons why KM efforts are divorced from the day-to-day activities of knowledge workers is that those involved in designing and building KMS do not understand the nature of ‘working knowledge’. We argue that this problem did not arise in the first participatory action research study described in this paper due to the high degree of participation of a range of knowledge workers from the UNFPA in the design of the IT artefact and its deployment and role as a KM Tool in their in-house KMS. Furthermore, the participants in the project were highly experienced in the administration and delivery of the UNFPA’s programs globally and were active in building Knowledge Assets in the pilot Knowledge Asset Development System (KADS) and transferring them to the Portable Knowledge Asset Development System (pKADS). This contributed to the success of the KM initiative in the UNFPA. The Government Department implemented a similar strategy, albeit in a more aggressive timeframe, and only time will tell if it is equally successful. Pfeffer and Sutton (2000, p. 21) have observed that ‘‘[k]nowledge management systems seem to work best when the people who generate the knowledge are those who store it, explain it to others, and coach them as they try to implement the knowledge.” This paper indicates that this is exactly what happened at the UNFPA and in the pKADS deployment, and in the Government Department. Another reason for the success of pKADS and KADS was that incorporated into the design of these applications is the concept of ‘community’, which was implemented as a Knowledge Network of subject matter experts – the Government Department KMS also has this feature

It is significant that, from the outset, the UNFPA balanced its attention and efforts across the three dimensions of technology, people, and processes encompassed by their KMS. This was also occurred in the Government Department’s KM initiative, as the Mobilization Phase was first implemented with a primary focus on mapping and understanding key administrative and business processes, and in creating a knowledge sharing ethos in staff. This paper’s findings therefore illustrate that those who employ IT artefacts for knowledge sharing need to recognize that such tools will be of little use unless the social and organisational issues surrounding their implementation receive adequate and full attention – as Sambamurthy and Subramani (2005) argue, the people, processes and IT artefacts that constitute KMS complement each other in making effective knowledge sharing a reality Yet, as Misra (2007) argues, selecting an appropriate technology is vital if knowledge management is to play an integral part in e-Government initiatives in public administrations aimed at ‘informating’ rather than just ‘automating’ administrative processes (Friis, 2002). The problem is to achieve the correct balance between each of the critical success factors for KMS implementation (Chua and Lam, 2005; Jennex and Olfman, 2005, 2006; Butler et al., 2007). This is not as easy as it appears, given the historically high failure rates associated with KM initiatives. The challenge facing public administrators is, therefore, to avoid the pitfalls that led to the high failure rates of KMS in the private sector. The present study provides firm guidance for public administrators who need to ensure that problems related to the design of IT artefacts for KM are adequately addressed. This appears to be particularly relevant given the increasing relevance of KMS to e-Government, as indicated in the opening section of this paper.

In illustrating how IT can provide support for capturing, storing, transferring or applying firms’ knowledge resources or assets, this paper illustrates that Drucker (1999) concern that IT is failing to support knowledge work is no longer valid (cf. McDermott, 1999). The core IT artefacts described herein have proven their ability to do just that. The study also illustrates how public sector organisations can avoid the ‘‘great trap in knowledge management” identified by McDermott (1999 p. 104) by focusing on designing IT artefacts to make explicit the tacit knowledge in people’s heads, and not in the information contained in document repositories. In order to proper validate this study’s findings, future research on KMS should, perhaps, investigate the similarities and differences of the features and functions of IT artefacts in public sector and private sector organisations.

In conclusion, it is clear that KM in government and non-government organisations has come of age (Friis, 2002; Leitner 2003; Misra, 2007). The authors’ experiences over the past three years confirm the observation that technology is one of several critical issues to confront public sector organisations undertaking KM – however, ceteris paribus, it is clear from past KM failures that if technology decisions are suboptimal, then so will the outcomes of the overall KM initiative. This paper’s articulation of the conceptual underpinnings of a core IT artefact for KM and its description of the artefact’s functions and features should hopefully inform practice and help government administrators address the challenges presented to them by the design and implementation of the core IT artefact in a KMS. It should also highlight to research and practice that the core technology need not be overly complex; rather it should address the issues at hand without ambiguity and unnecessary complexity.

## Appendix A. Knowledge Asset Conceptual Model

(1) Knowledge Asset Domain Name: A knowledge domain simply describes the context for a collection of related Knowledge Assets (formally called Super Knowledge Assets by UNFPA practitioners). In organisations, the knowledge domain may refer to say the finance, marketing or manufacturing functions. Individual Knowledge Assets belonging to that domain then describe the various areas of interest, activities, etc., which together constitute the recognisable body of knowledge that constitute it.

(2) Complementary KA(s): Knowledge Assets will be related to others in a domain, or indeed to those in other knowledge domains. These are to be considered complementary KAs, which augment individual KAs. Hence, it will be necessary to capture these relations by providing the name of the related KA and that of its native domain. Also included would be pointers (hyperlinks/URLs/references) to complementary assets.

(3) KA Description: A Knowledge Asset is a tangible representation of the explicit and implicit, experiential and technical knowledge of organisational actors – individual and/or collective.

(4) KA Keywords: These help end-users identify the relevance of the KA to their area of interest.

(5) KA Authors: While documenting a KA is a collective endeavour, a KA document will have a primary author (KAPA) who will typically manage the asset during its life. However, because knowledge is distributed in organisations, the creation and evolution of a KA will involve the contributions of a network of authors – the KA Author Network (KAAN); however, the primary author will coordinate the contributions of the network. Another group of KA authors act in a secondary role by elaborating on specific dimensions of a KA – these are the KA Secondary Authors (KASA)

(a) KAPA (1)

(b) KAAN Members (0–n)

(c) KASA (0–n)

(6) The initial user will typically be the primary authors. He/she may nominate KAAN or KASA members, or they may add their names to the relevant list when making their contribution.

(7) KA Objective: Assets need to be leveraged to be of use. Some will have single use applications, other multiple. The objective, role and purpose of a KA need to be made explicit.

(8) KA End-Users: The audience of end-users who will apply the KA to achieve its stated objectives needs to be delineated by the KAPA and KAAN authors.

(9) KA Categories: Several knowledge sub-topics coalesce to describe a KA – these are termed KA Categories. Categories describe the major descriptive elements of a KA and consist of a descriptive component and a set of related questions and answers for each category. KA Category elements are:

(a) KA Category Name

(b) KA Category Description

(c) KA Category Questions 1–n

(d) KA Category Answers 1–n

(e) Complementary KA Categories (In this or related KAs/Domains etc., this elaborates on high level detail provided in the Complementary KA attribute)

(f) Additional Informational Resources.
