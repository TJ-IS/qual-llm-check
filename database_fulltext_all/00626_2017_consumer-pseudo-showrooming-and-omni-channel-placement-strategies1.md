# CONSUMER PSEUDO-SHOWROOMING AND OMNI-CHANNEL PLACEMENT STRATEGIES

Zheyin (Jane) Gu University of Connecticut, 2100 Hillside Road, Unit 1041, Storrs, CT 06269 U.S.A. {jane.gu@business.uconn.edu}

Giri Kumar Tayi

State University of New York at Albany, 1400 Washington Avenue,

Albany, NY 12222 U.S.A. {gtayi@albany.edu}

Recent advances in information technologies (IT) have powered the merger of online and offline retail channels into one single platform. Modern consumers frequently switch between online and offline channels when they navigate through various stages of the decision journey, motivating multichannel sellers to develop omnichannel strategies that optimize their overall profit. This study examines consumers’ cross-channel search behavior of “pseudo-showrooming,” or the consumer behavior of inspecting one product at a seller’s physical store before buying a related but different product at the same seller’s online store, and investigates how such consumer behavior allows a multichannel seller to achieve better coordination between its online and offline arms through optimal product placement strategies.

We develop a stylized model in which a multichannel firm offers a product line consisting of two horizontally differentiated products. Consumers are uncertain about the true value of either product. A consumer’s uncertainty regarding a particular product’s value is fully resolved after inspecting that product in person, and can also be partially resolved after inspecting the other related product. By selling only one product through the dual channel and the other product through the online channel exclusively, the firm induces consumer pseudo showrooming for the online exclusive product. Our analysis shows that this product placement strategy generates a greater profit than selling both products through the dual channel, if the fit probability of individual products and consumers’ cost for returning a misfit product are both in the intermediate range. Moreover, we find that over a large parameter region, consumers also enjoy a greater total surplus under the firm’s product placement strategy that induces consumer pseudo-showrooming. Furthermore, we find that the firm garner the most benefit from inducing consumer pseudo-showrooming by selling the higher-quality product or the higher-demand product through the online channel exclusively. Collectively, our study offers a compelling demand-side justification of the commonly witnessed practice among multichannel sellers to offer products online exclusively when offline selling is feasible.

Keywords: Pseudo-showrooming, fit uncertainty, product placement strategy, omni-channel strategy

## Introduction

Recently, a growing number of successful pure-play online sellers are opening their physical stores as “showrooms.” Warby Parker, the online eyewear maker known for selling cool frames at affordable prices, opened a flagship store in New York City in 2013. Bonobos, a men’s apparel online retailer, now operates 20 physical “Guideshops” nationwide in major cities including Seattle and Boston. Blue Nile, the online jewelry dealer, opened its first physical store in June 2015 in Garden City, NY. This notable trend of online sellers to add offline arms, together with the now common practice among local sellers to operate online branches, bring attention to an ongoing revolution in the retail industry, that is, the merging of online and offline channels into one single platform. This revolution in the retail industry is powered by recent advances in information technologies (IT), including architecture, synchronized data, and real-time intelligence, and is supported by major information technology companies such as Intel and IBM. Major players in the retail industry including Nordstrom, Bonobos, and Sephora have invested heavily in innovative technologies that facilitate consumer cross-channel shopping (Reddy 2015). This business environment challenges practitioners as well as researchers to understand how sellers operating both online and offline channels can take full advantage of the IT-enabled cross-channel shopping platform to devise omni-channel strategies.

In this study, we examine how multichannel sellers can design omni-channel product placement strategies by considering consumers’ cross-channel search behavior of pseudoshowrooming. We use the term pseudo-showrooming to refer to the consumer behavior of inspecting a product at a seller’s physical store before buying a related but different product at the same seller’s online store. Note that this behavior is distinct from the consumer “showrooming” behavior (Balakrishnan et al. 2014; Mehra et al. 2013) of inspecting a product at a seller’s physical store before buying the same product from a different seller’s online store. Modern consumers customarily switch between a seller’s online and offline channels while navigating through various stages of the decisionmaking process (Hybris 2012). Evidence suggests that many consumers conduct pseudo-showrooming to enjoy the benefits of shopping at multichannel sellers, by first visiting a seller’s physical stores to inspect and experience products and then browsing the seller’s large selection of online offerings to make the final pick. An IBM (2013) study reports that in eight tracked product categories nearly half of online purchases resulted from shoppers visiting physical stores, and that nearly a quarter of these online shoppers intended to buy their items in the store, but ultimately purchased online. Advances in mobile technologies also contribute to the growing prevalence of consumer pseudo-showrooming behaviors. For example, the consumer electronic chain Best Buy provides in-store shoppers free iPad use for making online purchases. Consumers’ cross-channel search and shopping behaviors provide sellers opportunities to build synergized dual channel operation under omni-strategies that optimize overall business rather than any individual channel (Brynjolfsson et al. 2013; Verdegroup 2011).

A common product placement strategy of multichannel sellers is to offer a larger online product assortment than is available at their brick-and-mortar stores (Verdegroup 2011). Online exclusive products are commonly offered at department stores (e.g., Macy’s, Kohl’s, and Neiman Marcus), specialty stores (e.g., The Limited, Banana Republic, and J. Crew), as well as discount stores (e.g., Target). When pure-online sellers open their physical stores, they typically design the stores as “showrooms,” which do not carry many products that shoppers can take home, but are primarily places for customers to try products and get fashion advice. For example, at Bonobos’ physical stores, shoppers can browse and try on different styles in any size, and then need to order online. And at Blue Nile’s physical store, shoppers can try on 300 sample rings, and then need to pick a stone from the 200,000 diamonds Blue Nile sells on its website (Kapner 2015). Existing research suggests that withdrawing products from the physical store and making them available exclusively at the online store helps a firm reduce the cost of operating physical stores, such as the inventory carrying cost (Bhatnagar and Syam 2014). This supply side argument alone, however, cannot explain the incentive of successful pure-online sellers to open physical stores and operate dual channels. To fully understand the incentive behind multichannel sellers’ strategy to offer online exclusive products, one needs to also consider demand-side factors, or the impact of such a product placement arrangement on consumers’ cross-channel search and purchase behaviors.

A seller by offering a product through its physical store provides shoppers with unique opportunities to touch and feel a product, which allows consumers to find out prior to purchase whether the product fits their specific needs (Gu and Liu 2013). By withdrawing a product from the physical store, however, the seller deprives consumers of this personal inspection opportunity, leaving them uncertain about the product value, and consequently dampens consumers purchase intentions. This dampening of consumer purchase intention is more severe for product categories such as apparel and furniture, where the rich features of products and the high idiosyncrasy in consumer tastes make personal inspections essential to determine product fit and value. Interestingly, operating a dual channel allows a seller to provide consumers a “pseudo” personal inspection experience for an online exclusive product when the seller offers a different but related product at its physical store. In this situation, consumers, by visiting the physical store and inspecting the product available there, can make inferences about the true value of the online exclusive product. As such, by offering at its physical store an informative selection of its full product collection available online, the seller induces consumer pseudo-showrooming for the online exclusive products, with the benefit of alleviating consumers’ uncertainty regarding the online exclusive product and enhancing their purchase intentions.

Empirical evidence supports the prevalence of pseudoshowrooming behaviors among modern consumers and such behaviors’ fit-revealing benefits. We conducted a survey on Amazon Mechanical Turk and collected 86 responses (details of the survey are provided in the appendix). A majority (85%) of respondents reported that they have bought from online a product that is different from but related to the one they inspected at the same retailer’s physical store, and 22% reported to have done so more than three times in the past 12 months. Respondents reported that they have conducted pseudo-showrooming in a variety of categories, including home appliances (13% of respondents), furniture and home improvement (16%), home tools (19%), women’s apparel, shoes, and accessories (33%), men’s apparel, shoes, and accessories (36%), kids’ apparel, shoes, and toys (16%), consumer electronics (38%), and sports gear (7%). We also asked respondents how the product they bought from online is different from the product they inspected at the local store, and a majority of responses indicated a difference in product horizontal features, including size (35% of respondents), color (57%), designing style (38%), product type (26%), and model (39%); 14% of responses indicated a difference in product quality. Moreover, 72% of respondents reported agreement or strong agreement to the statement “In-store inspection helped me understand the brand/product line better”; 83% of respondents reported agreement or strong agreement to the statement “In-store inspection provided me useful information about the product that I later bought online”; and 78% of respondents reported agreement or strong agreement to the statement “In-store inspection helped me make better choice when I later shop online.” This empirical evidence provides strong support to the notion that consumers’ pseudoshowrooming behaviors partially resolve their fit uncertainty regarding the online exclusive product.

In this study, we investigate several important issues regarding firms’ omni-channel product placement strategies. First, how do consumers’ pseudo-showrooming behaviors affect a multichannel seller’s decision on which products to sell through the dual channel and which to sell through the online channel exclusively? Second, how does a seller’s product placement strategy of selling a larger assortment online than offline, by inducing consumer pseudo-showrooming, affect its overall profit from operating the dual channel? Finally, how does a seller’s product placement strategy of selling a larger assortment online than offline affect consumer welfare?

## Results

To address our research questions, we develop a stylized model and consider a firm that maximizes its profit from operating a physical store and an online store. The firm offers a product line that contains two horizontally differentiated products and decides for each product whether to sell through the online store exclusively or through the physical store as well. Consumers have heterogeneous preferences and are uncertain about their true value with either product. The essential assumption behind our model is that pseudoshowrooming helps resolve, but only partially, consumers’ fit uncertainty regarding an online exclusive product. We operationalize this idea by assuming there are two dimensions of product fit uncertainty, one associated with horizontal features of the product line and the other associated with horizontal features of individual products. The horizontal features of the product line apply to both products belonging to the line, which consumers can learn about by inspecting any product from the line. In contrast, consumers learn about the horizontal features of a particular product only by inspecting that product. By visiting the physical store and inspecting a product in person, a consumer learns her true value with the locally-available product, and also acquires an improved although imperfect knowledge about her true value with the online exclusive product. A consumer learns her true value with an online exclusive product only after she has made and received the online order; and the consumer can return a misfit product for full refund after incurring a return cost, which includes not only monetary expenses but time and psychological burdens involved in the product returning process.

Our analysis shows that by offering only one product through the dual channel and the other different but related product through the online channel exclusively, the firm induces consumer pseudo-showrooming and generates a greater overall profit than offering both products through the dual channel, if the fit probability of products and the consumer return cost are both in the intermediate range. While consumers’ improved knowledge with the online-exclusive product enhances their purchase intentions, consumers’ remaining uncertainty about the online-exclusive product can benefit the seller when the return cost discourages some online buyers from returning misfit products. The resulting expanded demand for the online exclusive product allows the firm to better exploit consumer surplus by charging a higher price, although the firm is also under pressure to induce initial purchases for the online exclusive product by charging a lower price. When the fit probability of products is in the intermediate range, consumer uncertainty exists, but does not go too high to impede consumers’ initial purchases. And when the product return cost is in the intermediate range, the firm benefits from the expanded demand for the online exclusive product, but does not suffer too much loss in margin from its effort in inducing initial purchases.

Moreover, we find that over a large range of parameter values, consumers also obtain a greater total surplus when the firm offers a product exclusively online. As such, we identify an interesting win–win region, where the multichannel seller’s effort to coordinate product offerings across its online and offline channels benefits both the firm and consumers. This result highlights the social efficiency gains stemming from multichannel sellers’ omni-channel product placement strategies. Furthermore, our analysis shows that by selling one product through the dual channel and the other product through the online channel exclusively, the multichannel seller generates a greater profit than the total profit of two single-product sellers, one selling its product through the dual channel and the other selling its product through the online channel exclusively, as long as the fit probability of products is low. This result provides further support to our insight that the benefit a multichannel seller enjoys by inducing consumer pseudo-showrooming comes from enhanced coordination between the online and offline product sales, which goes beyond the benefit of selling products through a single channel, online or offline.

Finally, we find that when the firm offers two products of different vertical qualities, it can extract the most synergistic benefit from inducing consumer pseudo-showrooming by selling the higher-quality product through the online channel exclusively. This is because by selling the higher-quality product exclusively online the firm makes a larger room for exploiting consumer surplus, which allows it to profit more from the expanded demand for the online exclusive product. And when the firm offers two products with different demand, it garners the most benefit of inducing consumer pseudoshowrooming by selling the higher-demand product through the online channel exclusively. This strategy allows the firm to better leverage the demand expansion benefit with regard to the online exclusive product. Our theoretical predictions are supported by observed business practices.

## Contributions

An important contribution of our study is to demonstrate that a multichannel seller can effectively synergize its dualchannel operation by coordinating its online and offline product assortment decisions. Our study is anchored in the current turbulence in the retailing industry, where the recent advances in IT technologies have powered the merger of online and offline selling into one single platform and consumers more and more switch between online and offline channels in their shopping process. Making an effort to explore this new and exciting business environment, our study makes a unique contribution to the literature on online retailing. Early works in this literature focus on pure online store operation (e.g., Bakos 1997) and the competition between a physical store and an online store (Balasubramanian 1998). Some later studies examine whether a traditional retailer should add an online branch to its offline channel and investigate the strategic implications of such online channel expansion. Lal and Sarvary (1999) consider a model where each consumer knows the fit with one of two competing products and shows that introducing an online channel affects consumers’ search behaviors and may result in higher equilibrium prices. Huang and Swaminathan (2009) examine how introducing an online channel affects retailer prices. Zhang (2009) examines retailers’ incentive to provide local price information via the online channel and finds that adding an online channel will always benefit a monopolistic traditional retailer, but may intensify price competition and hurt profits of competitive retailers. Ofek et al. (2011) find that when differentiation among competing retailers is not too high, having an online channel can actually lead to increased investment in physical store service and reduced retailer profits. Different from these research efforts, our study is the first to investigate how retailers can take advantage of a synergized cross-channel shopping platform.

Several recent studies examine firms’ product assortment decisions across online and offline channels. Dukes et al. (2009) show that strategic assortment reduction by traditional retailers decreases consumer welfare. Brynjolfsson et al. (2003) argue that strategic assortment reduction at the traditional retailer coupled with assortment expansion at the online store would actually increase consumer welfare. Bhatnagar and Syam (2014) suggest that products can be withdrawn from the physical stores and made available exclusively at the online store to save on carrying costs. While these studies focus on supply side factors that drive retailers’ cross-channel product assortment decisions, our study suggests that the demand side factor of consumer cross-channel searching adds another layer of strategic motivation to such retailer assortment arrangements. Our unique research perspective generates interesting insights regarding how a multichannel seller can achieve the best market performance by strategically placing various product line items with different features such as style, design, quality, demand, and fit probability across online and offline channels.

Another important contribution of our study is to recognize consumers’ cross-channel activities of pseudo-showrooming as a fit-revealing mechanism and to demonstrate the strategic implications of such consumer behavior for firms’ crosschannel assortment strategies. As such, our study adds to the literature on consumer fit uncertainty and firm fit-revealing strategies. Davis et al. (1995) show that a retailer can profitably offer a money-back guarantee for returns to reduce consumer risk and encourage trials. Heiman et al. (2001) show that whether a firm should use demonstrations or money-back guarantees to reduce fit uncertainty depends on consumers’ product knowledge. Chen and Xie (2008) investigate how consumer reviews influence a monopolistic firm’s incentive to provide fit-revealing information. Gu and Xie (2013) examine how competitive firms that offer different quality products adopt different fit revelation strategies. Moreover, some research shows that firms may benefit from consumer fit uncertainty and thus have incentive to keep consumers uninformed (e.g., Shulman et al. 2009). Consistent with this insight, our result shows that by partially revealing product fit information prior to purchase through inducing consumer pseudo-showrooming, a firm can enhance its own profit as well as consumer welfare.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. First, we set up the main model and then we solve it. Next, we discuss several extensions of the main model. We then conclude the study.

## Model

We consider a firm that operates both an online store and a physical store. The firm offers a product line that includes two product items, 1 and 2, with the same vertical quality G $( G > 0 )$ . We model two dimensions of product horizontal features, one associated with the product line and the other associated with individual products. As such, the two products share common styling features that characterize the product line, and are also differentiated with their unique designs. We assume the firm incurs zero cost to sell a product through either the online or the offline store to abstract out supply side factors that affect the firm’s product placement decisions. We also assume that all consumers incur zero cost to visit the firm’s physical store or to buy from the online store to abstract away the impact of channel accessibility on the firm’s product placement decisions. Given this assumption, the firm optimally charges the same price for a product that is available through both stores. We let $p _ { 1 }$ and $p _ { 2 } ( p _ { 1 } , p _ { 2 }$ $\geq 0 )$ denote the two products’ prices respectively. The products’ vertical quality $G$ and prices $p _ { 1 }$ and $p _ { 2 }$ are common knowledge, which consumers can acquire through the firm’s informative advertising, third party reviews, or word of mouth.

The time sequence proceeds as follows. In the first stage, the firm decides for each of the two products whether to sell through the online channel exclusively or sell through both the online and the offline channels. Then in the second stage, the firm decides the selling price for each of the two products, taking the product placement decision as given. Finally, in the third stage consumers make purchase decisions based on their product knowledge and product prices. We assume that the firm makes the product placement decision before the price decision because it is generally believed that price decision is easier to change than product placement decision and thus has a shorter strategic span.

Below we specify the details of the demand and the supply side models.

## Consumers

The market contains two types of consumers, 1 and 2, each with a unit mass. Type 1 consumers have demand for product 1 only and type 2 consumers have demand for product 2 only; no consumer has demand for a product of a different type from hers. This assumption simplifies model analysis in the main model and will be relaxed in model extension. We specify a type $i ( i = 1 , 2 )$ consumer’s utility from purchasing product i as

$$
U _ {i} = v _ {i} - p _ {i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} G - p _ {i} & i f \quad f _ {i} = g \\ \beta G - p _ {i} & i f \quad f _ {i} = b \end{array} \right.\tag{1}
$$

In equation (1), $\nu _ { i }$ is the consumer’s perceived value of product i and depends on two factors: (1) the consumer’s perceived fit with product $i , f _ { i } \in \{ \mathrm { g } , \mathrm { b } \}$ , which captures the consumer’s perception of the horizontal features associated with individual product i, and (2) the consumer’s perceived misfit tolerance with the product line, $\beta \in [ 0 , 1 \bar { }$ , which captures the consumer’s perception of the horizontal features associated with the product line. An individual consumer is endowed with her true fit with product $i , f _ { i } ,$ and her true misfit tolerance with the product line, $. \beta .$

A consumer’s true fit with product $i , f _ { i } ,$ captures how the product’s horizontal features satisfy her idiosyncratic needs (Chen and Xie 2008; Gu and Xie 2013). The same product’s horizontal features provide different fits for consumers with heterogeneous tastes, and the same consumer perceives different fits with products characterized by different horizontal features. For example, consumers with different skin tones or different body shapes are likely to find fit with different product items. We model that a type $i ( i = 1 , 2 )$ consumer finds a good fit with product $i , f _ { i } { = } g$ with probability $\alpha \left( 0 \leq \alpha \leq 1 \right)$

and finds a bad fit with the product, $f _ { i } = b ,$ , with probability 1 – α. We label α as the product’s fit probability; a higher fit probability α indicates a lower chance of finding a misfit product. We also model that a consumer perceives independent fit with the two horizontally differentiated products.

Belonging to the same product line, the two products share common designing, styling, and manufacturing features, which affect consumers’ perceived product values in similar ways. Our model captures this common impact of the product line features on consumers’ product evaluation by modeling that a consumer suffers the same degree $( 1 - \beta )$ of product value loss when a misfit occurs with either product line item, with $\beta$ evenly distributed on the interval [0, 1] among both type 1 and type 2 consumers. A greater misfit tolerance level $\beta$ indicates that the consumer perceives a smaller loss in product value when a misfit occurs with a product line item. Consumer heterogeneity in $\beta$ may come from the unique cost each consumer incurs to accommodate a misfit product. For example, a consumer who finds herself capable of working with the fabric used in an apparel line can make an easy fix if a misfit occurs, suggesting a low cost to accommodate a misfit and a high level of misfit tolerance. On the other hand, a consumer who finds herself incapable of doing so will have to hire a professional tailor to fix a misfit, indicating a high cost to accommodate a misfit and hence a low level of misfit tolerance.<sup>2</sup> Note that each individual consumer is endowed with a unique misfit tolerance leve $\cdot \beta ,$ bu $_ { \cdot \beta }$ materializes in the utility function only if a misfit is realized.

Ex ante, consumers have no knowledge about the horizontal features associated with any individual products or with the product line. In particular, consumers know the distribution of $f _ { 1 } , f _ { 2 } ,$ and $\beta ,$ but not their own endowed values for these parameters. In business practice, brands often adopt new styles, change designers, or even invent new technologies when they introduce new product lines or new season’s collections, all of which lead to consumer fit uncertainty regarding the product line. Without knowledge about the product line features, consumers are uncertain about their misfit tolerance level regarding the line of products. A consumer finds her misfit tolerance level regarding the product line, $\beta ,$ after inspecting either product line items, 1 or 2. In our model, if either of the two products are available at the physical store, the consumer can visit the store to inspect that product, through which she learns her endowed misfit tolerance with both product line items.

Moreover, without knowledge about either product’s design features, consumers are uncertain about their true fit with the two products. A consumer finds her true fit with a product only after she has inspected that product in person. In particular, inspecting product i informs a consumer her true fit with product $i , f _ { i } ,$ but not her true fit with the other product j $( j \neq i ) , f _ { j } .$ Conceptually, a product’s fit is specific to each product’s unique horizontal features, and a good fit with one product does not guarantee the same good fit with another product, despite them being related. For example, even though a shirt of a particular brand upon inspection fits a customer perfectly, there is no guarantee that a pair of pants offered by the same brand would fit the customer perfectly as well. This feature of consumer fit uncertainty makes it distinct from consumer uncertainty in vertical quality, which is typically consistent across related products belonging to the same product line. In our model, if a product is available at the physical store, consumers can visit the store to inspect the product and find its fit prior to purchase. If a product is available solely at the online store, however, consumers have to first buy the product from the online store and then wait until the order arrives to find the product fit. Ex post, consumers have the option to return a misfit product for a full refund after incurring a return cost of rG $( 0 \leq r \leq 1 )$ , which includes not only monetary expenses involved in the product return process but time and psychological burden. We assume a consumer incurs the same return cost no matter whether she returns the product to the physical store or the online store.

Given our assumption of zero store visiting cost, all consumers visit the physical store prior to purchase to acquire more information about the product they desire. When the firm makes both products available at the physical store, both types of consumers learn their misfit tolerance level with the product line as well as the true fit with the product they desire. A type $i ( i = 1 , 2 )$ consumer’s utility from buying product i is thus

$$
U _ {i} ^ {L} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} G - p _ {i} & i f \quad f _ {i} = g \\ \beta G - p _ {i} & i f \quad f _ {i} = b \end{array} \right.\tag{2}
$$

In equation (2), the superscript L indicates that product i is available at the physical store in addition to the online store. Since the online buying cost is zero, consumers can buy product i at the physical store or at the online store. And because consumers’ highest willingness to pay for a product is ${ \bf G } ,$ the firm has to set $\mathfrak { p } _ { \mathrm { { i } } } \leq \mathrm { { G } }$ to generate demand.

When the firm sells one product i at both the online and the physical stores, but sells the other product j at the online store exclusively $( \mathrm { i } , \mathrm { j } = 1 , 2 ; \mathrm { i } \neq \mathrm { j } )$ , a type i consumer by visiting the physical store learns her true value with product i and her utility from buying product i as defined in equation (2). A type j consumer by visiting the physical store and inspecting product i learns her misfit tolerance level β with the product line, but remains uncertain about the true fit f of her desired product. In this case, the consumer forms ex ante expected utility from buying product j by rationally anticipating her ex post behaviors. Particularly, ex post, if the consumer finds a good fit with the product, she always keeps the product as long as $G - p _ { j } \ \geq 0$ . But if the consumer finds a bad fit ex post, she needs to decide between whether to keep the product, which generates a utility of $\ B G - p _ { j } ,$ or to return the product, which renders a disutility of -rG. The consumer rationally keeps the product if and only if $\beta G - p _ { j } \ \ge \ - r G ,$ which happens when the consumer has a sufficiently high level of misfit tolerance, $\beta \ge \frac { p _ { j } } { G } - r$ . Ex ante, the consumer expects the good fit and the bad fit to realize with probabilities α and (1 – α) respectively, and forms expected utility from ordering product j at the online store as

$$
E _ {f} U _ {j} ^ {E} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c c} \alpha (G - p _ {j}) + (1 - \alpha) (\beta G - p _ {j}) & i f & \beta \geq \frac {p _ {j}}{G} - r \\ \alpha (G - p _ {j}) + (1 - \alpha) (- r G) & i f & \beta <   \frac {p _ {j}}{G} - r \end{array} \right.\tag{3}
$$

In equation (3), the superscript E in $U _ { j } ^ { E }$ indicates that product j is available at the online store exclusively. The subscript f in $E _ { f }$ indicates that the expectation is made on the product fit.

It is important to note that the essential assumption behind our model is that consumers perceive more than one dimension of product uncertainty, and pseudo-showrooming clears only one dimension of the uncertainty but not the other. In our model, we assume that pseudo-showrooming clears a consumer’s uncertainty in misfit tolerance that is specific to the product line, but not in product fit that is specific to the particular product.<sup>3</sup>

Note that our approach to modeling “product fit” and “misfit tolerance” is in the same spirit as Kuksov and Lin’s (2010) approach to modeling “product quality” and “quality preference.” Kuksov and Lin posit that a consumer learns a product’s quality only after inspecting that product and learns about her quality preference after inspecting either of the two competing products. Our study, however, differs from Kuksov and Lin in two ways. First, Kuksov and Lin study consumer preference for vertical quality, whereas we focus on consumer preferences for horizontal fit. Second, Kuksov and

Lin study two products sold by competing sellers, in which case one seller’s effort to help consumers learn their quality preferences provides the other seller a free-riding opportunity. We consider two products sold by the same seller, in which case the seller’s effort to help consumers learn their misfit tolerance facilitates pseudo-showrooming.

## Seller

The firm maximizes its total profit from product sales at both the physical store and the online store. We assume the firm incurs a cost of hG to handle each unit of product return received at the physical store or the online store, where parameter h $\left( h \ge \ 0 \right)$ measures the magnitude of the return handling cost. We focus on the interesting case when h is not too high ( ) so that selling a product exclusively online h ≤ 1 can be profitable.<sup>4</sup>

We consider three product placement strategies that the firm can formulate: (1) selling both products through the dual channel (case LL), (2) selling product 1 through the dual channel and product 2 through the online channel exclusively (case LE), and (3) selling product 1 through the online channel exclusively and product 2 through the dual channel (case EL). We ignore the case when the firm sells both products through the online channel (case EE) in the main model to focus on multichannel strategies and will discuss case EE in model extension.

## Analysis

We solve the multichannel firm’s optimal product placement strategy by comparing its maximized market payoffs under different strategic options (cases LL, LE, and EL). We then evaluate how the product placement strategy that induces consumer pseudo-showrooming (cases LE/EL) affects firm profit and consumer surplus.

## Firm Sells Both Products through the Dual Channel (Case LL)

When both products are offered through the dual channel, both types of consumers visit the physical store and after inspection both learn the true value of the product they desire. In stage 3, a proportion α of type i (i = 1, 2) consumers find a good fit with product i and have a utility of $U _ { i } ^ { L } = G - p _ { i }$ These consumers will always buy product i as long as $p _ { i } \ { \leq } G .$ The remaining proportion $1 - a$ of consumers find a bad fit with product i and perceive a utility of $U _ { i } ^ { L } = \beta G - p _ { i }$ from buying the product. These consumers will buy product i only if their misfit tolerance level is sufficiently high, $\beta \ge \frac { p _ { i } } { G }$ Given $p _ { i } \leq G ,$ , the total consumer demand for product i is

$$
D _ {i} ^ {L L} = \alpha + (1 - \alpha) \left(1 - \frac {p _ {i}}{G}\right)\tag{4}
$$

In stage 2, the firm maximizes its total profit, which can be obtained by maximizing its profit from selling each product separately. In particular, the firm chooses the selling price $p _ { i }$ for product $i ( i = 1 , 2 )$ to maximize its profit from selling product $i ,$

$$
\pi_ {i} ^ {L L} = \Big (\alpha + (1 - \alpha) \Big (1 - \frac {p _ {i}}{G} \Big) \Big) p _ {i}\tag{5}
$$

Our theoretical insight provides an interesting explanation to the observed practice among multichannel retailers to sell high-end products exclusively through the online channel. ## Robustness of Main Model Results

The key driver of our main results is that pseudoshowrooming helps resolve, but only partially, consumers’ uncertainty about the online exclusive product that they desire. We have kept the core model simple to highlight this key insight. In a more general setting, our main results will continue to hold as long as fit-uncertain consumers who desire the online exclusive product have access to the seller’s physical store to conduct pseudo-showrooming. Next, we demonstrate the robustness of our main results under seller competition and under market dynamics.

## Robustness of Results under Seller Competition

It is important to note that our study is positioned in the research context where product fit is important for consumers’ purchase decisions, such as in the apparel and furniture categories. In these product categories, marketers typically offer horizontally differentiated products that compete on design, style, and other horizontal features. A seller carrying a uniquely styled product line is able to develop a unique market position against its competitors and gain monopoly power among consumers who have strong preferences for its unique design. In such a competitive market, our key insights and core results continue to hold as long as the multichannel seller attracts consumers with strong preferences for its product line over its competitor’s. Indeed, the empirical evidence presented in the “Introduction” shows that, in reality, consumers conduct pseudo-showrooming quite often and in a variety of product categories, despite seller competition.

To demonstrate the robustness of our results in the competitive setting, we extend the main model and consider two sellers, A and B. Seller A operates both an online store and a physical store, and offers two products, A1 and A2. Seller B operates a local store and offers a product B2. Type 1 consumers have demand for product A1 only, and perceive a good fit with product A1 with probability $\alpha ( 0 \leq  { a } \leq 1 )$ . ## Robustness of Results under Market Dynamics

Our main model considers a multichannel seller’s product placement decision in a single period. Over multiple periods, our key insight that pseudo-showrooming helps partially resolve consumer fit-uncertainty regarding the online exclusive product will continue to drive a multichannel’s incentive to offer online exclusive products, as long as consumer fituncertainty remains. In practice, sellers constantly introduce new products over time and consumers constantly flow in and out of the market, causing persistent existence of consumer fit uncertainty. Below we demonstrate the robustness of our results in a two-period dynamic model.

We consider two scenarios of the dynamic market environment. First, we consider the scenario when the seller introduces a new product for the old product line in the second period. This can happen when the two periods are contiguous. For example, apparel labels such as Banana Republic often introduce new styles several times within a season (e.g., in winter). The styles introduced in the same season usually come from the same designer, follow the same fashion trend, and are made by the same factory, and therefore share common product line features with the products introduced in the first period. We capture this scenario by modeling that the seller introduces a new product 2n to replace product 2 in the second period, which has different horizontal features from product 1 or 2, but shares the same product line features with products 1 and 2. After the first period, a size $k ( 0 \leq k \leq 1 )$ of type 2 consumers exit the market, and the same size of new type 2 consumers enter the market. This assumption allows us to abstract out the impact of market size change across time periods on the firm’s product placement decision. In the second period, each type 2 consumer has a unit demand for product 2n. Those type 2 consumers who have stayed in the market after the first period know their misfit tolerance with the product line from past shopping experience, but remain uncertain about their true fit with the newly introduced product 2n. And those type 2 consumers who newly enter the market in the second period are uncertain about their misfit tolerance with the product line as well as their true fit with product 2n. These consumers can learn about their misfit tolerance level by inspecting product 2n, if it is available through the physical store (in case LL), or otherwise through pseudo-showrooming (in case LE). As such, both the old and the new consumers know their misfit tolerance level prior to purchase, and both learn their true fit with product 2n, $f _ { 2 n } ,$ prior to purchase in case LL, but after purchase in case LE. It is easy to see that the demand condition in the second period is the same as in the main model. As such, the seller’s optimal pricing and product placement strategy in the second period is the same as in the main model.

Second, we consider the scenario when the seller introduces a new product line composed of two new products in the second period. This happens when the two periods are relatively far apart in time. For example, across seasons (e.g., in winter and in summer), apparel labels often change designers, follow new style trends, or change suppliers. And it is a common complaint that the actual measurements of a “size 2” may change from season to season. Therefore, product lines offered in two time periods far apart can be viewed as having correlated but different features, which hinders consumers from applying their past product knowledge to evaluate the new product offerings. Moreover, over time consumers tastes may change (e.g., getting older), or their body shapes may change (e.g., losing or gaining weight); as such, consumers’ past product knowledge and experience may not help with their current decisions. We capture this scenario by modeling that in the second period the seller introduces a new product line, which is composed of two new products 1n and 2n, to replace products 1 and 2 from the old product line. All type 2 consumers stay in the market after the first period and no new type 2 consumers enter. A proportion w $( 0 \leq w \leq 1 )$ of type 2 consumers are able to correctly infer their misfit tolerance with the new product line from their past experience. The remaining proportion 1-w of consumers remain uncertain about their misfit tolerance with the new product line. These consumers can learn about their misfit tolerance level by inspecting product 2n, if it is available at the physical store (in case LL), or otherwise through pseudo-showrooming (in case LE). As such, all consumers know their misfit tolerance level prior to purchase, and all learn about their fit with product 2n, $f _ { 2 n } ,$ prior to purchase in case LL, but post purchase in case LE. Again, in this scenario, the demand condition in the second period reduces to that in the main model. The seller’s optimal pricing and product placement strategy are again the same as in the main model.

In both of the above dynamic situations, a win–win region arises in the second period where the seller generates a greater profit as well as a greater consumer surplus by selling product 2n online exclusively (case LE) than by selling both products through the dual channel (case LL).

## Model Extensions

We discuss several extensions of the main model to obtain further insights regarding the benefit of consumer pseudoshowrooming and to further demonstrate the robustness of our key results.

## Inducing Consumer Pseudo-Showrooming Versus Pure Online Selling

Our main model results show that a multichannel seller can obtain a greater profit by offering a larger product assortment online than offline and inducing consumer pseudoshowrooming for the online exclusive product. To this end, we compare the firm’s maximized payoff in case LE/EL with that in case EE when the firm sells both of its products through the online channel exclusively.

## Pseudo-Showrooming Assists Consumers in Learning Product Fit

In the main model, we assume that consumers through pseudo-showrooming learn about their misfit tolerance level, but remain uncertain about their true fit with the online exclusive product. Now we consider an extended model where pseudo-showrooming also helps consumers infer the true fit of the online exclusive product. This can happen when a consumer by inspecting horizontal features of the product available at the physical store learns the scale to evaluate the horizontal features of the online exclusive product. For example, by trying on a piece of apparel a brand offers, a consumer may learn about the styling philosophy and design features of the brand, which helps the consumer evaluate her fit with the brand’s other offerings she has not yet tried on. We assume that a proportion $\rho \left( 0 \leq \rho \leq 1 \right)$ of consumers correctly infer their true fit with the online exclusive product after inspecting the product available at the physical store, whereas a proportion 1-ρ of consumers remain uncertain about their true fit with the online exclusive product after such product inspection. We assume that consumers capability to infer their true fit with the online exclusive product through pseudo-showrooming is independent of their perceived fit with either product. Other specifications of the main model apply. When ${ \rho } = 0$ , this model reduces to the main model.

We solve the model following a similar approach as in the main model. When the firm sells both products through the dual channel (case LL), its optimal pricing strategy and maximized payoff are the same as in the main model (see Lemma 1). When the firm sells product 1 through the dual channel and product 2 through the online channel exclusively (case LE), type 1 consumers’ purchasing behaviors are the same as in the main model, leading to the same optimal firm strategy. Below we derive the firm’s optimal pricing strategy for selling product 2.

In stage 3, a proportion ρ of type 2 consumers learn their true fit with the online exclusive product 2 after inspecting product 1. ## Demand Overlap between the Two Products

In the main model, we assume that consumers of a particular type only have demand for the product of that type, in which case a firm is able to price the two products independently. Now we relax this assumption and consider the case when the demand for the two products fully overlap and an individual consumer may buy either product that offers a higher nonnegative utility. As such, there is no difference between type 1 and type 2 consumers. A key implication of this relaxation is that demand for the two products now ceases to be independent. This allows the firm to coordinate pricing policies in addition to product placement decisions so as to achieve better synergy between the two products and to pursue even greater profits.

We first consider case LL when both products are offered through the dual channel. ## Conclusion

Our study examines consumer pseudo-showrooming, the cross-channel product search behavior of modern consumers to inspect one product at a seller’s physical store before buying a related but different product at the same seller’s online store. Our analysis demonstrates that a multichannel seller can profit from inducing consumer pseudoshowrooming for online exclusive products by carrying at its physical store an informative selection of its full online offerings. This profit gain comes from the coordinated multichannel operation that is facilitated through exploiting consumer pseudo-showrooming behavior, which goes beyond the benefit of selling products through a single channel, online or offline. In particular, our results show that compared to selling both products through the dual channel, the firm obtains a greater profit by selling only one product through the dual channel and the other through the online channel exclusively and inducing consumer pseudo-showrooming, if the fit probability of products and consumers’ cost of returning a misfit product are both in the intermediate range. Moreover, over a large range of parameter values, consumers also enjoy a greater total surplus when the firm adopts the product placement strategy that induces pseudoshowrooming, suggesting an interesting win–win situation. We further show that the profit a multichannel seller obtains from selling two products when it adopts the product placement strategy of facilitating consumer pseudo-showrooming is greater than the total profit of two independent singleproduct sellers, one selling its product solely through the online channel and the other selling its product through the dual channel, as long as the fit probability of products is low. Finally, we show that when the firm offers two products of different vertical qualities, it garners the most benefit from consumer pseudo-showrooming by selling the high (versus low) quality product through the online channel exclusively.

And when the firm offers two products of different demand, it benefits the most from inducing consumer pseudoshowrooming by selling the high (versus low) demand product through the online channel exclusively. Our study offers interesting insights into how consumers’ product search behaviors on the demand side, in addition to supply side factors, may motivate firms to offer a product online exclusively even when offline selling is feasible.

In our model, we treat the consumer return cost as exogenously determined. We have considered an extended model where the firm can help reduce consumers’ return cost at its own expenses and demonstrated the robustness of our results in this case. Moreover, we abstract out consumers’ store visit decision by assuming zero cost for consumers to visit the local store or order from the online store. Our key insight and core results continue to hold as long as fit-uncertain consumers have access to the seller’s physical store to conduct pseudo-showrooming. We have demonstrated the robustness of our key results in an extended model where the consumer’s store visit cost is explicitly modeled. Details of these two extensions are included in the appendix.

The objective of our study is to demonstrate that in the modern cross-channel shopping environment, the consumer behavior of pseudo-showrooming provides sellers opportunities to synergize online and offline channel operation through designing effective product assortments across channels. To highlight this key insight, we abstract out factors that may affect the performance of a single channel but not the cross-channel synergy, such as the cost of operating online and offline stores. Adding these factors back will not change our key insights or core results. In business practice, however, a marketer will need to take into account all these missing factors together with the pseudo-showrooming effect in making final product placement decisions.

We model that the firm makes pricing decisions taking the two products’ fit probabilities as given and believe this setup provides a reasonable simplification of the realistic business environment. First, the decision on product fit probability is made at the product design/product development stage, typically before the pricing decision. Moreover, changing a product’s price is easier than changing a product’s design features. As such, a firm’s pricing decision is commonly viewed as having a shorter strategic span than the product design decision and is typically modeled as happening in a later stage of the decision sequence. Our model can be viewed as focusing on this later stage of a firm’s decision sequence when the product features have already been determined. Moreover, our modeling approach actually captures an even more general business context where the retailer makes pricing and product placement decisions for products offered by its upstream manufacturers. It would be interesting to investigate a marketer’s product fit decisions. One consideration is that manufacturers may not have full control over the fit probability of products. After a product is developed, a manufacturer can find out its fit probability through marketing research such as a survey and a test market. But predicting a product’s fit probability is usually very difficult before the product is fully developed and launched. Another consideration is that when the product fit decision is made by independent manufacturers, the fit probability decision may be complicated by the vertical channel relationship as well as manufacturer competition.

In this study, we focus on investigating how pseudoshowrooming helps partially resolve consumers’ fit uncertainty regarding the online exclusive product. Our key findings hold under this assumption regardless of the specific way of modeling which piece of information pseudoshowrooming reveals to consumers. Moreover, consumer pseudo-showrooming may have other impacts, such as enhancing consumers’ brand loyalty. We leave these interesting issues to future research.

## Proof of Robustness of Main Model Results Under Seller Competition

We first solve the worse case that the multi-channel seller A encounter, that is when type 2 consumers always prefer seller B’s products to seller A’s products, and thus always buy product B2 as long as her utility from purchasing product B2 is non-negative. We solve the model backwardly.

In case LL, seller A sells both products through the dual channel. In stage 3, type 1 consumers’ behavior are the same as in the main model and therefore the seller’s optimal pricing strategy is also the same as in the main model. We focus on analyzing type 2 consumers’ behavior. Under our assumption, type 2 consumers who find a good fit with product B2 and those who find a bad fit with product B2 but a sufficiently high misfit tolerance $\beta _ { B } \geq \frac { p _ { B 2 } } { G }$ always buy product B2. Only a proportion $\begin{array} { r } { ( 1 - \alpha _ { B } ) \frac { p _ { B 2 } } { G } } \end{array}$ of consumers who find a bad fit with product B2 and have low misfit tolerance level $\beta _ { B } < \frac { p _ { B 2 } } { G }$ will consider buying seller A’s product A2. Among these consumers, only those who find a good fit with product A2 and those who find a bad fit with product A2 but a sufficiently high misfit tolerance level $\beta \geq { \frac { p _ { A 2 } } { G } }$ will buy product A2. We write down the demand for product B2 as

$$
D _ {B 2} ^ {L L} = (\alpha_ {B} + (1 - \alpha_ {B}) (1 - \frac {p _ {B 2}}{G}))\tag{10}
$$

and the demand for product A2 as

$$

## Cost to Visit the Local Store

In the main model, we assume zero cost to visit the local store or order from the online store to abstract out consumers’ store visit decision. Our analysis reveals the interesting result that even when fit-uncertain consumers have access to the offline store, the multi-channel seller still benefits from selling a part of its product line through the online channel exclusively. Our key insight and core results continue to hold as long as fit-uncertain consumers have access to the seller’s physical store to conduct pseudo-showrooming. When consumers have to incur a cost to visit the local store, they decide between (i) paying the store-visiting cost and partially resolving uncertainty regarding the online exclusive product prior to purchase, or (ii) not visiting the store and making online purchase decisions under full uncertainty about the online exclusive product. Consumers will take option (i) when their store visiting cost is sufficiently low. Once these consumers have arrived at the local store, their store-visiting cost becomes sunk and does not affect their subsequent product purchase decisions or product return decisions any more. As such, pseudo-showrooming affects these consumers’ choice behaviors and the firm’s strategic activities in the same way as in the main model. And our main model results regarding the benefits of offering online exclusive products and inducing consumer pseudo-showrooming continue to hold.

We extend the main model to consider two types of consumers in the market with low and high costs to visit the local store. We model a proportion $s ( 0 \leq s \leq 1 )$ of consumers incur zero cost to visit the local store and thus always do so. These consumers’ behaviors are the same as in the main model. The remaining proportion 1 s of consumers incur a sufficiently high store visiting cost that practically impedes a store visit and always buy from the online store directly. These consumers’ choice behaviors are the same as modeled in case EE (Section 4.1). We solve the model through numerical simulation. Consistent with the main model results, our analysis reveals a win-win region where the seller generates a greater profit as well as a greater consumer surplus when it sells one product exclusively online. For example, if $s = 0 . 5 ,$ $G = 1$ $\alpha = 0 . 5$ , and $r = 0 . 3$ the seller’ maximized profit in case LL and case LE are 0.5169 and 0.5261, respectively; and the consumers’ total surplus in case LL and case LE are 0.0198 and 0.0962, respectively.

We have also examined the case when some consumers already know product fit and their misfit tolerance. These consumers are indifferent between online and offline shopping if the cost of visiting the local store is zero and will order from the online store if the cost of visiting the local store is non-trivial. These consumers base their purchase decisions on the product’s price, and will not return a product if a purchase is made. For these consumers whether a product is offered through the local store is not relevant. As such, the existence of these consumers in the market does not impair our key insight regarding fit-uncertain consumers who visit the local store. We model that a proportion $k ( 0 < \mathsf { k } < 1 )$ of consumers are uncertain about the products’ fit and their misfit tolerance level before inspecting the product, and the remaining proportion 1-k of consumers are fully informed about the product fit and their misfit tolerance level even before inspecting the product. We solve the model through numerical simulation. Consistent with the main model results, our analysis reveals a win-win region where the seller generates a greater profit and also a greater consumer surplus when it sells one product exclusively online. For example, if $k = 0 . 7$ $G = 1 , \alpha = 0 . 4$ , and $r = 0 . 2 5$ , the seller’ maximized profit in case LL and case LE are 0.4167 and 0.4394, respectively; and the consumers’ total surplus in case LL and case LE are 0.0822 and 0.1894, respectively.

The optimal price is $p _ { 2 } ^ { L E * } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } G$ , which renders a maximized firm profit of $\pi _ { 2 } ^ { L E * } = { \textstyle \frac { 1 } { 2 } } G$ . The consumer surplus at $p _ { 2 } ^ { L E * }$ can be derived as

$$
\gamma_ {2} ^ {L E *} = \alpha (G - p _ {2}) + (1 - \alpha) \int_ {0} ^ {1} (\beta G - p _ {2}) d F \beta = \frac {1}{2} (G - 2 p _ {2} + G \alpha) = \frac {\alpha G}{2}\tag{62}
$$

(ii) ${ \frac { 1 } { 2 } } G < p _ { 2 } \leq G$

In this case, the firm’s profit function is $\pi _ { 2 } ^ { L E } ~ = ~ \alpha p _ { 2 }$ . The optimal price can be solved as $p _ { 2 } ^ { L E * } = G$ , which renders a maximized profit of $\pi _ { 2 } ^ { L E * } = \alpha G$ . The consumer surplus can be derived as $\gamma _ { 2 } ^ { L E * } = \alpha ( G - p _ { 2 } ) = 0$

Strategy (i) dominates (ii) if and only if $\alpha < \frac { 1 } { 2 }$

Our analysis confirms the existence of a win-win region where $\Pi ^ { L E * } > \Pi ^ { L L * }$ and $\Gamma ^ { L E * } >$ $\Gamma ^ { L L * }$ are both satisfied when the fit probability of products is not too high or too low. Figure (A6) illustrates the win-win region when $h = 0 . 1$
