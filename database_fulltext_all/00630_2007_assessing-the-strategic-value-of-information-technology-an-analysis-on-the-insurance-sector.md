# Assessing the strategic value of Information Technology: An analysis on the insurance sector

Paolo Neirotti <sup>\*</sup>, Emilio Paolucci

Department of Business and Production Engineering, Politecnico di Torino, Torino, Italy Received 21 February 2006; received in revised form 6 September 2006; accepted 20 May 200 Available online 23 July 2007

## Abstract

Even though companies today are well aware of the pitfalls in making IT investments and that many IT applications have become commodities, IT projects still fail. Obviously IT has a strategic value to firms but they have not determined how IT management capabilities and IT governance schemes explain commonalities and differences in their use of IT.

We analyzed the insurance industry in the US and Europe through case studies and an analysis of 30 Italian firms. Twenty case studies carried out between 1998 and 2003 led us to conclude that technological and business path dependencies, along with time compression diseconomies, resulted in diversities in IT adoption dynamics due to their differences in IT governance and management practice. Our analysis showed that most of the firms in the Italian insurance sector increased their productivity through IT regardless of their IT management capabilities. It also showed that competitive advantages were not correlated with IT spending levels nor with the kind of IT investments that made general productivity growth in the industry possible. <sup>#</sup> 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: IT returns; IT governance; Competitive advantage; IT capabilities

## 1. Introduction

The capabilities required for managing investments in IT have become complex and now include a greater variety of competencies. This increased complexity is primarily due to the pervasiveness of IT in the organization, its rapid evolution, and the multiplicity of fields now under the control of IT managers. In this context, appropriate governance and management approaches have a primary importance for the success of IT projects [5,7,15,29].

The management systems – determining the way decision rights and accountability are distributed among line, senior, and IT managers [41] – define a ‘‘track’’ that drives the planning for and implementation of IT. With IT spending decisions made throughout the enterprise and new opportunities arising from emerging technologies and competition, local executives must make funding decisions more frequently.

However, even though the importance of appropriate IT management systems is known to executives, many companies fail in implementing their IT projects [16,26]; this means that a more in-depth analysis of the actual strategic value of IT is required. Should companies really cut IT investments and carefully manage the risks stemming from failure? Is IT a resource that can bring about growth in productivity if combined with organizational change, but one that cannot provide any firm with a lasting competitive advantage?

We found that little attention had been given to analyzing the effect of IT management capabilities on firms’ IT resources accumulation processes over a longtime horizon, and to considering the implications of this process on competitiveness. Therefore, we focused our attention on such issues while trying to understand how to recognize competitive advantage in the use of IT.

Our emphasis was thus on identifying similarities and differences in the firms’ changes driven by IT. More specifically, we focused on the effect that IT spending, IT management systems and the quality of the decisions had on the firm’s accumulation of IT resources. To do so, we analyzed the insurance industry by using a sequential approach [18] based on combining a qualitative analysis of 20 case studies of American and European insurance firms and a panel study on a sample of 30 insurance enterprises doing business in Italy. Data on Italian companies were focused on the relationships between investments in IT, the organizational changes driven by IT and performance. The results were obtained through a sequential triangulation [13]; this made it easier to understand how the differences among companies in terms of their IT management explained their productivity and competitiveness.

We chose to analyze this topic on a single industry for two reasons. First, this allowed us to control the differences in the competitive environment and the business processes in which IT applications were adopted. Second, the insurance industry was particularly suitable, given its information-intensive nature and the significant changes that have occurred in the competitive environment in the 1990s as the result of deregulation. Along with an evolution in customer demand, these changes have led insurance firms in both the United States and Europe to redesign their business and organizational models.

## 2. The antecedents of successful IT investments

In order to use IT effectively, companies must establish a priori the role that it has in their business, the way the IT architecture is managed (i.e. what IT applications must be standardized to support data integration and favor IT efficiency) and the way IT activities must be organized within the company (i.e. which services should be outsourced, which ones should be implemented enterprise-wide, and what service level requirements should be defined). Large companies must maintain control over these issues even though they often do less IT work ‘‘in-house’’ now than they in the past. This is because the design of the IT systems entails firm-specific knowledge [28], a longterm view, and a focus on achieving the company’s fundamental goals. In fact, the definition of the IT management model is based on:

\- the way the planning process is organized and thus the way decision making power about IT investments is distributed among IT, line and top managers; this denotes a firm’s IT governance system;

\- the methods used to evaluate IT projects [35,42]; and the criteria to prioritize them and ensure that they are aligned with the company’s business processes [25];

\- the CIO’s involvement in business strategic planning;

\- the CIO’s autonomy in determining budgets for IT, and the degree of centralization in allocating authority for strategic IT decisions [30];

\- the internal organization of the IT department, its coordination mechanisms with business functions to identify their needs, and with external IT vendors and systems integrators to manage IT infrastructures and systems solutions;

\- ‘‘make or buy’’ choices about IT activities, sourcing decisions for IT vendors and the management of outsourcing contracts [24];

\- attention of the IT department on emerging technologies;

\- the role IT managers have in change management.

As Duh et al. [14] demonstrated, organizational, human and technical impediments can make it difficult for a firm to develop an IT adoption model that can effectively support its business strategy. The choices made by companies in the fields of IT management mentioned above affect the kinds of impediments companies can encounter. This is confirmed by some quantitative studies that have shown the positive effects of a well-structured IT management system. For example, Jeffery and Leliveld [22] found a positive association between correct IT models and financial performance. Newkirk and Lederer [32] showed that great efforts in organizing an extensive and formalized process for planning investments in IS proved to have positive affects in uncertain competitive environments. By applying a resource-based view [4], Powell and Dent-Micallef [33] discerned a positive impact on profitability due to variables like top management commitment, communication between the IT department and corporate functions, and alignment of IT and business strategies. Bharadwaj [6], Santhanam and Hartono [38] found that companies with high IT capabilities had greater overall profitability than their competitors.

Nevertheless, the significance of several studies (for a complete review see [40]) has been limited by problems in measuring competitive advantage. For example, Ray et al. [34] found that IT management capabilities could create competitive advantages that are observable at the process level without necessarily being reflected in performance at the company level. Moreover, the studies on the driver of some companies success – by suggesting that stable superior performance depend on a complex array of organizational and management capabilities [12] – indirectly suggest that finding a direct link between IT resources and lasting superior profitability is difficult. In addition to metrics problems, simultaneity bias impedes finding immediate answers to this question, since it could affect the empirical studies that show a positive association between IT and performance [1]. In fact, in some cases causality could be reversed on the way that the relationship between IT and performance was usually hypothesized: for example firms with a competitive advantage with its roots in assets other than IT could afford to invest greater financial resources in IT, thanks to their superior profits.

In addition, current empirical studies on IT have not analyzed a firm’s IT adoption dynamics in the long term [21] and have not considered whether the IT management model can produce delays or provide advantages in the adoption of key technologies that persist over time. This can be traced back to the difficulties that quantitative studies have encountered in building long time-series for analyzing features of the firm’s IT adoption models, such as: interdependency between applications introduced in different years, the flexibility of the IT architecture, the time between implementing an IT application and experiencing positive returns due to it. These problems have limited analysis of a firm’s ‘‘dynamic capabilities’’ [39] in adapting its technology and business processes to the new external conditions. Turbulence often leads firms to deal with phases where their routines, their technical competencies on IT, and their IT architecture must be rapidly adapted to the changes required by new technology or business conditions. Considering such turbulence, Keen [23], Weill et al. [43], Sambamurthy et al. [37] all claimed that the flexibility of the IT architecture and its internal integration, determine the degree of freedom that a firm has in developing its new strategy.

Thus long-term efficiency in an innovation due to IT depends on factors other than investment in human capital and work practice described as the success factors for IT investments in the ‘‘organizational imperative’’ view [8,27]. Similarly, the ‘‘commoditization’’ vision, based on the idea that the shift from a proprietary system to a standard application makes IT incapable of providing competitive advantage [10,11], does not provide a framework for analyzing the returns from IT in a long-term perspective. In addition to overlooking the importance of dynamic capabilities, this does not acknowledge the high firm-specificity of innovations in a company’s IT portfolio. Although hardware and software architectures are undergoing standardization, companies still have freedom of action in composing their applications portfolio, in deciding when to introduce a new technology, as well as in defining the way of integrating their applications and the ‘‘make or buy’’ choices about a particular system and its maintenance operations. Thus, even though the ‘‘ingredients’’ on the market are the same for each competitor, the results of combining the same ingredients may not be the same. IT architectures that enable perfect integration among different applications are clearly not a commodity since their development requires experiential learning, time, and effort [31].

## 3. The research methodology

The current state of the art suggests that IT management capabilities play a significant role in explaining both the differences in the composition of a company’s IT portfolio and the rapidity with which a firm reacts to the availability of a new technology for changing the organization of business processes.

In order to understand whether this effect has a strategic value and can explain competitive differentials in an industry, the case studies used an inductive approach for analyzing the following constructs (see Table A1 in Appendix A for a description of the aspects investigated):

\- The business strategy of the firm, and the role IT has in its business model. These aspects should affect top managers’ propensity to invest in IT, their involvement in the IT strategy planning process as well as how involved the CIO is in defining the business strategy. As such, they should affect the design of the IT governance model, the amount of financial resources allocated to IT and the adoption choices taken.

\- The IT governance schemes and the decision-making process for IT investments.

\- The investments in IT realized, their degree of fit and alignment with the firms’ business goal.

\- The impact of such investments on the organization of business processes and performance.

The model in Fig. 1 shows the links among these constructs that were the object of our analysis. The focal point of the study was IT adoption choices. Our intent was to understand the differences existing in IT resources across companies, to assess whether these diversities persisted over the years, significantly constrained the subsequent adoption choices and affected the use of IT for creating new business capabilities. The case studies thus allowed us to understand if IT enabled some companies to obtain a competitive advantage in certain business processes and to assess if such an advantage had a visible effect on the company’s overall operating profitability. A further strength of the case studies was the possibility to investigate a firm’s impediments in designing and implementing an agenda of IT investments able to support its business strategy. In order to understand the IT adoption choices, our focus was on identifying how past technological choices constrained the leeway subsequently available to CIOs in the decision-making processes. A survey based on a structured questionnaire would have not made it possible to analyze these aspects.

Case studies were carried out through three different waves between 1998 and 2003. The first wave was realized in 1998, while the second and the third ones were carried out in 2001 and 2003. This approach allowed us to assume a long-term perspective in analyzing IT investment strategies and their impact on processes and performance; in this way, our observation was free from possible biases due to the temporary hypes on IT existing in each period. Overall, we analyzed 20 medium and large insurance companies doing business in the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, France and Italy. Five companies were investigated through a longitudinal approach during at least two of the three waves of investigation undertaken. This permitted us to observe the steps that characterized a company’s IT strategy at different moments, as well as the interdependencies among the choices taken by the company in correspondence with two different moments of investigation. Table A2 in Appendix A lists the companies that were analyzed. In each phase, the case studies were based on semistructured interviews with CIOs. The way the case studies were triangulated with the subsequent quantitative analysis on Italian enterprises is described in the following paragraphs.

## 4. Appraising the value of IT: commonalities and differences in IT adoption dynamics

In investigating our case studies, the strategic value of IT was assessed by analyzing the IT management model and the IT projects realized in each of the firms between 1998 and 2003. The goal was to identify similarities and differences in the change processes driven by IT investments.

## 4.1. The similarities

## 4.1.1. The organizational changes favored by IT

Our case studies all found a process of pervasive organizational change resulting from IT that had its roots in the late 1980s; this was apparently based on reengineering the underwriting process. The way companies reorganized this process consisted of two phases:

\- Starting around 1995, companies computerized their agency network and began to downsize their back office. Agencies were connected and provided with software into which the business rules for rating customer risk had been incorporated. Coordination costs among the technical core and agencies were still high because of batch exchange of data among the nodes.

\- By about 2000, through the introduction of XMLbased systems, companies had implemented real-time exchange of information between agencies and the core. This allowed companies to be more efficient, because they could update the rating criteria immediately throughout the agency. The improvement in the coordination between the technical core and the agencies led to the reduction of the time to issue a policy. This gives greater market responsibility to the agent, but has also made it possible for companies to increase their control on the selected risks.

After 2000, the real time connection of the agencies to the core was completed in all the companies. They agreed that these changes boosted improvements in productivity. This was the consequence of a logic based on unbundling technical activities from sales activities and led to the polarization of the organizational structures around:

\- a core, where knowledge and control about rating and managing risks was centralized;

\- a multi-channel sales network, where market knowledge was decentralized, making agents free to concentrate on cross-selling practices and acquiring new customers. Furthermore, the coordination led most companies to broaden the scope of their product portfolio and increase diversification.

These dynamics represent the common course of action, which shows that the organizational imperative behind the use of IT was grasped and applied by almost all the companies studied. As a consequence the reengineering of the underwriting process occurred with the same modalities in all the companies and the applications (the underwriting engines) are now a commodity. Today, companies do not differ significantly in the way they rate and underwrite risks, and even medium-sized companies are aligned with large companies in terms of the technology used. As one CIO stated: ‘‘The ‘computerization’ of the underwriting process through ‘underwriting engines’ completely transforms risk underwriting, which evolves from a ‘core IT process’ to a ‘commodity IT process’. . . Since this software is becoming a commodity, the source for a competitive advantage is no longer located in the underwriting process, but moves to the relationship with the customer and the capability of proposing the right range of products to the right customer’’.

In addition, most of the companies shared the same strategies, the dominant feature of which were to pursue high growth-rates through large product diversification and cross-selling.

## 4.1.2. IT spending and compliance requirements

The way budgets for new IT investments were spent has also become similar among companies. Companies adhere to the regulations that different countries have adopted around financial controls (the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in the US or Basel II in the European Union), privacy (such as the EU Data Protection Act), and fraud (such as anti-money-laundering legislation). According to Gartner [9], in 2006 15% of IT capital expenditures in the US were devoted to Sarbanes-Oxley Compliance. Since Basel II and the Sarbanes-Oxley Act introduced a requirement for Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery Plans, a part of the IT investment budgets goes to investments that cannot differentiate a company from its competitors. Because of compliance needs, the laggards in IT adoption had to increase their spending in IT and accelerate the renovation of their IT portfolio and dismantle the worst of their legacy systems.

However, the way companies adhere to external standards can also be different. Our case studies showed that most of the companies had merely complied, while others had exceeded prevailing norms and identified greater possibilities in providing a business continuity system. Some companies had effective business continuity management by the end of the nineties, while in 2003 others were still having to develop adherence to the obligations.

## 4.1.3. Centralization of IT governance

The third commonality we found was in the centralization of the decision-making power regarding choices about IT architecture, data and software standards, etc. This was primarily determined by the need to improve customer relations and cross-sell capabilities; it was in contrast to the decentralization of the strategic IT decisions to business units. In the late 1990s, the decentralization of IT management became a constraint on business growth of these companies. Changes in the competitive environment made it essential to increase the customer lifetime value and to have a unified view of the customer for effective cross-selling strategies. Decentralization (by making it difficult to enforce standards) prevented business units from sharing information about customers because of the problem of matching data defined with different formats by each business unit.

In several companies, we noticed a return to the centralization of IT management between 2000 and 2003. At the beginning, this process was expensive due to the migration of IS to new platforms designed to permit integration among departmental applications. In 2003 federative models began to be popular. They required a corporate IT function responsible for establishing standards and on local IT functions responsible for developing and maintaining applications that complied with the standards.

## 4.2. The differences

Although there is little room for differentiation in the applications used in core processes and IT governance, there are two main reasons why IT still matters in strategic decision making. First, today most of the companies still developed some of the applications that support their core processes in-house. Second, companies are dealing with IT management problems that are highly firm-specific, as they are related to their previous decisions about technology. Thus the degree of freedom available to a CIO has been reduced by previous choices about adoption of applications and architectures. Some firms have been delayed in IT adoption due to poor decisions made in the past and to problems due to: (1)

the lack of alignment between business strategy and IT planning; (2) the lack of focus on projects aimed at rationalizing the IT portfolio; and (3) the constraints produced by path dependencies due to previous technological choices or to business transformations, such as mergers and acquisitions. As one CIO stated, ‘‘assuming that IT is a commodity is basically the same as saying that in a manufacturing company, factory equipment plays a marginal role in allowing the achievement of the company’s strategic goals in productivity and quality output levels’’.

## 4.2.1. Alignment between business development and IT planning

Some companies failed to obtain a strategic advantage through IT projects because of weak connections between the business and IT planning processes. In some companies this was due to a topdown approach to allocating resources; then, the budget for IT expenditures was determined on a fixed percentage of the premiums written and only marginally by considering the actual technological need of the business line. As one CIO said: ‘‘with the senior management dictating budget cuts, at a certain point the danger is cut into the muscle’’. This means that budget cuts may impede strategic investments in IT, but may also limit maintenance operations, with consequent reduction in the capability to sustain current and future business needs.

Another very common reason for the weak connection was the poor coordination between the IT department and business functions due to narrow views of both line and IT managers, who often did not share common organizational goals. Companies in which the CIO did not have a business background and where line managers had a ‘‘superficial’’ IT culture failed to identify opportunities. This is another consequence of a poorly designed IT governance model.

Some of the companies analyzed were proof that lack of coordination between the IT strategy and business planning produced inefficiencies in the IT portfolio. As one CIO stated: ‘‘In the last five years we have often launched huge projects, with long release times. After these projects were released, we often realized that the systems introduced only partially allowed us to cope with some of our business process needs. This led us to launch other projects and to search for other systems or ‘patches’ that would allow us to cope with the limits of previous projects. This lack of a long term view and our inability to understand what business functions demanded led to an incredible duplication of assets with which we are still struggling today’’. The eight IS used by this company for product management were obvious sign of this asset duplication.

## 4.2.2. The lack of focus on consolidation investments

The planning of IT investments must not merely be focused on aligning technological decisions with business needs, but also on introducing new applications. This also makes it necessary to eliminate obsolete applications: for this reason, the consolidation of the portfolio of IT applications becomes important, but few companies actually carried out massive consolidation projects.

The need to carry out such projects is not necessarily the consequence of past errors, but is often caused by the evolution of IT over three or more decades. Since today the IT supply includes a wide range of integrated and more flexible systems, the applications portfolio of insurance companies is now more stable. Nevertheless, the IT adoption processes have not been incremental for several years, as companies had to deal with ‘‘dismantlement phases’’ of their previous IT projects, due the fact that over the years new applications more flexible, and more open to the subsequent introduction of new features, became available. Thus, positive results of past investments were often nullified by the introduction of new applications.

The lack of consolidation projects has produced time compression diseconomies [36] that have stopped some companies from obtaining positive returns from emerging technologies. We found that companies who kept their integration of the applications portfolio under control managed to exploit the advantages of emerging technologies sooner than those that were still struggling to rationalize their IT portfolio. This produced differences among companies in customer relationship management: companies with a good IT management system could introduce sooner business intelligence applications and determine their customers’ habits and behavior, benefiting from it.

Thanks to continuous attention on consolidation projects and standardization, Prudential Insurance, in the U.S., implemented a business intelligence project that dramatically improved its knowledge of customers and its retention capabilities. In 2003, through a data warehouse that is fed by 65 different operational databases, the company was able to obtain a single view of their 18 millions customers and to implement predictive statistical models. This made the company one of the first to be able to forecast which customers might leave the company on the basis of their asset performance.

Companies that carried out consolidation projects were also able to cut maintenance costs by eliminating applications written in outdated programming languages. Therefore, the companies that undertook consolidation of their IT portfolio reduced their running costs, thereby eliminating a relevant part of the IT budgets spent to allow outdated systems to exchange data with each other. The savings were used in developing new capabilities. Therefore, while consolidation efforts impeded the adoption of emerging technologies in the short term, they were necessary to get positive returns on new IT projects and to allocate financial resources to new technologies.

## 4.2.3. Path dependency effects: the constraints produced by business transformations

In some companies, the consolidation efforts were driven to integrate back office operations after a merger or acquisition. In these cases, the cost of integrating the IT infrastructure of the companies were often underestimated because an assumption had been made that there would be greater economies of scale in the combined operations. However, the integration of the IT architectures proved to be particularly long, temporarily producing diseconomies of scale and delaying other strategic investments.

The case of Generali Assicurazioni gives some interesting insights. Between 1999 and 2001, its investment agenda was almost entirely focused on updating and integrating the IS of INA Assitalia, which was acquired in the second half of the 1990s. Considering that INA’s IS was implemented in the late 1970s (as did the applications used by another company of the group, Alleanza Assicurazioni), it was essential to update the two IS to support daily operations. The systems were very expensive to maintain and grossly inadequate in terms of the millennium bug and changeover to the Euro. Furthermore, between 2001 and 2005 in its agenda of IT investments Generali had to give priority to the standardization of IT architectures and data formats (to convert personal customer data to a single format). Because of this, by 2003 the entire group had reduced the number of IT applications from 250 to 30 and had become ready to undertake actual strategic IT endeavors.

Here, the lack of consolidation efforts made IT a source of serious problems and consequently a relevant part of its budget had to be spent in non-strategic areas with the exclusive goal of retaining operations. Furthermore, the CIO’s area of action was considerably constrained by past IT management choices. This led to time compression diseconomies, which delayed the standardization of customer master data at the group level and business intelligence projects.

## 4.3. Implications from the case studies

IT governance choices and the firms’ endowment of IT management capabilities produced differences in the IT adoption processes analyzed in our case studies. Moreover, our case studies added three additional points regarding the link between IT resources and performance:

\- There were differences in IT adoption because of time compression diseconomies. Consequently, in some firms the benefit of introducing new technologies was slow to manifest itself.

\- Companies with appropriate IT management systems were often able to build competitive advantage. Although the source of these were not enduring, the companies maintained their superiority over time and were able to adopt key technologies sooner than competitors.

\- The advantages produced by superior IT management systems were not necessarily reflected in superior profitability. The scope of IT innovation was quite narrow compared to the multitude of processes in a business, and often the advantages provided by IT were restricted to a single process, preventing the achievement of superior operating profitability. IT may not produce visible benefits on the company performance. In the property and casualty segment, this result can be explained considering that operating profitability depends primarily on the ability to manage claims and identify customer fraud. While IT deployment was found to have produced benefits in the internal coordination costs needed for policy underwriting, its impact on the ability to manage claims has been marginal for years because of the complexity of claims management. The case studies showed that only around year 2003 companies with superior capabilities in data standardization begin to set-up business intelligence projects where their goal was to identify customer fraud.

These considerations illustrate the complex nature of IT investment: spending is not related to the capabilities of managing IT resources. Time compression diseconomies make it difficult for laggards to make up for their delay rapidly by increasing their investments in IT. In the past years, the organizational transformations complementary to IT investments did not allow companies to achieve lasting competitive advantage. The easy duplicability of these transformations imply that most of the companies exploited the value embedded in them to improve their productivity, regardless of the level of their IT management capabilities. But improvements in productivity do not necessarily imply greater competitiveness as these improvements are affordable to all the competitors and do not change the relative advantage of any of the players.

## 5. Evaluating the effects of IT on operating performance

## 5.1. Research hypotheses

The case studies allowed us to generate an in depth conceptualization of the way that IT management shapes the patterns of IT adoption through inductive reasoning. However, some findings needed further quantitative validation. For example, the possibility to improve productivity through the reorganization of the coordination between the technical core and the agencies had to be tested on a quantitative base in order to be accepted as an aspect occurred in all competitors in the industry. We therefore defined three hypotheses:

H1. IT investments have a positive effect on a company’s productivity through better coordination between the agency network and the technical core.

H2. Considered as the extent of IT budgets, IT expenses are not related to the achievement of a competitive advantage.

H3. In the long run, investments in IT enable a general improvement in productivity ratios in the entire industry, regardless of the endowment of IT management capabilities available in each company.

The idea behind these hypotheses was that IT investments and their related organizational changes helped productivity growth at the level of the entire industry and that IT management capabilities and IT governance systems—being quite different among companies (as shown by the case studies), were irrelevant in explaining the productivity growth. These hypotheses were validated by examining data from annual surveys carried out by the ANIA (the Italian Association of Insurance Enterprises) between 1992 and 2001. These were highly reliable since data was collected each year using the same protocol. Furthermore, their reliability was enhanced by the reputation that ANIA holds in the Italian industry.

## 5.2. The sample

The sample included data about 30 insurance companies. Some of these are Italian subsidiaries of large European groups and therefore their IT management strategies reflect the approaches used at the group level. Companies dealing exclusively in life insurance and small insurance carriers with less than 500 employees were excluded from the analysis, since their organizational changes induced by IT were rather different. Also their IT portfolio is not as complex to manage and IT operations are often outsourced. Companies in the sample represented about 55% of the total and employed about 60% of the employees. In terms of size and productivity, the sample did not suggest any non-response bias against the population of Italian insurers.

## 5.3. The scope of the analysis

Hypotheses H1 and H3 were tested by jointly considering productivity trends and the effect on productivity ratios due to IT spending and some proxies of the extent of organizational change that IT investments enable. Productivity was analyzed considering three indicators: (1) the net premiums written per employee (PR); (2) the loss ratio (LR), which compared claims reimbursed and premiums, showing the firm’s ability to select and rate risk; (3) the expense ratio (ER), which compared administrative and sales costs with premiums written, thereby considering the efficiency of information processing during the entire policy life cycle (from sale to policy and claim management).

In order to estimate IT’s effect on productivity we used a regression models from prior research on insurance companies [17,19,20]. In the models, we estimated the effect on productivity of IT spending, the extent of the organizational changes favored by IT and the positioning of the company in the industry in terms of diversification strategies and relative amount of life lines in the revenues. Therefore, the models estimated consisted of five regressors:

\- The annual budget for IT expenses divided by the number of employees (IT). It was measured by requiring companies to report both period expenses and the depreciation and amortization expenses related to expenditures in IT that were capitalized.

\- The ratio between the number of employees in central staff and the total number of employees (TS\_CEN). This can be considered a proxy of the centralization of technical skills within the company. High values of the ratio correspond to a high level of centralization due to the downsizing of the intermediate lines connecting agencies with the technical core. It was expected that TS\_CEN positively affected productivity by reducing control costs.

\- The ratio between the number of installed and on-line computers and the number of agencies (MS\_DEC). This measured how much IT allowed market responsibility to be decentralized to the agencies.

\- The number of life policies divided by the number of property damage policies (LIFE). This was expected to provide a positive contribution to productivity because the life insurance sector generally offer standardized products for which it is easier to evaluate customer risk.

\- The level of diversification among motor, property and casualty, life and index-linked lines. This has been studied by considering the index $\mathrm { D I V } = 1 - ( M ^ { 2 } + \mathrm { P C } ^ { 2 } + L ^ { 2 } + \mathrm { I L } ^ { 2 } )$ , where M, PC, L and IL, respectively correspond to the ratio of net premiums written in the auto, property and casualty, life and index-linked lines.

Finally, in order to test hypothesis H2, we compared the extent of IT spending in the three groups into which the sample had been split considering the firm’s profitability: (1) ‘‘best performers’’, with an operating profitability higher than the annual median level shown by the sample for each year between 1992 and 2001; (2) ‘‘low performers’’, with an operating profitability lower than the median value of the sample for each year between 1992 and 2001; (3) ‘‘intermediate performers’’, whose operating profitability had significantly varied between 1992 and 2001.

Annual operating profitability was measured by considering the combined ratio (shown in the tables as CR), which corresponded to the complement to 1 of the sum of the expense and the loss ratios. Thus it is the ratio between the operating income and the premiums written.

## 5.5. Estimates of the IT effect on productivity

Although there were no significant improvements in the operating profitability at the industry level, the regression models clearly showed that IT favored improvements in the expense ratio and premiums written per employee. The estimation method for the regression analyses was based on the Arellano and Bond model [2], which avoids the sources of bias due to simultaneity between the contemporaneous regressors and the disturbances. Moreover, by considering prior performance this kind of model does not overstate the significance of the IT resources in determining performance. We carried out the tests indicated by Baltagi [3] for the hypothesis that there is no second-order serial correlation for the disturbances of the first-differenced equation and the Sargan test for over-identifying restrictions. Both tests gave positive results. From Table 4 we can draw the following conclusions:

\- The coefficient of the MS\_DEC variable is significant in the models with PR and ER as dependant variables. This confirmed that there were advantages to be gained in productivity by using IT to decentralize market responsibilities.

\- The coefficient relating to the product of TS\_CEN and IT is significant and negative in the model with LR as a dependant variable. This indicates that high expenses in IT, when combined with a centralization of technical skills, improved the loss ratio.

\- The coefficient related to DIV is significant in the model with ER as a dependant variable, whereas the coefficient related to the product of MS\_DEC  DIV is significant in the models with PR and LR as dependant variables. This indicated that product diversification could improve a company’s cost efficiency. Furthermore, the positive effect that product diversification had on premiums per employee indicated that companies with a broader product range had an advantage in pursuing crossselling strategies.

## 5.6. IT spending and competitive advantage

Finally, splitting of the sample into three groups according to their profitability ratio corroborated the low strategic potential associated with both the budget for IT expenses and the IT enabled decentralization of market responsibilities to agencies. Indeed, ANOVA and the Kruskal–Wallis non-parametric test highlighted showed that there was no association between these variables and profitability ratios permanently above the average of the market. Except for some years, the level of the budget for IT expenses divided by net premiums written, as well as the number of online terminals per agency, did not significantly change among high and low performers (see Appendix B for the results of the ANOVA and non-parametric tests).

All the results showed that IT had a positive effect on cost efficiency, thanks to the reengineering of information flows between agencies and the core, but it did not bring about a general improvement in profitability. We therefore concluded that the available data confirmed the scenario of the hypotheses (see Table 5 for a synthesis).

## 6. Conclusions, managerial implications and limitations

This article showed that the major differences among firms in their use of IT for developing new business capabilities can be traced back to the way that IT projects were managed and to the governance systems used to allocate decisions and responsibilities for IT investment. In our study, companies where IT produced visible returns had better IT management practices that allowed them to adapt their organizational routines and IT portfolio rapidly to meet business needs. We can conclude from these results that appropriate systems of IT management favor the construction of an IT portfolio that is unique and not easily imitable because of the path dependencies that constrain a CIO’s level of freedom in planning IT strategies. This implies that the successful returns some companies have from IT investments is not based on the introduction of a ‘‘single hit’’ application, but is rooted in a continuous series of correct investment decisions.

By showing that superior profitability is not related to either IT spending or the computerization of an agency network, our study supported the fact that today some aspects of IT driven innovation are commodity-like, since they are the result of a general learning process. As such, these factors can have a positive effect on productivity of the entire industry, but cannot provide any firm with a lasting competitive advantage.

Nevertheless, building a governance system where decision-making processes on IT investments are based on discipline, and consistency with the strategic goals requires a complex accumulation of new routines. In our study the governance models that work best were the ones based on highly intangible and idiosyncratic factors (i.e. line managers and IT executives speaking ‘‘the same language’’), which cannot be acquired by simply hiring qualified IT managers. These factors are more likely to occur in certain industries (like the insurance sector and other information intensive businesses), but rooting them in a firm’s organizational capital can be more difficult in sectors where IT has traditionally had a marginal role in the business model. This makes the adoption of IT and its use a more difficult challenge for managers than what was traditionally believed.

The image emerging from our findings supported a resource based view of the firm and showed that – though IT could bring about improved productivity – it also represented a competitive weapon that could cause problems. Allocating high budgets for IT investments without designing an adequate management system for IT decisions could cause misuse of the technologies.

In addressing the issues, our study suffered from certain limitations. First, being based on a single industry, it is not clear to what extent the results can be generalized. We believe that, although the challenges in IT management discussed in this article are quite common across industries, the sectors with a low pervasiveness of IT management capabilities may have a small strategic importance. A second limitation is that we were unable to consider the effect on operating profitability of either IT management variables and IT expenditures.
