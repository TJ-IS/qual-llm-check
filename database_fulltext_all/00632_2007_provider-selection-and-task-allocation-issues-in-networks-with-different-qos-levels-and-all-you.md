# Provider selection and task allocation issues in networks with different QoS levels and all you can send pricing

Nihat Kasap <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Haldun Aytug <sup>b</sup>, S. Selcuk Erenguc

<sup>a</sup> Faculty of Management, Sabanci University, Istanbul, Turkey

<sup>b</sup> Decision and Information Sciences Department, The Warrington College of Business, University of Florida, Gainesville, FL 32611, United States Received 5 May 2005; received in revised form 27 May 2006; accepted 8 October 2006 Available online 14 December 2006

## Abstract

We investigate an optimization problem a firm faces when acquiring network capacity from multiple providers. We define two types of tasks the firm performs using data networks, and show that the time, bandwidth and quality requirements of each type are quite different. We formulate the associated problem as a cost minimization problem subject to quality and capacity requirements and offer multiple solution approaches. We analyze how different prices, quality and task distribution affect the optimal behavior of the firm. We also implement Generalized Bender's Decomposition to solve a relaxation of this problem in order to obtain a tight lower bound. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Quality of service; Sourcing; Telecommunications; Generalized bender's decomposition; Bin packing

## 1. Introduction

Usage of data networks encompasses not only the traditional data applications but also newer applications such as real-time audio/video streaming, voice over TCP/IP, real-time transactions, asynchronous messaging and other batch transactions over digital networks. Each application has different capacity and quality of service (QoS) requirements. Each is affected differently by network reliability and speed. Major network providers have already started efforts to accommodate the QoS demand generated by these applications.

Providers charge differently for the capacity they sell. For example, Internet service providers (ISPs) offer a combination of fixed price for a fixed maximum bandwidth (all-you-can-send) or pay per hour (or minute) for again a maximum bandwidth. Wireless phone companies charge for text messaging based on bytes sent. Certain calling plans offer different per minute charges for phone conversations depending on when the call is placed.

Even though it is possible to buy services directly from network operators such as AT&T, MCI, and SPRINT, it is also possible to work with multiple brokers that offer bandwidth from multiple providers. For example, IT Quotes (http://www.itquotes.com/quotes/carrier\_ bandwidth\_center.htm) sells bandwidth for voice, data, Internet and videoconferencing applications. IT Quote sells these services as a bundle or individually. As for pricing, they advertise “… Private lines can be leased by the month or the year, and at data rates ranging from 56 Kb/s up to gigabits per second.” Another broker Bytemarks (http://www.bytemark.co.uk/hosting/prices/ index.html), a web hosting service, sells bulk capacity at sizes of 25, 50, 75, 100 GB per month, so does SCVNET (http://www.scvnet.net/connection.html) for collocation services. Another popular pricing scheme is unit pricing based on quantity discounts, such as the one offered by Netromedia (http://www.netro.ca/pricing.asp). Yet another provider, SKL Services (http://www.sklservices.com/ colocation/colocation\_pricing.cfm), offers both bulk and unit pricing based on the service type.

The flurry of investments in infrastructure during the late 1990s by the network operators (such as Global Crossing, AT&T) created excess capacity, which the providers still cannot fully utilize [27]. Excess capacity in infrastructure stays with the provider until the demand catches up with supply, which usually is a very slow and uncertain process. One way to manage this excess is to sell it in bulk, albeit at reduced prices. Brokers or third party providers can buy this capacity in bulk and sell it in more flexible and lucrative terms since their cost structure is generally more flexible than the operator. We believe, until the demand catches up with supply the all-you-can-send pricing will remain a popular option especially for the network operators.

A firm uses data networks to perform and support business operations, which we will call tasks. For example, a videoconference is a type of task, so is a remote web site update. The obvious commonality is that both tasks require network resources. A network resource is characterized by its capacity (bandwidth and duration) and QoS. We assume that the contract signed between the firm and the provider specifies the amount of bandwidth and quality promised at a given time. Duration specifies the duration of the contract and hence the period during which the resource is available.

There are two types of costs associated with using data networks. The first one is the resource (i.e., bandwidth, capacity) acquisition cost. The second is the opportunity cost incurred due to insufficient quality achieved in performing certain tasks such as video conferencing. Given a heterogeneous set of tasks and resources that differ in bandwidth and quality requirements, it might be in the firm's best interest to use either multiple providers or sign multiple contracts with different bandwidth and quality requirements. For example, while resources with lower quality can be used for data applications, more expensive resources might be utilized for real-time applications with high QoS requirements. Therefore, the problem that the firm has to solve is a cost minimization problem that reflects a trade off between the cost of acquiring resources and the opportunity cost of degradation in observed quality of tasks performed.

It is in this setting that we wish to model the problem from the firm's point of view. We consider the firm's cost minimization problem in an environment in which the firm can buy network capacity at competitive prices from different providers with different service quality. We also assume that the providers can offer any quality of service and some capacity at competitive prices. In order to complete planned tasks the firm has to first acquire (lease) the capacity (bandwidth) from providers (bandwidth broker firms or network operators) then allocate the tasks into that capacity (Fig. 1).

The quality of service (or the lack of it) of the resource affects the customer in two ways. First, size-fixed tasks might be delayed beyond acceptable deadlines. Second, the realized quality of a time-fixed task such as a videoconference might be unacceptable creating an opportunity cost for the customer. In general, opportunity cost reflects the importance of a task. The more important the task is, the higher the penalty for not achieving desired targets (such as picture or sound quality). Consequently, what matters is the magnitude of the opportunity cost relative to other tasks and relative to the unit cost of a resource. A cost minimizing decision maker will consider the trade-off among these costs when assigning tasks to resources.

Common measures of resource quality are delay, jitter and loss probability. Delay specifies how long it takes for data to travel across the network from source to destination [4]. Jitter represents the variance in delay. Audio and video applications are quite sensitive to delay and jitter, whereas data services are insensitive to either of them [24]. In an audio application, jitter appears to the user as awkward pauses in the middle of a word. Packet loss represents data dropped or irrecoverably damaged by the network. It typically stems from data collision and buffer overflows. Sudden changes in jitter may also cause packet loss [28]. In general, the receiver has two options when a packet is lost: it can (1) ignore the lost frames or (2) send a retransmission request to the sender. Ignoring lost frames is appropriate for real-time (time-fixed) applications such as videoconferencing as they are insensitive to small number of lost packets. All data applications, which we will call size-fixed, require the second action. For example, a file transfer task requires submitting all data.

Section 2 presents an overview of related research. Section 3 describes our assumptions and the model. Section 4 is devoted to finding a heuristic solution and a simple lower bound. Section 5 discusses the development of a tight lower bound using the Generalized Bender's Decomposition procedure. Section 6 presents our conclusions and future research directions.

## 2. Literature review

Existing literature on QoS can be classified into two groups. The first group considers the design and implementation of network infrastructure and operating policies including QoS issues. The main concern addressed in this line of research is guaranteeing a promised level of quality through bandwidth allocation, buffer management and scheduling. There is extensive work on these issues in electrical engineering and computer science fields that describes the necessary infrastructure (including buffer management tools and packet scheduling algorithms) and service models to support applications with varying QoS parameters ([3,13,16,20,23–25,31,32]).

The second group in the literature includes supplyside strategic issues such as pricing and QoS. Gupta et al. [12] argue that a pricing mechanism can be used as a tool to differentiate between the different traffic requirements considering the desired QoS of firms. They discuss the characteristics of feasible pricing mechanisms that support multi-service traffic. They argue that (1) pricing should not be at the packet level to make implementation easy and inexpensive, (2) prices help congestion control by encouraging network use when it is less congested, and (3) multiple priorities are necessary to handle different applications with different QoS levels.

Several pricing structures have been studied in the literature. Reichl et al. [26] proposed a new model called the “cumulus-pricing” model. It is based on a flat-rate scheme (in which a fixed charge is imposed once per period, usually a month). Cumulus-pricing specifies types of services with expected user requirements and a flat-rate for each service.

Einhorn [8] and Gupta et al. [11] suggest priority pricing as one of the Internet service pricing schemes. Einhorn [8] argues that for the network providers it is too difficult to specify in advance what precise menu of service offerings (charging schemes) is appropriate due to complexity of user needs. In designing the service menu each provider should balance the benefits and costs of increasing service diversification. The article also discusses the need to control the traffic during some specified periods such as busy hours. Users should be able to reserve bandwidth for specified periods and instantaneous arrangements should be available for short run applications as well. Service offering and pricing scheme could vary by bandwidth, hours of use, and delay and loss probability tolerance. Gupta et al. [11] describe a priority pricing approach for modeling user service request as a stochastic process and networks nodes as priority queues. A user request can be fulfilled by several alternatives (different service classes) where each alternative has a different price and an expected performance (waiting time or delay). Parameswaran et al. [22] describe a double auction based resource allocation mechanism that compliments the DiffServ model discussed in literature. Simulations show that a network running only with pricing controls responds to congestion quickly. Ogino [21] describes a reverse auction mechanism where network providers bid based on the clients specifications that include QoS, media and duration. Jukic et al. [14] show that using congestion based pricing of best-effort and QoS based traffic improves resource allocation when compared to a fixed apportionment between best-effort and QoS based traffic.

Altmann and Chu [1] and MacKie-Mason and Varian [18] studied usage-based pricing. They argue that users and providers prefer a pricing scheme where flat-rate is charged for basic services, usage-based charge is applied for services with higher quality. With this pricing scheme, users will benefit from both using unlimited access to basic services and choosing the service with higher QoS when they need it. By usage-based pricing, congested resources can be prioritized so that those who are willing to pay more get a higher priority. Mackie-Mason and Varian [17] have found that the equilibrium price and capacity will maximize net social benefit in the competitive market where resources are provided with connection fees and usage prices. They argue that since one user's usage of network resources lowers the value of usage for everyone else, pricing should reflect both direct and external cost of usage so that firms will use the resources efficiently.

Courcoubetis et al. [6] studied different charging schemes with effective bandwidth as a basis for usage charge. Courcoubetis et al. [5] argue that calculating the effective bandwidth can be problematic due to moment generating functions of cell arrival processes that are difficult to model and show that charging for usage with effective bandwidths can be well approximated by charges based on simple measurements. Courcoubetis et al. [6] argue that charges for services are substitutable for one another when the ratio of charges for different services is in proportion to the ratio of their effective bandwidths. They introduce delay and buffer overflow (packet loss) QoS constraints into charging schemes. They argue that the network operators will provide only a small set of QoS differentiated services and real-time traffic is offered for no more than one or two QoS levels. They formulate the total charge as a function of duration of connection and total number of packets carried. They argue that tax-band charging is a suitable scheme in case of variable rate traffic, since a source, which is more bursty, will pay a greater charge under this policy creating an incentive to smooth the network traffic. Kelly [15] also formulated a pricing structure based on time and number of cells carried over a call. He shows that effective bandwidth depends on user's expected transmission rate. This pricing scheme encourages the user to use expected mean transmission rate. Courcoubetis et al. [7] have described a pricing scheme for Available Bit Rate Services in ATM. In their model, prices depend on demand and can be iteratively adjusted. However, in best effort services, prices depend on average cell rate. According to their scheme, a connection is charged by considering the sum of the price per unit of bandwidth on all links along its path. In addition, users are free to adjust their bandwidth request during their call by taking into account unit bandwidth price announced by the network provider. They show that in the case of optimal social welfare, the prices are determined by the congested links.

Except for a few results on social welfare there is no work pertaining to the firm's procurement strategy when faced with a set of providers potentially offering different pricing schemes. Thomas et al. [29] have showed the existence of a social welfare (sum of all user benefits) function. They determine the amount of resources required for each type of service in order to maximize the sum of the users' utilities. Each user's utility function measures the amount of network services the user consumes. They incorporate QoS requirements into an optimization problem and construct an algorithm for finding a welfare maximizing solution. Mackie-Mason and Varian [17] also studied social benefit maximization as described earlier. They show that net social welfare is maximized if the resources are provided with connection fees and usage prices in the competitive market. In the monopoly case, the usage price that maximizes profit is different from the social optimum price.

## 3. A provider selection model

We consider an environment in which the firm can buy network capacity with different service qualities at different prices. We model the capacity and the loss probability requirements explicitly, but formulate delay and jitter as level matching constraints. We consider the all-you-can-send pricing scheme ([6]) in which the firm pays a fixed price for a fixed bandwidth available for a fixed duration. We also assume that a real-time task incurs an opportunity cost if its transmission rate falls below a desired level.

## 3.1. Definition of tasks, resources and QoS requirements

In general, two types of tasks are performed using data networks. A task is time-fixed (real-time, sizecompressible) if its size can be changed without disrupting its completion but the transmission duration cannot be compressed or extended. Most audio/video tasks are time-fixed. A task is size-fixed (timecompressible) if all bits have to be transmitted but the duration is not fixed. Most data applications such as file transfer, database transactions are of this category. Even though a more detailed breakdown of tasks and related QoS requirements is possible [30], the two-task type classification is sufficient for our purposes, and it provides a reasonably general representation of real-world applications.

A data application can tolerate comparatively longer delays before completion. However, high packet loss ratio and delay can postpone the completion of the task since unlike time-fixed applications all data has to be transmitted. Conversely, a time-fixed task such as a videoconference is highly sensitive to delay and jitter. Resources with high delay and jitter reduce the realized quality of the task (such as noise in speech, frozen frames in video, etc). We incorporate the impact of different types of QoS parameters into our formulation differently. Packet loss lowers the available bandwidth (called effective bandwidth). For size-fixed tasks the net impact is increase in time if the transmission rate is not changed. For, time-fixed tasks the impact is a reduction in realized transmission rates at the sink node, which lowers quality; hence potentially creating an opportunity cost. Delay and jitter are modeled as categories. In general, we assume that a contract specifies the minimum guaranteed delay and jitter parameters of a resource. We convert these specifications into categories.

A resource is characterized by its bandwidth, duration and quality. We assume that a given resource can be used to carry out any task so long as it meets or exceeds the QoS level required by the task.

Problem P1 considers the tradeoff between the cost of acquiring capacity and the cost of not meeting target transmission rates in real-time tasks. We assume that available capacity is purchased at a fixed price for a specific bandwidth and duration (all-you-can-send pricing).

Constraint set (2) guarantees that we can use only up to available capacity. Constraint set (3) ensures that the resources satisfy the minimum QoS requirements of the jobs that are assigned to it. Constraint (4) ensures that all time-fixed tasks assigned to a resource are completed when the resource is available (time dimension). Note that a similar constraint is not needed for size-fixed tasks since constraint set (2) guarantees that a size-fixed task is only assigned if there is enough capacity and can be completed on time since we can arbitrarily vary the transmission rate (i.e. morph its shape). Constraint set (5) prevents using more bandwidth than available at any time (bandwidth dimension). Constraint (6) along with (13) ensures that a task is assigned to only one resource and all tasks are assigned. Constraint sets (7) and (8) guarantee that the jobs are actually allocated the required amount of time slices. Constraint set (9) guarantees that a network resource is selected only if at least one task is assigned to it. Constraint set (10) ensures that a task is assigned to a network resource only if it occupies a time slice on it. Constraint set (11) states that transmission rate for a time-fixed job j should be high enough to satisfy the minimum transmission (reception rate) at the sink node. Constraint set (12) enforces the target transmission limitation for all jobs.

Unlike a bin-packing problem (BPP) P1 allows, as Fig. 2 illustrates, the duration of the tasks in $A _ { \mathrm { T } }$ to be fixed but the areas to be variable since the transmission rate can vary. Similarly, the areas of the tasks in $A _ { \mathrm { { S } } }$ are fixed but the shapes are variable since the transmission rate and the transmission time can both vary as long as the size remains fixed. The shape of items in the set $A _ { \mathrm { { S } } }$ could be a single rectangle or a combination of several rectangles. At any time, the summation of transmission rates of active tasks has to be less than or equal to the bandwidth of resources.

The constraints in P1 do not guarantee that a task is completed without interruptions. That is, once a task starts its transmission rate might drop to zero for dt time units and then start again at the same rate. Even though this may not be acceptable during real-time, it is a feasible solution in this formulation.

Even though obtaining an exact solution is difficult, simple heuristics that yield good solutions can be constructed. We discuss one such heuristic in the next section.

## 4. Heuristic A

Heuristic A obtains an initial solution for P1 by setting the transmission rate of time-fixed tasks to their upper bounds. Under these conditions the opportunity cost is zero but the capacity acquisition cost is maximum. The problem is, in effect, a two dimensional BPP. The main idea in the heuristic is to reduce the number of resources acquired (reducing the cost of acquiring capacity) by decreasing the transmission rate of time-fixed tasks just enough (creating opportunity cost) so that other unassigned tasks can now use the slack capacity created by the reduction. When sufficient slack capacity is created, one of the used resources is eliminated and the procedure is repeated until no slack capacity can be created.

The heuristic (Heuristic A) is given below.

Input vectors $\overline { { x } } , \Delta t , \alpha , q , Q , \beta , L , c , c ^ { o }$ and sets $A _ { \mathrm { S } } , A _ { \mathrm { T } } ,$ I. Let $I _ { i }$ be the set of jobs assigned to bin i. Let B be the index set of all unused bins, $C _ { i } { = } \beta _ { i } L _ { i }$ represent the total capacity of the bin and $U _ { i } = C _ { i } - \Sigma _ { j \in i } x _ { j }$ be the unused capacity of bin i.

Output Total cost, bin assignments and transmission rates (r<sub>j</sub>).

The steps of the heuristic are given below.

Step 1 Calculate effective bandwidth, $\beta _ { i } \gets \beta _ { i } \boldsymbol { \alpha } _ { i } ,$ , available bin size $C _ { i } { \gets } C _ { i } { \alpha } _ { i } ,$ and unit cost $\begin{array} { r } { \overline { { c } } _ { i } = \frac { c _ { i } } { C _ { i } } } \end{array}$ for each bin and sort them in ascending order of their unit cost and reorder the indexes so that i = 1 refers to the least expensive bin.

Step 2 Find the available bin set for each task considering QoS requirements of tasks. Sort them in decreasing order first by their size then by their QoS requirements.

Step 3 Let $r _ { j } = \overline { { r } } _ { j } ^ { U } , j \in A _ { \mathrm { T } } .$ Assign tasks to bins starting with the least expensive bin such that $I _ { i } \{ - I _ { i } \cup \{ j \} $ only ${ \mathrm { i f } } q _ { j } \geq Q _ { i }$ .

Step 4 Compute $\hat { c } _ { i } { = } c _ { i } / ( C _ { i } { - } U _ { i } ) ^ { 2 } , i { \in } I / B$ . Sort $I \backslash B$ in decreasing order of ${ \hat { c } } _ { i } ,$ calculate the current total cost based on Eq. (1) (objective function of P1).

Step 5 Reduce the number of used bins if it is feasible and economical. If the solution changes, go to Step 4 otherwise STOP.

Step 5 selects the bin that has the highest $\boldsymbol { \hat { c } _ { i } }$ value (call it bin1) and crashes (reduces the transmission rate) the time-fixed jobs that has the lowest opportunity cost so as to create enough slack capacity to fit the jobs of bin1 in other bins. This can be carried out by resolving the basic bin-packing problem exactly. However, we solved it using a heuristic that swaps the jobs among the bins so as to fit all of them.

$\boldsymbol { \hat { c } _ { i } }$ is designed to select the most expensive bins that are used the least. For example, if two bins have approximately the same per unit cost based on usage $\mathrm { ( i . e . , } c _ { i } / ( C _ { i } -$ $U _ { i } ) )$ Step 4 places the least used one earlier in the order.

## 4.1. A lower bound (Problem P2)

It is possible to obtain a lower bound for P1 by solving the following problem (P2).

Since P2 allows splitting a task and assigning it to two separate resources by relaxing the integer decision variable $y _ { i j }$ Eq. (15) and prorating the bin cost by considering used capacity of resources as in Eq. (14), P2 gives a lower bound for P1. No integer solution of P1 can have value lower than P2.

A simple algorithm that solves P2 (ignoring the QoS constraints for simplicity) can be specified as follows.

1. Set $\scriptstyle x _ { j } = { \overline { { x } } } _ { j } ^ { U }$ and sort the bins in ascending order of $\overline { { c } } _ { i } .$

2. Assign all the jobs starting with the first bin.

3. Starting with the job that has the lowest $\overline { { c } } _ { j } ^ { o }$ set $x _ { j } = \overline { { x } } _ { j } ^ { L }$ while $\overline { { c } } _ { j } ^ { o } { \leq } \mathbf { M a x } _ { i } \ \overline { { c } } _ { i }$

We use this algorithm to compute a lower bound to determine the quality of the solutions obtained by Heuristic A.

## 5. A lower bound using the generalized benders decomposition

The results of our experiments show that even though the heuristic seems to be performing well the gap between the heuristic solution and the lower bound can be improved. The likely suspect here is the lower bound since the relaxation of all integrality constraints of P1 creates a much larger feasible region. A one dimensional BPP might capture most of the important characteristics of P1. For this reason, it would be fruitful to investigate a relaxation of P1 that keeps integrality and some of the capacity constraints. In this section we present such a relaxation and develop a tighter lower bound for P1 using the Generalized Benders Decomposition method (GBD) given by Geoffrion [10]. GBD extends the results of Benders [2] to a more general problem definition. A creative implementation of GBD for a projectscheduling problem is given in Ref. [9].

We briefly describe this procedure below. Details can be found in Ref. [10].

5.1. Description of the generalized benders decomposition algorithm

Geoffrion [10] describes a procedure to solve problems of the following form:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {(NLP) Maximize f(x,y)} \\ \text {subject to G(x,y)\geq 0 ,} \\ x \in X, y \in Y \end{array}
$$

where $y$ is a vector of complicating variables because of the fact that NLP is a much easier optimization problem to solve in x when y is temporarily fixed. $G ( x , y )$ is an mvector of constraint functions.

The general idea is solving first a subproblem (SUB) in x for fixed (feasible) values of $y$ to generate dual multipliers and then to use these multipliers to setup and solve a relaxed master problem (RMP) in y. The solution of RMP is then used to setup the next SUB. The procedure continues in this fashion until the objective value of SUB (lower bound) equals (or within a tolerance) of the objective value of RMP (upper bound).

## 5.2. A lower bound formulation for P1

A lower bound for problem P1 can be obtained by considering only the capacity and QoS constraints and relaxing the time and the bandwidth availability constraints. The corresponding formulation (P3) is given below.

$$
\text { Max } z = \sum_ {i \in I} \left(- c _ {i} v _ {i} - \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} \bar {r} _ {j} ^ {U} c _ {j} ^ {o} y _ {i j} + \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} r _ {j} \alpha_ {i} c _ {j} ^ {o} y _ {i j}\right)\tag{P3}
$$

subject to

$$
\sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{S}}} \overline {{x}} _ {j} y _ {i j} + \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} \alpha_ {i} \Delta t _ {j} r _ {j} y _ {i j} \leq \alpha_ {i} \beta_ {i} L _ {i} \quad \forall i \in I
$$

$$
q _ {j} \geq Q _ {i} y _ {i j} \quad \forall i \in I, \forall j \in J
$$

$$
\sum_ {i \in I} y _ {i j} = 1, \quad \forall j \in J
$$

$$
y _ {i j} \leq v _ {i}, \forall j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}, \forall i \in I
$$

$$
r _ {j} \alpha_ {i} y _ {i j} \leq \overline {{r}} _ {j} ^ {U} \quad \forall j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}, \forall i \in I

## 5.3. Adaptation of the generalized benders decomposition to P3

GBD is a relatively complicated procedure. To keep discussion focused we only highlight the critical issues in adapting GBD to P3.

A critical step in GBD is creating the following constraint for RMP that requires solving the maximization problem:

$$
y _ {0} \leq \underset {r} {\text {Max}} \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \sum_ {i \in I} \left(- c _ {i} v _ {i} - \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} ^ {-} r _ {j} ^ {U} c _ {j} ^ {o} y _ {i j}\right) + \sum_ {i \in I} \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} r _ {j} \alpha_ {i} c _ {j} ^ {o} y _ {i j} \\ \quad + \sum_ {i \in I} \overline {{u}} _ {i} \left[ - \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{S}}} \overline {{x}} _ {j} y _ {i j} - \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} \alpha_ {i} \Delta t _ {j} r _ {j} y _ {i j} \right. \\ \quad \left. + \alpha_ {i} \beta_ {i} L _ {i} \right] + \sum_ {i \in I} \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} \overline {{\omega}} _ {i j} \left[ \overline {{r}} _ {j} ^ {U} - r _ {j} y _ {i j} \alpha_ {i} \right] \\ \quad - \sum_ {i \in I} \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} \overline {{\psi}} _ {i j} \left[ \overline {{r}} _ {j} ^ {L} y _ {i j} - r _ {j} \alpha_ {i} \right] \end{array} \right\}\tag{16}
$$

where $\overline { { u } } _ { i } , \ \overline { { w } } _ { i j } , \ \overline { { \psi } } _ { i j }$ are the dual prices computed by solving the previous sub problem (SUB).

Since some of the terms are constant with respect to $r _ { j } ,$ , Eq. (16) can be rewritten as follows.

$$
y _ {0} \leq \left( \begin{array}{c} \sum_ {i \in I} \left[ - c _ {i} v _ {i} + \bar {u} _ {i} \alpha_ {i} \beta_ {i} L _ {i} - \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} \overline {{r}} _ {j} ^ {U} c _ {j} ^ {o} y _ {i j} - \bar {u} \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{S}}} \overline {{x}} _ {j} y _ {i j} \right. \\ \left. + \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} \overline {{\omega}} _ {i j} \overline {{r}} _ {j} ^ {U} - \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} \overline {{\psi}} _ {i j} y _ {i j} \overline {{r}} _ {j} ^ {L} \right] \\ + \operatorname * {M a x} _ {r} \left\{\sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} r _ {j} \sum_ {i \in I} \alpha_ {i} y _ {i j} (c _ {j} ^ {o} - \Delta t _ {j} \overline {{u}} _ {i} - \overline {{\omega}} _ {i j}) + \alpha_ {i} \overline {{\psi}} _ {i j} \right\} \end{array} \right)\tag{17}
$$

Theorem 1 shows that the problem posed in Eq. (17) can be reduced to a simple decision rule.

Theorem 1.

$$
\text { Let   } r ^ {*} \text {   be   a   solution   to   } \max _ {r} \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} r _ {j} \sum_ {i \in I} \alpha_ {i} y _ {i j} \\ (c _ {j} ^ {o} - \Delta t _ {j} \overline {{u}} _ {i} - \overline {{\omega}} _ {i j}) + \alpha_ {i} \overline {{\psi}} _ {i j} \end{array} \right\}\tag{18}
$$

then $r ^ { * }$ satisfies:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {if} M a x _ {i} (c _ {j} ^ {o} - \Delta t _ {j} \overline {{u}} _ {i} - \overline {{\omega}} _ {i j} + \overline {{\psi}} _ {i j}) \leq 0, \forall i \Rightarrow r _ {j} ^ {*} = \overline {{r}} _ {j} ^ {L}, \forall j \\ \text {if} M a x _ {i} (c _ {j} ^ {o} - \Delta t _ {j} \overline {{u}} _ {i} - \overline {{\omega}} _ {i j} + \overline {{\psi}} _ {i j}) > 0, \exists i \Rightarrow r _ {j} ^ {*} = \overline {{r}} _ {j} ^ {U}, \forall j \end{array}
$$

Proof. Since $r _ { j } 2 0 , \alpha _ { i } { > } 0 , y _ { i j } { \geq } 0 , i { \in } I , j { \in } J ,$ and one and only one $y _ { i j } = 1$ for a fixed j, we need to consider the cost coefficients, $( c _ { j } ^ { o } - \Delta t _ { j } \overline { { u } } _ { i } - \overline { { \omega } } _ { i j } ) + \overline { { \psi } } _ { i j } ,$ one at a time based on i (rather than the sum based on an arbitrary number of ${ } ^ { 6 6 } i ^ { 9 } \mathrm { s } )$ . If one of them is positive, setting $r _ { j }$ to its maximum maximizes Eq. (18). Since each $r _ { j }$ can be determined independently we can focus on a rule for an arbitrary task j. In short, we have to maximize the following inner summation term for each $j \in A _ { \mathrm { T } }$

$$
r _ {j} \sum_ {i \in I} \alpha_ {i} y _ {i j} (c _ {j} ^ {o} - \Delta t _ {j} \overline {{u}} _ {i} - \overline {{\omega}} _ {i j} + \overline {{\psi}} _ {i j}).\tag{19}
$$

Let $( c _ { j } ^ { o } - \Delta t _ { j } \overline { { u } } _ { i } - \overline { { \omega } } _ { i j } + \overline { { \psi } } _ { i j } ) > 0$ for some i. Then Eq. (19) is maximized by setting $r _ { j } { = } \overline { { r } } _ { j } ^ { U }$ . Let $( c _ { j } ^ { o } - \Delta t _ { j } \overline { { u } } _ { i } - \overline { { \omega } } _ { i j } + \overline { { \psi } } _ { i j } ) \leq 0$ for all i. Eq. (19) is maximized only when $r _ { j } = \overline { { r } } _ { j } ^ { L } . \qquad \bigsqcup$

It is important to note that solving the maximization problem in Eq. (17) fast to setup the constraint is crucial. In Proof of Theorem 1 we were able to exploit the fact that r and (y,v) are separable, an important property the problem must have for GBD to be suitable [10]. Theorem 1 shows how to solve this problem in $O ( | I | | J )$ time that makes setting up the RMP very fast.

## 5.4. GBD procedure for P3

As outlined before GBD expects an initial solution for variables y and v. A good assignment is likely to reduce the number of iterations. For the initial assignment, we used the same procedure used to find an initial solution of Heuristic A modified to reflect that P3 is a relaxation of P1.

In general, solving RMP does not guarantee creating a feasible subproblem for the next iteration. Fortunately we can impose the following constraints on RMP (in addition to Eq. (17)) to guarantee a feasible setup for the next subproblem.

$$
q _ {j} \geq Q _ {i} y _ {i j} \quad \forall i \in I, \forall j \in J\tag{20}
$$

$$
\sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{S}}} \bar {x} _ {j} y _ {i j} + \sum_ {j \in A _ {\mathrm{T}}} \alpha_ {i} \Delta t _ {j} \bar {r} _ {j} ^ {L} y _ {i j} \leq \alpha_ {i} \beta_ {i} L _ {i} \quad \forall i \in I\tag{21}
$$

These are the quality and capacity constraints of P3. An assignment found by solving RMP including Eqs. (20) and (21) guarantees that the solution obtained in this step yields a subproblem formulation that that has a feasible solution.

## 5.5. Computational experience

We adopted the GBD procedure to develop a lower bound for P1 that is better than the one obtained by solving P2. Since Step 2 of the GBD procedure still requires solving an NP-hard problem we wanted to test how well GBD scales if we insist on solving P3 optimally. Hence, the set of experiments in this section is designed to test the running time for P3 and to find the maximum problem size that can be solved in a reasonable time. We compare the solution quality of P2 and P3 as a lower bound for P1.

The experiment revealed that when the problem size exceeds a certain level, the convergence rate of GBD drops drastically and finding an optimal solution is impractical. When the total number of tasks exceeds 15–20, and the number of tasks per bin exceeds 3–4 the convergence rate drops drastically. For example, when there are 10 bins and 50 tasks with 10 tasks per bin, it takes more than 8 h to complete a single iteration after the first several iterations. This behavior is explained by the fact that Step (2) of GBD for this problem is in effect a bin-packing problem with additional constraints on the binary variables. Due to these difficulties we have solved a set of problems that are smaller in size than those solved in Section 4.2.

Looking at the behavior of GBD on P3 we observe that it converges to a set of resources (bins) quickly and spends the rest of its time swapping tasks among these bins while recomputing the transmission rates accordingly after each iteration. Intuitively, finding the set of bins should take a short amount of time. There are more possibilities for swapping tasks especially when the transmission rates change from one iteration to the other. Depending on which tasks are assigned together in a bin, the transmission rate of a time-fixed task might change from one iteration to another just because the associated dual cost of the bin and the opportunity costs of other tasks are different.

We have created 120 problems on which we can make a comparative analysis for the quality of two lower bound procedures for P1. Table 8 reports the gap between the solution found by Heuristic A and solutions of P2 and P3 as well as how many instances were proven to be optimal by the lower bounds. P3 yields a tighter lower bound than P2 for P1. By using P3 we were able to show that Heuristic A solves 36 of the 120 problems optimally $( z _ { \mathrm { P l } } = z _ { \mathrm { P 3 } } )$ . In 10 instances, GBD was terminated prematurely but yielded the same objective function value as Heuristic A.

## 6. Summary and future research

We presented a novel formulation to solve the firm's network resource acquisition problem subject to QoS requirements and opportunity costs. We demonstrated that the task set, which is heterogeneous, presents some interesting challenges. We also suggested a mechanism to handle QoS requirements. We specifically focused on the all-you-can-send pricing where provider charges a fixed price for a specific bandwidth and duration. Experiments show how different prices, quality and task distribution affect the optimal behavior of the firm. The following results are obtained by experiments.

• The strategy of using multiple providers is optimal since choosing resources from other providers that are expensive but small reduces total cost when their utilization is high.

• The total cost decreases as the efficiency of the resource decreases. It might be economical to buy less efficient resources when the unit cost of effective bandwidth is constant.

• Due to the relative inflexibility of time-fixed tasks, total costs increase in all cases of opportunity costs when the relative percentage of time-fixed tasks increases.

Our results have an interesting implication for network providers. P1 models a cost-minimizing firm who is quality conscious. In general, such a firm should opt for solutions that trade off cost and quality efficiently, that is, pick the least expensive resource that meets quality requirements. However, every so often the capacity constraints force the solution to pick a provider that is not the lowest cost provider. An obvious and not so interesting implication is that providers who are dominated will eventually be forced out of the market if they cannot move to the efficient frontier by reducing prices or improving quality. A more interesting implication is about the portfolios offered when providers use all-you-can-send pricing. Efficient providers have to offer more selections for capacity to guarantee that they do not loose business to dominated providers.

We show that P1 is at least as hard as 2D-BPP and suggest a heuristic that can take advantage of the trade-off between quality related costs and capacity costs. We also show how GBD can be used to solve a relaxation of the original problem. Our discussion on GBD can be used as a starting point for future work that has a BPP type formulation where the size of a task varies due to trade-offs.

P1 assumes that size-fixed tasks are not penalized even if they are completed very slowly. The main reason for this is that the types of tasks we envision are batch tasks such as synchronizing databases, updating web pages when there is capacity available. A simple way to handle this additional requirement is to change #constraint (11) to impose a lower bound on transmission rates of sized-fixed tasks rather than assigning opportunity costs. Both Heuristic A and GBD procedure would need to be modified slightly but general logic of both procedures would remain the same.

Initial investigation suggests that Generalized Bender's Decomposition is a viable approach for solving a relaxation of the original problem [2,9,10] since fixing the integer variables creates linear sub problems. However, solving a bin-packing problem at each iteration is very costly (in Step 2). Since a near optimal solution in Step 2 suffices, it is worth investigating whether using a heuristic at this step improves the speed of the procedure.

From an application point of view it is also worth studying different pricing schemes suggested in literature, as some pricing schemes will change the problem characteristics quite drastically. A comparison of the firm's strategy under different pricing schemes would be a worthwhile managerial contribution.

It is possible to change problem P1 to that of an intermediary rather than of the end user (firm). The model can be modified as a profit maximization problem (with an appropriate revenue structure) for network intermediaries. In such a model, relaxing the constraint that all jobs have to be completed emphasizes the trade off between the opportunity cost and revenue. As with all capacity acquisition problems, a natural extension of this research is to study the acquisition strategies when there is uncertainty about the size and the timing of both the resources and the tasks.
