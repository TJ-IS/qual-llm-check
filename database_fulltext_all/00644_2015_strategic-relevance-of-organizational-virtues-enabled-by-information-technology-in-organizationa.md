ABSTRACT: The central theme of this paper is that information technology (IT) can serve to create ethical organizations endowed with virtuous characteristics, and that such ethical organizations can innovate better in today’s dynamic market environment. Drawing on the notion of virtue ethics propounded by the Greek philosopher Aristotle, we theorize that core organizational IT affordances influence the development of organizational virtues, which in turn influence organizational improvisational capabilities and innovation. We propose the “IT-virtues-innovation” (IVI) model and test it using a cross-organizational survey of 250 employees from various organizations in the United States. Our findings largely support our proposal that IT affordances positively influence organizational virtues, which then influence organizational improvisational capabilities, thus improving organizational innovation. This paper contributes to the understanding of organizational innovation by articulating the strategic usefulness of IT-enabled organizational ethics, and it explains how IT-enabled ethical competence (virtues) influences strategic competence (improvisational capabilities and innovation).

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: ethical organizations, information technology affordances, information technology strategy, organizational capabilities, organizational courage, organizational innovation, organizational justice, organizational memory affordance, organizational temperance, organizational virtues, organizational wisdom, process management affordance, virtue ethics.

## Information Technology and Organizational Innovation

The strategic management literature generally agrees that innovation is crucial to an organization’s performance and the maintenance of its sustainable competitive advantage [59]. Innovation is the development or implementation of new or altered products, services, or processes that create new value for a firm’s stakeholders [63]. In view of the importance of organizational innovation, a long tradition of research has focused on its determinants.

In parallel, information systems (IS) researchers have investigated whether information technology (IT) is a key determinant of organizational innovation and sustainable competitive advantage. Such research efforts have been primed by the observation that IT provides a basic foundation for better and newer organizational products and services [37]. Although a wealth of IS literature has linked IT and organizational innovation, the results are sometimes equivocal [6]. The majority of studies have found a link between IT and innovation, but crucially, others have not (e.g., [18, 38, 70]). Questions about the efficacy of IT in producing organizational innovations can also be asked given that about 90 percent of innovations fail [70].

Given that most organizations today are IT enabled, these failures call for consideration of the role of IT in enabling organizational innovation. Further, recent research has shown that IT capabilities made a significant difference in the 1990s but not in the 2000s [18]. It is no wonder that academics often struggle to explain the organizational value of IT capabilities [38].

Because of the varied findings in the literature, extensive research has focused on the relation between IT and innovation for insight into how IT can engender organizational innovation. One widely embraced perspective is that IT often affects innovation in a mediated fashion [6]. Multiple IS studies have subscribed to the notion that innovation is driven by mediating variables (e.g., [90, 116, 120]), referred to as the “mediation hypothesis” [6].<sup>1</sup> Inspired by the mediation hypothesis, IS scholars have sought to determine what variables are influenced by organizational IT and how these variables, in turn, influence organizational innovation.

Prior research shows that although many mediating factors in the IT–innovation link have been investigated—such as innovation-supportive culture [5], dynamic capabilities [103], or organizational structures [141]—no study has considered the presence of an ethical mediating factor. We contend that this is a major oversight; focusing on ethics is not really a choice for organizations, it is a necessity, because unethical behavior can destroy an organization’s reputation and brand value, as well as its ability to sustain interfirm linkages crucial to the practice of innovation [19].

Joining in the quest to identify key mediating constructs in the IT–innovation link we introduce the fundamental relevance of ethics to innovation, and argue that organizational ethics is an overlooked but important construct in this link. Ethics are relevant because they: encourage organizational innovation [136]; sustain competitive advantage [36]; improve economic performance [20]; influence individuals’ participation in innovative practices [41]; and build trust and commitment among internal and external stakeholders.

Given the importance of ethics to organizational innovation and the centrality of IT within modern organizations, we investigate the role of IT in engendering organizational ethics as an unexplored link between IT and organizational innovation. This paper introduces a previously uninvestigated set of IT-enabled ethical antecedents to organizational innovation, that is, a set of ethical characteristics or virtues an organization can possess. Drawing on Aristotelian virtue ethics, we propose that virtues influence organizational capabilities and that these organizational capabilities—which are necessary for an organization’s survival and competitive well-being [104]—ultimately contribute to an organization’s innovativeness. Further, we propose that certain IT affordances engender organizational virtues. Specifically, this study explores the following research questions:

## 1. How does IT influence organizational virtues?

2. What is the relevance of organizational virtues to organizational capabilities and innovation?

A fundamental contribution of this study is the development and testing of a theoretical model that explicitly identifies IT-engendered organizational virtues as strategic elements crucial to organizational success. In developing our “IT-virtuesinnovation” (IVI) model, we not only reinforce our arguments regarding the positive effects of fostering an ethical organization but also propose that organizational virtues influence the development of improvisational capabilities crucial to innovation. In pursuing these objectives, we answer calls in prior research for novel insights into the antecedents of organizational capabilities for innovation (e.g., [105]). We argue that IT-enabled organizational virtues are among these antecedents.

Our research makes another important contribution to the IS literature. Innovation research in IS has focused mostly on IT capabilities, but scholars have also questioned the influence of IT capabilities on organizational innovation [18, 70]. We contend that this circumspection could have arisen from an incomplete choice of antecedents, particularly in terms of capturing how organizational structures appropriate IT for organizational benefits.

We contend that IT as an exogenous variable is captured best by IT affordances. Support for this notion can be found in recent observations in the literature that distinguish IT capabilities from IT affordances, and imply that the lens of IT affordances is more powerful for understanding the role of IT in organizations [16]. IT capability is “the possibility and/or right of the user or a user community to perform a set of actions on a computational object or process” [16, p. 1084]; in contrast, IT affordances are defined as “the possibilities for goal-oriented action recognized by a specified user group” [16, p. 1084]. In fact, the same IT capability can be differently appropriated in diverse contexts due to the mutability and negotiability of goal-oriented actions, thus giving rise to different affordances in different situations, as captured succinctly below:

An actor who uses a 3-D visualization capability [an IT capability] to show the aesthetics of a building’s geometry is appropriating a different IT affordance than an actor who uses the same 3-D visualization capability to improve geometric accuracy in constructing the building. Actors can always choose to … appropriate different affordances of the same IT capabilities. [16, p. 1084]

IT affordances reflect the user’s goals and how the user appropriates the IT capability to realize those goals. Affordances can be understood as how the materiality of IT—understood as the arrangements of physical and digital aspects of IT into particular forms [77]—facilitates or constrains its various potential uses within an organizational context [162] and represent potential for “action that emerges out of the interrelationships among the technical features of a system, people’s ability and predisposition to use these features in certain ways, and the organizational context within which this takes place” [42, p. 1372; emphasis added].

IT affordances incorporate both IT capabilities and the ways in which organizations and their social structures appropriate those capabilities, potentially providing a more comprehensive explanation of organizational outcomes than just IT capabilities or other IT-related variables alone.

Such observations support our contention that organizational IT is better captured by IT affordances than IT capabilities. Our investigation of the role of IT affordances in organizational innovation, as mediated by organizational ethics and capabilities, yields a new focus in the IS literature on IT and innovation. It is also noteworthy that the definition of IT affordances also resonates with our conceptual underpinning of Aristotelian virtue ethics (which we elaborate later in the paper), where virtues are a way to achieve human goals such as excellence and flourishing.

Background Literature on Capabilities, Virtues, and Information Technology

Organizational Capabilities and Innovation

A dominant trend in the research on the determinants of organizational innovation— inspired by the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm—has been to recognize certain organizational capabilities that drive innovation, with the perspective that a firm’s capabilities are key factors in sustainable competitive advantage [59]. We define organizational capabilities as a firm’s ability to create value through the transformation of inputs into outputs [71]. According to RBV, these capabilities, especially when they are inimitable, are crucial to an organization’s competitive advantage precisely because they foster organizational innovation [104, 105]. The critical role of such capabilities in innovation has prompted many studies attempting to uncover how firms build them [105].

Recent research has endeavored to synthesize the capabilities literature to conceptualize the organizational capabilities required for organizational innovation, survival, and growth [104, 105]. Researchers have given increasing attention to one salient organizational capability—namely, improvisational capability—which is especially important to organizations in highly volatile and turbulent environments.<sup>2</sup> Improvisational capabilities enable organizations to react to market turbulence and uncertainty, in many cases without formal planning, and to engage in spontaneous action in which planning and execution often converge temporally [104]. Under some circumstances, improvisational capabilities take on increased importance for the survival and prosperity of an organization, because there may not always be the opportunity for formal planning, and improvisational capabilities enable spontaneous, innovative responses in novel situations [104]. Indeed, improvisation is a form of innovation [151]. We argue that organizational innovation requires improvisational capabilities, and we propose organizational virtues (which are themselves engendered by IT) as sources of these capabilities. Improvisational capabilities are most relevant in turbulent environments, including those associated with new technological developments [104].

Researchers have argued that improvisational capabilities allow organizations to use existing resources and capabilities to build new ones spontaneously and thus readily align the organization to the market environment [143]. For example, improvisational capabilities are crucial to developing and launching new products, because they provide an organization the wherewithal to respond to quickly changing customer and market demands [47]. Because improvisational capabilities are crucial to organizational innovation, it is worthwhile to investigate their relation to IT-enabled organizational virtues.

## Organizational Virtues

One of the key premises of our study is that virtues (as ethical capabilities) of ITenabled organizations influence improvisational capabilities, which organizations need to thrive in changing environments. Aristotelian virtue ethics is a prominent stream of thought in philosophy [19].<sup>3</sup> In Nicomachean Ethics [3], Aristotle describes certain virtues (e.g., wisdom and courage) that people must possess to be ethical. Virtues are best understood in the context of social collectives or communities and are described as qualities that, when possessed and exercised, allow people to achieve beneficial goals (e.g., flourishing, happiness) linked to the community of practice and, when lacking, prevent the realization of such goals [82]. Furthermore, virtues are not automatic attributes of such collectives (or communities of practice) but are nurtured and practiced within them, and are instrumental to their pursuit of excellence [82].

Conceptualizing organizations as communities of practice (COPs), prior research has argued that organizations can acquire such virtues [22]. Possessing these virtues enables organizations to behave as moral actors responsible for their actions. Virtues are also important for an organization’s pursuit of excellence and well-being, because they influence critical outcomes such as “profitability, productivity, innovation, quality, customer retention, and employee loyalty” [17, p. 174; emphasis added].

Research has delineated the key virtues of organizations [159]. Notable among them are four virtues: wisdom, courage, justice, and temperance (defined later); these represent the core, or cardinal, virtues of Aristotle’s ethics [33]. These virtues are described as cardinal because, according to Aristotle, all other ethical characteristics are either subsumed by or result from them. Strong agreement exists within the business ethics community that these four virtues are the core ethical characteristics of organizations and are especially applicable to business/organizational contexts [108]. An organization that consistently displays these four virtues is termed ethical [108].

## Relation Between Organizational Virtues and Organizational Capabilities

Virtues define the ethical competence of the organization [49] and are intricately related to organizational capabilities. Business ethics scholars have argued that through an enactment of their virtues, virtuous entities develop abilities and competence [39]. In particular, the Nobel laureate Amartya Sen [130] developed the “capabilities approach” to the study of collective virtues. The essence of this perspective is that a virtuous collective provides opportunities for its citizens to enhance their individual capabilities in a synergistic manner, thereby resulting in enhanced capabilities of the collective itself, which themselves result in desirable actions [8]. For example, a wise organization encourages its employees to engage in reflection and self-development; through this practice of self-development and learning, the organization acquires new capabilities to survive and prosper in volatile environments. Indeed, contemporary conceptions of virtues view them as being epistemic; that is, these virtues “guide the ways we deal with information … and acquire knowledge [to respond effectively]” [29, p. 584].

The strategic relevance of virtues lies in their ability to influence certain other capabilities [8]. For example, virtues may enhance the ability to counter organizational stress and increase an organization’s ability to respond to unexpected and challenging situations [15]. Virtues (as ethical capabilities) influence innovation capabilities that organizations need to thrive in turbulent environments. At the same time, virtues are adaptive and the same virtue can be interpreted in different ways in varying contexts (e.g., courage in the military compared with courage in business). Their adaptability makes them appropriate to changing environments both within and external to organizations, in which adaptation to change is key to developing organizational capabilities [134].

## Linking Organizational IT to COPs and Organizational Virtues

Virtues are defined within a COP, and when organizations develop virtues, they behave as COPs [91]. Organizations are often understood as COPs [40] that exhibit the structures, forms, and overall impetus characterizing COPs [142]. Given that IT is an intrinsic part of virtually every aspect of the modern organization, it is necessary to investigate the relevance of IT in sustaining such COPs and thereby to enabling organizational virtues. To do so, it is first necessary to identify the key characteristics of COPs.

One of the primary COP features identified in previous studies is learning [142]. That is, COPs are characterized by a knowledge base or knowledge domain [157]. The knowledge domain captures the collective learning within the COP that each COP member, as well as the COP as a whole, can draw upon.

Another primary characteristic of COPs is collaboration [14]. The individuals who constitute COPs exhibit certain interactive characteristics [142]; often, their interactions revolve around common concerns, problems, passions, knowledge, and expertise [157]. Collective learning is a key feature of COPs, in which knowledge is socially developed and disseminated [14]; this suggests that the development of COPs is dependent on establishing such collaborative characteristics. Effective collaborative mechanisms are crucial for engendering interactive characteristics and collective learning, and are critical to the emergence of a functioning COP [14].

The third core feature of a COP is process management. Indeed, seminal work on COPs has emphasized that knowledge-generating and -creating processes are inherent to COPs [76]. This work has conceptualized COPs in terms of fluid processes. Because organizational processes are knowledge-intensive, COPs must possess process management mechanisms to develop, share, align, explore, and exploit knowledge [119].

In summary, the existing literature has identified three primary features of organizational COPs: (1) the existence of a knowledge domain, (2) the existence of cooperation and collaboration, and (3) the management of processes. Many organizational scholars have argued that organizations, as collective entities, behave as COPs that exhibit these characteristics [156]. The key question then becomes: What enables the knowledge, collaboration, and processes that sustain an organization as a COP?

We believe the answer lies in identifying the role of organizational IT. The characteristics of IT that support these COP features could be instrumental in developing organizational virtues within COPs. A perspective that aligns itself readily with this line of reasoning is that of organizational IT affordances, which underlines the relevance of IT to organizations [162]. We thus propose that IT affordances sustain organizational COPs, thereby stimulating certain virtues that exist within COPs. Hence, we argue that IT affordances act as a causal antecedents to organizational virtues.

What affordances are relevant to the creation of organizational virtues? The answer lies in the three fundamental characteristics of COPs: knowledge, collaboration, and the management of processes. Three core IT affordances align well with these characteristics and are thus influential in developing organizational virtues. They are: (1) collaborative affordance, the IT-facilitated ability to instill cooperation within an organization, both in a collocated and distributed/virtual setting, on a one-to-one or many-to-many basis [162]; (2) organizational memory affordance, the IT-facilitated ability to create, store, transform, refine, access, mobilize, apply, and exploit organizational knowledge [65]; and (3) process management affordance, the IT-facilitated ability to design, visualize, prioritize, and monitor work processes, as well as allocate and manage appropriate resources to enable action and decision [162].

Each of these affordances sustains the corresponding COP characteristic. For example, the organizational memory affordance aligns with the existence of a knowledge domain. Although these affordances are not independent of one another—one could argue, for example, that the collaborative affordance promotes the knowledge (organizational memory) affordance—they are conceptually distinct enough to warrant scrutiny separately [162].

We expect IT affordances to play a central role in engendering organizational virtues by supporting the development and viability of COPs. Given that virtues develop within COPs, organizational virtues can emerge through the leveraging of IT in the form of the affordances IT provides. We substantiate this relationship and the rest of our theoretical model in the next section (Figure 1).

## Research Model Development

## Improvisational Capabilities and Innovation

Improvisational capabilities are most crucial when (1) an environment becomes highly turbulent, (2) past experience provides little direction, (3) formal planning cannot be leveraged, and (4) spontaneous and novel solutions are needed [104]. Improvisation is a deliberate and repeatable process that follows certain patterns of responding to novel circumstances [154]—the capability to improvise can, therefore, be enhanced with practice.

The emergency landing of the US Airways plane on the Hudson River on January 15, 2009, executed after a flock of birds disabled both engines, is an example of improvisation without formal planning [104]. These capabilities draw upon preestablished and rehearsed organizational procedures and routines to allow spontaneous reactions to novel situations [151].

Because the ability to improvise involves the ability to be spontaneous and creative, it should be an important factor of organizational innovativeness [27]. The ability to improvise properly is often pivotal to innovation, and the development of new products is strongly influenced by an organization’s improvisational capabilities [104].

Hypothesis 1: Improvisational capabilities have a positive influence on organizational innovativeness.

## Organizational Virtues and Organizational Improvisational Capabilities

Organizational wisdom

As can be inferred from our discussion above, we contend that the adaptive nature of organizational virtues helps organizations develop strategic capabilities to compete in modern, dynamic business environments. Among these virtues, the notion of organizational level wisdom has become a recent focus for organizational researchers. Organizational wisdom is “the collection, transference, and integration of individuals’ wisdom and the use of institutional and social processes (e.g., structure, culture, leadership) for strategic action” [66, p. 297]. Organizational wisdom amounts to using judgment and specific knowledge in appropriate contexts [133]. The importance of organizational wisdom lies in the ability to factor in contextual/ situational elements [112], and synthesize rational and nonrational decision making by dynamically integrating cognition, affect, and reflection [161].

An organizations’ ability to synthesize rational as well as nonrational decision making makes it more flexible than if it engaged in only one kind of decision making. This flexibility is a salient factor of improvisational capabilities. Improvisational capabilities involve the ability to address environmental urgency, unpredictability, and novelty [104]. Wisdom facilitates the ability of an organization to flexibly sustain itself in an environment of sudden change, competition, and uncertainty [53].

Flexibility enabled by organizational wisdom is key to the development of an organization’s capability to “think and act wisely” [53, p. 82] and to dynamically shape and reshape its identity. This is the essence of organizational improvisation: the simultaneous deployment of contemplation and action [28]. Further, organizational wisdom is relevant to an organization’s ability to appropriate its resources in a manner that allows it to deal proactively with sudden environmental changes [96].

Scholars have argued that wise agents can readily identify an ambiguous situation and develop a way of navigating through it, resulting in an ability to handle unexpected situations [132]. Although the enactment of improvisation can be sudden, the ability to improvise builds upon years of perception and experience [154]. Given that wisdom blends rationality, cognition, and emotion and uses prior experience, agents can respond to suddenly arising situations by drawing upon their previous experience [132].

Finally, organizational wisdom is vital to an organization’s capability to adapt its identity within a short time span [13]. Given that organizational improvisation involves simultaneous contemplation and action [28], we assert that wisdom influences an organization’s ability to improvise and deal with sudden environmental changes [96]. To summarize, the capability to improvise follows from organizational wisdom [23].

Hypothesis 2: Organizational wisdom has a positive influence on improvisational capabilities.

## Organizational courage

The virtue of courage often involves accepting challenges and risking failure to achieve organizational goals [67]. Researchers have maintained that courage can be exhibited by a collective entity [113]. Collective courage is socially constructed in organizations and arises from activities, discourses, relationships, and narratives capturing organizational life [52].

Courage is crucial to organizational innovation; it forms the basis of innovative efforts (specifically, efforts that involve the risk of financial loss) that support organizational success [72]. Courage engenders improvisational capabilities because it signifies the ability to proceed in spite of danger and the risk of failure [72].

Improvisation requires risk taking and experimentation, resulting in extemporization without formal planning [104]. Risk taking involves an implicit acknowledgment of the possibility of failure [64]; thus, an organization must possess courage in order to proceed in light of risk of failure. Given that courage is the willingness to act in the face of probable risk and failure [158] and given that organizational improvisation is characterized by such possibilities, courage is an important factor of an organization’s ability to contemplate and enact improvisation.

Furthermore, courage can involve the seizing of sudden opportunities. Lack of courage can lead to excessive cautiousness and a reluctance to take risks [104], having a negative effect on the ability to improvise. Namely, courage enables organizations to make spontaneous changes (i.e., to improvise) to improve their efficiency and effectiveness [123].

Hypothesis 3: Organizational courage has a positive influence on improvisational capabilities.

## Organizational justice

Aristotle’s conception of justice as a virtue involves mainly two forms: distributive and corrective [24]. Of these, only distributive justice is relevant to our context, because corrective justice deals mainly with legal matters, such as remedying the loss suffered by a plaintiff at the hands of a defendant [110]. Hence, our study focuses on distributive justice, both in theory development and operationalization.

Distributive justice enables trust [146], a key component of social capital [93]. Social capital represents the resources an individual or social unit can possess by virtue of a network of relationships [93]. Arguably, social capital enables the performance of tasks necessary to the development and practice of improvisational capabilities, such as management of the acquisition, integration, recombination, and release of organizational tasks and resources [12], because it helps organizations comprehend both the social context [125] and social structures that are critical to the optimal allocation of tasks and resources [64]. The improvisational capabilities of organizations are rooted in social capital, because organizational improvisation is a collaborative creation dependent on relational trust, shared goals, shared responsibilities, and a common outlook incorporating the relational aspects of a social system [151]. Perceptions of justice are also known to foster an environment of change and experimentation, which are key drivers of improvisational capabilities [34].

Improvisation at the collective level involves sharing a common mind incorporating relational aspects of a social system [151], thus building social capital [156]. As articulated in [11, p. 5], “human agency is also inherent in discussions of improvisation, which portray actors deviating creatively from plans.” In a collective context (e.g., organization, group), sensemaking, coordination, and integration in a spontaneous manner—the key characteristics of improvisation [104]—cannot be achieved without the existence of collective social capital. Hence, organizational justice, by enabling social capital within organizations, also influences improvisational abilities.

Another element of organizational justice crucial to improvisation is the ability of an organization to rebalance itself. Rebalancing is critical to continued improvisation, because it “unblocks” organizational resistance to change by engaging with organizational justice [155]. Arguably, rebalancing is achieved by actively engaging in, and promoting, organizational justice.

Hypothesis 4: Organizational (distributive) justice has a positive influence on improvisational capabilities.

## Organizational temperance

Building upon prior work [36], we define organizational temperance as an organization’s ability to maintain restraint and to refrain from excessively emphasizing any singular objective (e.g., allocating most of its resources toward innovation at the cost of daily operational efficiency). The key principle underlying temperance is moderation or balance, which refers to the ability to avoid extreme actions/reactions [33], especially in situations of organizational change.

Organizations must develop self-control (e.g., temperance) to be successful [44]. Temperance enables organizations to respond to uncertain and unpredictable environments [31]. Organizations characterized by temperance strike a careful balance in actions such as decision making, the allocation of resources, and balancing continuity and change [115]. Too much change (in terms of both extent and frequency) creates chaos in organizations, whereas too little change inevitably creates organizational inertia [58].

A typical situation in which temperance becomes relevant is radical organizational change [57]—a situation often associated with organizational improvisation [106]. A critical aspect of managing radical change is the capability of an organization to retain an element of stability while implementing the change [58], that is, the capability to balance change and continuity. This balance is necessary because radical changes trigger emotional upheavals that may encourage resistance to organizational change [58]. To improvise in these contexts, it is important to avoid extreme actions/reactions (i.e., to practice temperance) [83]. An organization characterized by temperance thus possesses the ability to improvise effectively by avoiding anxiety, panic, and despair, which can lead to chaos [58].

The well-known jazz metaphor for organizational improvisation substantiates the importance of organizational temperance to improvisational capabilities. In jazz improvisation, the amount of adjustment must be optimal: although it deviates from a strict pattern to incorporate novelty [144], improvisation should still play out in a harmonious manner by balancing consonance and dissonance to create an ultimate harmony [83]. Likewise, in organizational improvisation, temperance achieves this goal by striking an optimal balance between rapid change and stability [58].

Hypothesis 5: Organizational temperance has a positive influence on improvisational capabilities.

## IT Affordances and Organizational Virtues

## Collaborative affordance

Collaborative IT affordance refers to how IT allows individuals to work together and share, convey, and integrate each other’s knowledge, often through virtual, ITsupported media [162]. Collaboration can take the form of virtual teams, online electronic networks of practice, or other new patterns of collaboration; it can also be embodied as many-to-many collaboration (e.g., via the Internet) or one-to-many collaboration (e.g., through list servers) [162].

Collaborative affordance can promote dialogue in virtual and distributed settings in which members have not met in person. Such organizational dialogue, enabled by IT, “serves to socialize individuals to a set of values and norms or engender collegial and collaborative relationships” [69, p. 377; emphasis added]. Organizations engage in coordinated conversation, and such dialogical collaborative endeavors are important in developing collective thinking and wisdom, because organizational wisdom is engendered through collective and interactive processes [161]. Wisdom evolves through a dialogical process among organizational members that integrates multiple perspectives [4].

As Surowiecki’s [139] classic work on the wisdom of crowds argues, wisdom in a collective is achieved through the aggregation and creation of information across such a collective. Collaboration within a COP generates collective wisdom [43]. Employees can use collaborative affordance to help others make wiser judgments [84].

IT allows organizational members to review ideas in a collaborative manner and weed out dysfunctional ideas, providing for enrichment and further development of new ideas during cumulative knowledge generation [35]. By supporting cooperation and mutuality among members, the affordance ensures convergence of ideas [94], which is crucial to collective wisdom. In this context, the concept of convergence clarifies the importance of the collaborative affordance. The IT-enabled collaborative affordance provides a platform to “converge” separate user experiences, leading to synthesis and further insights [160]. This affordance creates a sociotechnical ecosystem, which co-opts different organizational stakeholders and builds upon their diverse experiences to cumulatively synthesize interactional learning to engender collective wisdom [10].

A precondition for the generation of collective wisdom (e.g., organizational wisdom) is a diverse body of individuals whose nuanced experiences enrich the wisdom of the collectivity [152], because a group’s intellect is often superior to that of an individual [140]. “There may be wise individuals in the organization, but the organization does not become wise unless individuals’ wisdom is articulated and transferred to others” [9, p. 609]. This exchange can be accomplished through IT-enabled collaboration, which supports the creation of organizational wisdom. Organizations are increasingly investing in collaboration technologies because they allow for the leveraging of individual intellectual resources, which ultimately embeds collective wisdom in the organizational fabric [85].

Hypothesis 6: The collaborative affordance in organizations has a positive influence on organizational wisdom.

The IT affordance of collaboration also influences courage, because the interconnection among employees has a widespread effect on organizational members [67]. For example, tales of courage can influence others to act similarly [48]. We argue that such tales diffuse through organizations partially because individuals engage in IT-enabled interactions and collaboration, especially in distributed and virtual settings. Courage within organizations is often a function of the subjective norms of referent others [127]. In a virtual or distributed setting, knowledge of such norms can be gathered only through the interactions that IT-enabled collaboration provides. Courageous organizations are those where a higher frequency of courageous acts are both performed and observed [67]. The observation of such courageous acts is supported by the collaboration afforded by IT.

The practice of courage requires employee socialization [123]. In a distributed setting, the collaborative platforms provided by IT are an important support for this socialization. Organizational virtues emerge through social interactions [128], in which individuals observe others and align their conceptions of courage, so that the collective becomes a synergistically courageous entity [51]. Courageous individuals, through social interactions, shape the courageous identity of an organization [72]. In general, organizational employees cooperatively engage in a sensemaking process to foster development of a courageous collectivity [72]. Arguably, this sensemaking process is triggered by a contagion effect, where courageous exemplars are observed and reflected upon [50].

How does technology support such social identification and collective sensemaking, especially in a distributed and virtual setting? The answer lies in the collaborative affordance provided by organizational IT. For example, Paul and Morris [101] show how a collaborative tool enhanced collective sensemaking. Collaboration technologies, if properly designed and appropriated, afford collective reflection and thus enhance collaborative sensemaking, which is crucial to solving organizational problems, often by challenging and modifying beliefs and work practices [111].

Perhaps the most significant example of the ability of IT to instill collective courage is the recent political uprisings in Egypt and the Middle East [145]. In these events, a significant portion of the country’s population was united as a collective entity, using social media to communicate and collectively act, to courageously protest against oppressive regimes. Specifically, whether in a political or organizational context, organizational IT, including but not limited to social media, allows individuals to observe and coordinate resilient action—thereby instilling courage in the collective [145].

Hypothesis 7: The collaborative affordance in organizations has a positive influence on organizational courage.

Collaborative affordance is also related to organizational justice. Successful collaboration implies higher perceptions of justice [26]. Organizational justice is a shared perception; unless this perception is shared across the organization, it is hard to create a collective view of organizational justice. This implies that for justice perceptions to arise within a collective entity, there must be a collaborative sharing of goals, perceptions, and information. Organizational members who interact regularly have similar perceptions of justice [147].

Research has investigated the influence of organizational structure on how justice is enacted and perceived in an organization [124]. Individuals engage in social comparisons to referent others to generate perceptions of distributive justice [68]. Such social comparisons are supported by the collaborative affordance of IT.

Employees often collaborate through telecommunications; however, organizations frequently engage in monitoring [87]. Although this is an encroachment upon autonomy, it has been argued that organizational monitoring can lead to improved distributive justice perceptions. When an individual is subjected to increased monitoring, the individual’s performance is more apparent to the supervisor, and the individual therefore expects that the subsequent distribution of rewards will be in accordance with this observed performance [95].

Related arguments substantiate the link between the collaborative affordance and organizational justice. As Chatterjee et al. [20, p. 146] note, IT-enabled collaboration requires accountability on the part of the collaborators; specifically, while collaborating, “all participants should be accountable for the activities they undertake and inputs they provide in the course of the collaboration.” This creates expectations that contributions will be suitably rewarded, raising perceptions of distributive justice.

This affordance enables justice due to the increased closeness and collaboration it provides [75] and its ability to increase monitoring, visibility, and accountability in an organizational context. We conclude that the collaborative affordance of IT, by allowing interactions that promote collaborative sharing, influences the systematic implementation and perception of organizational justice.

Hypothesis 8: The collaborative affordance in organizations has a positive influence on organizational justice.

## Organizational memory affordance

Organizational memory affordance refers to the ability of IT to create, store, transform, refine, access, mobilize, apply, and exploit organizational knowledge [65]. The ability of IT to promote the creation and implementation of knowledge structures codified within an organizational memory plays an important role in the realization and active use of the organizational memory affordance [65]. This affordance supports the acquisition, retaining, maintaining, and retrieval of knowledge codified in IT systems [137]. Examples include asset management systems, content management systems, and databases and data warehouses.

The value of organizational memory in organizations cannot be underestimated. In this digital age, heterogeneous knowledge resources need to be integrated within an organizational repository so that new knowledge is generated [160]. IT-enabled organizational memory supports social processes related to the articulation, exchange, and information sharing to promote shared interpretations [137], which are crucial to developing organizational wisdom.

Organizational wisdom combines knowledge and expertise to benefit the organization [135]. There are two important characteristics of wisdom: (1) it is based on observation, and (2) it is informed by experience [121]. Prior observations and experiences (giving rise to wisdom) are held in organizational memory [153], which “is the means by which knowledge is stored for future use” [56, p. 90]. Organizational memory is instrumental in organizational decision making because it serves as a repository that helps organizations make intelligent decisions [55]. If an organization does not create, mobilize, share, refine, or store organizational knowledge, then it cannot use such knowledge to foster organizational wisdom.

To make wise decisions, top management must leverage organizational memory [150]. When organizational members, especially senior managers, collectively leverage knowledge embedded in organizational IT, better and wiser decisions emerge, and the organization as a whole gains wisdom [32]. Wise leaders are crucial to the creation of wise organizations [62]; therefore, the organizational memory affordance that enables wise decision making contributes to the store of organizational wisdom. Organizational memory triggers conceptual processing of organizational leaders that invoke it. Meaningful inferences are made and then applied, both by organizational leaders and employees [164], giving rise to collective enlightenment on how to better run the organization.

The organizational memory affordance of IT allows past knowledge to be integrated and applied by organizations, resulting in increased organizational wisdom [137]. Further, this affordance captures “who knows what and who knows whom,” thereby assimilating individual knowledge structures into organizational structures to form a collective, homogenized “metaknowledge” [78, p. 797]. This affordance allows for the development and integration of organizational “explicit knowledge, which codifies organizational wisdom” [100, p. 124].

Hypothesis 9: The organizational memory affordance has a positive influence on organizational wisdom.

Organizational justice is often related to fairness observations within organizations [73, 80]. The practice and perception of justice are also influenced by how organizational events and their resolutions are stored in the organizational memory and then recollected at a later time [118]. The storage of information about past organizational events leads to a promotion of organizational justice. For example, when past events relate to organizational injustice, organizational memory helps to prevent the same mistakes [118]. Individuals retain prior events in the organizational memory (e.g., an employee might save a critical e-mail for future reference). resulting in important implications for how people behave and perceive situations at a later point in time [129].

From the perspective of virtue ethics, organizations are composed of the three main characteristics of organizational memory [2]: (1) communities where the relations between the members are supported by organizational memory, (2) containing a narrative character, and (3) engaging in a system of reciprocity. The preservation of organizational memory and an awareness that the organization has historically engaged with its employees in a fair manner helps the organization to build and maintain a positive identity and culture [2]. Organizational memory allows an organization to envision a “perfect” future on the basis of the conversations and rhetoric that have historically permeated the organization, which are important resources to justify involvement in organizational knowledge creation [97, p. 1188].

The existence of organizational memory is viewed as a positive characteristic by the organization and its employees. An organization that promotes the development of relationships among its employees is likely to engage in all forms of organizational justice, including distributive justice. If individuals feel that they do not receive just rewards, then dissent and rivalry will arise, and the opportunity to develop interpersonal relationships will be lost.

The affordance of IT in developing and preserving organizational memory becomes crucial in the context of this discussion. IT-based organizational memory incorporates organizational rules and procedures [98], which include those that enact organizational justice. Beyond such rules and procedures, organizational memory should help in capturing prior situations (e.g., previous e-mails, recorded conversations, or even electronic documents), a process that creates a reference point for engagement in organizational justice. For example, a top manager could refer to prior situations and decide on a future course of action, and this should be justifiable to the employees reporting to that manager. In essence, event stored in organizational memory act as reference points for current perceptions of justice, because individuals try to connect/relate their situation(s) to organizational precedents, thereby arriving at their perceptions of justice [118].

Hypothesis 10: The organizational memory affordance has a positive influence on organizational justice.

## Process management affordance

We define process management affordance as the allowance provided by IT to design, prioritize, coordinate, implement, and monitor work processes that enable action and decision making that lead to desired process outcomes [162]. IT enables process management by empowering organizations to assign and coordinate individuals and tasks to effectively achieve desired goals [61]. IT-enabled process management systems—such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems and workflow automation—remain a crucial management focus because they enable the modeling, automating, and optimizing of organizational processes, which in turn support better decision making [126].

Managing organizational processes is a multifaceted endeavor [162]. For example, this affordance allows organizations “to visualize the entire process in action from ‘end to end,’ representing it through language, imagery, or physical artifacts to make decisions about next steps when alternative actions can be taken” [162, p. 753]. Supported by technological developments such as business process management tools, this affordance helps provide real-time work status, allowing organizations to proactively optimize and balance work processes; this affordance is key to identifying and preventing problems [162].

The process management affordance also enables visualization of process interdependencies, thus allowing optimal allocation of resources [162]. This affordance also provides the ability to recombine and configure processes for optimal sizing and decision making [117]. IT-enabled organizational processes have increased agility, in turn allowing organizations to proactively balance the acquisition, deployment, combination, and reconfiguration of their resources [81].

Given that organizations often face resource constraints (e.g., lack of labor, expertise, and funds) they can use IT to engage in a “bricolage” process, where they balance and optimize combinations of existing resources, coupling technology with organizational routines and networks [138]. For example, customer relationship management (CRM) technologies can be used to improve cross-functional balance by leveraging different functional areas in an appropriate and optimized manner [21]. IT thus plays an important role in managing and implementing business processes and is key to coordinating, balancing, and optimizing such processes to address the immediate needs of the organization [162].

We assert that this affordance has relevance in optimizing organizations and their processes, and that it influences organizational temperance in many ways. For example, this affordance helps organizations provide real-time work status, identify process interdependencies, and develop optimized or balanced processes, which are essential for organizational temperance [62]. Finally, it regulates change by controlling an organization’s constituents, such as tools, resources, roles, and tasks [162], thus avoiding over- or underreaction (i.e., to be temperate) [86].

In the context of workflow management systems, the virtue of balancing (i.e., temperance) is crucial. As noted earlier, organizational processes benefit from balancing competing demands and resources [114]. Workflow management systems often help maintain this balance. For example, they can help organizations balance quality and performance by dynamically weighing push-and-pull approaches [74].

Hypothesis 11: The process management affordance in organizations has a positive influence on organizational temperance.

## Methodology

## Research Setting and Data Collection

The data were collected via an online survey. The instrument was piloted and refined in two stages by sixty-six professionals with substantial work experience. Following the instrument development and refinement process, the data were collected by contracting the services of a third-party, online market research company. This method of data collection has been established as an effective way of gathering professional responses across many that can lead to greater generalizability and anonymity and stronger sampling than approaching organizations directly [109, 148]. The contracted sample size was 250,<sup>4</sup> data were collected from November to December 2012.

The participants were business professionals in the United States with at least five years of work experience and were compensated by the online market research company for completing the survey. Due to the ubiquitous nature of IT in the organizational workplace, it was expected that every respondent had some knowledge of the relevance of IT to their organization’s work. An open-ended item intended to capture the participants’ concerns and comments failed to identify any participant concerns related to lack of knowledge about IT or what role IT played in their organization.

The survey was designed to increase the generalizability of our theory by collecting data across various industries, organizations, and employee levels. Tables B1 and B2 in Appendix B summarize the individual and organizational demographics, respectively. Details regarding the measurement instrument are presented in Appendix A.

## Testing for Common Method Bias

Researchers have critiqued the prior recommendations of the Harmon’s one-factor approach to test for common method bias (CMB) and have suggested two more advanced approaches [107], one of which is referred to as the marker-variable approach. We carried out this test by including a nonrelated construct that is subject to social desirability bias (specifically, propensity to trust) and examined whether this construct significantly affected the dependent variable. Given no theoretical relevance between this construct (propensity to trust) and the innovativeness of the organization, we would expect no relationship between the two constructs, supporting a lack of CMB. Our analysis revealed that the relationship was indeed insignificant $( \beta = 0 . 0 5 7 3 ;$ std. $\mathrm { e r r o r } = 0 . 0 7 9 4 ; z = 0 . 8 6 ; p = 0 . 3 9 0 )$ . In addition, Pavlou [102] note that if a study does not suffer from highly correlated constructs $( \mathrm { e . g . , \ } > \ . 9 0 )$ , it cannot suffer from CMB. None of our constructs exhibited that level of correlation with other constructs, reinforcing our confidence that CMB was not present in our study. The lack of CMB was expected because we used techniques such as randomized items, using different kinds of scaling, attention traps, and so on, to reduce it.

## Post Hoc Mediation Analysis

Although our primary focus was to test the hypotheses, our model also implicitly recognizes the literature that argues for a mediation effect of IT on organizational capabilities and innovation. In view of this research, we felt it necessary to empirically analyze the mediating effect of the organizational virtues in our model.

The results of the mediation analysis are shown in Appendix C. There is either a full or a partial mediating effect for every affordance-virtue-capabilities set that was theorized in the paper (organizational justice was excluded because it did not influence the improvisational capabilities; therefore, the mediation test is unnecessary). This increases the contribution of the research, because it positions organizational ethicality as an important mediating variable in the IT–innovation link.

## Discussion

## Discussion of Results

Within the context of our U.S. sample, improvisational capabilities overall exerted a very strong influence on organizational innovativeness. Although the U.S. economy is developed and relatively mature, the recent economic crisis has created a more turbulent environment. Recalling that improvisational capabilities become more salient in turbulent environments, we contend that the strong effect of improvisational capabilities may be attributed to the recent environmental turbulence experienced by our survey respondents.

Second, among the four virtues, wisdom had the strongest effect on improvisational capabilities. This was expected, because organizational wisdom is purported to be a critical answer to the challenges faced by modern organizations. For example, researchers have argued that wisdom is key to a successful merger and acquisition process (often an uncertain and tumultuous situation) because it ensures familiarity with the cultures, structure, and interfirm linkages of the acquired company [7].

Third, courage also had significant effects on improvisational capabilities. Courage involves the seizing of spontaneous opportunities, which is critical to improvisation, particularly in turbulent environments. Given the environmental turbulence during the recent recession, the relevance of organizational courage has arguably grown.

Fourth, an interesting (and unexpected) finding was that organizational justice had a significant but negative effect on improvisational capabilities. One potential explanation is that the application of distributive justice is costly (in terms of time and effort), and this challenge obviously increases in quickly changing or even turbulent situations. Another possible explanation is based on the argument that distributive justice may be related to personal outcomes (e.g., job satisfaction) as opposed to organizational ones (e.g., organizational capabilities) [88].We contend that this could also be an artifact of our sample; the reader may refer to the endnote here, which elaborates this contention.

Organizational temperance had a positive and significant influence on improvisational capabilities, suggesting that the virtue of temperance—not giving in to excesses and maintaining balance and stability in the midst of organizational change—is appreciated in an organizational context. An organization characterized by temperance conducts a delicate balancing act in terms of decision making and the allocation of resources, that is, pursuing innovation while engaging in operational efficiency.

There is a strong effect of the collaborative affordance in the IVI (IT-virtuesinnovation) model. Organizations are often conceptualized as collaborative entities exhibiting ubiquitous IT-enabled collaboration. It is notable that “among the various ICTs, collaborative technologies have been most extensively used” [163, p. 557; emphasis added]. Our results are consistent with observations on the preeminence of IT-based collaboration in modern organizations.

The influence of the process management affordance is consistent with our IVI model. IT-enabled organizational memory affordance has a stronger effect on organizational wisdom than does collaborative affordance. Organizational wisdom is often stored in organizational memory enabled by IT (such as databases) [46]; therefore, this strong influence of the organizational memory affordance on wisdom is expected.

Post hoc mediation results provide additional insights. Organizational virtues play an important mediating role in the link between IT affordances and improvisational capabilities. Collaborative affordance affects improvisational capabilities via a full mediation of wisdom and courage. Organizational memory affordance affects improvisational capabilities via a full mediation of wisdom. Finally, process management affordance affects improvisational capabilities via a partial mediation of temperance.

These mediation results indicate that improvisational capabilities are mostly affected by a full mediation mechanism of organizational virtues, with wisdom and courage being particularly salient in this regard. This is not surprising—wisdom becomes critical in turbulent environments because it allows the quick application of knowledge held in organizational systems [122]. Further, wisdom is inherently an ethical and therefore social construct; hence, a nonhuman entity (i.e., a computer system) cannot be wise [122]. This means that although IT affordances cannot be wise, they can engender wisdom, which is essential to handling turbulent environments through improvisational capabilities.

Courage, likewise, has an important role in the context of organizational improvisation. Notably, “organizations are systems with established routines and control mechanisms designed to maintain the status quo … courage is needed to interrupt routines, thereby improving organizational functioning” [72, p. 65]. Courage enables organizations to make spontaneous changes to improve their efficiency and the effectiveness of their processes. Although IT systems may allow organizations to carry out operations/activities, they can lead to improvisation only if they engender the courage necessary to carry out risky endeavors such as improvisation.

Finally, organizational temperance acts as a partial mediator of the effect of the process management affordance on organizational improvisation capabilities. IT-enabled organizational process management becomes crucial to improvisation when it also engenders temperance. Processes, if not tempered, may be wasteful and foster redundancy, which is detrimental to improvisation; indeed, unwasteful (i.e., tempered) processes are crucial to organizational improvisation. Hence, process management needs to be suitably tempered to make improvisation possible.

## Contributions to Research and Theory

This study was motivated by two main considerations. The first was devoted to understanding the IT-innovation link by positioning organizational virtues as an important concept in this link. The second was to explain the contrasting results between IT variables and organizational innovation by proposing a new focus on IT affordances rather than other variables.

The first major contribution of this study is that it introduces organizational ethics as a lens for IT-enabled innovation research. By systematically investigating the mediating role of organizational virtues in the IT–innovation link, we provide a new ethical perspective on organizational strategy, stemming ultimately from its appropriation of IT. The quest for mediating variables in the IT–innovation link continues, and our research contributes to this perspective by proposing a set of organizational ethical characteristics (virtues) that are key mediators in this link. There have been calls for the inclusion of ethics within IS research; our paper also contributes on this front. The importance of ethicality for innovation and competitive advantage is relatively new in IS research. Beyond unearthing a potentially uninvestigated set of mediating variables in the IT-innovation link, our study provides impetus for this emerging ethical perspective in IS research. Introducing ethical theory to the organizational innovation literature allows for the expansion of the theoretical base of innovation research and also showcases the relevance of virtue ethics to modern businesses.

An associated contribution of this study is the use of virtue ethics as a theoretical lens that may offer better insights into organizations than commonly used constructs, such as organizational cultures or climates. Organizational cultures are essentially based on values, but do not necessarily have a strong normative component. Likewise, organizational climates are rooted in organizational values, but they essentially describe organizational environments with respect to a “[specific] situation and its link to thoughts, feelings, and behaviors of organizational members” [30, p. 644]. Virtues are not organizational cultures or climates—they enable more general, insightful, adaptable, and malleable understandings of organizations.

The second contribution of this study is that it identifies and substantiates a means by which IT ultimately affects innovation. In light of the skepticism expressed by IS scholars regarding the effects of IT on innovation, it is imperative that we gain a better understanding of whether and how IT affects innovation. Given that extant studies in this research stream have focused mostly on IT capabilities as the key antecedent (and found contrasting results), we propose the use of IT affordances, which may better capture how IT is related to innovation. Consistent with recent developments in IS research, we find that affordances represent a better way to capture the role of IT and are strongly linked to innovation.

Our contribution is bolstered by the fact that business ethics scholars have argued that virtue ethics represents a promising and relevant lens for understanding organizational ethicality. Extant literature has relied mainly on deontology and utilitarianism to analyze ethical issues in organizations; the state of affairs in IS literature is no better, with few research articles mentioning virtue ethics. Consequently, there have been calls to explicate the notion of virtue ethics and its relevance and implications for organizations. This study addresses this call by exploring the relationship between IT in organizations and organizational virtues and investigating how these virtues influence the organizational capabilities that ultimately influence innovation.

## Contributions to Practice

Our theoretical framework has significant implications for practice. First, our framework allows organizations to appreciate the importance of cultivating virtues, and understand how to implement and appropriate organizational elements properly (e.g., by building appropriate organizational structures to support these virtues) to achieve that objective. The significant and positive influence demonstrated between organizational virtues and improvisational capabilities (and in turn organizational innovativeness) provides an additional incentive to organizations to strategize about developing virtuous capabilities. Realizing virtuous communities of practice within an organization has associated benefits. For example, virtues such as wisdom and temperance can guard against questionable reactions to challenging times, for example, engaging in extreme layoffs in response to an economic crisis. Also, organizations may market their virtues strategically to promote a positive brand image to achieve long-term positive outcomes in terms of consumer relations.

Our theoretical framework also provides direction on how to reconfigure IT capabilities to engender organizational virtues. First, our model suggests that IT capabilities should be considered not in absolute terms, but rather in terms of affordances, which include the idea of effective appropriation of those capabilities. In other words, it is important for organizations to effectively harness their IT infrastructure, and not just have an IT infrastructure. We believe an intention to develop organizational virtues provides a useful focus for harnessing organizational IT effectively. Second, the significant relationships found between IT affordances and organizational virtues suggest that configuring IT resources and mobilizing tactics for their effective appropriation (not an unusual aim for an organization) are essential for engendering key organizational virtues. In particular, our model emphasizes the importance of the affordances of IT, suggesting that preferential investment in these aspects of IT (e.g., those that support process management) may be desirable. Third, the affirmation of the mediating role of organizational virtue between organizational IT and improvisational capabilities provides a further point of emphasis and allows organizations to leverage their IT infrastructure to create virtuous organizations that can be strategically potent.

Finally, the salience of organizational virtues in organizations suggests that it is important for organizational leadership to focus on both developing and maintaining organizational virtues. Because leaders influence the ethicality of organizations, they should cultivate virtues and guard against the vices that threaten such virtues.

## Limitations and Future Research Opportunities

Like any other study, ours has potential limitations. One is the use of a crosssectional survey, which can present concerns regarding CMB, as well as difficulties in determining causality. Empirically, we tested for CMB and found no issues. However, cross-sectional surveys do have other advantages; for example, they allow researchers to collect empirical evidence from a broad range of practitioners and thus to increase generalizability or investigate relationships between many variables. Given the nature of this study and the meta-level focus on ethics, affordances, and organizational capabilities, a survey can be considered an appropriate design. However, because no one empirical method is perfect, future research should employ other techniques, such as qualitative methods.

Another constraint of the study is that it was tested in the U.S. context. It would be illuminating to test this model across different countries and cultures beyond the United States— especially in developing and emerging economies in Asia and those in Europe and Latin America. Even within a single country, one could test this model in different companies such as traditional, hierarchical companies and more open, horizontally structured companies.

One final potential limitation is that while executing the organizational survey, we did not specifically require a certain level of IT experience for the employees. This was driven by our intent to capture a robust sample of organizational IT users; we did not want to limit our sample based on a specific amount of IT experience. We assumed that because IT is ubiquitous in today’s organizations, it would be unlikely to find an employee with five years’ experience (our requirement for the survey) who has not used IT in any form or who is completely ignorant about how IT is used in the organization. An open-ended survey item captured and supported this assumption. Nonetheless, we believe this issue could potentially be viewed as a limitation, and future research should be designed to address it.

Future research can be designed to go beyond our cross-sectional approach to engage in a longitudinal evaluation of our model, with a possible feedback loop from innovation to IT affordances. Recall that organizational virtues influence innovation through organizational capabilities. Therefore, it could be determined whether virtues at Time 1 will result in newer/modified IT affordances at Time 2, thereby demonstrating how virtues influence organizational IT in a longitudinal fashion. This theorization, of course, would require new hypotheses as well as longitudinal data collection, which could be a part of future research initiatives. A second way to investigate this phenomenon would be to conduct a qualitative study, perhaps using a structuration lens, where the mutual influence between IT structures and organizational ethical structures (virtues) is investigated.

## Conclusion

In this study, we have articulated a new, previously unexplored IT-enabled ethical perspective on organizational innovation. We have shown that an organization’s improvisational capabilities to innovate are strongly influenced by the ethical nature of the organization, which in turn is engendered by the affordances provided by IT. Therefore, this study reinforces the premise that ethicality leads to business success. This premise will likely become an important consideration as the business world grapples with corporate scandals and economic downturns and may provide a blueprint for helping organizations conduct themselves as successful, yet ethically responsible, entities.

## NOTES

1. For example, researchers have noted that IT investments are strongly associated with organizational structures that drive innovation rates [141]. In addition, the studies of Pavlou and colleagues cited in this paper support the mediation hypotheses: in these studies, the effect of IT leveraging capability on competitive advantage in new product development (a typical example of innovation) while mediated by organization capabilities (e.g., improvisational capabilities/dynamic capabilities) enhance innovation. Elsewhere, it has been shown that organizational capabilities are relevant to service innovation [1], whereas IT affects such organizational capabilities [131]. It can be reasonably inferred that the notion that IT affects innovation in a mediated manner is substantiated by prior IS research. Finally, Benitez-Amado et al. [5] argue that IT resources help build an innovation-supportive culture, thereby implicitly arguing for the mediating effect of IT on organizational innovation.

2. In this paper, we focus on improvisational capabilities. Organizations also have dynamic capabilities [103], but these are beyond the scope of this paper.

3. The other two prominent ethical theories in philosophy are deontology and utilitarianism.

4. Based on an expected small effect size, we calculated that the minimum sample size needed to test this model is 201; thus, a sample of 250 would provide an adequate amount of power to test these relationships overall.

5. This result could be an artifact of our study sample. There is reason to contend that the hypothesis linking justice to improvisational capabilities might have tested differently in other countries/cultures. For example, one could theorize that in Chinese cultures, the effect of distributive justice on organizational improvisation would be more pronounced, due to the guanxi that has been embedded in Chinese culture for more than 5,000 years [99]. Guanxi is an “intricate and pervasive relational network that contains implicit mutual obligations, assurances, and understanding” [99, p. 455]. Because guanxi is a construct that “guides the ascription of credit to people” [92, p. 788], it is inherently distributive in nature, and the influence of distributive justice on improvisation would be more pronounced in its presence. In other words, the existence of guanxi implies that societal arrangements are already distributive and relationally oriented [79] and therefore improvising based on this already existing distributive and relational network is easier. In the United States, where such relational networks are not systematically ingrained in the culture, the effects of distributive justice on an organization’s improvisational capabilities may be quite different, as our negative effect shows. We call for future research to investigate this link further.

## Appendix C. Mediation Analysis

Testing for Mediation of Organizational Virtues in the Model Using Advanced Bootstrapping Analysis<sup>6</sup>

In order to find out whether organizational virtues truly mediate the influences of IT affordances on organizational capabilities, we conducted an extended mediation analysis as a post hoc test. Specifically, we used bootstrapping to construct confidence intervals of the mediation effects.

The usual tests for mediation have been the Baron and Kenny and Sobel tests. However, with more computing power available to researchers, other methods have become more prevalent. The bootstrapping method represents one such approach that has gained in popularity in recent times and has just been introduced in IS research and we refer the reader here for more details on this method [149].

We followed the procedures outlined in [149] to bootstrap the effects of our three IT affordances on each of the organizational virtues with respect to the dynamic/ improvisational capabilities. The results are shown below.
