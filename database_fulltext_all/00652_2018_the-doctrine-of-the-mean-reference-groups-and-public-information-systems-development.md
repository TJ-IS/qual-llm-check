# The doctrine of the mean: Reference groups and public information systems development

Kui Du<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Ye Dai<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Management, University of Massachusetts Boston, 100 Morrissey Blvd., Boston, MA 02125, USA

<sup>b</sup> College of Business, Southern Illinois University Carbondale, 1025 Lincoln Drive, Carbondale, IL 62901, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Electronic government Reference group Prospect theory Institutional theory Public sector

## A B S T R A C T

This paper studies the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of reference groups on developing public information systems. It hypothesizes that municipal governments dynamically adjust the development of their public information systems towards the average levels of their reference groups, a behavior embodying the classic doctrine of the mean in Confucianism. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, both trait-based and institutionbased reference groups can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the e-Government development of municipal governments, and superordinate provincial governments enhance the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of institution-based reference groups but dampen the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of trait-based ones. Analyses based on the development of municipal government web portals in China between 2009 and 2013 provide support for these hypotheses.

## 1. Introduction

Researchers of information systems have been long interested in understanding organizations decisions regarding information systems development (e.g., Dewan et al., 1998; Kobelsky et al., 2008; Mithas et al., 2013; Salge et al., 2015). Many scholars pursuing this line of inquiry found that such decisions were not merely of the free will of the decision-making organizations, but also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their institutional environments (e.g., Gosain, 2004; Newman and Sabherwal, 1996; Teo et al., 2003; Wang, 2010). Despite these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the <sup>fi</sup>eld has not adequately explored topics about the interaction between information systems development and institutional environments (Mignerat and Rivard. 2009: Orlikowski and Barley. 2001). One such topic is the development of information systems in the public sector (Pang et al., 2014a). Although researchers found that organizations in the public sector often succumbed to institutional influences when developing public information systems (Jun and Weare. 2011: Zheng et al., 2013). these researchers have not examined adequately the dynamics in which organizations adjust their information system development in response to institutional environments.

To address this research gap, our study examines how reference groups in<sup>fl</sup>uence governments’ development of their electronic government systems (“e-Government” hereafter). A reference group is a group of selected peer organizations with which a focal organization associates itself, or to which a focal organization compares itself, so that the perspectives, decisions, behaviors, or outcomes of these peers in<sup>fl</sup>uence those of the focal government (Shibutani, 1955). Previous studies have examined the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of reference groups in strategic decision making (e.g., Fiegenbaum and Thomas, 1995; Massini et al., 2005). This research builds on these studies and strives to make the following three contributions.

First, this study clari<sup>fi</sup>es the directionality of peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the public sector. Institutional theorists recognize the existence of mutual directionality of peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence. However, they focus almost all on a unilateral peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence that superior performers in<sup>fl</sup>uence inferior ones, or the leading adopters of innovations in<sup>fl</sup>uence laggards (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Haveman, 1993). The neglect of in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the opposite direction may have blinded researchers from exploring the countervailing forces in peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that, in developing their e-Government systems, government organizations appear to move towards a moderate leve of development. That is, while government organizations lagging in their e-Government development try to catch up, leading ones tend to slow down their e-Government development to avoid overly deviating from the average of their reference groups. The countervailing forces that arise from the reference groups to slow down the e-Government development of the leading organizations appear to be consistent with a classic Confucian doctrine, the doctrine of the mean, which suggests that pursuing only a moderate leve of achievement helps an individual maintain balance and harmony, and thus direct his or her mind to a state of constant equilibrium (Watson, 2007). By revealing the intricate dynamics of peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence, this study contributes to the studies of institutional environments and information systems development.

Second, this study explores how government organizations utilize reference groups in making decisions. Peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence occurs only if peers are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place. Therefore, identifying who constitute reference groups is crucial to understanding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of peers on information system development. Our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that, in deciding their e<sup>f</sup>ort for e-Government development, government organizations appear to rely on at least two di<sup>f</sup>erent reference groups, the selection of which is based on prospect theoretic thinking (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) and institutional conformity (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983) respectively. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings deepen our understanding about the functioning of organizational reference groups.

The last issue that this study addresses is the dearth of research on public information systems. It remains largely unknown whether existing theories and <sup>fi</sup>ndings, including those from extant institutional-IT studies (e.g., Newman and Sabherwal, 1996; Teo et al., 2003; Wang, 2010), are applicable to government organizations that face unique challenges in developing and using information systems (Caudle et al., 1991; Cordella and Iannacci, 2010; Rocheleau and Wu, 2002). The practical importance of public information systems to modern society has urged greater research attention to the under-explored topic of public information systems development (Huang, 2007; Irani et al., 2007; Pang et al., 2014a). In responding to this call, this study joins forces with a few previous studies (e.g., Jun and Weare, 2011; Zheng et al., 2013) to extend the inquiry of institutional-IT problems to the public sector.

## 2. Literature background

## 2.1. Information systems development in the public sector

Despite their commonalities, the development of information systems in the public and private sectors di<sup>f</sup>ers from each other in important ways. Organizations in the public sector face unique problems, such as the pressure of public scrutiny, bureaucratic and political hurdles, and shorter budgeting cycles. Therefore, the development of public information systems tends to be incremental, gradual, and short-term oriented. It also requires more extensive testing and higher levels of accountability, openness, and representativeness (Bozeman and Bretschneider, 1986; Caudle et al., 1991). The objectives of information systems development in the public sector are broader, which include, besides e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness, democracy, compliance, accountability, and responsiveness (Ahn and Bretschneider, 2011: Huang, 2007: Justice et al., 2006: Li et al., 2017). Moreover, political leaders are not involved in the strategic planning of information systems as much as business executives are (Dufner et al., 2002), and even this limited involvement is often interfered by political agendas and party a<sup>fi</sup>liations (Ahn and Bretschneider, 2011; McNeal et al., 2003; Pang, 2017). Consequently, organizations in the public sector tend to have a higher degree of risk aversion and prefer incrementalism when developing public information systems.<sup>2</sup>

As a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of public information system, e-Government is de<sup>fi</sup>ned broadly as any IT-based systems that facilitate governments’ delivery of their services to, and interaction with, the citizens and businesses that they serve (Heeks and Bailur, 2007; Irani et al., 2007; Yildiz, 2007). The past decades witnessed tremendous progress in e-Government services, as its speci<sup>fi</sup>c forms evolved from static, informational webpages to integrated, transactional web-based service platforms (Yildiz, 2007).

However, researchers have described the development of e-Government as a constant battle between rapid technological advancement and conservative legislative and administrative processes (Strejcek and Theil, 2003). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, on the one hand, emerging information technologies provide opportunities to improve the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, transparency, and accessibility of governmental services. On the other hand, the risks of information system failures instill a sense of pessimism to e-Government initiatives. Public o<sup>fi</sup>cials have been depicted as “recalcitrant, suspicious, and skeptical adopters of IT” (Gold<sup>fi</sup>nch, 2007, pp. 917),

Various risks contribute to the reluctance to develop e-Government. Financially, advanced e-Government systems, such as those involving online transactions for public services, require signi<sup>fi</sup>cant upfront investment and subsequent expenditures on maintenance. However, returns on these investments often cannot be easily quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed and measured, especially in the short term, which challenges the planning and budgeting processes. In addition, governments face shorter budget cycles and more rigid budget controls (Bozeman and Bretschneider, 1986; Caudle et al., 1991). Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>nancing a major information systems project can drain <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources that cannot be replenished quickly. The lack of immediate, justi<sup>fi</sup>able returns on the investment also exposes the decision makers to political risks.

Operationally and technologically, implementing and upgrading e-Government systems demands a higher level of IT capabilities, which many government organizations do not possess. Researchers have attributed system failures and IT service outages of e-Government systems to the lack of IT capabilities in governments (Lee, 2008). Meanwhile, technical risks that could severely destruct government IT infrastructure, such as the risk of cyber intrusion into government websites, have been increasing (Halchin, 2004). Protecting sensitive data from malicious attacks and Internet espionage is another major concern when a government migrates its public services online (Zhao and Zhao, 2010).

With all these risks, governments are especially cautious about their decisions of e-Government development. They need to decide an optimal level of e-Government development based on the assessment of a set of economic, social, and political factors, and such a decision is not immediately obvious. Consequently, e-Government development becomes particularly susceptible to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of peer organizations (Ho and Ni, 2004; Jun and Weare, 2011).

## 2.2. Peer influence on public information systems development

Peer organizations play a major role in shaping the decisions of public-sector organizations (e.g., Ashworth et al., 2009; Berry and Berry, 1990; Tolbert et al., 2008). When public-sector organizations face risky decisions such as whether to adopt a new practice, they often <sup>fi</sup>rst search for clues from their institutional environments. Particularly, they tend to anchor their own choices around the decisions and outcomes of their peer organizations. In fact, empirical evidence has shown that, compared with private and non-profit organizations, public-sector organizations are more susceptible to mimetic pressure from their peer organizations (Frumkin and Galaskiewicz, 2004). Two factors might explain this susceptibility. The <sup>fi</sup>rst factor is bureaucratic inertia, contributing to the frequent adoption of incrementalism in public-sector organizations (Frumkin and Galaskiewicz, 2004). This adoption inclines public-sector organizations to avoid disruptive innovations and make only minor deviations from their own practices or their peers’ prevailing practices. The second factor is the lack of objectivity in evaluating the performance of public-sector organizations. While the performance of private-sector organizations can often be measured and compared based on objective metrics such as revenue, pro<sup>fi</sup>t, or stock price, the performance of public-sector organizations, containing more dimensions, are more di<sup>fi</sup>cult to quantify (Frumkin and Galaskiewicz, 2004). Further, negative outcomes are more conspicuous than positive outcomes, leading to conservatism in publicsector organizations—they prefer avoiding risk rather than improving outcomes (Bozeman and Kingsley, 1998). Following the choices of peer organizations can help an organization avoid high pro<sup>fi</sup>le failures and thus often becomes a preferred option.

The above discussion about peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence may apply to decisions regarding the development of public information systems. However, empirical studies of peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence on public information systems development are sporadic and provide mixed results. Jun and Weare (2011) found that the adoption of e-Government services in one municipality was positively in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the adoption decisions of its same-state peer municipalities. Ho and Ni (2004), however, failed to <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence that the adoption of e-Government websites in one county was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the adoption decisions of its neighboring counties. Similarly, Zheng et al. (2013) did not <sup>fi</sup>nd support for the hypothesis that mimetic pressure from peer organizations would ultimately lead to the adoption of inter-government information systems. These mixed results suggest that the actual mechanisms of peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence on public information systems development may be more complicated than previously assumed, and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence may be contingent on additional factors.

## 3. Theory development

For e-Government development, there are two types of potential gains concerning a government: gains from economic e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and gains from legitimacy. Concerns for economic e<sup>fi</sup>ciency motivate governments to pursue the substantive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that an e-Government system can produce, such as the reduced costs of service provision. Thus the governments will try to pinpoint the optimal level of e-Government development that can yield the highest risk-adjusted substantive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Concerns for legitimacy gains motivate governments to conform to social expectations and standards. The governments will then try to make su<sup>fi</sup>cient progress in e-Government development to meet the expectations of their critical stakeholders in institutional environments. Below we argue that these two concerns lead governments to consider two reference groups in their decision process.

## 3.1. Trait-based reference groups in e-Government development

We <sup>fi</sup>rst build on prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) to study e-Government development from the perspective of economic decision making.

The classic expected-utility theory in economic decision-making predicts that, when facing trade-o<sup>f</sup>s between risks and returns, a rational decision-maker will pursue the option that can maximize its expected return. Prospect theory challenges this prediction. It instead proposes that decision-making is reference dependent: decision-makers assess the expected utility of a risky decision based on the return’s relative position to some reference points rather than the absolute value of the return (Barberis, 2013). Moreover, the same decision-maker can have di<sup>f</sup>erent risk preferences, depending on his or her position relative to a reference point (Barberis, 2013; Levy, 1992). In particular, the decision-maker tends to be risk seeking in the domain of a “loss” (i.e., a negative deviation from the reference point) and risk averse in the domain of a “gain” (a positive deviation from the reference point). As numerous experimental studies con<sup>fi</sup>rmed these claims (Barberis, 2013), the theory has evolved into a general theory of economic decision making beyond its original application in individual settings. Scholars have applied the theory to decision making at the organizational level, such as <sup>fi</sup>rms’ strategic choices (e.g., Fiegenbaum and Thomas, 1988; Shimizu, 2007) and governments’ politica decisions (e.g., Levy, 1997; Mercer, 2005) and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed its validity in these settings.

A critical question in applying prospect theory is how decision-making organizations determine their reference points (Fiegenbaum and Thomas, 1988). Prospect theory itself focuses on the generic patterns of decision-making under risk, but it does not specify how reference points are selected (Tversky and Kahneman, 1986).<sup>3</sup> Filling this void, the behavioral theory of the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Cyert and March, 1992) suggests that a decision-making organization can rely on its historic performance, its aspirational performance, or its referent organizations’ historic performance as reference points in decision making (Blettner et al., 2015; Gavetti et al., 2012; Shinkle, 2012). In particular, facing either high competitive pressures or great uncertainty from the environments, organizations are inclined to focus more on external sources to create their reference points (Blettner et al., 2015; Hu et al., 2017; Shoham and Fiegenbaum, 2002). In doing so, an organization often selects peer organizations to form a reference group and uses the average status of the group as its reference point in making decisions (Blettner et al., 2015; Fiegenbaum and Thomas, 1988, 1995).

Using the average status of an external reference group as the reference point provides two major bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for decision making. First, when a decision-making organization uses a reference group for self-appraisal, comparing its own status to the average of the external group is a convenient approach to assess the overall situation and evaluate its own status relative to the group’s (Fiegenbaum and Thomas, 1988; Shibutani, 1955). Second, the average status of the reference group members is likely to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the “wisdom of the crowd.” Reference group members are not necessarily all perfect rational decision-makers: they could be overly risk taking or risk averse, for example. Even if the decision-making organization can pinpoint the best performers, without su<sup>fi</sup>cient inside information, it may still not be able to identify factors contributing to the best performance or to assess well whether the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed factors are relevant to the decision-making organization (Shoham and Fiegenbaum, 2002). Some reference group members could make wrong decisions. However, the average status of the group often re<sup>fl</sup>ects a choice that may not be optimal, but is nevertheless satis<sup>fi</sup>cing, in dealing with the common situation faced by all the group members

Further, organizations tend to select other organizations that share similar traits as their referents for self-appraisal, thus forming a trait-based reference group. Among business <sup>fi</sup>rms, such traits might be <sup>fi</sup>rm locations (e.g.., Massini et al., 2005), industry af-<sup>fi</sup>liation (e.g., Fiegenbaum and Thomas, 1988; Massini et al., 2005), or strategic group membership (e.g., Fiegenbaum and Thomas, 1995). For example, Frecka and Lee (1983) found that, using same-industry peers as a reference group, <sup>fi</sup>rms adjusted various <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios towards their industry averages. For another instance, Fiegenbaum and Thomas (1988) reported that <sup>fi</sup>rms became more risk taking when their performance was below the industry average but became more risk averse when their performance was above it.

The rationale of using trait-based reference groups is that <sup>fi</sup>rms sharing similar traits are more likely to face similar problems and constraints, and therefore the choices of trait-based referents are of higher referential values to the decision-making organization. Di<sup>f</sup>erent from receiving explicit knowledge from various sources, an organization using reference groups in its decision making incurs an increased risk of superstitious learning. That is, the organization may infer a plausible causal relationship between the observed behaviors and outcomes of other organizations but the relationship may not necessarily hold (Levitt and March, 1988). Such superstitious learning is more likely to occur when the learning organization does not have a chance to verify with the learning target about the implications and lessons derived from the observations (Gioia and Manz, 1985: Zollo. 2009). Thus, to make better sense of the observations and reduce superstitious learning, a rational organization is inclined to use organizations sharing common or comparable traits (e.g., size, industry a<sup>fi</sup>liation, etc.) as their learning targets (Baum et al., 2000; Haunschild and Miner, 1997).

In making decisions concerning e-Government development, municipal governments may follow similar patterns as discussed above. E-Government solutions have been evolving rapidly, and most municipal governments have only limited histories of running e-Government systems. Thus we argue that a municipal government will resort to external reference groups and use the average e-Government development levels of the groups as its own reference points. The candidates for these reference groups, as argued earlier, are comparable governments that share common traits pertinent to e-Government development, such as population size and internal resources. Developing e-Government systems exposes a municipal government to a considerable amount of <sup>fi</sup>nancial, political and technical risks. Thus, as prospect theory predicts, when a municipal government perceives itself as lagging behind its reference point, it tends to be risk seeking and tries to catch up in the next period by accelerating the development of its e-Government systems. In contrast, when a municipal government perceives itself as exceeding the reference point, it tends to be risk averse and tries to avoid excessive risks by slowing down or even holding back its subsequent e-Government development. Overall, the e-Government development of a municipal government will gradually move towards the average level of its trait-based reference group. In summary, we hypothesize the following relationship:

H1. A municipal government will adjust its e-Government development level towards the average e-Government development level of its comparable governments that share common traits (i.e., trait-based reference group). The more this government deviates from the average in the previous year, the more adjustment it will make in the following year.

## 3.2. Institution-based reference groups in e-Government development

We then build on institutional theory (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983) to study e-Government development decisions from the

perspective of institutional conformity and legitimacy.

Organizations always operate in institutional environments that consist of rules and regulations, standards, norms, taken-forgranted practice, and commonly shared values, beliefs, de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, and mentalities among various agents (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Scott, 2001). Institutional theory argues that only a few standards, forms, structures, and practices are deemed legitimate in an institutional environment. Organizations in this environment thus face a set of coercive, normative, and mimetic forces to adopt and adhere to only these legitimate standards, forms, structures, and practices (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Scott, 2001). Failure to do so will result in legitimacy loss and increased di<sup>fi</sup>culties in establishing exchange relationships with other key constituencies of the institutional environment (Singh et al., 1986; Suchman, 1995).

A legitimacy-pursuing organization is subject to the evaluation and judgement made by legitimacy providers, or observers that have the power to assess the conformity of the organization’s behavior to socially constructed standards (Barreto and Baden-Fuller, 2006, pp. 1561). Legitimacy providers categorize organizations into groups. They then compare and evaluate the organizations choices and actions against the institutionalized standards and norms of the groups to which the organizations belong. Based on the results of comparison and evaluation, legitimacy providers decide whether to grant legitimacy to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c organization (Barreto and Baden-Fuller, 2006). Therefore, a legitimacy-pursuing organization cannot choose its reference group completely of its own free will: the organization is supposed to compare its own choices and actions to those of peer organizations used by its legitimacy providers as evaluation benchmarks. In other words, the focal organization succumbs to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of an institution-based reference group. The choice of this reference group is largely determined by legitimacy providers who are authoritative, possess high status in the socia system, or simply control critical resources needed by the focal organization.

We argue that pursuing legitimacy will prompt a focal municipal government to choose its peer municipal governments located in the same province as referents. Both formal administrative structures and social conventions contribute to this tendency. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, <sup>fi</sup>rst, in many countries and regions, a provincial government, as the immediate superordinate of its subordinate municipal governments, possesses legislative and administrative power to evaluate the performance of the municipal governments. Additionally, the provincial government may adopt the e-Government development of its municipal subordinates as an evaluation metric for their performance (Chen et al., 2009). In such a case, the provincial government assumes the role of legitimacy provider and compares and ranks its subordinate municipal governments. This forces each of the subordinate municipal governments to use their peer municipa governments in the same province as a reference group.

Second, local businesses and residents, as another group of legitimacy providers, also play an important role in determining a municipal government’s choice of peers for a reference group. Local residents and businesses pay taxes and fees that are often the single most important revenue source of most municipal governments. In addition, legislation and democratic processes can also hold municipal governments responsible for making decisions considered appropriate by the local residents and businesses. Thus, these entities serve as another group of legitimacy providers for municipal governments. This group also tends to group municipal governments in the same province together for comparison and evaluation for two reasons. First, cities within the same province are more likely to appear together in historical records, formal administrative and legal documents, local media reports, folk tales, and even daily conversations. Thus, local businesses and residents often group these cities and their municipal governments together in making sense of information related to any of these cities and their governments. Second, population moves more frequently among cities within the same province, and more social ties exist among residents and businesses of these cities. Thus, local residents and businesses may <sup>fi</sup>nd it more meaningful and convenient to conduct within-province comparison and evaluation (Ho and Ni, 2004). Therefore, to align with the preferences and practices of its legitimacy providers, a municipal government may also use its same province peer municipal governments as an institution-based reference group.

By identifying peer municipal governments in the same province as referents, the focal municipal government forms an institution-based reference group. This municipal government relies on this reference group to assess the conformity of its e-Government development to the institutional expectations. Insu<sup>fi</sup>cient e-Government development increases the risk of legitimacy loss, but an excessive level of development also represents a deviation from the institutional norm. Because over-investment in e-Government may expose the municipal government to extra <sup>fi</sup>nancial, political, and technical risks, legitimacy providers may question whether the government makes the best use of its discretional resources and may not approve the excessive level of e-Government development. Thus, once the e-Government development of a government passes the threshold in convincing legitimacy providers about its conformity, additional investment will not yield proportionate legitimacy gains. Under this circumstance, a rational decision for a municipal government is to maintain its e-Government development at the average level of its institution-based reference group. When its e-Government development lags behind the group average, to avoid legitimacy loss, the municipal government will make radical investments in e-Government systems to catch up with its referents in the next period. When its e-Government development exceeds the group average, to avoid exposure to excessive risks not compensated by additional legitimacy gains, the municipal government will slow down or hold back its e-Government development in the next period. Based on these arguments, we hypothesize the following relationship:

H2. A municipal government will adjust its e-Government development level towards the average e-Government development level of its peer municipal governments in the same province (i.e., institution-based reference group). The more this government deviates from the average in the previous year, the more adjustment it will make in the following year.

## 3.3. The moderating efects of superordinate organizations

The bureaucratic structure of government organizations has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the development of public information systems (Ahn, 2011; Lee, 2008). Unlike private-sector organizations mainly responsible for shareholders, most local governments are held at least partly accountable to their superordinate governments in the political hierarchy. Superordinate governments usually serve a dual role of both providers of institutional and administrative infrastructure and providers of legitimacy. We argue below that the two roles can either dampen or enhance the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the two reference groups.

## 3.3.1. Superordinate organizations and trait-based reference groups

As a provider of institutional and administrative infrastructure, superordinate organizations establish and maintain legislative processes, organizational structure and processes, the delegation of decision rights, critical personnel policies, internal governance and control mechanisms, and the division of labor across the hierarchy of governments, etc. (Tolbert et al., 2008).

The degree of e-Government development often re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree to which the institutional and administrative infrastructure of a government are favorable towards e-Government development (Fountain, 2001). Previous studies identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several key elements of institutional and administrative infrastructure that facilitates e-Government development, such as legislative committees dedicated to e-Government development, administrators with IT backgrounds, autonomous and competent IT departments, and routinized IT management and operation procedures (Tolbert et al., 2008). The maturity of such infrastructure further relies on the development of more fundamental institutional and administrative infrastructure in favor of changes, reforms, and innovation adoptions (Tolbert et al., 2008). Governments with mature institutional and administrative infrastructure can more easily achieve high levels of e-Government development. By contrast, when the institutional and administrative infrastructure of a government is underdeveloped, its e-Government systems are also more likely to stay underdeveloped. For example, when a government is divided (e.g., when di<sup>f</sup>erent parties control its legislative and the executive branches), its initiatives of public information systems development will face greater legislative and administrative hurdles, and thus the public information systems are likely to be underdeveloped (Pang, 2017). For another instance, a government with a conservative ideology is more likely to avoid signi<sup>fi</sup>cant investments in public information systems (Pang, 2017). Following the reasoning, we argue that the e-Government development of a provincial government re<sup>fl</sup>ects the maturity of its underlying institutional and administrative infrastructure that facilitates the development. We further argue below that a superordinate provincial government with such infrastructure can substitute trait-based reference groups in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the e Government development of a subordinate municipal government

The substitution e<sup>f</sup>ect occurs to compensate for the limitations of trait-based reference groups. As discussed earlier, using peer municipal governments to form trait-based reference groups may lead to superstitious learning. To mitigate the risks of superstitious learning, the decision-making government needs to seek information and decision support from other sources to guide their e-Government development. A superordinate government with advanced e-Government systems can leverage its underlying institutional and administrative infrastructure to help in two ways.

First, a municipal government can resort to the same institutional and administrative infrastructure that enables the e-Government development of its provincial superordinate government. Although governments at di<sup>f</sup>erent hierarchical levels face di<sup>f</sup>erent demands for and constraints on e-Government system development, they usually adopt similar legislative and administrative processes. To help its subordinates make better decisions concerning e-Government development, a superordinate provincia government can utilize its own decision-making processes and information processing capabilities to guide the decision making of the subordinate municipal governments. Even if the superordinate government does not provide direct support, the same institutional and administrative infrastructure maintained by the superordinate government can still regulate and guide the decision-making processes of the subordinate governments. For example, a municipal government often inherits the same budgeting procedures from its superordinate government. If the superordinate government creates e<sup>f</sup>ective budgeting procedures for e-Government development, its subordinates can adopt the same procedures from the superordinate government and make better e-Government development decisions on their own. Thus, bene<sup>fi</sup>ting from the institutional and administrative infrastructure of the superordinate provincial government, subordinate municipal governments can avoid their over-reliance on observing comparable peers in inferring an adequate level of e-Government development.

Second, a superordinate government with advanced institutional and administrative infrastructure for e-Government can learn more e<sup>f</sup>ectively from other governments and transfer the acquired knowledge to its subordinate governments. This transfer of learning reduces the necessity of the subordinate governments to learn by themselves. The cost incurred in searching for and collecting information from nation-wide comparable peers could be daunting for municipal governments, as they are usually resource constrained and their search for information and solutions tends to be myopic. For example, a perfectly comparable peer government may exist but it is geographically and politically distant from the focal municipal government. Identifving this peer government and collecting its information could be cost prohibitive for the municipal government. By contrast, it could be relatively easy for superordinate governments to leverage its institutional and administrative infrastructure to conduct national search for such referents and collect information from them. For example, a dedicated IT committee and/or an IT department of a provincial government can constantly scan the e-Government landscape nation-wide for e-Government information of other governments, including those comparable peers of a subordinate municipal government, and relay the information and its interpretation to the subordinate government. This will signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce the burden of the subordinate government to monitor these peers by themselves. Thus, a superordinate government that has achieved a high degree of e-Government development can reduce its subordinates’ direct use of trait-based reference groups for decision guidance. We thus hypothesize the following relationship:

H3a. A superordinate government’s e-Government development level dampens the influence the trait-based reference group on the foca municipal government’s e-Government development.

## 3.3.2. Superordinate organizations and Institution-based reference groups

As discussed, a superordinate government also serves as a legitimacy provider for its subordinate governments. It in<sup>fl</sup>uences the e-Government development of its subordinates by serving as a judge for their conformity. In ful<sup>fi</sup>lling this role, a superordinate provincial government compares its subordinates against each other based on certain legitimacy-conferring factors. Some of the factors are explicit, such as GDP (Gross Domestic Product) or employment rates, but others often remain a partial mystery to subordinate municipal governments. In other words, a municipal government needs to compare themselves with its institution-based reference group along a set of metrics, part of which is unobvious to the municipal government itself. The municipal government thus needs to seek clues about the preferences and criteria of its superordinate provincial government by studying the decisions and choices of the provincial government.

If a provincial government achieves a high level of provincial e-Government development, its subordinate municipal governments will interpret it as a signal that the superordinate organization pays particular attention to e-Government development among many other potential legitimacy-conferring factors. They may infer that they are likely to be compared against each other based on their e-Government development. This consideration motivates the municipal government to increase its focus on the institution-based reference group to make sure it does not lag behind the group in e-Government development. This practice embodies the famous adage by Mencius, an ancient Chinese Confucian philosopher: “What the superior loves, his inferiors will be found to love exceedingly.”(Legge, 2006, pp. 48). By contrast, the insu<sup>fi</sup>cient e-Government development of a superordinate provincial government may reduce the perceived importance of e-Government in conferring legitimacy among its subordinate municipal governments. As the perceived importance decreases, the municipal governments will have increased discretion over their own e-Government development. This increased discretion allows them to focus more on the idiosyncrasies that they face and reduces their tendency to closely monitor peers in their institution-based reference groups. With the above arguments, we hypothesize the following relationship:

H3b. A superordinate government’s e-Government development level enhances the influence of institution-based reference group on the focal municipal government’s e-Government development.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Research context and sample

We tested our hypotheses in the context of e-Government development practice in China from 2009 to 2013.<sup>4</sup> The population of our study consists of all prefectural-level cities and their municipal governments in mainland China.<sup>5</sup>

Our primary data source is the annual assessment of the content and the functionality of Chinese governmental web portals,<sup>6</sup> conducted by China Software Testing Center (CSTC), a research institute a<sup>fi</sup>liated with the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) of China. CSTC annually publishes their assessment results that cover the government web portals of all centra government ministries, provinces, and prefectural-level cities, as well as selected county-level cities. This study focuses on the assessments of the web portals of prefectural-level cities. Our initial dataset consists of 334 Chinese prefectural-level cities in a <sup>fi</sup>ve-year time window from 2009 through 2013.<sup>7</sup> The assessment methodologies and results are publicly available on the website of CSTC and brie<sup>fl</sup>y summarized in Appendix A.

We matched the CSTC assessment data with the demographic and economic data of Chinese cities, collected from the Chinese City Statistical Yearbooks compiled annually by the National Bureau of Statistics of China. The number of cities in our sample was reduced to 261 after this matching process for two reasons. First, the Chinese City Statistical Yearbooks did not cover cities located in the <sup>fi</sup>ve autonomous regions in China. Second, the yearbooks had missing data for certain control variables for some cities. In addition, as explained in our model speci<sup>fi</sup>cation below, we used the di<sup>f</sup>erence between the assessment results in two consecutive years for some measurements and thus excluded the observations of the <sup>fi</sup>rst year 2009 from data analyses. Our <sup>fi</sup>nal sample consisted of 927 observations from 261 cities in a four-year time window spanning from 2010 to 2013. It is an unbalanced panel dataset because a few new cities were upgraded to the prefectural level during our studied timeframe and a few others were not covered by the CSTC

assessment in some of the years.

## 4.2. Measurement

## 4.2.1. E-Government development of municipal governments

As mentioned, we used the CSTC assessment scores to measure a municipal government’s e-Government development among Chinese cities. Each year, CSTC inspectors assess and assign numeric scores to governmental web portals based on a set of predetermined metrics, as summarized in Appendix A. Based on these scores, CSTC computed a composite e-Government score for each governmental web portal. The same set of evaluation metrics was applied to all municipal governments in the same year, although the metrics changed over years.

Our theories concern the relative positions of organizations in terms of their e-Government development. Therefore, instead of using the absolute score reported by CSTC, we computed a relative e-Government score as our measurement for the degree of a municipal government’s e-Government development in each year and used it in our statistical analyses. Using relative scores ensures cross-year comparability even with the change of the evaluation metrics over years. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, for each year’s observations, we normalized the CSTC-reported annual e-Government scores across all the cities. Thus, for example, a municipal government scoring 1 (or −1) in a year indicates one standard deviation above (or below) the average of the e-Government development of all the Chinese municipal governments in this year. Changes in the e-Government development scores of a government over years re<sup>fl</sup>ect the changes in its relative positions among all municipal governments in China over the same time periods.

## 4.2.2. E-Government development of trait-based reference groups

We constructed trait-based reference groups based on the similarity of cities in two traits: population size and revenue. The population of a city represents the demand base of its governmental services. The revenue of a city, including its tax income and administrative charges, serves as a proxy for the city’s resource abundance that a<sup>f</sup>ects municipal government’s discretional investments. For each city in our sample, we constructed its trait-based reference group by searching nationwide for other cities whose population and revenue were each in the 80–120% ranges of those of the focal city in the same year. In constructing this reference group, we excluded the cities located in the same province of the focal city.<sup>8</sup> Each city in our <sup>fi</sup>nal sample was successfully matched to at least one comparable peer in each year in the study timeframe. The number of nationwide comparable peers ranges from 1 to 48, with an average of 11. We then used the mean of these peers’ CSTC-reported e-Government scores as the average e-Government development of this reference group.

## 4.2.3. E-Government development of institution-based reference groups

We constructed the institution-based reference group of a focal city by including all other prefectural-level cities under the same administrative supervision of a provincial government. In our dataset, the number of same-province peers ranges from 1 to 20, with an average of 13. The mean of these peers’ CSTC-reported e-Government scores was used as the average of the e-Government development of this reference group. The focal city was excluded in computing the average.

## 4.2.4. E-Government development of superordinate provincial governments

We used the CSTC-reported e-Government score of a provincial government as the measure of the level of its e-Government development. Similar to the e-Government measure of municipal governments, the e-Government development levels of provincia governments were also normalized across all provincial governments each year. Thus, a provincial government scoring 1 (or −1) in a year indicates one standard deviation above (or below) the average of the e-Government development levels of all the provincial governments in this year.

## 4.2.5. Control variables

To rule out alternative explanations, our estimation models controlled for variables for several city- and reference-group-level characteristics.

The city-level control variables included those capturing characteristics associated with the demand for e-Government services and resources that could be used to develop e-Government practices. First, our model included population size because previous studies found that population size a<sup>f</sup>ected e-Government adoption decisions (Ahn, 2011; Moon, 2002; Norris and Moon, 2005). Second, we controlled for the internet penetration level of each city, measured as the number of internet subscribers divided by the population, because the demand for e-Government services was based on the availability of the Internet. Last, previous studies found that the number of educated people in a city was positively associated with the likelihood of the city’s adoption of e-Government practices (Tolbert et al., 2008). Thus our model also included the number of higher education institutions in a city as a proxy for the overall educational level of the residents in the city.

The development of an e-Government practice is also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by resources controlled by the government (Moon, 2002). Previous studies reported that a city with abundant <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources is likely to invest more in its e-Government web portal (e.g.,

Moon, 2002; Tolbert et al., 2008). Thus, the average wage and the GDP per capita in a city was included as two proxies for the overall wealth level of the city and the revenue of a city government was included as a proxy for discretional resources that a municipal government can allocate to its e-Government development.

When a government has already achieved a very high level of e-Government development, further improvement could become di<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve due to constraints on technology feasibility and lack of business motives. We thus controlled for a city’s e-Government annual development gap, measured as the di<sup>f</sup>erence between the CSTC-reported e-Government score of the focal city and the highest score reported in a group of cities including the focal city itself and all the members of its trait-based reference group in a year. The maximum value of this measure is zero, representing that the city is the best performer of e-Government development in the group in the year. The greater the deviation from the highest score is, the more room this city has in improving its e-Government.

We controlled for the dispersion of e-Government development levels in both reference groups in a year. Our theories suggest that a municipal government observes and follows its referents. However, the government may encounter more challenges in tracking and making sense of their peers’ information when the development of e-Government varies considerably within a peer group. This reduces the overall in<sup>fl</sup>uences of its referent organizations. We used the standard deviation of the CSTC-reported e-Government scores within a reference group (either trait- or institution-based) to measure the dispersion of e-Government development levels in each group.

Finally, to control for the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the national trend of e-Government development and other unmeasured periodic disturbances, we included year <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects in our models.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

This study examines how trait-based and institution-based reference groups in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of the e-Government systems of Chinese municipal governments. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that Chinese municipal governments anchor their e-Government development around the average of those of their reference groups: when a municipal government lags behind the average of its referents, it will try to catch up in the subsequent year; when it exceeds the average of its referents, it will slow down the development. Thus, Chinese municipal governments appear to adjust dynamically their e-Government development towards the average status of chosen reference groups.

The adjustment pattern of the Chinese municipal governments is consistent with a widely accepted wisdom that too much of a good thing can be ultimately bad. This wisdom is re<sup>fl</sup>ected from the Chinese proverb, “going too far is as bad as not going far enough,” and a similar Western one, everything in moderation; nothing in excess. It can be traced back to the thoughts of ancient philosophers such as Confucius and Aristotle and has had enormous in<sup>fl</sup>uences in both eastern and western societies. Modern philosophers labeled these principles as the doctrine of the mean or the golden mean. More recently, management scholars have started to investigate the instantiation of such wisdom in business administration (Pierce and Aguinis, 2013). By elaborating the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the two reference groups, our study o<sup>f</sup>ers explanations of this phenomenon: based on prospect theory, we propose that an organization tends to be either active or passive in its development activities, depending on its position relative to its trait-based reference group; and based on institutional theory, we propose that an organization tends to avoid di<sup>f</sup>erentiating itself from its institution-based reference group to reduce the risk of legitimacy loss. The two theories explain why governments appear to adhere to the doctrine of the mean in their e-Government development when facing di<sup>f</sup>erent reference groups.

This study contributes to the literature on public information systems in general and the studies of e-Government in particular. The question of how governments develop e-Government systems is of enormous practical importance. The U.S. federal government spent \$75.7 billion, or \$237 per capita, on information systems in $2 0 1 2 ;$ and the U.S. state governments typically invested 5% of its revenue (Pang et al., 2014a). However, despite the practical importance, the past decade only witnessed a very limited number of studies of public information systems in premium information systems journals (Pang et al., 2014a). The lack of theoretical and empirical advancements in this domain has prevented us from developing a complete understanding of government decisions regarding public information systems. This paper fills this research vacuum by highlighting the influence of reference groups and the moderating role of superordinate governments in such decisions.

This paper also contributes to the literature on institutional-IT studies in general. We can learn from the literature that the development, adoption, and use of IT systems are subject to institutional in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Mignerat and Rivard, 2009; Orlikowski and Barley, 2001). Studies have also shown that, in making critical IT decisions, organizations often follow prevailing practices among their same-industry peers or leaders (e.g., Gosain, 2004; Liang et al., 2007; Ruckman et al., 2015; Wang, 2010). Organizations also imitate peer organizations that share similar traits (Ruckman et al., 2015) or those that are geographically or socially proximate to the focal organizations (Angst et al., 2010). These studies have demonstrated the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence on information systems development. Our study advances this line of inquiry in two ways. First, previous studies have not adequately examined the mechanisms through which referent organizations exert their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on information systems development. Building on prospect theory and institutional theory, our study explains the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of two di<sup>f</sup>erent peer groups. Second, previous studies have focused only on a one-way peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence that laggards follow leaders. Our study instead includes the simultaneous functioning of countervailing forces when an organization follows its reference groups. Our results enrich the literature by highlighting organizations tendency to gravitate towards the average of their peers. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the results show that, while organizations with superior performances or cutting-edge technologies often lead institutional changes by setting standards, the institutional environment generates a countervailing force that prevents the organizations from being too di<sup>f</sup>erent from their peers.

Researchers may wonder whether the research context, China, is itself a boundary condition for our theories and <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The doctrine of the mean is one of the most fundamental principles in Confucianism. Hofstede (2001) characterized China as a typical collectivist society in which members maintain a high level of interdependence. In this culture, individuals are expected to act in the best interest of the groups; individual opinions are not so strongly encouraged as conformity to social norms; personal achievements largely depend on social networks; and social ties play an important role in de<sup>fi</sup>ning individuals’ identities (Hofstede, 2001). Thus, the unique cultural and institutional environments in China might dictate the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study. However, other considerations also suggest the likelihood of the generalizability of our results to other cultural contexts. For example, the two primary theoretical lenses that we adopt in this study—prospect theory and institutional theory—were found to be applicable to a wide range of cultural contexts including East Asia (e.g., Sharp and Salter, 1997; Teo et al., 2003; Zheng et al., 2013) even though they were originally developed in the West. For another instance, principles similar to the doctrine of the mean in Confucianism, such as staying in moderation and avoiding excess, have also been in<sup>fl</sup>uential in western cultures since the age of Aristotle (Pierce and Aguinis, 2013). Thus, the wide acceptance of this wisdom in both the East and the West may instantiate the functioning and interplay of the prospect theoretic thinking and institutional conformity logics of organizations. However, determining the universal application of our theory still needs future research e<sup>f</sup>orts

Particularly, future studies can build on and extend our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in two directions. First, in addition to testing whether our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are applicable to di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures, researchers can more explicitly examine the role of national cultures in developing public information systems. A few cross-national studies have already been done along this line of inquiry. Among them, Khalil (2011) showed that a few national cultural dimensions, such as institutional collectivism, performance orientation, and uncertainty avoidance, were key determinants to the e-Government readiness of a country; and Zhao et al. (2014) also found evidence that national culture signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly a<sup>f</sup>ected e-Government di<sup>f</sup>usion. Building on these evidence, future organizational studies using multi level data can examine the moderating roles of various national cultural dimensions in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing organizations choice of their reference groups.

Second, future studies can extend our results by explicitly examining the role of political systems in developing public information systems. The Chinese political system is often characterized by high concentration of power. Provincial governments in China have tighter control over their subordinate municipal governments than in many other political systems. Thus, it is plausible that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings could be partially driven by some unique characteristics of the Chinese political system. However, a few studies in the contexts of the U.S. political environments also conceptually argued for or empirically reported signi<sup>fi</sup>cant peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence on loca governments’ e-Government development (e.g., Ho and Ni, 2004; Jun and Weare, 2011). To the best of our knowledge, it has remained unclear whether or how di<sup>f</sup>erent political systems a<sup>f</sup>ect local governments’ choice of reference groups. It thus can be revealing if future studies can identify and theorize about some characteristics of political systems and directly examine their in <sup>fl</sup>uences on the formation of reference groups.

The public sector, as the major research subject of multiple academic disciplines, still remains a largely unexplored terrain for information systems studies (Pang, 2017). It will be very fruitful if scholars in multiple disciplines, including information systems, public administration, and political science, can join force to advance our knowledge in this domain. We thus end this paper by calling for more research attention to the public-sector information systems studies.

## Appendix A. e-Government assessment metrics and methods

China Software Testing Center (CSTC) annually publishes their grading rubrics used in assessing the quality and functionality of Chinese government web portal, in addition to the numeric grading results. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c rubrics changed each year during the studied period (2009–2013). In general, the grading rubrics in each year consist of a set of assessment criteria applied to various metrics that pertain to the content or the functionality of a government web portal. These metrics were usually grouped into three levels of categories (Level I, II, and III), with Level III metrics as the most granular ones. In 2013, CSTC used 169 Level III metrics, grouped into 71 Level II metrics subcategories and then 18 Level I metrics categories. Table A.1 provides an abridged version of the hierarchy of the metrics categories used by CSTC in 2013 for assessing the web portals of prefectural level governments. Because CSTC has been continually improving its assessment rubrics over years, assessment rubrics used prior to 2013, which are usually the subsets of the 2013 metrics, follow a similar format but di<sup>f</sup>er moderately.

CSTC assigns human inspectors to evaluate government web portals annually. For a given web portal, inspectors assign a numeric score to each Level III metrics based on the above grading rubrics. CSTC then computes a composite e-Government score for each government web portal as a weighted average of the scores of all the rated metrics. The weight for each metric was determined by CSTC and published in its methodology documents. In each year, the same rubrics and weights are applied to all the web portals of prefectural-level Chinese cities.
