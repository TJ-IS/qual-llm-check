# Beyond Overall Treatment Effects: Leveraging Covariates in Randomized Experiments Guided by Causal Structure

Ali Tafti,<sup>a</sup> Galit Shmueli<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business Administration, University of Illinois at Chicago, Chicago, Illinois 60607; <sup>b</sup> Institute of Service Science, National Tsing Hua University, Hsinchu 30013, Taiwan

Contact: atafti@uic.edu, https://orcid.org/0000-0002-4450-3668 (AT); galit.shmueli@iss.nthu.edu.tw, https://orcid.org/0000-0002-0820-0301 (GS)

Received: December 31, 2018 Revised: October 8, 2019; February 17, 2020 Accepted: March 30, 2020 Published Online in Articles in Advance: September 17, 2020

https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2020.0938

Copyright: © 2020 INFORMS

Abstract. Researchers using randomized controlled trials (RCTs) often subgroup or condition on auxiliary variables that are not the randomized treatment variable. There are many good reasons to condition on auxiliary variables—also referred to as control vari ables or covariates—in randomized experiments. In particular, designing and conducting RCTs is costly to researchers and subjects, and therefore it is important to derive greater value from RCT data; measuring not just the average treatment effect (ATE), but also finding more nuanced insights about the underlying theoretical mechanisms and gen eralizing the inferences. Unfortunately, there are many confusing and even contradictory guidelines on the use of subgroups or auxiliary variables in RCTs. We show how re searchers can leverage covariates without biasing their causal inferences, by applying a few simple rules based on Judea Pearl’s causal diagramming framework. We demonstrate how to create a causal schema, through careful and deliberate operationalization of auxiliary covariates, in order to analyze the intermediate effects along a causal chain from the treatment to outcome; and we discuss some other ways to leverage covariates for theory development and generalization of findings from RCTs. We present a criterion for distinguishing pre treatment and posttreatment variables that is based on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). We provide a succinct set of guidelines to help readers begin to employ some essential techniques of DAG-based causal analysis. Finally, we provide a series of short tutorials (with accompanying simulated data and R scripts) to help readers explore the connections between RCT and observational contexts in causal diagramming. This commentary aims to raise awareness of the DAG methodology, explain its usefulness to experimental research, and encourage adoption in the IS community for studies using RCTs as well as observational data.

## 1. Introduction and Motivation

Randomized experiments, or randomized control trials (RCTs), have long been an important part of the research and product development process for firms in many industries—most notably the pharmaceutical industry, where randomized clinical trials are one of the requirements to obtain government approval of new products. RCTs have become popular tools for empirical research in information systems (IS) and many other fields, as they are considered to be the gold standard for accurate causal inferences and as digital technologies have helped reduce their implementation costs. RCTs are used for a variety of goals, ranging from simple generalization of an effect to a larger population, to building and testing theory (Deaton and Cartwright 2018).

However, designing and conducting RCTs remains costly to researchers and subjects. Although major firms now openly acknowledge running thousands of experiments at any given point of time on their live platforms, the economic and social costs borne by users are substantial even if not well understood (Goel 2014, Clarke 2016). Therefore, it is important to derive from RCT data as much value and knowledge as possible. One way to economize the use of RCTs is not just to measure the average treatment effect (ATE), but also to find more nuanced insights about the underlying theoretical mechanisms and to generalize the inferences to other contexts.

As is well known, the random assignment of subjects to treatment groups ideally removes endogenous influences, thereby making it possible to identify an overall population causal effect of the treatment on an outcome. Assuming the experiment is perfectly executed, auxiliary variables that would otherwise be needed to identify the causal effect of a treatment on the outcome—as in a regression model to analyze observational data—are rendered by the random assignment to be unnecessary for identifying the expected ATE. Hence, in theory, the auxiliary variables—also known as control variables, subgroups, or covariates—are not required for inclusion in regression models to prevent omitted variable biases or endogeneity biases. That covariates are not required in analyzing randomized experiments is often stated as their strength over observational studies. Yet Deaton and Cartwright (2018) point out an important drawback: to the extent that researchers rely on experiments to avoid thinking about covariates and their underlying causal structure, it can hamper the cumulative development of science and theory.

Despite the theoretical justification for avoiding the use of covariates for estimating an overall ATE, in practice, researchers often use covariates in experiments (see, e.g., the survey by Montgomery et al. 2018). Often, and especially in large-scale experiments, researchers estimate ATEs for subgroups. Equivalently, researchers run a regression or analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model, conditioning on a set of control variables. This practice is popular in experimental studies in economics, epidemiology, management, political science, information systems, and other areas where researchers commonly report multiple versions of models that include different subsets of control variables, along with the overal treatment effect; this is also known as poststratification, covariate adjustment, or conditioning on auxiliary variables. This widely used practice suggests that scholars see value in the information provided by auxiliary variables. In addition to utilizing auxiliary variables in the analysis (e.g., in a regression model or ANCOVA), some researchers also include interaction terms between the auxiliary variables and the treatment variable (Imbens and Rubin 2015, sections 7.5–7.6). Researchers use auxiliary variables for different reasons: to allow ATEs to be identified for specific subgroups of interest, to help identify moderating (interaction) or mediating effects that reveal underlying theoretical mechanisms, and, from a statistical point of view, to improve the precision of the ATE estimator (Kahan et al. 2014). Although the collection and use of data on auxiliary variables is widespread in RCTs, we argue that researchers have not been extracting the ful extent of information that such auxiliary variables could provide, which would have both theoretical and practical value. Current guidance on the use of auxiliary variables in RCTs is focused on avoiding bias to the ATE estimator. Not only has prior literature provided confusing and sometimes contradictory guidelines on avoiding bias to the ATE, but moreover, it does not provide clear guidance on how to extract further information using auxiliary variables, beyond the ATE.

In this research commentary, we present guidelines for using subgroups and auxiliary variables in RCTs based on the causal diagramming framework by Pearl (2009).<sup>1</sup> This framework provides clear guidelines on how to leverage the information in auxiliary variables, and we illustrate its usefulness with an example in the IS context. We do not add anything fundamentally new to Pearl’s methodology, nor do we provide a comprehensive introduction to it. Rather our fivefold contribution, which we have not seen in prior published work, includes the following: (1) We demonstrate how to create a causal schema, through careful and deliberate operationalization of auxiliary covariates, in order to analyze the intermediate effects along a causal chain from the treatment to outcome; and we discuss other ways to leverage covariates for theory development and generalization of findings from RCTs. (2) We present a criterion for distinguishing pretreatment and posttreatment variables that is based on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). (3) We provide a succinct set of guidelines to help readers begin to employ basic and essential DAG-based causal analysis, and help orient readers on where to reference other work for further guidance. (4) We point out issues of particular relevance to online digital experiments. (5) We provide series of short tutorials, and make the accompanying simulated data and R scripts available, to make our points more concrete to readers who wish to learn to implement some of the techniques. Our fivefold con: tribution aims to raise awareness of the DAG methodology, explain its usefulness to experimental research and encourage adoption in the IS community for studies using RCTs as well as observational data. Readers can find a more thorough grounding in the theoretical underpinnings of causal diagramming, and some exposure to its broad domains of applica tion, in the textbook by Judea Pearl (2009). Pear et al. (2016) offers a more basic workbook with useful examples. Pearl and Mackenzie (2018) is a delightfully readable introductory text that puts the development of Pearl’s framework in historical context. Morgan and Winship (2015) also provide an effective intro duction to causal diagramming for the social sciences

## 1.1. Confusing and Contradictory Guidelines in Current Literature

Among various scientific disciplines where auxiliary variables are used in the analysis of RCTs, there has been some disagreement and confusion on the practice of which auxiliary variables should be included in the analysis of randomized experiments.

For example, based on surveying 50 clinical trials, Pocock et al. (2002, p. 2928) find inconsistencies regarding the use of covariate-adjusted analyses “perhaps largely because their rationale and statistical properties are poorly understood.” They also highlight the lack of clear guidelines on covariate selection. Examining the guidelines provided in a variety of textbooks and papers, we identified several types of guidelines and conditions:

1. Guidance based on correlation of the auxiliary variable with the outcome variable: The classic textbook by Shadish et al. (2002, pp. 304–307) prescribes including in the ANCOVA of an RCT “variables that are highly correlated with the outcome whether or not they distinguish between [the treatment] groups at pretest” while also advising against “adding covariates that do not predict outcome or that are highly correlated with each other.” A related, but different, approach is the inclusion of covariates shown in the past to correlate with the outcome—these are called prognostic covariates. For example, Kahan et al. (2014) recommend including known prognostic covariates for the purpose of increasing power.

2. Guidance based on correlation of the auxiliary variable with the group imbalance: Raab et al. (2000, p. 330) advocate always conditioning on balanced covariates, while adjusting for unbalanced covariates only when the sample size is large. Yet Mutz et al. (2018, p. 5) explain why using significance tests for determining unbalanced covariates is inappropriate (“One should choose covariates for their anticipated relation to the dependent variable. To alter this choice because of a balance test is to choose based on a relation with the independent variable”).

3. Guidance based on pretreatment versus posttreatment variables. Montgomery et al. (2018) document a widespread misuse of auxiliary variables in the analysis of RCTs; in particular, they find research publications routinely adjust for “post-treatment variables” in ways that are known to introduce bias in causal estimates. We use quotes, because the terms pre- and post-treatment variables are ill defined in the papers we reviewed (see Section 2.1.3). Though we agree with Montgomery et al. (2018) and others, that simply conditioning on “post-treatment variables” is usually better avoided, we point out that (i) a clear definition of “post-treatment” and “pre-treatment” variables should be based on causal structure and (ii) some variables labeled “post-treatment variables” can provide essential insight, if used in the right way.

Based on a review of published experiments as well as the confusing, ambiguous, and sometimes contradicting guidelines from the statistics literature, it is clear that we need more systematic guidance on how to leverage the information of auxiliary variables in RCTs. As Hernan et al. (´ 2002) explain, strategies that rely purely on statistical criteria to determine whether to condition on auxiliary variables are inadequate Deaton and Cartwright (2018) argue that we need to pay more attention not just to obtaining unbiased overall treatment effects, but also to how to use the results of RCTs for theory building and generalizing results beyond narrowly circumscribed experimental settings: “If trials are to be useful, we need paths to their use that are as carefully constructed as are the trials themselves” (Deaton and Cartwright 2018, p. 10). Deaton and Cartwright (2018) explain that covariates can be useful to generalize results and enhance theory implications from RCT studies, but their commentary does not provide methodological guidance on how to do this. Although some econometrics texts offer limited guidance on how to avoid “bad control variables” (e.g., Angrist and Pischke 2008), Pearl (2015) presents a series of causal scenarios and “toy problems” in which even those helpful texts and the prevailing econometric frameworks prove inadequate. Thus, a systematic framework is needed to leverage covariates beyond estimating an ATE. In this commentary, we aim to provide information to make it easier for empirical researchers in IS to begin applying the causality framework in RCT studies in order to properly leverage information from covariates that is useful for theory building and generalizing experimental results.

## 1.2. Causal Diagrams for Bridging Theory and Practice for RCTs in IS Research

Causal diagrams explicitly encode a limited set of relatively precise and narrow theoretical claims that relate variables to one another. In doing so, Pearl’s framework enables more insight into theoretical mechanisms by properly identifying subgroup-specific effects, causal mediation, intermediate effects, and other insights of theoretical value that can be leveraged from auxiliary variables (Pearl 2009). Moreover, the framework offers quantitative methods for generalizing RCT results, enabling researchers to extend and transport the estimated causal effect from a tightly circumscribed RCT setting to nonexperimental settings (Bareinboim and Pearl 2016).

Despite Pearl’s extensive body of work, there remains a gap between theory and practice: The causal diagramming framework is still perceived in fields such as econometrics as not amenable to practical applications (Imbens 2019). Thus far, Pearl’s work is comprised primarily of “toy examples,” relatively simple template diagrams that do not represent realworld data, particularly in the context of RCTs. This commentary elaborates on a domain-specific example based on a recently published RCT study (Bapna et al. 2016).

We hope to encourage use of Pearl’s causal framework as a conceptual tool in observational studies as well as in RCT studies, which can be used along with the library of existing econometric and statistical tools. However, we choose to focus specifically on RCTs because, considering their rising importance in empirical research, they are a useful starting point to introduce Pearl’s framework for a number of reasons. First, empirical researchers with at least passing familiarity of Pearl’s DAG framework generally recognize its usefulness for observational data contexts. Yet, the importance of DAGs to RCTs is not nearly as well appreciated; this is evident and implicit in the recent commentary by Imbens (2019). Second, when leveraging covariates in RCTs for theory and generalization, one encounters many of the same challenges as with observational settings. Third, there is a key advantage of RCTs over observational settings when leveraging covariates: In RCTs, especially in digital settings, researchers can exert more control over the setting to define specific covariates of interest in order to narrow the possible causal paths from treatment to outcome. In turn, one can leverage covariates for greater insight into the underlying theoretical mechanisms by estimating intermediate causal mechanisms and by generalizing the causal estimates to other relevant observational settings. We demonstrate this in this commentary. Fourth, our focus on RCTs is strongly motivated by their rising popularity and importance in IS research, and thereby a sense of urgency to address the challenges for RCT research raised by Deaton and Cartwright (2018). Although our focus is on RCTs, we also point out how the guidance extends to observational data settings, and we provide a series of short tutorials to help readers explore the connections between RCT and observational contexts in causal diagramming.

## 2. Conceptualizing RCTs Using Causal Diagrams

Causal diagrams are visual representations of causal structure that include nodes representing variables and arrows representing causal relationships. Researchers often implicitly have a causal narrative or even diagram in mind when they design an RCT. Indeed, “a picture is worth a thousand words.” Yet causal diagrams are rarely encountered in RCT papers, reports, and textbooks. Even in disciplines such as IS and marketing that use path model diagrams to depict causal relationships, such diagrams are rare in published RCT studies.

## 2.1. Causal Diagrams and Terminology

A causal diagram represents causal relationships between variables using directed arrows. To be amenable to causal inferences, such diagrams are preferably in the form of directed acyclic graphs (DAG): every causal arrow has a single direction and there are no cycles. As demonstrated later in this commentary, whenever a causal diagram does not take the form of a DAG, it suggests a need for some experimental manipulation or redefinition of variables that transform it into a DAG. The terms causal diagram and DAG are commonly used interchangeably.

2.1.1. Types of Nodes. Nodes in a DAG represent variables. Solid circles represent observed variables, while hollow circles (or those labeled U) represent unobserved variables. In an RCT, we have nodes representing the treatment variable(s) (the causes), nodes representing the outcome variable(s) (the effects), and nodes for auxiliary variables. Auxiliary variables can be of different types, depending on the directions of the arrows pointing from and to them, and on their location relative to the cause and effect

Consider two simple examples depicted in Figure 1, where X is the treatment variable and Y is the outcome variable. In Figure 1(a), Z1 is a chain node, meaning it has an incoming arrow showing it is directly influenced by X and also has an outgoing arrow showing that it directly affects Y. The direction of the arrows represents the direction of the causal effect. Z1 also happens to be a posttreatment variable, as we shall define the term later in this paper; it is also referred to as a mediating variable. Generally, conditioning or stratifying on a mediating variable blocks the flow of information (causal path) from the treatment to the outcome of interest and therefore would bias estimates of the overall treatment effect (Montgomery et al. 2018). Thus, in this example, Z1 should not be considered as simply a control variable, but rather it can be used in mediation analysis (Pearl 2014) or in a causal identification strategy known as front-door adjustment (for details on front-door adjustment procedures, see Pearl 2009 and Glynn and Kashin 2017, as well as Online Appendix Tutorial 3). The node Z2 is a fork node, in that it has two outgoing arrows, one showing a causal effect of Z2 on X and another showing a causal effect on Y. Z2 happens to also be a pretreatment variable, as we shall define the term later in this paper. It should be noted that randomized interventions on the treatment variable, as seen in most RCTs, have the effect of removing arrows going into $X . ^ { 2 }$ It is generally appropriate to use Z2 as a control variable, regardless of whether it has an arrow to X.

Figure 1(b) shows a collider node Z. A collider is a node with two or more incoming arrows; being separated by a collider implies that X and Y are causally (and therefore statistically) independent. However, these two variables become statistically dependent when conditioning on Z. This particular scenario could manifest as Berkson’s paradox due to selection bias (Berkson 1946, Greenland et al. 1999) or as Simpson’s paradox (Simpson 1951, Pearl 2009). A spurious effect of a treatment variable (X) on the outcome variable (Y) could appear when stratifying or conditioning on the collider Z, even if the real effect of X on Y were nonexistent.

2.1.2. Types of Paths. A causal path (or directed path) from variable X to some other variable Y is a path that can be traversed from X to Y following exclusively the direction of the arrows in the causal diagram. In other words, if it is possible to follow the arrows from X to reach Y, such that every node traversed on the path is a chain node with arrows pointing in the same direction, then we say there is a causal path from X to Y. For example, in the chain node X → Z1 → Y in Figure 1(a), the path from X to Y passing through Z1 is a causal path. By contrast, there is no causal path from X to Y in Figure 1(b).

A spurious path (or back-door path) from X to Y is one that contains an arrow into X, and such that the other arrows in the path(s) from X to Y can be traversed in either direction along or against the direction of the arrows (Pearl 2009, p. 79). For example, the path from X to Y passing through the fork node Z2 in

Figure 1(a) is a spurious path. We will revisit these terms in the examples below.

2.1.3. Pretreatment and Posttreatment Variables. The distinction between pretreatment and posttreatment variables offers a useful starting point in thinking about how the variables are to be used in subsequent analysis. Although many authors use the terms pre treatment and posttreatment (or “pre-test” and “posttest”) variables, there is a multitude of definitions across papers and books, and sometimes even within the same paper. Some rely on time of measurement relative to the random assignment, some rely on time of measurement relative to the treatment, and others rely on whether the variable is affected (or plausibly affected) by the treatment or by the assignment For example, Imbens and Rubin (2015, pp. 15–16) define pretreatment variables as “a priori known to be unaffected by the treatment assignment. This knowledge often comes from the fact that they are permanent characteristics of units, or that they took on their values prior to the treatment being assigned, as reflected in the label ‘pre-treatment’ variables.” By default, any other variables, aside from the treatmen and outcome variables, are considered by Imbens and Rubin (2015) as posttreatment. Similarly, Steiner et al. (2017) define pretreatment variables as those that are not affected by random assignment, and “thus all covariates are ideally measured before treatment assignment.” In contrast, Gerber and Green (2012 p. 97) define posttreatment covariates as “measured after the intervention and are therefore potentially affected by the experimental assignment,” while pre treatment are variables for which “values are assumed to be fixed constants that are observed prior to the random assignment of subjects.” Montgomery et al. (2018, and online appendix) use a variety of criteria for coding a set of papers based on whether they used “post-treatment variables”: “whether or not variables were measured before or after the treat ment”; variables which authors “show [are] posttreatment using a statistical model or graph”; the combination of “(a) measured after the treatmen and (b) could have plausibly been affected by the treatment” is determined to be posttreatment; a variable that “was collected after the intervention” is considered posttreatment; a variable “affected by the treatment” is considered posttreatment, while a pre treatment variable “must not be affected by the ex perimental randomization.” Because the definition of pretreatment and posttreatment variables is critical for determining if and how they should be included in the analysis, we propose a simple and visual definition that considers the causal path:

We define a posttreatment variable Z, relative to the treatment variable X, to be any variable for which there exists a causal path from X to Z in the causal diagram. A pretreatment variable Z is any variable for which no such causal path from the treatment X to Z exists.

This distinction between pretreatment and posttreatment variables provides a convenient, though incomplete, rule of thumb for selecting auxiliary variables that can be included in the conditioning set. The causal diagram provides a logical basis to make the distinction, but there are cases where it is insufficient to rely on the distinction. Such cases are best understood with the use of causal diagrams. To explain this, we need to introduce a few more concepts.

In particular, it is useful to invoke the concept of “blocking a path” (also referred to in Pearl’s framework as d-separation). According to Pearl (2009, p. 16), a path is blocked (or d-separated) by a variable set $Z = \{ Z 1 , . . . , Z k \}$ if one or both of the following holds:

1. The path contains a chain X → Zi → Y or a fork X ← Zi → Y such that Zi is in Z.

2. The path contains a collider X → C ← Y such that C is not in Z and, furthermore, Z does not contain any descendant of C.<sup>4</sup>

Determining whether an auxiliary variable should be conditioned upon depends on how the auxiliary variable node opens or blocks the flow of causality from the cause node to the effect node. In principle, the goal is to condition or not condition on variables (nodes) to accord with the following three guiding principles (Pearl et al. 2016, p. 61): (a) block all spurious paths from cause to effect, (b) leave all causal paths from cause to effect unperturbed, and (c) do not create new spurious paths. Table 1 lists the practices fulfilling each of these principles.

To determine whether a node opens or blocks the flow, Pearl (2009, p. 79) introduced the “back-door criterion.” Given a causal diagram, the back-door criterion for causal inference is as follows: Any spurious (i.e., back-door) path can be effectively blocked by including in the set of control variables (the conditioning set) at least one variable that lies on a fork or chain node on the path, or alternatively, by excluding from the conditioning set at least one variable that lies on a collider node on the path (Pearl 2009, p. 79). In observational studies, this is what regression models essentially do when they include a list of control variables, although most econometrics and statistics textbooks do not explicitly address the role of colliders. The distinction between causal paths and spurious paths is important in assessing which auxiliary variables to include in the analysis: “The backdoor [i.e., spurious] paths in the diagram carry the ‘spurious associations’ from X to Y, whereas the paths directed along the arrows from X to Y carry ‘causative associations’” (Bareinboim and Pearl 2016, p. 7348).

Note that the back-door criterion does not always agree with the common notion that one must include all auxiliary variables that are correlated with both the treatment and the outcome. In the case of collider variables or their descendants in a causal diagram, some of those variables should specifically be excluded from the conditioning set. When multiple auxil iary variables can serve as controls, the diagram makes transparent the subsets that are sufficient.<sup>5</sup>

In the Figure 1(b) example, although Z is correlated with both X and Y, as a collider variable it must be omitted from the list of control variables, because the back-door path X → Z ← Y is already blocked by virtue of Z being a collider, whereas conditioning upon Z actually serves to reopen that path, just like in Simpson’s (Berkson’s) paradox (Pearl 2009). Note also that conditioning on any descendant of collider variable Z would have the same effect of opening up a spurious path through Z and should be avoided. It is useful to know the causal positioning of some variables that are unobserved. For example, by virtue of being excluded from a conditioning set, an unobserved variable that occupies a collider position in a spurious path serves a useful role in identifying a causal effect. Therefore, it is especially important to be aware of such excluded variables and their essential role in a causal structure even if they are unobserved or unmeasured.

Consider the following “M-form” scenario where the distinction between pretreatment and posttreatment variables becomes insufficient (Figure 2). Here, the causal effect of X on Y is identified even if Z is not observed. Such a scenario can arise in an RCT somewhere along the intermediate steps of a causal chain from treatment to outcome. It can also arise in an RCT with imperfect randomization. A glance at the diagram reveals that the causal effect of treatment on outcome is identified, because the back-door path is blocked by the collider Z. Thus, A, B, or Z need not be observed or included as controls. Conditioning on A and/or B is harmless, because they do not block the causal path or create a new spurious path. Controlling for Z induces a bias by opening a back-door path. Though Z would be classified as a pretreatment variable according to the classification systems that we mentioned, controlling for it introduces collider bias. Therefore, the causal effect of X on Y can be computed simply as the difference in conditional expectations $\hat { E [ Y | X = 1 ] } - E [ Y | X = 0 ]$ and can be estimated with only the bivariate information on X and Y (and this can be generalized to continuous variables). Standard econometrics texts do not offer a solution to this case; for example, the guidelines in Angrist and Pischke (2008, section 3.2.3 on “bad control”) offer valuable insight but do not provide the conceptual tools to navigate this or more complex versions of this causal scenario.<sup>6</sup> Tutorial 5 in the online appendix provides more guidance on handling colliders in an RCT context.

## 2.2. The Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of Using Causal Diagrams

Causal diagrams help avoid common pitfalls in deciding which subset of variables to include as controls and which variables have a posttreatment or mediating role, thereby requiring a special way of incorporation into the analysis that differs from simply being included as control variables. Without causal diagrams, it can be hard to know the researchers assumptions and how each measured (or unobserved) variable fits into the causal story. It is worthwhile con sidering some of the reasons for using causal diagrams and, in particular, how they can be useful in discerning the role of auxiliary variables in RCTs.

First, and perhaps most importantly, the mental process of constructing the causal diagram helps the researcher clarify and later communicate his/her assumptions about the relationships between the cause(s), effect(s), and other factors that affect or are affected by the treatment or RCT environment. Such a diagram then makes the researcher’s assumptions explicit and transparent, thereby strengthening the motivation of why the experiment is being conducted in the first place, and specifically, why observational data would not be sufficient. Experiments not only involve costs in implementation expended by researchers or their funding institutions, they can also exact heavy social or economic costs and risks to the subjects of the experiment; hence, they should be employed judiciously. Presumably, the RCT is needed to infer causal effects when unobserved variables confound the relationship between the treatment and outcome. Such unobservable variables should be depicted in the causal diagram, even when their manifestation is not well understood. For example, it is common to depict a set of unobserved variables with a generic label U, to show a causal positioning of variables attributable to a set of vaguely understood factors such as genetics or cultural factors.<sup>7</sup> Just knowing about the existence of such unobservables and their role in a causal diagram can clarify the need and give justification for the RCT. Some of the assumptions embedded in a causal diagram can be tested and confirmed statistically. In particular, a causal diagram will sometimes yield directly testable implications about conditional independence of specific pairs of variables, which can be confirmed in the data.

Second, the causal diagram serves as a conceptual aid helping the researcher find, among the full set of available auxiliary variables for a study, which subset of variables is sufficient for identification, and among those, the most convenient and optimal for measurement and inclusion in the model. The causal diagram helps clarify whether an auxiliary variable can, should, or should not be controlled for. Such a distinction helps provide some guidance on how the variables are to be used in subsequent analysis. That is, not every variable correlated with both the dependent and independent variables must be included as a control; and in the case of collider variables or their descendants, some should specifically be excluded from the conditioning set. When the system of variables becomes large, the mental computation to find which subsets of variables are sufficient, necessary, and optimal for conditioning becomes too taxing without the aid of causal diagrams; and the researcher ends up spending valuable time and resources measuring auxiliary variables that are unnecessary, or even perhaps forbidden for inclusion, given the causal structure.

Third, RCTs often encounter complications such as imperfect randomization, noncompliance, and interference/leakage issues. Researchers can test the statistical independence between pretreatment variables and the treatment assignment, to verify that the treatment variable has been effectively randomized among subjects. If a pretreatment variable is found to bias the treatment assignment, researchers can adjust for the selection bias by conditioning on that variable. Other issues such as noncompliance and interference/ leakage can be modeled with posttreatment variables, and an emerging literature provides methods for dealing with those issues (Balke and Pearl 1997, Frangakis and Rubin 2002, Bareinboim and Pearl 2016). By placing such complications within the causal diagram, it becomes clear which causal relationships are affected by the complications and what variables contain information that should be conditioned on.

Finally, causal diagrams support efforts for extrapolating causal inferences of the RCT to other empirical contexts where observational data are available but conducting the same experiment is infeasible (Pearl and Bareinboim 2014, Bareinboim and Pear 2016). If we understand the causal structure in which pretreatment and posttreatment variables relate to the main variables and outcomes of interest in the study, we can provide clarity on how to use those variables to better evaluate the design of an RCT, interpret its results, and understand its implications.

## 3. Example: The Effect of Anonymous Browsing on Matching in Online Dating (Bapna et al. 2016)

To illustrate the usefulness of causal diagrams, we revisit the RCT by Bapna et al. (2016), who study the effect of user anonymity in an online dating platform. Since Bapna et al. did not actually show causal diagrams, we hope to convey how the underlying goals of their field experiment—in terms of both the empirical strategy and theoretical implications—become more salient with the use of causal diagrams. The ability to browse anonymously is the treatment variable in their study. The outcome of interest is the number of matches. The key causal relationship of interest is the effect of anonymity on the number of matches. Anonymity was a premium feature on that platform, meaning that users could pay additional fees for the ability to browse the profiles of other users anonymously—that is, without revealing their identities to the users whose profiles they view. In general, a user can signal interest in another user by sending a message. Without the anonymity feature, viewing another user’s profile on the site also sends a signal of potential interest (albeit a weaker one). At the same time, when a user is aware that browsing other users’ profiles sends such an implicit signal, this awareness might inhibit extensive browsing in search of a match. By reducing the inhibition of browsing anonymity could in that sense lead to broader and more extensive searching that in turn leads to a match, perhaps the premise for this feature having been priced as a premium feature. Given the distinct causal mechanisms leading to potentially opposing effects, the question of interest is to what extent the anonymity feature provides an advantage in terms of the outcome sought by the users—finding a match on the site. Moreover, the study sought to understand the causal mechanisms through which the anonymity feature affects the outcome by considering the messaging and viewing behaviors between users.

## 3.1. Creating the Diagram

Our illustrations are made using the online tool DAGitty.net, which is free and open-source, and also available as an R package (Textor et al. 2011). The steps in creating the diagram are as follows.

1. Set the treatment node and outcome node(s)

2. Add nodes for measured pretreatment variables.

3. Add nodes for potential mediating variables. These are variables of interest that, based on theory, lie on the causal path (i.e., directed path) from the treatment to the outcome.

4. Add nodes for unmeasured (i.e., unobserved) variables that may lie on the spurious path from any of the nodes to the outcome.

5. Add arrows to and from each variable wherever theory or logic suggests the possibility of a direct causal effect of one variable on another, carefully con sidering the direction of arrows. Excluding an arrow indicates an assumption of no direct causal influence.

Note that pretreatment variables can potentially affect mediating and outcome nodes and must be considered in an analysis that aims to distinguish the causal paths of theoretical interest. If it becomes apparent that certain pretreatment variables can affect the treatment variable despite the randomized intervention, this is a potential source of concern because it is likely that unobserved variables are also confounding the causal inference. There could be situations where, by design, the randomized experiment cuts off the causal arrows to the treatment from all but a well-defined set of observed pretreatment variables. If any such pretreatment variable—with causal path to the treatment—also has a causal path to the outcome not passing through the treatment variable, then it must either be included in the conditioning set or else the back-door path on which it lies must be blocked by some other means (as per Section 2.1). If any pretreatment variable—having a causal path to the treatment—has no causal path to the outcome that does not pass through the treatment variable, then it could be useful as an instrumental variable, without threatening to confound the causal inference through a back-door path (see Brito and Pearl 2002 and Pearl 2009 (p. 248).

A comment about moderating or interaction effects is in order here. Typical path diagrams used in the management research communities (IS, marketing, etc.) show a moderating effect whenever one is hypothesized, by having one arrow point to the middle of another arrow. This practice is, by convention, avoided in causal diagrams because such diagrams are nonparametric by design. Whenever two or more variables have arrows pointing to another node in a causal diagram, the interaction effects are implicitly assumed to potentially exist and so there is no need to draw them explicitly. We revisit this point in the example below.

## 3.2. Interpreting the Diagram: The Researcher’s Assumptions and ATE Estimability

From this diagram, we can confirm that there is no problem in estimating the total overall effect of

AnonBrows on Match, because the only paths from the treatment variable to the outcome variable are causal paths, and there exist no unblocked spurious paths from the treatment to the outcome (i.e., paths that do not contain a collider, as per Section 2.1.2). PreTreatmentVars refers to the set of pretreatment variables, which in this study includes demographic variables such as gender, age, race, and sexual orientation, as well as online behaviors measured prior to the random treatment assignment. This was verified by the statistical independence tests reported in the study, suggesting effective randomization of the treatment assignment. Note also that the set of unobserved variables U and the pretreatment variables have the same causal positioning in the diagram; they both point to the same set of variables and have no incoming arrows.

In general, by conditioning, stratifying, or subgrouping on specific pretreatment variables, we can gain more insight into the theoretical mechanisms underlying the results. Subgrouping the effects could be done on any other pretreatment variable(s); in this case, one can subgroup by race, sexual orientation, age, or other socioeconomic variables, with the caveats stated earlier that the pretreatment variables do not open spurious pathways through collider variables. Assuming that the data qualifies for regression analysis, a straightforward way to do this is to perform separate regression analyses by subgroup, such as the separate models by gender reported in Bapna et al. (2016). Sometimes it is informative to measure the difference in effect size between different subgroups (males versus females), and in regression models we can measure the moderating influence of gender on the treatment effect using interaction terms. The social sciences literature provides guidance on how to analyze moderating effects, with methods that are entirely compatible with and beyond the scope of Pearl’s nonparametric framework (e.g., Franzese and Kam 2009). Alternatively, one can avoid parametric assumptions, such as would be required when deciding on orders of interaction to include in a model, by using nonparametric conditioning formulas, as typically seen in the causal diagramming literature. For example, we can calculate treatment effects as differences in conditional expectations (see Appendix Tutorial 3); in some cases, the results turn out remarkably close to the regression estimate.

As we noted earlier, potential moderating effects are implied in a DAG whenever two or more arrows are pointing to a node. For example, in the Figure 5 model MsgRcd = f(ViewsSnt, AnonBrows), ViewsSnt is defined in the paper to represent the number of profile views, regardless of anonymity. Based on this variable definition, MsgRcd depends on the interaction of ViewsSnt and AnonBrows. Users began receiving fewer initial messages when browsing anonymously— i.e., the effect of ViewsSnt on MsgRcd changes with AnonBrows. Similarly, in the Figure 4 model MsgSnt = f(ViewsSnt, AnonBrows), MsgSnt depends on the interaction of ViewsSnt and AnonBrows. The theory represented by Figure 4, ultimately unsupported by the study’s results, is that anonymity makes browsing less inhibited, leading viewers to see more profiles that they liked and thereby to initiate more messages. A more subtle effect, possibly contradicting that hypothesis, is that although browsing became less inhibited, the sending of messages to initiate contact became more inhibited because when users were able to view profiles anonymously, they no longer felt the need to message people whose profiles they had visited.

It may sometimes be desirable to compare treatment effects among different values of posttreatment variables.

Consider this question: How would the treatment effects compare between users of high and low values of ViewsSnt? This is a counterfactual question, since users observed with high values of ViewsSnt would perhaps have browsed less extensively had they not been in the treatment group. In light of Figures 4 and 5, we would caution against interpreting any regression models that include intermediate variables (ViewsSnt MsgRcd, and MsgSnt) as control variables. Using simulated data that approximate the main characteristics of the data reported in Bapna et al. (2016), we show the severe bias induced by controlling for posttreatment variables. In Online Appendix Tutorial 2, the regression model in column (2) of Table A2 shows a near-zero coefficient estimate for the treatment effect. The most likely reason for this downward-biased estimate is that the intermediate variables block the causal path from treatment to outcome. There could also be one other source of bias: Those intermediate variables are colliders that open paths to the unobservable U’s. This would be similar to conditioning on the collider in the M-form in Figure 2. For an example that isolates the bias due to colliders, we provide a separate tutorial simulation in Online Appendix Tutorial 5.

Since posttreatment variables are influenced by the treatment, the design and interpretation of counterfactual questions requires care. In some settings, imposing a linearity assumption on functional forms yields valuable insights. This is not the case in the current model (e.g., Figure 5), where in the absence of interaction effects, the causal effects stratified along posttreatment variables would not differ from the overall treatment effect. It is more likely that interaction effects are present here, and this brings even more complications. Generally, counterfactual analysis for posttreatment variables requires assuming some parametric structural model and knowing the number of higher-order interaction terms to include in the mode (Pearl 2009). Since we do not know the exact form of the parametric models, this analysis would be subject to a high degree of uncertainty, though such analysis may be more fruitful in other settings. The final chapter of Pearl et al. (2016) provides some useful techniques for handling counterfactuals. Unfortunately, it is still common to see RCT studies condition on posttreatment variables without these considerations.

## 4. Additional Methods Enabled by Causal Diagrams

Although our explication of the Bapna et al. (2016) study focused on the role of intermediate variables, causal diagrams enable additional methods that leverage auxiliary variables for theoretical insight and generalizability of the causal inferences. These methods are relatively new and continue to be developed; to date, we do not see their widespread use in published social science RCTs, but they are gaining recognition. These methods highlight how causal diagrams complement traditional econometric and statistical approaches. We refer the reader to three classes of methods in which causal diagrams are proving to be instrumental and represent new opportunities for IS research:

1. Causal mediation: In recent years, a new family of methods for inferring causal mediation has been developed (Imai et al. 2010, Imai et al. 2011;

see Preacher 2015 for a survey). Software packages are now available in R and Stata that implement new techniques for analyzing causal mediation (Tingley et al. 2014). Without the aid of causal diagrams, the assumptions for estimating causal mediation effects can be quite stringent, requiring random assignment of both the treatment and mediating variables (e.g., Imai et al. 2011). Such random assignment can be achieved only when the mediating variable, like the treatment variable, is also subject to carefully designed intervention. More recently, Pearl (2014) and subsequent work shows how some of those requirements can be relaxed and applied to more general settings with DAGs. 11

2. Handling imperfect experiments: A class of methods known as principal stratification makes use of posttreatment variables to deal with issues such as imperfect compliance or other complications associated with the RCT (Frangakis and Rubin 2002). A small literature on principal stratification methods provides various stratification formulas on posttreatment variables, much like causal mediation-effect estimators, but differs in theoretical motivation (Gilbert et al. 2011). Causal diagrams and their accompanying analysis can help researchers deal with general complications stemming from posttreatment variables in a DAG framework, such as imperfect compliance (Balke and Pearl 1997) and to adjust for the use of surrogate variables (or proxy variables) of the treatment when the treatment variable of interest is unobservable (Bareinboim and Pearl 2016). For example, sometimes an RCT yields the estimate of the effect of treatment X (e.g., a vegan diet) on outcome Y (e.g., heart disease), but we are really interested in the effect of Z (e.g., cholesterol) on Y; where X is known to affect Z as per causal effects estimated from a separate study.

3. Extrapolating or generalizing the causal estimates to other settings: Auxiliary variables can have an essential role in generalizing the causal effects inferred from the results of an RCT to other settings (Deaton and Cartwright 2018). To date, most published experimental studies have provided only vague information on how their results extend to other subgroups of the population (see Teele 2014). Recently, some scholars have begun to provide formal methods for extrapolating and mathematically transforming the causal effect from an RCT in one setting to other settings where only observational data on the same (or comparable) variables are available (Hernan´ and VanderWeele 2011, Pearl and Bareinboim 2014, Bareinboim and Pearl 2016). This stream of literature is particularly important in light of the call by Deaton and Cartwright (2018) for more attention on generalizing the results of RCTs, and thus we provide an example in Online Appendix Tutorial 4.

## 5. Understanding the Criticisms of Pearl’s Causality Framework

Although causal diagrams have started to appear in the epidemiology and biostatistics literature, until now, we see little scholarship applying Pearl’s framework in economics, information systems, and other adjoining areas of business scholarship (with the notable exception in IS by Zheng and Pavlou 2010). Recently, some prominent scholars in economics have shown interest in these methods (e.g., White and Lu 2011, Heckman and Pinto 2015), and interest is growing among economists (Imbens 2019). A new econometrics textbook features a chapter on DAGs (Cunningham 2019). Given the importance of causal inference, it is worthwhile considering why we have not witnessed greater adoption of Pearl’s causality framework in economics and adjoining disciplines.

The first issue underscored by Heckman and Pinto (2015) and Imbens (2019) is that DAGs do not encode information about the extent to which one variable is causally responsive to another. There is no easy way to encode functional form restrictions, such as monotonicity or linearity, into causal diagrams; notwithstanding recent attempts to address this limitation, such as that by Steiner et al. (2017). Functional form restrictions are essential underpinnings of im portant methods such as regression discontinuity designs (RDDs) and instrumental variable (IV) methods. By contrast, rather than beginning with functional form restrictions, in Pearl’s approach, the researcher derives whatever causal information can first be extracted relying on nonparametric assumptions and then ap plies functional form assumptions as a subsequent and separate layer of analysis. Pearl’s approach of applying functional forms as a separate layer of analysis in application to DAGs has proven to be a valuable alternative, yielding useful results and new techniques for partial identification (Balke and Pearl 1997), analyzing necessary versus sufficient causes (Tian and Pearl 2000, Halpern and Pearl 2005), and generalizing the application of instrumental variables methods (Brito and Pearl 2002), among other contributions in which functional form assumptions were applied to DAGs. Researchers can supplement causal DAG methods with techniques from the econometrics and statistics literatures for tasks such as interpreting standard errors of interaction effects (Franzese and Kam 2009) and bootstrapping methods for estimating composite standard errors (Bellemare and Bloem 2019, Horowitz 2019), among other tasks in which the causal DAG literature is comparatively underdeveloped. Ultimately. Pearl's framework need not be superior to be considered a useful alternative or complement to traditional econometric approaches, as it has independently produced a number of insights, methods, and contributions that can be used alongside with established methods.

The second broad criticism is that the scenarios, in which causal diagrams might help to visualize complex interrelationships among multiple variables, have not proven to be useful in real-world economic and social science settings. Imbens (2014, p. 376) argued:

there have been few compelling applications of causal graphs to social science questions where the causalgraph approach has generated novel analyses or prevented researchers from making mistakes that other frameworks might have encouraged them to make. . . .

More recently, Imbens (2019) signals a shift toward more openness—still laden with skepticism— acknowledging that Pearl’s framework could, in theory and principle, imminently find useful economic applications. In particular, Imbens (2019) acknowledges that both the M-form and FDP models, among other DAG templates, could indeed generate novel analysis or prevent researchers from making mistakes that they would otherwise have made. However, he conveys a tacit impression that DAG-based methods are mostly intended for observational data, an impression shared by other economists quoted in his commentary.<sup>12</sup> Since economists now rely more on RCTs and natural experiments as they recognize that causal identification in observational data is unlikely to be tractable in many real economic settings, the subtext of his commentary is to still cast some doubt on the value of DAG-based methods. Additional issues of a more philosophical nature remain subject to debate (e.g., Dawid 2010). But the main practical criticism of Pearl’s DAG framework has evolved to become increasingly narrow, tentative, and empirically falsifiable; and it can be paraphrased as follows: “To date, we do not have realistic examples demonstrating how DAGs are useful in economic settings.” We thus hope that the realistic RCT-based example we elaborated on in this commentary will be useful toward that end.

## 6. Conclusions

Our paper elaborates on a framework for navigating complex causal scenarios and provides guidance on how to strategically exploit auxiliary variables in online digital RCTs. We pointed out how the guidance extends to observational data settings, and we provided a series of short tutorials to help readers explore the connections between RCT and observational contexts in causal diagramming. We hope to raise awareness of the DAG methodology and encourage adoption in the IS community for studies using RCTs as well as observational data. Especially with the rising popularity and importance of RCTs in IS research, we respond to the call for caution issued by Deaton and Cartwright (2018) against theory-blind reliance on RCTs, by providing and demonstrating specific guidelines to make causal diagrams integral to the process of motivating, designing, and analyzing RCTs—for reasons beyond visually communicating a theoretical model. First, perhaps most importantly, the mental process of constructing causal diagrams helps clarify why the RCT is being conducted in the first place and, specifically, why observational data would not be sufficient for causal identification. Second, the causal diagram serves as a conceptual aid for researchers to define covariates with a more precise understanding of how they operate in specific causa paths, in order to eliminate cycles among intermediate variables and analyze intermediate effects along a causal chain that are of theoretical interest. Third, the causal diagram makes it easier to find, among the ful set of available auxiliary variables for a study, which subset(s) of variables could be sufficient for identification and, among those, most convenient and optima for measurement and inclusion in the model. Although the distinction between pretreatment and posttreatment variables provides a convenient rule of thumb on selecting auxiliary variables, it is incomplete; and the task requires careful consideration of their role in the causal structure. Finally, causal diagrams enable a host of powerful and recently developed techniques to recover from selection biases, to generalize RCT results to observational contexts, and to account for RCT complications such as incomplete compliance and interference or leakage. We believe there is great potential for further development and refinement of causal diagramming methods, to which the IS community can contribute using observational as well as RCT data contexts. IS researchers can engage with the domain-specific challenges that arise in application of these methods; challenges that could not have been anticipated by Pearl and his collaborators, who focused on developing the theoretical foundations.
