Abstract. Due to digitization, new mechanisms have emerged for achieving organizational ambidexterity, defined as the ability to pursue both efficiency and flexibility while balancing exploitation and exploration. This study investigates the role of digitization in achieving organizational structural ambidexterity by undertaking both exploitation and exploration simultaneously. Given the complex interdependencies between digitization and multiple intrafirm and interfirm factors in practice, this study adopts a configurational theory perspective. We posit that ambidexterity is better explained as an outcome of aligning digitization with several intrafirm and interfirm factors, rather than of any individual factors in isolation. We empirically derive configurations for achieving ambidexterity by applying fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis to 1,325 Canadian firms that invested in new information technology system implementation. The results reveal the mechanisms in which digitization plays a multifaceted role in achieving ambidexterity Notably, the mechanisms differ among intrafirm-oriented configurations and interfirm strategic alliance configurations, and among large firms and small firms. In the intrafirm solution, digitization and centralization are essential for achieving ambidexterity, with other factors being peripheral; however, in the interfirm solution, digitization plays a peripheral, and even a counterproductive role, in achieving ambidexterity. Particularly, intrafirm collaboration is a necessary condition in both the intrafirm and interfirm solutions for achieving ambidexterity. Interestingly, the results also suggest that small firms seeking ambidexterity require a high level of digitization only while pursuing an intrafirm (but not for an interfirm) solution, whereas large firms require a high level of digitization for both intrafirm and interfirm solutions. New insights and implications for theory and practice for achieving organizational ambidexterity with digitization are discussed.

## Introduction

In competitive digitized environments where new products and services are introduced more frequently and disruptively through information systems (IS) (El Sawy et al. 2010, Yoo et al. 2010, Tiwana 2014), organizational ambidexterity, herein defined as the ability to pursue both efficiency and flexibility while balancing exploitation and exploration, is important to an organization’s success and even its survival (e.g., March 1991, Kane and Alavi 2007, Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008, O’Reilly and Tushman 2013, Lee et al. 2015). However, in the ambidexterity literature, it is well known that organizational ambidexterity is difficult to achieve, predominantly due to the complexity in managing the inherent tension between exploitation and exploration (e.g., March 1991, Tushman and O’Reilly 1996, Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008).

Pervasive digitization adds further complexity to the mechanism to achieve ambidexterity. Digitization brings major changes in business processes and the ways firms design and innovate their products and services (e.g., Tushman and O’Reilly 1996, Pavlou and El Sawy 2006, Zammuto et al. 2007, Yoo et al. 2012). Furthermore, digitization pervades both intrafirm and interfirm processes, often aiming at automating business processes for efficiency while supporting collaboration among business units within and across firms to facilitate both exploitative operations and explorative innovations (e.g., Barki and Pinsonneault 2005; Sharma and Yetton 2007; Im and Rai 2008, 2014).

Therefore, digitization may fundamentally change how organizations maintain the efficiency of their current business operations while also redesigning their existing capabilities and their products and services (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 2013, Tiwana 2014). Therefore, digitization presents both opportunities and challenges to organizations that wish to achieve such organizational ambidexterity.

The management literature has identified multiple mechanisms to achieve ambidexterity, including (a) simultaneously pursuing both exploitation and exploration with dual structures (i.e., structural ambidexterity) (e.g., Duncan 1976, Jansen et al. 2009); (b) focusing on either exploration or exploitation sequentially over time (i.e., temporal, sequential ambidexterity) (e.g., Brown and Eisenhardt 1997, Adler et al. 1999); (c) fostering a context that supports people to split their focus between alignment and adaptation (i.e., contextual ambidexterity) (e.g., Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004); and (d) relying on interfirm relationships, such as strategic alliances (e.g., Im and Rai 2008, Lavie et al. 2011). The IS literature has adopted these concepts to study IS phenomena, such as information technology (IT) ambidexterity (e.g., Gregory et al. 2015, Lee et al. 2015), exploitative and explorative IT implementation (e.g., Lyytinen and Rose 2006), and the agility view of ambidexterity (e.g., Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011).

Although several studies shed light on the link between digitization and ambidexterity, there is a paucity of research on explaining the role of digitization in achieving organizational ambidexterity and what new mechanisms have emerged as a result of pervasive digitization in practice. Also, although it is well known that firm size and the competitive environment have a significant role in ambidexterity (Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008, Lavie et al. 2011), less is known about how they affect the relationship between digitization and ambidexterity. In addition, although it is known that information technologies matter for both intrafirm and interfirm processes, it is unclear how organizations should leverage digitization along with intrafirm and interfirm approaches to achieve ambidexterity. Given the complex interdependencies between digitization and multiple intrafirm and interfirm factors in practice, organizational ambidexterity may be better explained by how digitization can be structurally aligned with other factors, rather than by any individual factors in isolation. Therefore, in this study, we answer this overarching research question: How can firms effectively achieve organizational ambidexterity by leveraging digitization along with other intrafirm and interfirm factors under different conditions?

To answer this research question, we herein define digitization as a firm’s effort and process to digitize its business processes by implementing, assimilating, and using information systems. Accordingly, we conceptualize an organization’s digitization in terms of its three key IT aspects in the definition: (a) IT spending on new IS implementation, (b) IT training to aid IS assimilation, and (c) IT usage intensity that captures the degree of actual IS usage. In other words, in this study we use digitization as an umbrella term that represents the three key IT aspects observed in practice, and this conceptualization allows us to explain in detail how digitization (generally) and its three IT dimensions (specifically) matter for achieving organizational ambidexterity. In particular, we explicate configurational recipes that explain how the three IT dimensions individually, and more importantly to gether, matter for achieving organizational structural ambidexterity that simultaneously pursues both exploitation and exploration by integrating internal as well as external resources through strategic alliances. To do so, we adopt a configurational approach, namely, a fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) that is best suited to handling the complex interdependencies among the multiple factors and thus empirically analyzing how they simultaneously combine into configurations to achieve ambidexterity (Ragin 2008, Fiss 2011). We apply fsQCA to longitudinal data from 1,325 Canadian firms that invested in major digitization projects between 2003 and 2006. The data set includes the three digitization dimen sions as well as intrafirm and interfirm factors (centralization, intrafirm collaboration, interfirm strategic alliances) and contingency factors (firm size and competitive market intensity), which have been shown to be key factors of existing studies on ambidexterity.

The results reveal several configurational recipes in which digitization plays a significant, multifaceted role in achieving ambidexterity, which is different between intrafirm-oriented configurations and interfirm strategic alliance configurations, between large and small firms. In the intrafirm solution, digitization and centralization are essential for achieving ambidexterity, with other factors being peripheral; however, in the interfirm solution, digitization plays a peripheral, and even a counterproductive, role in achieving ambidexterity. Notably, intrafirm collaboration is a necessary condition for both the intrafirm and the interfirm solutions to achieve ambidexterity. Interestingly the results also suggest that small firms seeking ambidexterity require a high level of digitization only while pursuing the intrafirm (and not for the interfirm) solution, whereas large firms require a high level of digiti zation for both the intrafirm and interfirm solutions.

This study makes several contributions. It contributes to both the IS strategy literature and the ambidexterity literature. It extends the traditional organizational structural ambidexterity framework by integrating digitization and ambidexterity from both the IS and the management literature, with multiple intrafirm and interfirm factors and also the key organizational and environmental contingencies. With the overarching framework, we theorize the significant role of digitization for structural ambidexterity and develop new digitally enabled mechanisms to achieve ambidexterity. Second, by adopting a configurational perspective, we extend the burgeoning IS literature on ambidexterity by generating new insights into the multiple pathways in the form of equifinal configurations to achieving ambidexterity and explicating the multifaceted role that digitization can play in achieving structural ambidexterity. Furthermore, our findings can inform numerous studies in the management literature that have focused on how some of individual factors in isolation facilitate ambidexterity by laying the foundation for exploring the role of these factors from a holistic configurational perspective. Third, we eventually build a middle-range theory that prescribes configurational recipes for offering specific guidance on how organizations, given the specifics of their strategy and size, can achieve structural ambidexterity with the aid of digitization, thus advancing our understanding of ambidexterity, particularly in today’s fused and digitized environments.

## Theoretical Background Organizational Ambidexterity

Prior literature has emphasized the tension between exploitation and exploration, as well as the difficulty firms encounter balancing exploitation and exploration given limited resources. Exploration implies adding variability to a firm’s products, knowledge, and competencies, experimenting with new capabilities and ideas, and departing from existing processes and products. In contrast, exploitation implies improving and refining current processes, products, and competencies (e.g., March 1991, Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008, O’Reilly and Tushman 2013). Ambidexterity, a firm’s ability to handle the trade-off between exploitation and exploration and sustain a healthy balance between the two, was shown to be crucial for both short-term and long-term success (e.g., March 1991, O’Reilly and Tushman 2013). If this balance is not achieved, then the firm risks developing strategic myopia by either overemphasizing exploitation (e.g., failing to adapt to changing market needs) or overemphasizing exploration, (e.g., repeatedly engaging in new ventures without sustaining profitability in existing initiatives).

Despite its importance, it is difficult for firms to achieve organizational ambidexterity due to the inherent tension between exploitation and exploration and their self-reinforcing cycles that so often result in success or failure traps (Levinthal and March 1993, Tushman and O’Reilly 1996). This challenge is also known as the paradox of administration between efficiency and flexibility (Adler et al. 1999), in that ambidexterity requires firms to have both efficiencyoriented and flexibility-oriented foci at the same time, such as both a centralized form with mechanistic control and a decentralized form with autonomy (e.g., Benner and Tushman 2003, Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008, O’Reilly and Tushman 2013). Accordingly, to be ambidextrous, organizations should understand the complex interdependencies between strategies, structures, and cultures that are different for exploitation and exploration given limited intrafirm resources and often intense external market competition.

Organizational ambidexterity has been studied in various literatures, such as organizational learning, strategic management, technology innovation, and organizational design (e.g., Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008), and multiple mechanisms to achieve ambidexterity have been proposed. First, firms can simultaneously pursue both exploitation and exploration with dual structures, each of which focuses exclusively on either exploitation or exploration (i.e., simultaneous, structural ambidexterity) (e.g., Duncan 1976, Jansen et al. 2009). This mechanism will thus enable the firm as a whole to enjoy the fruits of both modes simultaneously. Second, firms can achieve ambidexterity by focusing on exploration and exploitation sequentially over time (i.e., temporal, sequential ambidexterity) (Brown and Eisenhardt 1997, Adler et al. 1999). Third, Gibson and Birkinshaw (2004) identify another mechanism to achieve ambidexterity by providing employees with the incentives and collaborative contexts in which individuals can effectively split and allocate their time and effort between conflicting demands for alignment and adaptation (i.e., contextual ambidexterity). Finally, another emerging literature (e.g., Rothaermel and Deeds 2004, Lavie et al. 2011) has extended existing intrafirm structural ambidexterity mechanisms to the context of interfirm relationships, such as strategic alliances and joint ventures, as another way to achieve organizational ambidexterity.

## Digitization and Organizational Ambidexterity

The IS literature has adopted the concept of organizational ambidexterity and the multiple ambidexterity mechanisms to explain diverse IS phenomena. For example, some studies adopted a structural ambi dexterity perspective to explain exploitation and exploration in IT project management (e.g., Vessey and Ward 2013). Other studies explain how firms achieve sequential ambidexterity in IS development by sequentially switching exploitation and exploration over time according to the status of innovation maturity (e.g., Lyytinen and Rose 2006); other studies adopted contextual ambidexterity to explain how firms achieve alignment and adaptability simultaneously in the IS governance and management (e.g., Ramesh et al. 2012) or in interfirm relations (e.g., Im and Rai 2008, 2014). Some studies extend sequential and structural ambidexterity (e.g., Cao et al. 2013, Aubert et al. 2015) to interfirm IT outsourcing, whereas some studies adopt a dynamic capability and agility view of ambidexterity for investigating IT infrastructure flexibility and the business-IT alignment for rapidly sensing and responding to changing environments (e.g., Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011, Chakravarty et al. 2013, Park et al. 2017). Some studies have proposed substantive theories on the ways to achieve IT ambidexterity (i.e., the ability to both exploit and explore IT resources and systems to support and facilitate businesses) (Gregory et al. 2015, Lee et al. 2015).

Summarizing the literature, it is clear that prior IS studies have developed new insights into diverse IS phenomena with all types of ambidexterity in diverse settings by applying and testing the well-defined ambidexterity mechanisms. Although prior IS studies shed light on the relationship between IT and organizational ambidexterity, there is a paucity of studies on explaining how digitization is aligned with other intrafirm and interfirm factors to produce organizational ambidexterity and what role digitization plays over different contexts and as a result what new mechanisms have emerged. We aim to bridge this knowledge gap by examining the role of digitization in achieving ambidexterity, especially structural ambidexterity. We specifically investigate the relationship between organizational structural ambidexterity and digitization defined as a firm’s effort and process to digitize its business processes by implementing, assimilating, and using information systems.<sup>2</sup> Particularly, we conceptualize a firm’s digitization in terms of its three key IT aspects in the definition: (a) IT spending on new IS implementation, (b) IT training to aid IS assimilation, and (c) IT usage intensity that captures the degree of actual IS usage. Thus, in this study, we use digitization as an umbrella term that represents the three key IT aspects observed in practice, and this conceptualization allows us to explain in detail how digitization (generally) and its three IT dimensions (specifically) matter for achieving organizational ambidexterity. In general, digitization encodes analog information into a digital format, and its key traits, such as reprogrammability, homogenization of data, and selfreference, have a significant impact on a firm’s innovation process (Yoo et al. 2010). By transforming analog data to digital, digitization triggers changes in a firm’s information infrastructure, enabling firms to store, analyze, recombine, share, and distribute data more easily, thus changing the process of decision making and innovation (e.g., Henfridsson and Bygstad 2013). Furthermore, the malleable, generative nature of digital infrastructure allows stakeholders to cocreate services and products with novel business models (Tilson et al. 2010) while also changing the organizing logic of the managerial rationale for designing specific arrangements in response to a firm’s environmental and strategic imperatives (Sambamurthy and Zmud 2000). Digitization can also provide firms with digital options (Sambamurthy et al. 2003) for effective communication and collaboration, thus enabling organizations to quickly sense and respond to environmental changes. Digitization is therefore likely to change the innovation process and organizational ambidexterity mechanisms.

In particular, our conceptualization of digitization with three IT dimensions, in contrast to viewing digitization as a unitary firm-level construct, can better represent a firm’s complex dynamics of digitization, which in turn helps us to explicate in detail the link between digitization and organizational ambidexterity. The three IT dimensions have been extensively studied in the IS literature, as we elaborate in detail below.

IT Spending on New IS Implementation. A firm’s IT spending on new IS implementation captures how much an organization is relying on IS, a key indicator of an organization’s digitization (Cooper and Zmud 1990). IT spending pertaining to new major IT implementations (unlike upgrades to existing information systems) indicate intensive episodes for firms, during which structures and business processes are often disrupted and new opportunities are created (Cooper and Zmud 1990). Studies on the impact of IT spending have generally focused on the potential of new IS for increasing exploitation as well as for increasing exploration (Hitt 1999, Sahaym et al. 2007, Karim and Kaul 2015). Dos Santos et al. (2012) explain a tension between IT investment in current IT applications to maintain efficient business and in new IT applications to capture future opportunities.

Implementing and assimilating major enterprise information systems enables firms to automate routine business processes to improve task efficiency and support integration across functional units within and between firms for flexible coordination, facilitating the exploitative operation and explorative innovation in customer relations, inventory management, and supply chain (Barki and Pinsonneault 2005 Karim et al. 2007). For example, implementing and assimilating enterprise systems, such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, supply-chain manage ment (SCM) systems, and customer relationship man agement (CRM) systems, enables firms to streamline business processes with seamless information and data flows within the firm and throughout the value chain from its suppliers to the firm to customers, changing the traditional ways of operating and innovating across firm boundaries. Digital boundary objects, such as application programming interfaces (API), electronic data interchanges (EDI), and standard electronic business interfaces (SEBI) support knowledge sharing by establishing standards for the representation and transfer of data. This further facilitates the interpretation of information, sharing of schemata and prototypes for new product development, and mutual discovery of new knowledge and adaptation to change (e.g., Scott 2000, Malhotra et al. 2007, Im and Rai 2008, Yoo et al. 2012).<sup>3</sup>

IT Training to Aid IS Assimilation. Training is a major way in which firms acquire new knowledge for exploitation and exploration (March 1991) and is an important factor enabling both operational efficiency and flexibility (Adler et al. 1999). IT training communicates a firm’s IT policies to personnel and helps individuals to integrate the best use of information systems for their tasks (e.g., Attewell 1992, Gupta and Bostrom 2013, Liang et al. 2013). Further, IT training is instrumental in accessing and applying new knowledge that compensates for the depreciation of employee IT skills and complements or replaces existing practices (e.g., Gaimon et al. 2011, Karim and Kaul 2015). Prior studies show how IT training can facilitate the adoption of new business processes as well as product innovation (e.g., Jasperson et al. 2005, Sharma and Yetton 2007). Accordingly, we build on this work to capture the contribution of IT training toward achieving ambidexterity.

IT Usage Intensity (Degree of Actual IS Usage). A firm can make financial commitments to new IT projects but may not harvest their full benefits without a sufficient effort to impart IT skills and enable actual use of IT by employees to execute their tasks. A greater number of employees who use information systems means a higher degree to which their tasks rely on information systems (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007); thus, IT usage intensity represents the success of new IT implementation (DeLone and McLean 1992, Venkatesh et al. 2003), an important aspect of digitization that directly affects exploitation and exploration. The literature espouses the benefits of such targeted IT usage on various metrics of exploitation and exploration, such as product development (Nambisan 2003, Pavlou and El Sawy 2006) and learning (Kane and Alavi 2007).

Although some information systems incur low IT spending (e.g., open source CRM), they require significant effort by managers to train employees and incentivize usage, and other information systems require a large upfront expenditure (e.g., Salesforce), but they demand less effort in terms of IT assimilation and IT training. Thus, these three IT dimensions of digitization, while distinct, are interrelated, which means that to reap the benefits of digitization, firms frequently need to focus on more than one of these digitization dimensions in conjunction with other organizational factors. However, less is known about how the three digitization dimensions individually— or, more importantly, simultaneously—affect the ways to achieve organizational ambidexterity with other intrafirm and interfirm factors in different contexts.

## Overarching Theoretical Framework for Achieving Structural Ambidexterity with Digitization

Our theoretical framework focuses on explaining how the three IT factors interact with various intrafirm and interfirm factors to enable firms to achieve organizational structural ambidexterity (i.e., ability to simultaneously pursue both exploitation and exploration in a structural way). This is accomplished by integrating not only internally available resources but also externally available resources through strategic alliances. Thus, in this study, we posit that the role of digitization in achieving ambidexterity may be more effectively explained through the interactions of the three IT aspects with other intrafirm and interfirm factors under different contingencies (e.g., Zammuto et al. 2007), which can be best supported by a configurational approach (El Sawy et al. 2010).

Our theoretical development draws upon the strategic management and organizational learning literatures (e.g., Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008, Lavie et al. 2010, O’Reilly and Tushman 2013) that propose exploitative and explorative learning for a variation-reducing strategy for efficiency and a variation-increasing strat egy for effectiveness, both of which are simultaneously needed for achieving organizational structural ambidexterity. Given the novelty and complexity of the relationship between digitization and structural ambidexterity, in this study, we aim to build a new theory rather than test existing theories by adopting an abductive (aka retroductive) theory-building approach that leverages the benefits of both the deductive and the inductive theory development approaches (e.g., Ragin 1994, Bensaou and Venkatraman 1995, Misangyi and Acharya 2014, Park et al. 2017). Following this approach, besides the three IT factors, we select and conceptualize key intrafirm and interfirm factors based on extant studies on structural ambidexterity, with which we develop an overarching theoretical framework as depicted in Figure 1. With this framework, we empirically analyze data. Then, based on the findings, we develop theoretical propositions in the form of configurational recipes that explain how to configure the three IT factors with other intrafirm and interfirm factors to achieve organizational ambidexterity under different contexts. Thus, with this abductive approach, a detailed explanation of how we integrate all the concepts and their theoretical relationships is given in the proposition development in the Recipes for Achieving Ambidexterity with Digitization section.

## Intra<sup>fi</sup>rm, Inter<sup>fi</sup>rm, and Contingency Factors for Structural Ambidexterity

Structural ambidexterity represents a mechanism that firms pursue simultaneously both exploitation and exploration by integrating not only internally available resources but also externally available resources through strategic alliances. For example, Holmqvist (2004) explains that intrafirm exploitative and explorative learning can drive interfirm exploitative and explorative learning, and, conversely, learning from interfirm alliances can be applied to intrafirm learning. Gupta et al. (2006) argue that organizations in dynamic environments need to focus on explorative learning and gain exploitative knowledge from other firms that face stable environments and focus on exploitative learning.

In structural ambidexterity, a firm’s exploitation and exploration imply enterprise-wide processes and activities that require both intrafirm and interfirm resource allocation and structural changes. Thus, in reality, numerous factors can be related to a firm’s exploitation and exploration in some ways either directly or indirectly. However, because it is neither realistic nor valid to include all variables in a single study, especially with a configurational approach, it is important to select only key variables based on the structural ambidexterity literature that is known to be most relevant for the focus of the study (Ragin 2008, Greckhamer et al. 2013). Thus, we choose the three key constructs based on the well-developed literature on organizational structural ambidexterity and the link between digitization and structural ambidexterity, including centralization, intrafirm collaboration, and interfirm strategic alliance (Zmud 1982 Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999, Benner and Tushman 2003, Im and Rai 2008, Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008 Lavie et al. 2011, O’Reilly and Tushman 2013).

Centralization/cecentralization is a key variable in the structural ambidexterity literature (e.g., O’Reilly and Tushman 2013), representing how business units are functionally integrated (e.g., Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008). Higher centralization indicates the locus of authority and decision making is concentrated, with high centralization often associated with exploitative behavior and narrow communication channels for new information (e.g., O’Reilly and Tushman 2013). Centralization facilitates administrative efficiency, whereas decentralization facilitates innovation (Zmud 1982). Studies on the dual structure approach for intrafirm ambidexterity have treated centralization/ decentralization as a key structural determinant. This is because decentralized small business units are better able to conduct exploration, whereas centralized large units are better poised for exploitative activities due to scale efficiency (Benner and Tushman 2003, Gupta et al. 2006). In the IS literature, centralization and decentralization also represent IT governance structures related to the distribution of deci sion rights and control about IT budget and spending and how IT is aligned, deployed, and managed within a firm, either in a centralized or decentralized manner (e.g., Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999). Thus, centralization can potentially affect the role of digitization in ambidexterity. We thus examine how the three dimensions of digitization that are linked to cen tralization help organizations achieve ambidexterity.

Intra<sup>fi</sup>rm Collaboration. The literature suggests that collaboration across business units helps to overcome the difficulty in achieving and sustaining structural ambidexterity. Because these two trajectories of exploitation versus exploration diverge in terms of support structures, culture, and knowledge processes (Benner and Tushman 2003), there is competition for limited resources in the firm for pursuit of either exploitation or exploration (Levinthal and March 1993, March 1991). Thus, collaboration helps business units overcome the tension arising from this competition. For example, harmonious relationships between labor and management, and among employees, have often been positively linked to innovation (Andries and Czarnitzki 2014), and dense social relations are shown to increase firm innovativeness in competitive environments (Hansen et al. 2005).

## Inter<sup>fi</sup>rm Strategic Alliances

The literature recognizes the importance of strategic alliances for achieving ambidexterity, which extends the traditional intrafirm structural mechanisms (Lavie et al. 2011). Interfirm strategic alliances can reduce the tension within business units for simultaneously pursuing exploration and exploitation by complementing or substituting limited internal resources with external ones. Interfirm alliances enable access to external resources and knowledge that may be scarce within the firm for exploitation or exploration (Lavie et al. 2011). The value chain function of interfirm strategic partners can vary from those who provide new knowledge to those who refine existing knowledge. Thus, strategic alliances can foster both explorative and exploitative learning (Gulati 1998). For example, strategic alliances for research and development (R&D) typically represent explorative activities for creating new products and services, whereas alliances for marketing and production result in increased efficiency and refinement of extant products and processes (Rothaermel and Deeds 2004, Lavie et al. 2011). Only a few IS studies show how information systems can help firms achieve ambidexterity in interfirm relations (Im and Rai 2008) or how firms use IT outsourcing to achieve ambidexterity (Cao et al. 2013). Accordingly, we seek to add new insights by examining how the three IT dimensions of digitization are related with interfirm strategic alliance in achieving structural ambidexterity.

Contingency Factors for Ambidexterity. Prior studies have emphasized the importance of studying the effects of contingency factors and point out that firm size and the competitive environment have a potentially significant impact on the relationship between digitization and structural ambidexterity (Lee et al. 2013) as well as on the relationship between market factors and structural ambidexterity (Cao et al. 2009, Lavie et al. 2011).

Firm size is salient in the literature as a key structural contingency factor that plays a major role in the mechanisms to achieve ambidexterity (Benner and Tushman 2003, Gupta et al. 2006, Cao et al. 2009,

Lavie et al. 2011). Size affects firm structure, allocation of resources and authority, and information processing and decision making (Galbraith 1973, Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999). Strategic decision making for exploration and exploitation requires information processing and collaboration, not only within a single business unit, but also across business units. The interdependency and complexity in this process increases as firm size increases (Galbraith 1973, Daft and Lengel 1986). Size has been used to examine whether pursuing either exploitative, efficiencyoriented or explorative, flexibility-oriented strategies outperform those that seek to pursue both, the latter being akin to structural ambidexterity (e.g., Ebben and Johnson 2005). Thus, we assume that configurational recipes for firms to align digitization with intrafirm and interfirm factors to achieve ambidexterity can be different, depending on firm size, and we specifically examine its interdependencies with digitization to achieve structural ambidexterity.

Competitive Market Intensity. The literature has also treated competitive intensity as a key contingency factor that affects structural ambidexterity due to its direct relationship with resources that fundamentally creates the tension between exploration and exploi tation (Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008, O’Reilly and Tushman 2013). Greater competitive market intensity refers to more firms competing for limited resources. This implies limited growth opportunities (March 1991, Auh and Menguc 2005), explaining why the tension between exploitation and exploration tends to increase when market competition is intense. In highly competitive markets, new products face a higher risk of competition and crowding-out. This also reduces the ability to price-discriminate, thus reducing growth from existing markets. Accordingly, intense competition in the market likely puts pressure on organizations to pursue ambidexterity (Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008, Lavie et al. 2010). Prior research has included different environmental constructs, such as environmental munificence and dynamism, but all these share the common logic that is explained above in terms of limited resources for exploration and exploitation.

## Research Method

This study focuses on structural ambidexterity as an outcome that results from a systemic structural arrangement of our focal theoretical constructs—three IT factors of digitization and intrafirm and interfirm factors under different contingencies. Inferring such systemic arrangements among multiple elements is infeasible with the traditional deductive correlation-based approaches (Fiss 2007, El Sawy et al. 2010). Therefore, we herein use a fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), a set-theoretic configurational method (Ragin 2008, Fiss 2011) that enables us to investigate how multiple elements combine into multiple configurations to produce ambidexterity. An advantage of fsQCA is that it essentially implements the concept of equifinality, which posits that different systemic arrangements of constructs can be equally leading to the same outcome. Thus, fsQCA allows us to explain how organizations can become ambidextrous via multiple configurations (Fiss 2007, 2011, Meyer et al. 1993).<sup>5</sup> In IS studies, fsQCA has been increasingly adopted to capture the complex nature of digital phenomena (e.g., Fichman 2004, El Sawy et al. 2010, Rivard and Lapointe 2012, Dawson et al. 2016, Lee et al. 2019, Park and Mithas 2020). A common premise of all studies using QCA is that key elements are interdependent with each other and that the outcome is better explained by their simultaneous combinations, rather than by any individual element in isolation, which is best suited to supporting the main purpose of this study. QCA can measure and treat a fuzzy-set membership of each case in a certain condition (e.g., set of organizations with high IT spending) or outcome (e.g., organizations with high ambidexterity) (Ragin 2008). The set membership, then, allows us to examine the relationships between configurational elements (i.e., the eight variables we examine in this study) and outcome of interest (i.e., structural ambidexterity) in terms of their set–subset relations. This eventually leads us to identify which elements or set of elements is necessary or sufficient for achieving ambidexterity. A full explanation of fsQCA, which is beyond the scope of this study, can be found in articles elsewhere (e.g., Fiss 2007, Ragin 2008, Rihoux and Ragin 2009). We describe the data and calibration process of set memberships for all variables (with the truth-table analysis, an important step of fsQCA explained in detail in Online Appendix B).

## Sample

We used a longitudinal data set collected from the Workplace and Employee Survey (WES) conducted by Statistics Canada. The sample frame of WES was generated based on Statistics Canada’s Business Register, and we focused on firms (workplaces) with active employees. Each workplace was sent a copy of a survey instrument by Statistics Canada to inform respondents ahead of time and allow them to locate the relevant information for the survey items before being interviewed. Then, one manager from each firm completed the questionnaire during a personal interview conducted by a Statistics Canada staff member. The response rates for WES in the period of 2003 to 2006 ranged from 95.2% to 85.9%, which largely reflected the in-person interview survey approach and the extensive legal protections under the Statistics Act of Canada that ensure the confidentiality of responses. Several studies used the WES data (e.g., Zatzick and Iverson 2006). From the full data, we selected firms that were present in the panel in each of the four years (2003 to 2006)<sup>6</sup> and implemented new IT projects so that all cases have values for our focal three IT variables, thus supporting our research goals. We excluded firms that were classified as very small (<20 employees) based on the Business and Labor Market Analysis categories, and we discarded nonprofit, public firms with missing or invariant data for our principal variables. This sampling process yielded a sample of 1,325 firms over four years, a total of 5,300 data points.

## Measures and Calibration of Set Memberships

We calibrated fuzzy-set memberships of cases in each element and outcome, which transforms the value of each variable for a case into a membership score in a set of elements or outcomes. The value of membership resulting from the calibration process ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates full nonmembership, 1 indicates full membership, and 0.5 indicates crossover, the most ambiguous point as neither in nor out of a set. For example, fsQCA allows us to measure a set membership of a case with ambidexterity as a continuous value between 0 and 1—near zero meaning less likely to be a member of the set of ambidexterity and near one meaning more likely to be an ambidextrous organization. Calibration requires a researcher to decide such three criteria for full membership, crossover, and full nonmembership that reflects relevant theories grounded on the study context (Fiss 2007, Ragin 2008). We decided on three criteria for calibrating ambidexterity and the eight elements based on the literature (as we elaborate in the Conceptual Development), the WES survey questionnaire and data, and descriptive statistics as we explain below.

Ambidexterity. Over four years, we measured each firm’s ambidexterity. Specifically, we used four items related to the development of new products and processes that add variety (i.e., exploration), and the refinement of existing products and processes that focus on operational efficiency (i.e., exploitation) (e.g., Tushman and O’Reilly 1996, Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004, Cao et al. 2009). Following the literature (e.g., March 1991, Cao et al. 2009, He and Wong 2004), for each year, we classified a firm as ambidextrous if it both developed new products or processes and re fined its existing products or processes simultaneously. Thus, if a firm reported both exploitation and exploration, its ambidexterity was coded as “1” for that year. Then, over the four years, we summed the ambidexterity measure, of which values ranged from 0 to 4. This longitudinal data approach enabled us to effectively measure a firm’s ambidexterity over time. Finally, to calibrate the firm’s fuzzy membership into the set of ambidextrous organizations, we used the sum value ranging from 0 to 4. First, we defined the sum value 3 as the anchor for full membership in ambidexterity, meaning that if a firm had achieved ambidexterity for at least three out of the four years, then it was considered fully ambidextrous. Second, we defined 1 as the crossover point, meaning neither in nor out of the set of ambidextrous organizations. Third, we defined 0 as the anchor for full nonmembership, meaning the organization was not at all ambidextrous. With these three anchors, we calibrated the set membership of all firms using the direct method of calibration (Ragin 2008).

Following our literature review, there are multiple ways to measure ambidexterity and on-going disputes regarding each measurement’s validity, including the difficulty in measuring pure exploitation or exploration (e.g., a refinement of product could be viewed as exploration, whereas a new innovative product could be viewed as exploitation across different contexts). We define and operationalize exploration and exploitation based on seminal studies that have been typically adopted in the main streams of ambidexterity research. By doing so, our measurement of ambidexterity has some benefits by overcoming some limitations in the literature. First, our measurement of ambidexterity captures both the development of new products/processes and the refinement of extant products/processes simultaneously, and thus only a firm that achieved both new development and refinement is considered ambidextrous. This way of measurement can overcome some concern over the differentiation between exploration and exploitation. Second, unlike studies that used managers’ perceptions, our ambidexterity was measured with data on a firm’s actual explorative and exploitative activities, as reported by its management in a government survey. Third, our measurement reflects a firm’s capability to be ambidextrous over several years, which captures a firm’s ability to sustain ambidexterity over time, not just at one point of time, thus implicitly reflecting a firm’s dynamic capability. Lastly, the calibration allows our measure to have benefits from both quantitative and qualitative measurement—the number of ambidextrous years adds quantitative objectivity, and the fuzzy-set membership score calculated based on three anchors for ambidexterity can define a clearer boundary between ambidextrous and nonambidextrous firms. For example, no firm in the data set being ambidextrous for more than three years implies that there is no firm that can be fully qualified as ambidextrous, and thus the fsQCA results will show no valid configurations for achieving ambidexterity.

Compared with our measurement, extant studies do not have such detail. For example, an interaction term with a multiplication of two exploration and exploration measures, which has been typically used in ambidexterity studies (e.g., Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004, He and Wong 2004, Lee et al. 2015), does not show a similar clear, qualitative meaning. Further, if there is no actual case with ambidexterity in the data set, then a typical correlation-based measurement would extrapolate ambidexterity based on the linear tendency of the sample data, which is not the case of fsQCA. These characteristics of our calibration on ambidexterity and other elements support our argument that, although it is not directly used for the IS design for specific IT functionalities, our theory in the form of configurational recipes is both explanatory and prescriptive for firms (Gregor 2006) and would guide them toward configuring digitization elements with other intrafirm and interfirm elements to achieve ambidexterity.

Digitization Elements—IT Spending. IT spending was measured as the average of total IT implementation cost per employee over the four-year period. These included only new and major IS implementations (not upgrades). Based on the descriptive statistics given in Online Appendix C, we define \$2,000 spending for IT per employee (top 5% of firms in terms of IT spending) as the anchor for full membership in the set of high IT spending firms, \$20 per employee (bottom 5% of our cases) as the anchor for full nonmembership, and \$200 per employee (around the 50th percentile) as F the crossover.

IT Training. To quantify IT training, we used number of years out of the focal four-year span during which a firm hosted formal IT training as our measure. That is, we defined a firm as having full membership in the set of high IT training if the firm reported having an IT training program for at least three out of the four years. We classified a firm having full nonmembership in the set if it did not have IT training, and one year as the crossover.

IT Usage Intensity. We measured IT usage intensity as the ratio of the number of employees who use information systems for their main tasks over the total number of employees, averaged over four years. We calibrated this condition by setting the 95th percentile as the anchor of full membership for highly intensive IT usage, the 10th percentile as the anchor of full non membership, and the 50th percentile as the crossover.

Intra<sup>fi</sup>rm Collaboration. Two measures capture the strategic emphasis placed on internal mechanisms on a 1–5 Likert scale. The items asked if the firm enhanced labor management cooperation and if it increased employees’ involvement and participation. These items were averaged over 2003 to 2006 on a fivepoint Likert scale (1 = not important, 5 = crucial). We define the interval scale 4 (very important) as the full membership anchor for the set of high intrafirm collaboration, 1 (not important) as the anchor for full nonmembership, and 2 (slightly important) as the crossover. Then, the average value of the firm’s intrafirm collaboration variable was then transformed into a fuzzy-set membership score based on these three anchors.

Centralization. Two binary measures capture changes in centralization each year, referring to the integration among functional areas, and the increased degree of centralization. We summed the measures over four years. The full membership threshold was set at 3, full nonmembership at 0, and the crossover at 1.<sup>8</sup>

Inter<sup>fi</sup>rm Strategic Alliances. Studies on strategic alliances show that interfirm collaboration for R&D generally represents exploration, whereas collaboration for production and marketing generally represents exploitation (e.g., Rothaermel and Deeds 2004, Lavie et al. 2011). Thus, we used two items that measure for each year whether a firm had greater interfirm activities in R&D, production and marketing, and the degree of reliance on external suppliers of products/services. These were averaged over the four-year period—therefore, the summated value ranged from 0 to 4. The value of 3 was used as a threshold for the high interfirm strategic alliance set, 0 for full nonmembership, and 1 as the threshold for crossover.

Firm Size. Firm size was measured by the total number of employees, averaged over the four-year period. Following the definition of firm size made by the Business and Labour Market Analysis Division (BLMA), Statistics Canada, we defined 500 employees as the anchor for full membership in the large size, 100 employees for the crossover, and 20 for full nonmembership.

Competitive Market Intensity. This was measured by the number of competitors in the market. We used a variable in the survey to capture the number of competitors (1 = 0 competitor, 2 = 1–5 competitors, 3 = 6–20 competitors, 4 = more than 20 competitors). For calibration, we used 4 as the threshold for ful membership in the set of highly competitive environ ments, 1 for full nonmembership, and 2 as the crossover. Table 1 shows examples of the values of variables and the corresponding set membership scores after calibration for the conditions and outcome. In Online Appendix D, we summarize the details of this calibration process for all variables. After calibrating each case with the three set-membership anchors, a truthtable algorithm in fsQCA identifies configurations of causal conditions (i.e., our eight elements) that produce the outcome of interest (i.e., ambidexterity), which is explained in detail in Online Appendix B.

The results show multiple configurations that consistently produce high ambidexterity. The six configurations include core elements that are elements in the parsimonious solution resulting from counterfactual analysis and thus have a stronger causal relationship with the outcome. ## Recipes for Achieving Ambidexterity with Digitization

Next, we seek to theoretically interpret these empirical results of multiple configurations and offer a set of propositions. Based on the two main patterns (i.e., intrafirm vs. interfirm solution, and small vs. large firms) and our theoretical background, we first propose a contingency framework that allows us to integrate our key findings in a systemic way to effectively explicate theoretical links between digitization and structural ambidexterity, as depicted in Figure 4.

This framework allows us to build a context-specific middle-range theory in the form of configurational propositions to elaborate the ways to configure digitization with other intrafirm and interfirm elements to achieve ambidexterity under different contingencies. The first key pattern that we found with fsQCA results was that there are two general alternative ways of configuring digitization that firms can choose to achieve organizational structural ambidexterity: (1) an intrafirm configuration solution, or (2) an interfirm configurational solution. To theoretically interpret this finding, prior research on ambidexterity suggests a loosely coupled dual structure, which separates business units that focus on exploration from units that focus on exploitation, as a way to relieve the tension within a firm. However, such structurally separate business units cannot be purely independent from each other when it comes to the competition for limited resources, and thus the need to manage the tension between exploitation and exploration still remains. Our intrafirm configurational solution, reflected in C1, C2, and C3 configurations (Figure 2 and the left column of Figure 4), explains how firms can achieve ambidexterity internally by configuring digitization with intrafirm elements in a way to balance explorative and exploitative activities.

Another stream of research in interfirm relations has suggested strategic alliances as a means to extend structural ambidexterity to the interfirm level by using partner firms to build a dual structure (Rothaermel and Deeds 2004, Im and Rai 2008, Lavie et al. 2011) Interfirm strategic alliances enable firms to access external resources that are unavailable internally and thus reduce the tension for simultaneously pursuing exploration and exploitation (Lavie et al. 2011). Also, strategic alliances can foster both explorative and exploitative learning (Gulati 1998). For example, explorative R&D collaborative strategic alliances enable the firm to gain or create new knowledge, products, and processes, whereas exploitative collaboration for marketing and production involves sharing and integrating extant knowledge among firms, resulting in a refinement of the focal firm’s products and processes (e.g., Holmqvist 2004, Rothaermel and Deeds 2004). Our interfirm configurational solution, reflected in C4a, C4b, and C4c (Figure 2 and right column of Figure 4), reveals a way to achieve ambidexterity through interfirm strategic alliances. Furthermore, our findings showed strong evidence of substitution between these two solutions, implying that a firm should choose between an intrafirm solution and an interfirm alliance solution to achieve structural ambidexterity, but it may be difficult for a firm to simultaneously pursue both solutions.

Besides these two key contingency patterns (Figure 4), we show that intrafirm collaboration is a necessary condition for ambidexterity, implying that it should be included in all configurations that achieve structural ambidexterity. Intrafirm collaboration drives employees to actively participate in knowledge sharing among business units, thus enabling the recombination of extant knowledge and the creation of new knowledge (Kogut and Zander 1992, Karim and Kaul 2015). It also creates a context where trust between departments can be accumulated (Adler et al. 1999, Im and Ra 2014), which in turn helps to relieve the tension between exploration and exploitation in the competition for the limited resources available within the firm. Our findings also reveal that, as the market becomes more competitive, intrafirm collaboration becomes more important for firms to achieve ambidexterity. In a competitive market, there is intense competition for limited resources, thus making it more difficult for firms to launch new products due to the higher risk of premature product failure, eventually resulting in lower growth opportunities (Auh and Menguc 2005, March 1991). Therefore, in a competitive market, internal and external resources are more likely to be limited, thereby making collaboration among firms and their business units critical to harmonizing exploration and exploitation. Whereas intrafirm collaboration, by itself, cannot sufficiently achieve ambidexterity, without intrafirm collaboration, organizations cannot achieve ambidexterity, particularly in highly competitive markets.

## Con<sup>fi</sup>gurational Solutions for Achieving Organizational Ambidexterity with Digitization

Based on the contingency framework, we now the oretically explain these key findings to build propositions. First, the intrafirm configurational solution explains how firms achieve structural ambidexterity internally with a configuration in which centralization and digitization play a core role and intrafirm collaboration is a necessary condition. This intrafirm solution explains the key role of digitization in complementing a firm’s centralized structure, as well as managing the tension between exploitation and exploration within the firm. In our findings, centralization appears to indicate the existence of an exploitation, efficiency tendency. The literature explains that various structural mechanisms are beneficial for achieving structural ambidexterity in which centralization reinforces exploitative activities and subdues explorative ones (Jansen et al. 2006, 2009). Intrafirm collaboration helps firms address such tendency of centralization by supporting between-units knowledge sharing and trust building, thus reinforcing explorative activities and innovation (Kogut and Zander 1992, Karim and Kaul 2015). Besides such a positive impact of intrafirm collaboration on exploration, collaboration in a centralized structure supported by IT systems also helps to make between-unit processes more efficient.

Importantly, our findings reveal the essential role of digitization in the intrafirm configurational solution. Our theoretical interpretation is that digitization enables firms to better leverage centralization and collaboration simultaneously to achieve ambidexterity. IT systems enable seamless knowledge flows and facilitate employees’ active participation in recombination for new innovations, thus enhancing collaboration among units, which in turn enhances flexibility (Sahaym et al. 2007, Karim and Kaul 2015, Park et al. 2017) so that firms can complement the tendency to be biased toward efficiency with centralization. There can be some synergy among them since centralization together with digitization can enable organizations to more effectively integrate the diverse knowledge across different domains and units, thereby increasing exploration as well as exploitation.

By delving into the three dimensions of digitization, we explain in further detail the digitally enabled ambidexterity mechanisms behind our configurational solutions. First, IT spending means more slack resources, which allow firms to implement new information systems. Implementation of major IT systems, such as ERP systems with a central database and digital infrastructure, provides a bundle of IT functionalities that can automate routine business processes, visualize data for decision making, and enable seamless information flows among business units (e.g., Barki and Pinsonneault 2005, Pavlou and El Sawy 2006, Karim et al. 2007), thus enhancing the firm’s intrafirm collaboration. However, it is costly to implement such large-scale information systems that can provide IT functionalities for both exploitation and exploration. Therefore, intrafirm solutions (i.e., engaging in exploration and exploration alone with digitization) requires firms to have high IT spending.

Second, implementing new major IT systems requires firms to change their existing business processes (Karim et al. 2007), and thus they need to train employees to learn and use new functionalities provided by their information systems. Studies have illustrated the key role of IT training in communicating a firm’s IT policies to personnel and helping employees to assimilate the effective IT use for their tasks (Gupta and Bostrom 2013, Liang et al. 2013), thus enhancing exploitation. IT training goes beyond a one-shot training effort, and it represents a firm’s efforts over time to bring new external knowledge, such as industry best practices and emerging technologies to complement existing knowledge (Karim and Kaul 2015), thus increasing the potential for effective exploration. First, employees are imparted the requisite knowledge about IT functionalities and how their existing tasks can be automated or supported by the new IT systems. Second, IT training can render a comprehensive understanding of the business processes with which an employee is engaged, even if that spans multiple functional areas (Baskerville et al. 2000), thus initiating more active knowledge transfer and sharing across business units. Third, IT training also imparts knowledge about how the technology can be adapted to new business conditions and exposes future users to the knowledge held by other users, thus facilitating interactions among them and resulting in the exchange of richer information about IT (Santhanam et al. 2007). Thus, IT training augments the business skills of the IT staff and the IT skills of the business staff, which in turn facilitates greater use of IS and a better understanding of domain knowledge (Robey et al. 2002, Sharma and Yetton 2007). The literature espouses the benefits of such targeted IT usage on various performance metrics related to exploitation and exploration, such as new product development (e.g., Nambisan 2003, Pavlou and El Sawy 2006). Furthermore, a greater use of IT systems means a higher degree to which a firm’s tasks rely on

IT systems (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, Malhotra et al. 2007), and thus actual IT use is likely to influence the mechanisms for achieving ambidexterity.

This pattern is more applicable in large firms. As our findings show, large firms enjoy more benefits from digitization in achieving structural ambidexterity because of the greater need and use of IT sys tems for information processing when dealing with organizational complexity (Daft and Lengel 1986). Employees of large firms are likely to have more specialized knowledge in specific domains, thus increasing the potential for exploitation. Additionally, many processes span multiple domains, and business units in larger firms are more likely to be siloed, inhibiting information from flowing across the firm and making it difficult to integrate cross-functional business units. IT functions for communication and collaboration can help to tackle such problems in large firms to help in their pursuit of structural ambidexterity (e.g., Daft and Lengel 1986, Park et al. 2017).

On the other hand, our findings show that in firms pursuing interfirm strategic alliance solutions, digitization plays a peripheral, supportive role in complementing a large firm’s strategic alliances activities for exploration and exploitation, such as R&D, marketing, and manufacturing (Rothaermel and Deeds 2004, Lavie et al. 2011). For the interfirm solution, a firm does not necessarily implement and use IS to enable both exploitation and exploration with high IT spending. However, for large firms to overcome the complexity due to their large size, the same logic of IT training and IT usage for the intrafirm solution applies to the interfirm solution. Large firms need to provide their employees with regular IT training to better utilize IS specifically for their collaboration with external partners, as well as for intrafirm collaboration.

In sum, the implementation and actual use of IT systems enables efficient business processes and effective channels of communication and information exchange that complement the tendency of exploitation from centralization. IT training increases employees’ efficiency in their tasks, compensates for the depreciation of employee skills and increases knowledge sharing for explorative activities (Sahaym et al. 2007, Sharma and Yetton 2007, Gaimon et al. 2011). The role of digitization in complementing both collaboration and centralization is more pronounced in large firms, appearing in all intrafirm and interfirm configurational solutions in large firms.

Based on these empirical findings and our theoretical logic, we posit the following propositions of a configurational recipe for large firms to achieve organizational structural ambidexterity with digitization:

Proposition 1. Large firms can achieve structural ambidexterity with either the intrafirm solution that builds internally congruent configurations or the interfirm solution that relies on strategic alliances. Intrafirm collaboration is a necessary condition for both the intrafirm and interfirm solutions to achieve structural ambidexterity especially in highly competitive markets.

Proposition 2. When large firms pursue structural ambidexterity by following the intrafirm solution, they must configure a high level of IT spending, IT training, and IT usage intensity to complement collaboration and centralization and effectively handle the complexity due to their size.

Proposition 3. For the interfirm solution, rather than implementing new information systems, large firms should focus on IT training and active IT use for supporting their collaborative activities for exploitation and exploration with their strategic partners.

Compared with large organizations, our findings show that configurational recipes for small organizations to achieve ambidexterity can be different from those for large organizations. First, our findings support that the same theoretical logic of the intrafirm solution mostly applies to smaller firms. The intrafirm solution consisting of centralization and digitization also requires smaller firms to implement extensive IT functionalities with high IT spending and to use IT systems intensively to support both exploration and exploitation. However, small firms do not need to have intensive IT training due to having simpler structures compared to large firms. Smaller firms have a less complex structure regarding the allocation of resources and authority, and the related information processing and decision-making processes that they have are simpler than those of large firms (Galbraith 1973, Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999). In smaller firms, a small number of managers may participate in the information processing and decision-making, and they can have face-to-face group meetings more easily than in large firms. Thus, smaller firms may not need to have intensive IT training for supporting simpler decisionmaking and task structure.

For the interfirm solution, a high level of digitization should be absent in smaller firms, as only in large firms is digitization expected to help the firm cope with the complexity created by firm size on the interfirm alliances for exploitative and explorative activities. We then explain the underlying theoretical mechanisms for the interfirm solution between small firms versus large firms. Some studies showed that digitization is related to a decrease in communication costs with external partners, in particular when there are many channels between multiple business units, and thus digitization can facilitate more effective interfirm activities in strategic alliances among large organizations (Hitt 1999, Sahaym et al. 2007). However, this trend does not explain whether IS enables or constrains the allied firms to achieve ambidexterity, especially when it comes to smaller firms, because in reality, digitization can also play a dysfunctional role in interfirm strategic alliances, as we explain next.

Despite major advancements, current communication technologies still have limits in conveying rich information versus face-to-face communication, following media richness theory (Daft and Lengel 1986). Digitization can standardize and automate many interfirm collaborative routines, thus increasing efficiency. However, digitization may reduce flexible forms and personal contacts. Although information systems have smoothened knowledge sharing within firms, they are still encoded with firm-specific knowledge processes. These rigidities may make it difficult to adjust for a wider range of events and exceptions, thereby precluding richer forms of communication and collaboration between firms and precluding trust among partners. This is truer for small firms who have fewer resources and need to rely more on external resources, but they have less power relative to larger firms that enjoy more resources. In sum, these technology limitations may hurt a smaller firm’s ability to achieve ambidexterity, especially when it pursues ambidexterity with interfirm strategic alliances. These negative effects can be further amplified or at tenuated under specific contingencies (e.g., Majchrzak and Markus 2013). Hence, small firms do not need to have a high level of digitization for interfirm strategic alliances to achieve ambidexterity. Small firms need not replace core collaborative activities with digitized automation of their interfirm routines, but only they could use IS to support core collaborative activities within the firm. We thus posit the following propositions.

Proposition 4. Small firms can achieve structural ambidexterity with either the intrafirm or the interfirm solution, for both of which intrafirm collaboration is a necessary condition for achieving structural ambidexterity.

Proposition 5. When small firms pursue structural ambidexterity following the intrafirm solution, they need to configure a high level of IT spending and IT usage intensity to complement collaboration and centralization

Proposition 6. When choosing interfirm strategic alliances to achieve structural ambidexterity, small firms should avoid a high level of digitization across all three dimensions of digitization.

## Conclusion

## Contributions and Implications for Theory and Practice

This study contributes to both the IS strategy literature and the organizational ambidexterity, particularly, structural ambidexterity literature by extending the traditional organizational ambidexterity framework in strategic management and developing new mechanisms that leverage digitization to achieve ambidexterity. Our overarching conceptual framework, not only integrates digitization with key intrafirm factors that are prevalent in the structural ambidexterity literature, but it also extends structural ambidexterity to the interfirm level by integrating strategic alliances along with key contingency factors from a configurational perspective. The integrative framework enabled us to empirically validate that digitization significantly affects the traditional structural mechanisms and leverages digitally enabled mechanisms to achieve ambidexterity. Whereas extant studies mostly identified mechanisms for achieving ambidexterity as those that separate exploration and exploitation structurally or temporally, our findings suggest that digitization plays an important role in achieving ambidexterity without such separation of explorative or exploitative activities in time and space. In particular, for the intrafirm approach, digitization plays an essential role for both large and small firms in achieving structural ambidexterity by complementing centralization and collaboration between business units. Therefore, this study makes a new theoretical contribution by revealing the important role of digitization in achieving structural ambidexterity, implying that, in today’s pervasively digitized environment, firms cannot ignore digitization when they aim to simultaneously pursue exploration and exploitation. Furthermore, our findings show that the three IT dimensions of digitization (i.e., IT spending, IT training, and IT usage) are indeed viewed as theoretically distinct but interrelated to each other, playing different roles in the multiple viable configurations to achieve ambidexterity as either present or absent and either core or peripheral conditions.

Second, extant studies on organizational ambidexterity in the management and IS literatures have mostly focused on the net effects of individual elements on ambidexterity rather than their interdependent holistic effects on ambidexterity. For example, in the structural ambidexterity literature, centralization and decentralization have been focal variables for explaining ambidexterity (Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999, Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008, O’Reilly and Tushman 2013), showing how firms achieve ambidexterity by vacillating between a centralized and decentralized form over time (e.g., Boumgarden et al. 2012). However, by adopting a configurational theory perspective, we empirically show that no single factor alone, either centralization or digitization, is sufficient to produce structural ambidexterity. Rather, it is the interdependent interactions between the key factors that together achieve ambidexterity. Thus, structural ambidexterity is better explained by how digitization can be aligned with several intrafirm and interfirm factors, rather than by any individual factors in isolation, showing the importance of considering the complex interdependencies among digitization and multiple intrafirm and interfirm factors for studying structural ambidexterity in today’s fused digitized world (Zammuto et al. 2007, El Sawy et al. 2010, Bharadwaj et al. 2013). Also, in the am bidexterity literature, centralization has been treated as a typical characteristic of mechanistic structure to support organizational exploitation, but it is known to be difficult to reconcile with an organic structure, which better supports exploration (Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008). Our paper adds to this literature by focusing on digitization, and our findings inform this stream of theorization by suggesting that digi tization enables organizations to overcome the limi tation of centralized structure, in facilitating not only exploitation, but also exploration, thus rendering a higher degree of ambidexterity. Conversely, decen tralization allows individual units to be more responsive and hence alludes to the exploration benefits (Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999), although, at the same time, it is limited in delivering exploitation benefits (Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008). Interestingly, our findings did not reveal any viable pathway whereby digitization can alleviate this limitation and facil itate ambidexterity under decentralization. Thus, we call for further research that would investigate how digitization can support decentralized organizations to effectively pursue both exploration and exploitation simultaneously and accordingly achieve structural ambidexterity.

Third, in the structural ambidexterity literature, there has been ongoing discussion on whether interfirm or intrafirm approaches to ambidexterity are complements or substitutes (e.g., Kauppila 2010, Lavie et al. 2011) and what the role of digitization is in either one or both of these mechanisms for achieving ambidexterity (e.g., Im and Rai 2008, 2014). Our study empirically showed the tension between the two approaches as substitutes. In particular, digitization plays a greater role when pursuing the intrafirm pathway to achieve ambidexterity than it does for the interfirm approach. Thus, this study provides a new insight into the structural ambidexterity extended to the interfirm level with the aid of digitization. Particularly, it suggests that IT spending, IT training, and IT usage are complementing centralization and intrafirm collaboration so that organizations can effectively achieve structural ambidexterity. This finding integrates and validates the IS literature in which mechanisms for employee participation and involvement are essential for better exploitation of IT resources (Sykes et al. 2009, Saraf et al. 2013), as well as IT functionalities for supporting collaboration between departments.

Finally, with the theoretical categorization of our findings in the contingency framework, we theorize the links between digitization and structural ambidexterity and develop propositions of concrete configurational recipes on how to align digitization in terms of its three proposed IT dimensions with key intrafirm and interfirm factors depending on firm size, as well as a strategic orientation between intrafirm and interfirm strategic alliances. Thus, we suggest a context-specific “middle-range” theory that can offer more detailed guidance on how firms, given the specifics of their IT strategy and firm size, can achieve structural ambidexterity with the aid of digitization. This finding expands extant theories in terms of achieving structural ambidexterity and shows how future studies can develop a midrange theory in the form of configurational recipes built on a contingency framework and the configurational approach equipped with QCA.

On the other hand, this study also makes several practical contributions and implications. Configurational recipes in our propositions prescribe how to achieve structural ambidexterity. First, our findings suggest that both large and small firms need to invest in digitization with a high degree of centralization, this being termed as the intrafirm solution. This also suggests to policy makers and implementers of government subsidy programs aimed at increasing the economy using IT that they should benchmark the degree of centralization of firms that are offered support for digitization. Second, managers should strive to develop high levels of intrafirm collaboration before, or as they embark on, significant digitization initiatives. This also implies for managers that IT implementation efforts should involve a significant effort to establish employee involvement and participation in the form of training and actual use in their decision making and tasks to achieve organizational ambidexterity. Third, if small firms have significant operational linkages with other firms, then managers may find that high reliance on digitization will undermine the goals of achieving ambidexterity. Lastly, ambidexterity may be more important and thus typically seen in competitive markets—as our findings suggest, five out of the six configurations were in intensive market competition. Our findings also reveal one configuration that shows that large organizations can achieve ambidexterity with all three IT factors, irrespective of competitive market intensity, implying that a high level of IT spending, IT training, and IT usage may allow firms to achieve ambidexterity, regardless of the intensity of the market competition.

Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research Our study has several limitations that create opportunities for future research. First, the database at Statistics Canada from which we collected our data does not include qualitative, contextual information about firms, since the identity of the firms is kept confidential. However, unlike most studies, our data set is longitudinal and includes a rich array of measures that help us to find robust, consistent patterns. Future research can further investigate the configurations that we identified and their underlying mecha nisms with richer grounded theory approaches.

Second, although our data cover the 2003–2006 period, the focal IT variables being examined may not fully reflect newer functionalities of emerging digital technologies that were introduced afterward, such as the growing ubiquity of mobile phones. Although the timing of the data could play a role, our theoretical premise is that, to achieve the goal of this study, it does not matter exactly when the data are collected, because the focus of this study is on enterprise information systems, specifically the three IT dimensions of digitization, and not on some specific functionalities of new technologies. Although we admit a potential limitation in the generalizability of our findings to recent digital technologies, we suggest that the bigger message of the paper lies in the insights about the complex configurations for achieving organizational ambidexterity with digitization. At the same time, we call for research on the effect of new affordances and functionalities brought by emerging digital technologies (e.g., smartphones, augmented/virtual reality, artificial intelligence, social network, and cloud computing) on structural ambidexterity. The insights from such research will certainly complement and advance our findings. Moreover, given the novelty of the topic, we defined digitization at the firm level with three main aspects reflecting IT spending, training, and usage intensity. However, future research can develop a more detailed understanding of the relationships between ambidexterity and specific types of IS, such as ERP, CRM, SCM, business intelligence and digital communication technologies, plus emerging technologies and systems.

Third, this study showed that interfirm and intrafirm approaches to ambidexterity are substitutes, and thus firms should not pursue both simultaneously but choose one of them to achieve structural ambidexterity. However, firms may choose one approach at one time and change to the other approach over time. Thus, future study can investigate how firms achieve such temporal, sequential ambidexterity and how digitization matters for the sequential ambidexterity mechanisms.

Lastly, we acknowledge a limitation of fsQCA that is related to the sensitivity of the calibration process. The calibration in fsQCA required us to identify three anchors for full membership, full nonmembership, and crossover (Online Appendix D). Researchers could possibly be biased in their calibration. Thus, to avoid such bias, researchers should decide the anchors based on existing theories and knowledge of the data and context (Ragin 2008). In this study, for example, to determine our three anchors, we used the definition of firm size made by the BLMA Division of Statistics Canada. We believe this is a reliable measure. However, since we do not have objective criteria for other elements for calibration, we explicitly explained how we define our anchors for each factor based on sample data distributions (Online Appendix C), which is also widely used in QCA (e.g., Fiss 2011, Frambach et al. 2016, Park et al. 2017). In addition, we also performed a sensitivity analysis to check robustness of the findings (Online Appendix E). Furthermore, as we explained explicitly, we did not make any assumptions regarding easy counterfactuals and thus did not have an intermediate solution. Thus, future research can replicate our study based on our explanation for calibration and assumptions to test our theory and also use more objective data to test our theory.

## Concluding Remark

This study advances our understanding of the complex mechanisms underlying the achievement of organizational ambidexterity in today’s digitized environments. The holistic configurational approach that we used in this study paves the way for richer theory building for complex digital phenomena. In doing so, we seek to entice researchers to consider employing a more holistic configurational approach to further examine the systemic, equifinal, and nonlinear relationships among multiple digitization, organizational, and environmental elements to achieve, not only ambidexterity, but also other organizational capabilities toward building a competitive advantage with the aid of IT in today’s increasingly digitized environments.
