## a b s t r a c t

In order to appeal to consumers, mobile application stores face the challenge of <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to achieve the seemingly con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals of providing a large quantity of applications and simultaneously making the speci<sup>fi</sup>c applications that users desire easy to discover. Drawing on environmental psychology, this paper reports a study investigating how quantity-related facilitators and environment-related facilitators are related to application discoverability, which in turn impacts user satisfaction with the application store. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that quantity-related facilitators (perceived quantity-suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency, perceived quantity-overload, and information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search) and environment-related facilitators (application store coherence and user-generated reviews) all in<sup>fl</sup>uence application discoverability, which affects user satisfaction. These facilitators play a role in managing the con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals in mobile application stores.

## 1. Introduction

An important trend in information technology (IT) innovation is the growing use of applications on mobile devices. A mobile application is a software application that runs on a smartphone or other portable device. Mobile applications provide value to users not only by enabling them to use Internet services that were previously only available on desktop or notebook computers, but also by offering functionality that is speci<sup>fi</sup>c to mobile devices (examples include GPS navigation, location-based services, or QR-code scan product searches). As people adopt mobile applications for hand-held devices, the application stores where these applications can be obtained are becoming widely used in everyday life. For example, the Apple's App Store has over 700,000 applications, and surpassed 50 billion downloads in May of 2013. Approximately 15,000 new applications are submitted by developers to the App Store each week, and demand for mobile applications is expected to continue growing [52], with the number of global downloads anticipated to reach 76.9 billion in 2014, with a value of \$35 billion [37].

New smartphone users are attracted to application stores by the increasing numbers of applications, and the expanding user pool motivates application developers to offer greater numbers of applications and higher-quality applications to tap this growing market. Nonetheless, with so many applications to sort through, <sup>fi</sup>nding the right ones to download can be a painstaking task for users. Application discoverability, which is the ease of <sup>fi</sup>nding the right application, is a serious concern for application users and developers on various mobile platforms. Therefore, there is a critical need to understand the determinants of application discoverability and the attendant level of user satisfaction.

The purpose of the present study is fourfold. First, we highlight the role of discoverability facilitators in ultimately promoting user satisfaction with mobile application stores, by identifying them as quantity-related and environment-related facilitators. Second, we examine whether quantity-related facilitators (perceived quantity suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency, perceived quantity overload, and information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search) affect application discoverability in mobile application stores. Third, we investigate the supporting role of environment-related facilitators (application store coherence, providing user recommendation systems, and having different channels through which applications can be accessed) on application discoverability. Fourth, we explore whether there are cultural differences in the relationships between application discoverability and its facilitators, using two sub-samples collected from different countries, the United States and South Korea. In addressing these research goals, we draw on an environmental psychology perspective to develop our theoretical framework for explaining the con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals of application discoverability and application quantity in mobile application stores.

The paper is organized as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst describe mobile application stores and then outline the environmental psychology perspective as a theoretical base underlying our research. We then propose our research model and pose several hypotheses regarding how application discoverability and its quantity-related and environment-related facilitators are related. We discuss the research methodology used in this study, and present the empirical results of our Partial Least Squares (PLS) analysis. We conclude by noting the implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the limitations of our study, and directions for future research.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. The mobile application store

Mobile applications provide value to users by enabling them to live more productive and enjoyable lives as they gain near-instant access to the information and services that they desire [28]. Given the wide usage of mobile applications, researchers have studied many aspects of how people use mobile applications [10,19,46,67]. The number of applications for mobile devices is rapidly increasing and users who were traditionally passive consumers of information now play a more active role as prosumers with different usage patterns [10,67]. As a relatively new environment, mobile application stores are playing a critical role in revolutionizing the landscape of mobile applications [19]. A mobile application store is a service for users of mobile devices such as iPhones, Android phones, and BlackBerrys, allowing users to browse and download software applications for use on their mobile devices [19]. Applications are also available for tablet devices and personal computers. The most successful and best-known application stores are Apple's App Store, Android Market (rebranded as GooglePlay in 2012), and App World for BlackBerry users. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, mobile application stores provide an environment in which application developers deliver their software applications and users consume the applications [46]. As the number of applications increases, the need to sift through a high number of applications can cause users to spend more time and effort to <sup>fi</sup>nd the application they want [73]. Finding the appropriate application in a mobile application store can be regarded as a painstaking task for users. Therefore, application discoverability is becoming a serious concern for mobile application users, application stores, and application developers.

## 2.2. An environmental psychology perspective

In order to better understand the mobile application store phenomenon, we draw on the perspective of environmental psychology. Environmental psychology is the branch of psychology that is concerned with providing a systematic account of the relationship between person and environment [72]. From this perspective, human behavior is determined by the environment as a powerful and direct causal in<sup>fl</sup>uence. The environment plays a critical role as more than an antecedent of human behavior; it also affords opportunities for future action [72]. The environmental psychology perspective is useful when interpreting human functioning in the presence of large amounts of information, such as when consumers are confronted with a vast number of applications that they may wish to purchase.

According to the environmental psychology perspective, one way in which humans cope with processing information is through the use of cognitive maps. As an accumulation or summary of experiences, people's cognitive maps can be used to make their way through an environment [70]. The cognitive map thus appears to be a promising concept in the study of the relationship between environment and cognition [42]. In addition, people are motivated to use and extend their cognitive maps through environments designed to take advantage of the maps. People appreciate and are motivated to use information, which helps them expand previous knowledge contained in their cognitive maps [42]. On the other hand, people have trouble understanding information that is not connected to their cognitive maps, and thus, people are not motivated to use such information. That unconnected information contributes to cognitive overload is a central idea in environmental psychology [42,72]. Further, providing too much information creates an impediment to engaging the recipient's internal cognitive map [42]. Heavily information-laden environments are more likely to elicit unpleasant emotions, and they cause individuals to experience feelings that they have lost control over interaction with the environment [36].

The environmental psychology perspective emphasizes the importance of developing effective environments in which users can process information. Kaplan and Kaplan [42] developed a preference framework to describe how people use information to satisfy their need to make sense of and to explore an uncertain world. Their research proposes two cognitive processes crucial to human survival: making sense and involvement. They suggest that there is a natural tendency in humans to prefer those environments that are most favorable for promoting sense-making (that is, those that have coherence and legibility) and involvement (those that have complexity and mystery) [22]. Hence, environmental psychology explains how providing users with effective environments makes it easier for them to process information and function effectively [70].

We propose that offering effective environments to users plays a critical role in fostering user satisfaction and thereby sustaining the competitive advantage of a mobile application store. With the growth of mobile application stores, practitioners also regard the effective design of a viable environment as a key to success in the stores [55]. To simultaneously achieve the goals of increasing application quantity while improving application discoverability, the application store needs to provide users with the appropriate functionality in the form of “discoverability facilitators.” These include quantity-related and environment-related facilitators of application discoverability in mobile application stores. We hypothesize that these facilitators increase discoverability and, thereby increase user satisfaction with the application store.

## 3.1. Application discoverability

There is no doubt that the large number of applications in mobile application stores plays a critical role in sustaining their success. The quantity of applications matters a great deal, because the most useful application stores will be those that provide for the largest number of users' particular needs and wants [14]. Nevertheless, when users are presented with a large number of applications in an application store, <sup>fi</sup>nding the appropriate application can be a challenge.

Prior research has explained that easily <sup>fi</sup>nding the right application can be an important element in enhancing user satisfaction with environments such as websites, knowledge communities, and mobile application stores [20,63,76]. For example, with regard to web design, navigation mechanisms help people effectively use the website, not only increasing the ease of use but also improving the effectiveness of the information presentation [76]. Therefore, focusing on the user perspective in this study, we use the term, application discoverability to describe the level of users' con<sup>fi</sup>dence of <sup>fi</sup>nding the application that they need in the mobile application store. Thus, application discoverability plays an important role in sustaining the success of mobile application stores.

## 3.2. User satisfaction in the mobile application store

As a strong behavioral predictor, user satisfaction is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the users' evaluation and affective response to the overall product or service, or to the experience after they use a product or service. User satisfaction of service is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as experiential judgment of particular outcomes with regard to a pleasurable level of consumption-related ful<sup>fi</sup>llment [61]. Previous studies have regarded user satisfaction as a critical predictor for the net bene<sup>fi</sup>t of a system, or as a predictor of individual impacts such as user retention and loyalty, one that elicits repurchase intention, product usage, and dissemination of positive wordof-mouth [21,26,51,57,60,84,87].

In a mobile application store, user satisfaction can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by two dimensions: user satisfaction with the service provided by a mobile application store and user satisfaction with the application itself. First, service providers (such as Apple) provide different services, including technical support, update notices, <sup>fi</sup>rmware updates, security, user evaluations, and so forth. Service-related satisfaction re<sup>fl</sup>ects the user's feelings about the interaction with the service, which includes multiple instances where the user interacts with the service provider [78]. Hence, user satisfaction with the service is based on users' cumulative satisfaction with the services that they receive [18]. Second, the application itself is an important determinant of user satisfaction. User satisfaction with the application stems from users' perception of the application, and can be considered users' pleasurable ful<sup>fi</sup>llment with the application itself in the post-usage stage. We therefore emphasize the user satisfaction with the application store service, because this study concerns the user experience in the mobile application store, rather than post-purchase satisfaction with the application itself.

According to the environmental psychology perspective, the experience of higher cognitive demands and lower con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the outcome are associated with lower discoverability, as the users' decision quality is reduced, causing anxiety and stress [41,49]. As a result, with low discoverability, negative attitudes develop toward the mobile application store. Thus, the con<sup>fi</sup>dence with which users are able to <sup>fi</sup>nd the right application that they desire can be the key issue for user satisfaction. In sum, we hypothesize the following:

H1. Perceived application discoverability is positively associated with user satisfaction with a mobile application store.

## 3.3. Quantity-related facilitators in the mobile application store

## 3.3.1. Perceptions concerning quantity of applications

To explain the relationship between application quantity and discoverability, we focus on two distinct perceptions related to the quantity of applications in the context of mobile application stores: perceived quantity suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency and perceived quantity overload. First, perceived quantity sufficiency is the extent to which an application store user thinks that the quantity of applications in an application store is adequate to enable or support the <sup>fi</sup>nding of a desired application [27]; this perception of adequate quantity would not be reduced by the addition of more applications to the store. Second, perceived quantity overload is the extent to which an application store user thinks that a large number of applications in an application store is chaotic or overwhelming to consumers, making it confusing to <sup>fi</sup>nd the appropriate applications [24,42,59].

These two distinct perceptions, perceived quantity suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency and perceived quantity overload, can coexist during a particular search task, and they may exist in any combination. Low–low (low suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency, low overload) would be an application store perceived as neither suf<sup>fi</sup>cient nor cognitively demanding. High–high would be an offering perceived as suf<sup>fi</sup>cient but also overloading. Low–low and high–high might be compared to the perceptions of a small country store and a mega-mall, respectively. Low–high refers to an application store perceived as not having enough applications to support the search but still having so many applications as to be overwhelming, like a disorganized junk store or antique warehouse. Finally, high–low refers to an application store perceived as having enough applications without being overwhelming, perhaps like a well laid out big-box store for some shoppers.

Our approach differs from some prior research, in which perceived quantity of applications in the mobile application store refers to a unitary construct [30,41,59]. Our approach also differs from prior studies that have viewed perceived quantity of information as a continuum [31,59]. Some studies have focused on information overload as the single indicator of perceived quantity of information, de<sup>fi</sup>ning it as the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty people have in understanding an issue and making decisions in the presence of too much information [3,41,59]. Other studies have regarded information suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency as the predictor of individual information-seeking outcomes such as con<sup>fi</sup>dence in judgment, de<sup>fi</sup>ning information suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency as the amount of information that people need to deal adequately with a given risk [8,29]. While prior studies have selected one or the other of these dimensions for their particular purposes, our study will bene<sup>fi</sup>t from considering both of them, noting that people may have more than one perception concerning the same phenomenon [34]. Considering both perceived quantity suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency and perceived quantity overload will be useful in our quest to elucidate the concept of discoverability.

## 3.3.2. The effects of application quantity perceptions on application discoverability

Logic dictates that, all else equal, users would perceive that their chance of <sup>fi</sup>nding the application they desire increases as the perceived suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency based on the size of the application store increases. More generally, decision makers' tendencies are to seek information up to the point of possessing a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient amount of information to make a decision [59]. When they perceive that the quantity of information is suf-<sup>fi</sup>cient, they made higher-quality decisions with less cognitive effort and time, and their con<sup>fi</sup>dence may be increased. Thus, perceived quantity suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency is positively related to perceived application discoverability, as stated here:

H2a. For users of a mobile application store, perceived quantity suf<sup>fi</sup>- ciency is positively associated with perceived application discoverability.

Another quantity-related factor, however, is the possibility that the search through a large offering of applications can contribute to cognitive effort. According to the environmental psychology perspective, cognitive chaos or overload is an antecedent of cognitive effort and uncomfortable feelings in the environment. Due to the limited capacity in the context of human information-processing, overwhelming or excessive amounts of information may result in people's cognitive distraction which in turn leads to poor decision making performance [42]. Although most mobile application stores provide guides for searching and accessing the applications that users want (such as applications categorized by features, top-rated application rankings, etc.), it is not always easy to <sup>fi</sup>nd the desired application [15]. Having large numbers of applications has the potential to reduce the user bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of an application store by imposing additional processing cost on the store's users [12,53]. As a result the perception of overload stemming from a high quantity contributes to reducing the discoverability of the applications, as expressed in the following hypothesis:

H2b. For users of a mobile application store, perceived quantity overload is negatively associated with perceived application discoverability.

Thus, increasing the quantity of offerings in an application store is a two-edged sword. It can contribute to the perception of suf<sup>fi</sup>cient quantity, which positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the perceived discoverability, but it can also contribute to the perception of quantity overload, which negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the perceived discoverability.

## 3.3.3. The effect of information specificity of search on application discoverability

Information specificity of search, which refers to the extent the information about an application being sought is speci<sup>fi</sup>c and clear, is another quantity-related facilitator [85]. While both perceived quantity-suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency and quantity-overload have direct facilitating impacts on application discoverability, information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search plays an indirect role in facilitating application discoverability by regulating perceptions of application quantity. Because users perception of application quantity can be limited by both the information they possess and their search type, information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search can be a critical factor regarding quantity-related perceptions in mobile application stores. Since much of human action is purposeful and is directed by conscious goals, individuals' motivation and subsequent performance increase when they set speci<sup>fi</sup>c, dif<sup>fi</sup>cult goals with high valence [47,48,64]. Users' information is very speci<sup>fi</sup>c when they know the exact title of the application, whereas their information is much vaguer when they lack details about the application satisfying their speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs. For example, if a user knows that he or she is searching for the application “Angry Birds”, the user can easily <sup>fi</sup>nd that application in the mobile application store. However, when the user only knows that he or she seeks some game or puzzle that involves destruction and something about eggs and pigs, the user may spend more time and effort to search for and acquire the application in the mobile application store. Therefore, information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search has an impact on perceived application discoverability, as stated in the following:

H2c. For users of a mobile application store, information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search is positively associated with perceived application discoverability.

## 3.4. Environment-related facilitators in the mobile application store

Environmental psychologists provide means of understanding how to facilitate interactive experiences. They state that a person's cognitive map of the environment is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by providing information in many different ways — through signs, features, visual aids, and technological supports [42,70]. Environmental psychology provides a means of understanding how to facilitate the interactive experience through the concept of a cognitive environment, viewing environments as providing information in many ways, such as through signs, icons, words, layout, atmospheres, and other similar forms [42,70]. Three facilitators that promote application discoverability are considered in this study: application store coherence, user-generated reviews, and multi-channel engagement.

## 3.4.1. Application store coherence

Application store coherence refers to the degree to which the store environment is consistent and logical [42,70]. It can be achieved through grouping or aligning similar elements in the mobile application store [22]. A high level of application store coherence means creating congruity or harmony among features represented in the application store. Users feel more comfortable and are able to easily discover the application they seek, when the application store offers them consistent or similar features like similar content, store design, and search functions [42]. An example would be the coordinated menu bar for “Top 25” apps in the Apple App Store. The menu bar offers three categories, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting users' interests: ‘Top Paid’, ‘Top Free’, and ‘Top Grossing.’ By grouping or aligning similar applications in each category, the App Store provides the users with higher coherence. This feature thus helps users to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently <sup>fi</sup>nd the application they need.

H3a. For users of a mobile application store, the extent of perceived application store coherence is positively associated with perceived application discoverability.

## 3.4.2. User-generated reviews

Third, user-generated reviews are comments made by application users about applications. These reviews are then made available to other prospective users. User-generated reviews are considered to be a digitized form of word of mouth which helps consumers to <sup>fi</sup>nd the appropriate application, because the reviews from other users help them to understand what the application is and whether it is of high quality or not [54]. Ratings and reviews are considered to be valuable tools, helping users decide which applications should be downloaded [80]. User-generated reviews provide a venue for application users to share their opinions and experiences. The customer review venue offers valuable information about the applications [13], and as a result, reviews are likely to be helpful for application users to make more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and rational decisions for getting the appropriate application in the mobile application store.

H3b. For users of a mobile application store, the perceived helpfulness of user-generated reviews is positively associated with perceived application discoverability.

## 3.4.3. Multi-channel engagement

When people use an application store, they can generally access it via different channels, such as the way Apple App Store users are able to use an iPhone, iTunes, the website provided by the App Store, or the website of the application developer. Although most users in mobile application stores prefer searching the application in the store through their mobile device itself [80], they are more likely to feel uncomfortable in searching for the application on the mobile device due to its unique constraints (e.g., small screen size, limited bandwidth, and the simplistic yet diverse functionality) [1,2]. For instance, the compact size of mobile devices may lead to an aesthetically unpleasant information presentation, necessitating more intensive cognitive effort from users [2]. It has been noted that different channels such as iTunes attempt to mitigate the limitations of mobile devices in the search for and management of the applications in the mobile application store by providing multiple channels [82]. Providing rich or diverse options for accessing mobile application stores could be helpful for application users to identify the proper application from within a large number of applications. Thus, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne multi-channel engagement as the degree to which application users access and use an application store through different channels.

H3c. The extent to which users perceive multiple channels for <sup>fi</sup>nding mobile applications is positively associated with perceived application discoverability.

## 3.5. Control variables

To account for the differences among individuals or situations we also include control variables. These variables include mobile application store type and mobile application store usage variables (users' experience, frequency, and time spent using mobile application stores). We select these particular variables because of their potential impact on application discoverability as suggested in extant research.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Instrument development

For the survey instrument, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed existing measures in related literature that had been repeatedly tested and where strong content validity was exhibited. We then adapted them to our research domain. Some new items were developed based on the results of our literature review. The process of re<sup>fi</sup>ning our instrument began when the questionnaire was pretested using three MIS faculty and four doctoral students. They commented on each item's meaningfulness and readability. Re-<sup>fi</sup>nements to the instrument were made based on their suggestions. For example, we initially measured the constructs such as content manager and channel coherence without any explanations of constructs. They pointed out that those constructs were not understandable with low content validity. After re<sup>fi</sup>nements, we used multi-channel engagement and application store coherence instead. We also provided de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of those constructs in the survey instrument in order to help participants' understanding.

Before testing the relationships proposed in Fig. 1, we <sup>fi</sup>rst pilot tested our survey. The purpose of the pilot study was to identify potential problems with the instrument's wording, content, format, and procedures. Twenty-seven current mobile device users who had downloaded one or more applications from a mobile application store were included in our pilot study. These participants were asked to evaluate the questionnaire in a fashion similar to the pretest respondents. Based on the results of the pilot study, minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were made to the survey items to make them clearer and more straightforward. We also included a brief description of the constructs. The survey instrument appears in Appendix A, which also displays references to prior literature and relevant theoretical bases used for survey items. All items (except for demographic questions, types of mobile application store, and information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search) use a 7-point Likert scale. Information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search was measured as a categorical variable (for information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search, “when I visit the mobile application store, I usually know exactly which kind of application I plan to download” is coded as one; whereas “when I visit the mobile application stores, I am usually browsing with no plan of downloading any speci<sup>fi</sup>c application” is coded as zero). Appendix B shows demographic questions from the instrument.<sup>1</sup>

## 4.2. Sample and data

A total of 317 participants were selected from among the undergraduate and graduate students in two large universities, one in the United

States and the other in South Korea. For this, we contacted several professors at those universities to recruit our participants. The courses where we recruited include business courses (e.g., MIS, marketing, and operations management) and social science courses (e.g., public administration and political science). Out of the 317 participants, 39 responses were dropped due to inappropriate and incomplete responses. The total number of completed responses was 278 (response rate is 89.7%). Given the high response rate, non-response bias is not a concern. To additionally assess the possible existence of non-response bias, we compared the respondents with 22 available responses that we had dropped in the study. A t-test was performed on the responses of the two groups to see if they were different. The results of the t-test showed no differences in the responses of the two groups. According to business reports [71,79,80], young people ages 15–24 are more immersed in mobile technology such as smartphones and mobile application stores, and they may represent the most critical segment of the population. In addition, 93.9% of our respondents were Apple App Store and Android Market users, closely approximating the general statistic that iOS and Android users are 92.3% of all smartphone users [38]. Thus, we found no evidence of non-response bias.

Participation in the survey was voluntary. Participants received extra course credit to encourage their participation in the survey and to promote a serious attitude toward participation. The study was conducted in the classroom using a paper-based survey. We asked mobile application users who currently use a smartphone to participate in the research survey; current mobile device users who had downloaded one or more applications from a mobile application store were eligible for participation in this study. Once the participants were informed about the restrictions in the study and had agreed to participate, the survey was given to the participants. Participants then answered the questions on the survey instrument. Participants could withdraw from the study and quit the survey at any time.

Of the 278 respondents, 62.2% were male and 37.8% were female. Of these participants, 57.9% were Apple's App Store users, 36% were Android Market users, and 4.3% were BlackBerry App World users. On average, survey respondents have used a mobile application store for 13.74 months, and visited the store 3.66 times a week. In addition, they have approximately 46 applications on their device, and among those applications, they frequently use 11 of them. All measures regarding application store and application usage rely on users' perceptions, and unfortunately, self-reported usage is not always a reliable surrogate for actual usage [77].

## 4.3. Measurement model

Data analysis was performed using Partial Least Squares (PLS). Unlike covariance-based approaches, PLS makes fewer demands on measurement scales and sample size, and makes no distributional assumptions [16]. Moreover, PLS is well-suited to highly-complex predictive models [7,17]. In the case of this study, our model consists of over 100 parameters to be estimated. Using Bentler and Chou's heuristic of 5 cases per parameter estimated, and assuming normality conditions [9], a covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM) would require 500 cases. In addition, to generate accurate estimates, most CB-SEM techniques require normally distributed data. After conducting the normality test (e.g., Shapiro–Wilk test) for our data, we found that the distribution of most indicators was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from normal distribution. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the theory we proposed (especially the dynamics of quantity- and environment-related facilitators and application discoverability in the relatively new context of mobile application stores) has not been tested by IS scholars, and the nature of this study is more exploratory than con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory. Accordingly, we focused more on the predicted variance of application discoverability and user satisfaction rather than the goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t between model parameters and observed correlations. We used PLS-Graph version 3.02 for our analysis. We conducted our analysis in two stages. First, we tested the measurement model to ensure that the constructs had suf<sup>fi</sup>cient psychometric validity and then we addressed the structural model in which the hypotheses were tested. To ensure the stability of the model developed to test the research hypotheses, we used the PLS bootstrap resampling procedure with an iteration of 1000 sub-samples drawn with replacement from the initial sample (278 current mobile application store users in the U.S. and South Korea) to generate percentile bootstrap p values. The summary of factor analysis for testing the validity of our constructs is shown in Appendix C.

Measurement reliability was assessed using internal consistency scores, calculated by the composite reliability scores [83]. Internal consistencies of all variables are considered acceptable since they exceed 0.70, signifying acceptable reliability [58]. As can be seen in Table 1, the composite reliability for all constructs is greater than 0.90. All items exhibit high loadings on their respective constructs. Thus, all constructs in the model exhibit good internal consistency.

Convergent and discriminant validity are supported when the square root of each construct's average variance extracted (AVE) is larger than its correlations with other constructs [16]. As shown by comparing inter-construct correlations and AVE in Table 1, all constructs share more variance with their indicators than with other constructs since all AVEs are well above 0.50 [25]. We also carried out con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) for the measurement model. The t-values for the loadings of manifest variables were extremely high (ranging from 13 to 62), supporting the statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the estimated parameters [56]. These statistics for the reliability of our measures and analysis are summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

As with all self-reported data, there is a potential for common method biases resulting from multiple sources such as consistency motif and social desirability [65]. Common method bias is “variance that is attributable to the measurement method rather than to the constructs the measures represent” (p. 879) [65]. In order to assess common method bias in this study, we <sup>fi</sup>rst performed Harman's one-factor test [65]. In this test, all items are entered into an unrotated exploratory factor analysis to determine whether a single factor emerges or a single factor accounts for the majority of the variance. In our test, 26 factors emerged, the largest of which accounted for 34% of the variance. In addition, we followed the unmeasured latent method construct (ULMC) approach introduced by Liang et al. [44]. Liang et al. [44] noted that “if the method factor loadings are insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant and the indicators' substantive variances are substantially greater than their method variances, we can conclude that common method bias is unlikely to be a serious concern” (p. 87). Appendix D shows the results of common method bias analysis including constructs, the substantive factor loadings, the method factor loadings, and so on. The results revealed that only 2 (out of 25) of the method factor loadings were statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. They also demonstrate that the substantively explained variances of the indicators (average of 0.828) are substantially greater than their method-based variances (average of 0.005). Taken together from both results, we contend that the method is unlikely to be a serious concern for this study.

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

The major theoretical contribution of this study is the development of a research model explaining the relationship among quantity-related facilitators, environment-related facilitators, application discoverability, and user satisfaction. We identify the supporting role of environmentrelated facilitators for managing the con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals of application quantity and application discoverability. We also highlight how the environment-related facilitators of the application store (application store coherence and user-generated reviews) contribute to the application discoverability and thereby promote user satisfaction with the application store. User satisfaction can be increased when application stores are able to enhance application discoverability while simultaneously increasing the quantity of applications.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have theoretical implications for the study of information overload, con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals, and the offering of multiple channels. For overload, by emphasizing that the two distinct quantity perceptions – perceived quantity suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency and perceived quantity overload – can coexist, this study theoretically complements prior studies on cognitive overload. Two perceptions of quantity play distinct roles and can potentially extend traditional information overload research. For the study of con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals, many prior studies of organizations' long-term success have emphasized how to deal with con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals [68,69]. For example, ambidextrous organizations have the capability of being aligned and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in their management of con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals or strategies [68]. This study could provide insight in that stream of research because it shows a theoretical framework for understanding how mobile application stores handle their seemingly con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals of simultaneously providing the huge numbers of applications and making it easy to <sup>fi</sup>nd an appropriate application among them. For the study of multiple channels, our result questions the assumption that multiple channels are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial because the results did not support the importance of this feature. Perhaps this is because most users may use mobile application stores via their mobile device. This raises interesting questions for designers who may be concerned with multi-channel availability or channel consistency. Mobile devices face unique constraints due to their small screen size and limited memory, along with unpleasant aesthetics, low legibility, and small amount of information [2,86]. Hence, mobile application stores lead developers to use different approaches to its design than they would use for other channels [32]. According to our <sup>fi</sup>nding based on environmental psychology, we can stress that application store coherence can be one of the important facilitators for effectively designing mobile application stores. It remains for future research to elucidate how to develop application store coherence in the mobile application stores, and to suggest antecedents of application store coherence.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also suggest that the effects of application store coherence on application discoverability are more important in an Eastern culture, South Korea, than in a Western culture, the U.S. Prior studies regarding website design suggested that preferences for the navigational schemes which improve users' discoverability are expected to vary by culture [35,50,74]. As a result of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we surmise that the relative importance of application store coherence can be different between Western and Eastern culture, even though it is considered a critical facilitator in mobile application stores in both cultures.

As results of multi-group analysis across cultures, we also found that the impact of information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in a Western culture but not in an Eastern culture. Although this relationship is not supported in multi-group analysis for testing the difference of structural paths, information speci<sup>fi</sup>city of search can be regarded as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in the Western culture rather than in the Eastern culture.

## 5.2. Practical implications

From a practical perspective, the current study suggests that application stores would do well to focus on discoverability rather than just the quantity of applications. We believe that balancing or co-increasing application quantity and discoverability can be a critical factor for the success of mobile application stores. Recent research suggests that companies need to make applications more discoverable in order to enrich user satisfaction [40]. In mobile application stores, there are three ways to improve application discoverability: (1) inmarketplace search optimization, (2) reputation from outside the store such as third-party sites, and (3) customer reviews [40]. Practically, this means that facilitators for discoverability may be required to achieve higher levels of user satisfaction, which in turn promote application stores' success.

Recent research demonstrates that most application stores in the market offer the same core and popular applications [39]. So far, Apple's App Store and the Android Market/GooglePlay have been the top application stores in terms of application variety. However, if competing application stores carry the same or similar applications to users, the role of application quantity in mobile application stores may become less signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Rather, application discoverability appears to be more important for mobile application stores to be successful. Thus, our research model can also be useful for companies to help achieve user satisfaction in mobile application stores, by highlighting the roles of application discoverability and its facilitators over application quantity. One of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of expanding the user pool is that this motivates more developers to participate in the store, and it enables the store to offer higher-quality and lower-priced applications [31,33]. Thus, a virtuous circle may form, resulting in the growth and success of the mobile application store.

Furthermore, the expansion of cloud computing introduces the question of how to <sup>fi</sup>nd applications in the cloud, and at this point the most promising approach is to use the application store metaphor to deliver applications to users. Therefore, the importance of this research topic and our model could increase as more companies and users adopt cloud computing. For example, IT professionals will make choices that affect the organization and navigability of such application stores, which may be unique within each private cloud, and it would appear that understanding the drivers of application discoverability in an application store will become a very important issue for most organizations. Indeed, software vendors are heading in this direction. Microsoft recently announced Of<sup>fi</sup>ce 365 Marketplace, which incorporates the application store metaphor, as it brings its SharePoint customers to the cloud [11]. Oracle just acquired newScale, which provides an “app store” for delivery of IT services [62]. AppCentral (formerly Ondeego), a San Francisco startup, has software for operating private mobile application stores within the enterprise; its Version 2.0 can be used with both Apple iOS and Google Android devices [6]. These trends suggest that combining the cloud with the application store concept will be increasingly appealing and feasible.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

Other research could take the opportunity to more deeply investigate the tension between suf<sup>fi</sup>cient quantity and discoverability and may employ different approaches that can capture or test these con-<sup>fl</sup>icts. Therefore, we encourage further research that more directly addresses how the con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals can be balanced or reconciled in mobile application stores. We note that user perceptions of application quantity may be dependent on various contexts (possibly including motivation type, psychological distance, and task complexity). For instance, according to the construal level theory [45,81], the more psychologically distant an event is, the more it will be represented at higher levels of abstraction. Thus, when people don't need to search for the desired applications right now, they may be inclined to evoke abstract thought about the quantity of applications. Thus, the psychological distance such as temporal distance can be one of critical determinants for perceptions of application quantity. We encourage further research in the contextual antecedents and consequences of perceived application quantity.

This study found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant facilitators for application discoverability, and it is likely that these facilitators have antecedents which could be generalized and investigated. These antecedents for mobile devices are likely to differ from traditional human computer interaction recommendations at the most concrete level (although not necessarily at a more abstract or theoretical level). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the facilitating role of user-generated reviews in mobile application stores. Not all user reviews are helpful in online environments, suggesting that the strategic focus of user reviews shifts from the mere presence of user reviews to the user evaluation and use of the reviews [13,43,54]. Thus, helping application users to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently get quality information through user-generated reviews, rather than simply increasing the presence of user-generated reviews, will be important for application stores. Hence, we encourage further research in addressing how the quality of user-generated reviews is related to application discoverability.

While some have criticized the use of student participants in research, in this instance we believe that students are an appropriate population from which to select a sample. Most students use smartphones and other similar mobile devices, and they also utilize the applications downloaded from application stores. College students make up a large part of mobile application store users, with young people ages 15–24 more immersed in mobile technology such as smartphones and mobile application stores than any older segment of the population [79]. Furthermore, market research has noted that approximately 50% of iPhone users were aged 30 or younger [71]. Thus, we believe that the selection of our sample from this group is appropriate. In addition, the courses we recruited for survey were limited (e.g., business and social science students), and therefore, it could be another potential limitation of the results. It may be that the results are unique to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c student population and would not generalize to the entire student population and mobile application store users. Hence, we encourage additional studies with broader selection of participants to help ensure more generalizable results concerning the population of mobile application store users.

We also point out that investigating cultural differences was beyond the intent of this study, but we conducted multi-group analysis to complement the main results of the study. The results indicated that there is one difference of application store coherence between the two sub-groups. Therefore, we encourage additional research to focus on the cultural differences in user behavior in mobile application stores.

Finally, we endeavored to utilize clearly-worded survey questions by adapting existing measures from relevant prior literature where strong content validity was exhibited and by appropriately developing new items based on the results of our literature review. Nonetheless, we debated dropping three items (COH3, REV4, and SAT3) because their meaning may have been ambiguous to the participants. In the end we included them in the study because they loaded well, but additional research with similar items could serve to further validate these constructs.

## 6. Conclusion

This research focuses on the relationship between application quantity and application discoverability, and uses the environmental psychology perspective as it does so. Understanding application quantity and discoverability is important to both theory and practice in a wide variety of domains. This research contributes to the literature by highlighting that effectively combining discoverability facilitators such as quantity-related and environment-related facilitators with the mobile application store plays a critical role in enriching user satisfaction in mobile application stores. This bene<sup>fi</sup>t is also accomplished by enhancing application discoverability over an increasing quantity of applications. Moreover, our research framework motivates the effective design of the mobile application store environment for fostering application discoverability and user satisfaction.
