## Abstract

To realize value from their wealth of digital data, organizations are investing in data-driven organizational initiatives—efforts in which they must draw expertise in data, algorithms, and visualization together with knowledge and skills in business domains such as marketing and human resources. However, they face the challenge of crossing the knowledge divide between analytics groups and business groups. Exploring relationships between the two groups in 37 data-driven organizational initiatives, we develop a configuration-based model that explains analytics and businessdomain knowledge integration through the lens of synergy. Our configurational analyses revealed five configurations of relationships between the two, which bring about two distinct change outcomes: “dedicated data groups” and “multidisciplinary teams” lead to the emergence of new datadriven ways to work, and “analytics institutionalization,” “analytics resource optimization,” and “networked communities” produce convergence, through the sharing of data-driven ways to work. Each configuration displays a distinct element of the core processes identified (“developing group connectedness,” “exchanging analytics and business domain knowledge,” and “incentivizing organizational data use”) and yields either an emergence or convergence of data-driven ways of working. The findings demonstrate how data-driven organizational initiatives can benefit from a pervasive form of organizing that entwines analytics groups and business groups such that their members’ tools, mindsets, and behaviors are merged to profoundly change ways of working. Together, these findings and the configurational methodology used provide a nuanced picture of how organizations integrate the requisite specialist knowledge across domains to realize value from data.

Keywords: Business Analytics, Knowledge Integration, Synergy, Configurational Theory, Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA)

## 1 Introduction

In response to the digitization of business and society, coupled with the increasing availability of digital data, organizations are undertaking data-driven organizational initiatives to embed data analytics into various domains of their work (Grover et al., 2018) in order to maximize value generation from digital data assets (e.g., Chiang et al., 2018; Someh et al., 2019; Wixom & Ross, 2017). However, a recent survey of executives revealed that 69% of organizations fall short of actualizing this aspiration (Bean & Davenport, 2019). One reason for their delayed progress is a focus on traditional forms of organizing (e.g., a function-based or siloed approach) that encourage structural boundaries, which afford in-depth specialization yet inhibit the combination, integration, and synthesis of the organization’s various bodies of knowledge (Dougherty, 1992). For example, establishing analytics centers of excellence (COEs) increases analytics specialization but makes it challenging to systematically embed analytics knowledge and skills into processes, products and services, and organizational culture.

Previous research investigating data-driven initiatives has focused on identifying the resources and capabilities needed to reap value from analytics (e.g., Grover et al., 2018). Receiving less research attention meanwhile are the group-level processes through which analytics and business domain knowledge can be combined and through which higher-order capabilities grounded in data can be developed. Taking such an integrative stance entails challenges because of the functions’ separation but also because little knowledge, expertise, and language is shared between analytics and business groups (the former are centered on machine learning and computational techniques while the latter are versed in human resources, marketing, or other domains). These knowledge and structural gaps together slow or restrain the profound change sought via initiatives aimed at incorporating the knowledge and skills of analytics groups across various business domains (Someh et al., 2019). Thereby, the gulf reinforces incumbent ways of working. In response, we formulated the following research question: “How can relationships between analytics groups and business groups be configured to facilitate knowledge integration in data-driven organizational initiatives?”

To answer that question, we adopted the knowledgebased view (KBV) of the firm (Grant, 1996) for an overarching perspective on the nature of organizations’ knowledge-integration capability, and used the concept of synergy in conjunction with this (Nevo & Wade, 2010; Tanriverdi, 2006; Venkatesh & Bala, 2012) to shed light on causal pathways for the integration of analytics and business groups’ specialist knowledge. We built on the recent work of Park et al. (2020), which encourages the use of configurational approaches to break from the more traditional process or variance models and thereby capture complexity and theoretical multiplicity at play in digital phenomena. Accordingly, we collected qualitative data from 37 data-driven organizational initiatives and analyzed the data set to conceptualize the processes and outcomes of forming synergistic relations between analytics and business groups; then, we used qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) to perform cross-case analysis and identify various relationship configurations that facilitate knowledge integration.

Our work entails several contributions. Theoretically, our configuration-based research model articulates the processes and outcomes of synergy between two groups through which knowledge integration is realized, and it captures the causal complexity in how synergy is achieved (by considering multiple theoretical elements that yield an outcome jointly). Methodologically, it identifies five relationship configurations through QCA, thereby going further than statistical modeling of interaction effects to model interaction patterns between the two groups. Practically, our application of configurations points to the value in a pervasive form of organizing for datadriven work by means of purposeful configurations that leaders can adopt and apply to configure analytics and business group relationships.

## 2 Theoretical Background

Below, we first introduce KBV, highlighting that the knowledge must be blended, shared, and used if competitive advantage is to be achieved. Second, we turn to the notion of synergy to delineate how relationships between the groups may be configured for knowledge integration.

## 2.1 Integrating Analytics and Business Domain Knowledge

In his seminal paper, Grant (1996, p. 375) calls knowledge “the most strategically-significant resource of the firm” and argues that “the primary role of the firm, and the essence of organizational capability, is the integration of knowledge.” This suggests that integrating multiple types of knowledge is key to developing core competencies and gaining competitive advantage. For organizations undertaking data-driven initiatives, the knowledge involved spans the analytics and business domains, and the knowledge integration entails synthesizing knowledge between the domains to evolve first-order capabilities into higher-order ones.

According to KBV (Grant 1996), (1) effective datadriven initiatives facilitate blending knowledge: organizations must synthesize the special knowledge of analytics and business groups to generate novel approaches to work processes. This is challenging when little common knowledge exists and when the two domains lack a shared language and have distinct mindsets, behavioral norms, and problem-solving approaches. (2) Effective data-driven initiatives afford knowledge sharing: organizations must diffuse integrated knowledge organization-wide to enable change. This is challenging as it needs to optimize for both the breadth and depth of change, in terms of making sure numerous capabilities exist, where each entails the synthesis of multiple types of knowledge. Prioritizing competing demands can prove troublesome. (3) Effective datadriven initiatives facilitate knowledge application: success hinges on all decision-makers using the new, hybrid-form knowledge habitually. Business processes and products similarly require updating such that they are grounded in analytics insights. Inspiring and orchestrating localized user participation is a significant leadership task.

For organizations wishing to integrate knowledge and create higher-order capabilities, directives (e.g., operating manuals) provide a low-cost means of communicating specialists’ knowledge to others (Grant 1996). Directives translate tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge and communicate it at scale to many groups. The process of codifying knowledge, however, can create substantial knowledge loss. Grant notes that the routinization of behaviors can address this through formulation and automatic execution of a fixed response while giving specialists the opportunity to apply their knowledge in turn. Hence, knowledge integration depends on complex, informal coordination techniques and on emergent patterns of interaction between groups. The crucial role of interactions between groups becomes all the more accentuated.

Data-driven initiatives vary in their approach to creating value from data. Analytics knowledge might be applied to new product development, process redesign, culture transformation, performance management, or enabling evidence-based decisionmaking (Grover et al., 2018). The structure of a single analytics group cannot accommodate all such objectives. To tackle this inherent shortcoming, we sought to illuminate multiple relationship configurations via which analytics and business knowledge integration can occur.

## 2.2 Synergy

The synergy concept is rooted in seminal work by systems theorists (e.g., Churchman, 1971). Systems theory deals with systems as a whole rather than their components: the system, being greater than the sum of its parts, is derived from a synergistic relationship of all those parts. There are two aspects to synergy: its formation and associated outcomes (Someh et al., 2019; Someh & Shanks, 2013).

## 2.2.1 Forming Synergistic Relationships

Research that investigates how synergies occur between groups or systems is rare, save for a few notable exceptions. Nevo and Wade (2010, 2011) considered enablers of synergy: compatibility and integration effort between IT assets and organizational resources. Their notion of integration effort (i.e., management endeavors to bring resources together) is, however, not unpacked to explain the underlying mechanisms. To address this gap, we utilized three kinds of organizational theory (network, learning-based, and influence theory) that Venkatesh and Bala (2012) cited as key mechanisms in forming synergistic relationships.

Network theory expresses the idea that actors’ choices of action are shaped and constrained by the relations and structures within which they operate (e.g., Borgatti & Halgin, 2011). Employees not only search for the best solutions to problems but also seek structures in the environment that support their performance. The structures, created through formal ties or social ties, enable synergies through collaboration, knowledge transfer, and the creation of social capital.

Organizational learning theory assumes that employees have bounded rationality but still learn through reflecting on their past performance or by acquiring and processing information available to them in their immediate environment (Argote & Greve, 2007). Organizational learning enables synergies by helping analytics groups obtain business domain knowledge and allows business groups to learn how to apply analytics for decision-making.

Influence highlights the importance of providing motivation to change one’s attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors. There are diverse approaches to creating such incentives (Ajzen, 1991), including offering rewards or punishments, mandating participation (Venkatesh & Bala, 2012), and exerting peer-based influence. Motivation theory enables synergies by shedding light on how group members can adapt to data-driven transformation goals and mindsets.

While each of the theories can be applied to the relationship between analytics and business groups, none of them alone can fully capture how synergies may arise between the two. To address this, we cultivated a research approach that deals with the multiplicity of theoretical perspectives required for explaining how synergies can be achieved.

## 2.2.2 Outcomes of Synergistic Relationships

The creation of emergent capabilities: Taking a systems approach, the concept of synergy may be used to conceptualize the emergence of IT-enabled organizational resources from interactions between complementary resources. To create value, IT resources must be synergistically meshed with other resources. In this stream of research, synergy is defined as positive emergent capabilities. For example, Nevo and Wade (2010) cite the example of a system with the emergent capability of tele-laparoscopic surgery formed from a synergistic relationship between (1) a local medical team able to use surgical arms, (2) a telesurgery robotics system that can supply telepresence, and (3) a remote medical team with laparoscopy knowledge. The emergent capability cannot be traced back to any of the three parts alone.

Sharing of common capabilities: Rather than synergy from complementarities, another stream of research examines synergy from relatedness. This arises when common production factors are exploited to produce efficiencies and scale. The work of Tanriverdi (2006) exemplifies research in this vein, particularly in employing the construct of IT relatedness, whereby sharing IT infrastructure technologies and IT management processes across multiple business units can create between-unit cost synergies. From this perspective, synergy is viewed as the sharing of common capabilities, rather than as emergent capabilities.

We applied concepts from both streams of research to conceptualize the outcomes of synergistic relationships. Next, we explain our means of exploring the configurations by examining which organizations can realize both types of synergistic outcomes in their cross-domain knowledge integration.

## 3 The Research Method

## 3.1 The Configurational Approach

The goal of understanding complex causal influence inspired our choice to employ a configurational research approach, wherein a configuration is understood as “any multidimensional constellation of conceptually distinct characteristics that commonly occur together” (Meyer et al., 1993, p. 1175). The configurational approach helps researchers produce theories that link multiple sets of interconnected elements (i.e., attributes or causal conditions) that must be simultaneously present for a certain outcome to occur. These elements are the constituent concepts of a theory, and the relationship they exhibit is combinatorial.

Configurational theory has its roots in sociology (Ragin, 2008) and has long been advocated by management scholars (e.g., Fiss, 2011). Fichman (2004) and El Sawy et al. (2010) introduced the approach to the IS literature a decade ago, and several empirical articles in IS basket journals have been published since (e.g., Park et al., 2017; Tan et al., 2016). A recent MIS Quarterly methods article (Park et al., 2020) details a conceptual framework and prescriptive guidelines aimed at promoting the adoption of a configurational approach in the IS field. The piece emphasizes the method’s applicability for theory development and testing.

The configurational approach goes beyond traditional linear or interaction models of causality, to conjunctural causation, whereby conditions of multiple elements combine to produce an outcome (Ragin, 2008). This is particularly important for theorizing on complex digital phenomena (El Sawy et al., 2010) in which performance outcomes rest not on any single factor but on relations among multiple technological, organizational, and environmental conditions that jointly yield an outcome. Hence, the configurational approach can generate integrative theories under which multiple theoretical perspectives are valid and together hold explanatory power (Park, 2020). Accordingly, the configurational approach supports theoretical multiplicity; elements from several theories can be pinpointed, considered, and causally connected to explain complex digital phenomena (Park et al., 2020).

The configurational analysis produces “causal recipes” structured as formal statements based on Boolean algebra that explain how causally relevant theoretical elements combine into configurations that produce a target outcome. Typically, such analysis is performed by means of a truth table (a Boolean chart) for examining all logically possible element combinations, even those for which empirical instances are absent (with 4 elements, 2<sup>4</sup> combinations exist). Then, algorithms are applied to logically reduce the numerous elements to a condensed set of configurations. Causal recipes may be equifinal (more than one pathway might lead to a particular outcome) and asymmetric (elements that are related in one configuration might be unrelated or inversely correlated in others) (Fiss, 2011).

## 3.2 The Data

Recent IS studies have adopted the configurational method’s theoretical elements from prior literature and proceeded deductively with a large-scale survey (Park et al., 2017); however, the method lends itself especially well to smaller case-based data sets. In what is referred to as a small-N configurational study, the technique affords developing a deep knowledge of the individual cases (Crilly et al., 2012). We built a small-N data set by examining two organizations, the pseudonymous TechCo and FinCo (introduced in Table 1), which had begun a series of data-driven organizational initiatives. In keeping with our theoretical orientation, we selected the individual datadriven organizational initiative as a case. For each initiative, we studied the relationship between analytics and a business group. Across the two organizations, we identified 37 data-driven organizational initiatives (Appendix A lists these). This level of analysis permitted us to observe patterns of interaction between groups without being limited to or biased by larger, more formal organizational structures.

Our data collection spanned two years and involved primary and additional data sources (outlined in Table 1). The first step was to conduct semi-structured 1-2 hour interviews with 50 managers and senior leaders, 25 at each company, who actively initiated analytics projects. Ten interviewees worked with multiple cases (this was inevitable because of their seniority and role; we minimized confounding effects by focusing on the goals and approaches distinctive of each relationship and via triangulation). The questions probed for instances of cross-domain relationships, explored the practices that brought groups together, and captured results that groups in the two domains jointly produced. Also, we asked about the development of the groups’ skills and knowledge over time.

## 3.3 Data Analysis

Creating causal recipes for analytics/business relationships requires attention to two logics, which Park et al. (2020) denote as factorial and combinatorial logics. The former describes which elements are important for manifesting the outcome of interest (and why) and which are not. The combinatorial logic, in turn, explains, in an analytical manner, how the configuration’s various elements interact with one another to produce the outcome. The stages of our empirical work reflect these two logics.

In the first stage, “within-case analysis,” we followed a factorial logic, via qualitative case studies specific to the context of developing data-driven organizational initiatives (where each case was a cross-domain relationship). To identify processes and outcomes of synergy, we analyzed the data by using Gioia’s conceptdevelopment approach. Stage 2, “cross-case analysis,”

followed a combinatorial logic, wherein we conducted QCA across all 37 cases and produced causal recipes for how the processes systemically combine into configurations that yield the desired outcomes (Ragin, 2008). This approach, outlined in Table 2 and expanded on below, utilized our deep knowledge of the individual cases and the variations across them.

## 3.3.1 Stage 1: Within-Case Analysis

For identifying the processes and outcomes and their sub-dimensions, we used the Gioia Method (Gioia et al., 2012): We initially developed key categories inductively to organize the data. During this stage, we adhered closely to participants’ terms and language to preserve the intended meaning. After this, we distilled the categories to more abstract themes in light of the literature, particularly the concepts related to network, learning, and influence theory.

## 3.3.2 Stage 2: Cross-Case Analysis

Calibration framework and assigning fuzzy scores: we developed a calibration framework (Appendix C), to transform qualitative data into fuzzy sets and followed guidelines from Basurto and Speer (2012) and Legewie (2017), to assign fuzzy membership scores for each case. Within the calibration framework, we developed indicators for each theoretical element and outcome by considering the theory, other literature, and our knowledge of the cases (Basurto & Speer, 2012). For each element and outcome, we defined relevant variation in fuzzy-set membership and assigned anchor points. Scoped connectedness, scaled connectedness, emergence, and convergence were specified as crisp sets with membership anchors of 1 (member, “fully in”) and 0 (nonmember, “fully out”). For example, an initiative using Yammer to connect analytics and business groups received 1 for scaled connection. We specified knowledge exchange and motivation as fuzzy sets with anchor points of 1 (fully in), 0.67 (more in than out), 0.33 (more out than in), and 0 (fully out), characterizing all anchor points clearly.

In the process of calibration, for each initiative, we had to specify whether synergies had occurred and, if so, in what form (emergence/convergence). Scholars with an economics-oriented approach to operationalizing synergy often assess it by means of statistical interaction effects of 2-3 variables (Aral & Weill, 2007; Titah & Barki, 2009; Venkatesh & Bala, 2012). When they detect a positive and significant interaction effect, the resources in a synergistic relationship are deemed to create value over and above the additive effect. Similarly, negative synergies may exist. The statistical interaction, however, is simply the product of the values associated with the variables, rather than the emergence of a new capability or of any real thing with new properties or a novel concept. In line with our systems-oriented thinking, which favors bottom-up processes of emergence (Burton-Jones, 2014), we assessed synergistic outcomes by empirically observing the actual emergent properties, capabilities, or ways of working that arose. By analogy, one cannot predict the wetness of water based on the characteristics of hydrogen and oxygen. Accordingly, for each initiative, we examined the outcomes of the interaction between particular analytics and business groups, coding for what was achieved in the form of emergence or convergence.

Truth-table analysis: For our eight elements, we constructed a truth table listing all 2<sup>8</sup> logically possible configurations (each row corresponds to one configuration). Here, our 37 cases were specified as combinations of elements causally linked to the two outcomes. We deleted configurations that showed no association with our cases. To assure analytical rigor, a threshold number of cases can be assigned to a given combination in a truth table. We set a minimum for the number of cases, and subsequent analysis considered only those combinations with at least two empirical instances (per Ragin, 2008); furthermore, the truth-table algorithm we applied computes two parameters for fit: consistency and coverage. The former is the percentage of cases conforming to the relevant combination of elements for a particular value of the outcome, while coverage measures empirical relevance by indicating the extent to which the solutions explain all cases observed (here, of emergence and convergence). The range for both is 0-1, with values above 0.7 being recommended (Ragin, 2008).

Next, we used the truth-table algorithm provided by the fsQCA software (Ragin, 2008) to reduce the configurations and produce parsimonious and intermediate solutions. For the parsimonious solution, the algorithm uses counterfactual analysis for engaging with both the researcher and preexisting theoretical knowledge to speculate about plausible outcomes from those logically possible combinations not captured in the empirical data. This solution, which includes only the core causal elements that are essential and show the strongest evidence of producing the outcomes, is a subset of a less strict intermediate solution, which additionally encompasses elements considered peripheral (i.e., elements that are less important and perhaps expendable or exchangeable; these might be absent from the parsimonious solution on account of counterfactual analysis).

In QCA, it is important to include negative cases, or cases for which the desired outcomes were not achieved. Roughly 10% of our cases did not manifest emergence and convergence outcomes. One example is visible in FinCo’s attempt to create a virtual project-based reskilling program to reach those employees in its global offices who possessed data mining skills. The goal was for the employees to work on a real bank project. This program failed because collaborative action between the COE mentors and employees did not scale and the mentors found virtually oriented immersion in the underlying business problems difficult.

## 4 Findings

To elucidate the concepts identified (the processes and outcomes) and the evidence for them, we refer to three cases (see Table 3) that encapsulate the theoretical elements derived (“themes” in our data structure). All are from TechCo for easy-to-follow discussion and to illuminate the range of initiatives within an organization (we cite FinCo cases further on, in explaining our configurations). Then, we present the nomological network formed on their basis, which we subjected to QCA in cross-case analysis to identify patterns of interaction between elements.

## 4.1 Processes Associated with a Synergistic Relationship

## 4.1.1 Developing Group Connectedness

One process vital for forming synergistic relationships involves how analytics and business groups formed mutual connections (“group ties” in the network literature—Borgatti & Halgin, 2011). Two themes emerged from our data, connections that are “scoped” and “scaled,” distinguished by the number of groups participating. A scoped connection involves a small number of domain-specific groups establishing mutual ties to focus on decision-making problems in a local business domain. For example, when deciding to reengineer its sales-related business processes, TechCo co-located a local analytics group with the sales group. The analytics group shadowed salespeople to gain deep business domain knowledge (e.g., understand a day in a salesperson’s life), and salespeople accessed analytics knowledge relevant to their particular problems (e.g., algorithms to predict whether a sales lead will produce a deal). In this case, a one-to-one connection was formed between an analytics group and a business group. In other cases, one analytics group formed ties involving a small number of business groups—i.e., a one-to-few connection.

The notion of a scaled connection refers to occasions of numerous analytics groups and business groups forming ties to disseminate good practices applicable in multiple business domains, via either one-to-many or many-to-many connections across groups. In this extract pertaining to TechCo’s central dashboarding group, a business analytics manager offers an example of scaled one-to-many group connectedness in the purposeful use of communication methods able to reach large audiences:

I’ve got thousands of people to reach, so we have a website that we drive our primary communications through. Our emails refer back to the website, and we’ll say internally, as a barometer, “If we have to have the same conversation twice, there’s a bug with the website.” Because we are always trying to scale that message out. (Analytics Manager, TechCo)

Likewise, TechCo used Yammer communities to form a scaled many-to-many connection between analytics and business groups. Via Yammer, several disparate groups could interact with one another, post questions, and share ideas.

## 4.1.2 Exchanging Analytics and Business Domain Knowledge

The second key process associated with creating a synergistic relationship is related to how knowledge flowed between analytics and business groups, in what the literature characterizes as organizational learning (see Argote & Greve, 2007). Three themes emerged in our data: analytics education, collaborative action, and community engagement. Analytics education conveyed knowledge from analytics to business groups via training material, case studies, videos, user manuals, classroom presentations, and virtual classes. Training content covered generalizable problems relevant to many business groups. TechCo’s dashboarding group invested heavily in this area.

We held more than 60 online and live training sessions to launch our data tool, and we continue to hold more as we move along our deployment journey. In these sessions, we showed employees how to use the tool and the value they can get from it. We used real-world examples and showed visible support for adoption. We continue to build out and promote online, virtual, and live training. (Business Analytics Manager, TechCo)

Collaborative action conveyed knowledge by actively immersing groups from both analytics and business in problem solving within a specific business domain; later, group members could reflect on and learn from what occurred, how, and why.

Table 3. Three Illustrative Cases

Example 1: Reengineering sales business processes. Sales processes at TechCo were inefficient. Salespeople had to scan, manually select, and extract data from up to 80 distinct systems. To address this, the leaders established a local analytics group within sales to work with designated sales reps and reengineer core sales business processes (pipeline management, opportunity management, forecasting, etc.). This sales analytics group identified decision requirements and iteratively built solutions for sales data. Salespeople and this analytics group defined shared concepts (e.g., what “lead” or “pipeline” means). They then created a new, integrated sales platform, which filtered and consolidated sales data to produce 360-degree views of TechCo’s customer relationships. To encourage pervasive use of this platform, the analytics group tracked usage metrics across sales groups globally and reported to sales managers on them. Example 2: Promoting company-wide dashboard use. TechCo established a central dashboarding group to teach all employees about dashboards and how to monitor and respond to visualized shared performance metrics. This group relied heavily on strategies of frequent and scalable communication that could effectively reach thousands of employees at a time. The dashboarding group managed a website providing manuals, videos, and quick-start guides that answered “What is data, and how can I use data in my world?” The group also published monthly newsletters, offered online classes, and kept virtual office hours. To increase dashboard adoption and utilization, the group hired marketers for their skills in market segmentation, campaign planning, and collection of user-survey data, and it also performed segmentation-based personalization of training content for employees.

Example 3: Establishing virtual communities. TechCo created Yammer-based user groups specific to dashboarding and to data science tools. This fostered company-wide interaction among employees with shared interests and problems. Yammer-group members shared solutions/problems and answered each other’s questions. Expert workers were identified and tagged as “VIPs” so that others could follow their posts and adopt their practices. These Yammer fora became a “one-stop shop” for data-related discussion. The dashboarding group tapped the conversations to identify problems and gauge worker sentiments about the use of data and analytics for decision-making work across the organization.

This collaborative action involved exchanging analytics and business knowledge, experiencing business problems firsthand, and experimenting to understand effective practices. The sales analytics group and salespeople engaged in collaborative action when they jointly built the integrated sales platform and reengineered the sales-related business processes. The platform offered personalized insights that mattered uniquely to salespeople performing their work tasks.

Finally, community engagement facilitated the exchange of analytics knowledge among analytics and business groups as they engaged with one another socially. Through this engagement, the groups learned and copied peers’ appealing practices. We found this engagement to be characterized by the flow of analytics knowledge, (e.g., skills with dashboard templates and churn algorithms) across multiple business domains. Over time, the Yammer communities established by TechCo’s dashboarding group became independent communities exhibiting self-help capacity.

We intentionally built the [Yammer] community. We went from zero to 5,000 people in there. You’ve got to get to critical mass, where the community starts to selfhelp. Out of the gates, we had to be very active. Our entire group had a rotational schedule—posting new content and answering questions—but we got to a critical-mass point, where we had the community self-answering. We found champions starting to emerge. We tracked, via data, who the most active posters are, and we treated them as a VIP. And we give them roles, to be able to help that community go further. (Business Analytics Manager, TechCo)

## 4.1.3 Incentivizing Organizational Data Use

The final process identified for a synergistic relationship concerns the incentives (conceptualized in line with influence and motivation theories per Ajzen, 1991; Venkatesh & Bala, 2012) that encouraged employees to shift from their old ways of working and adopt data-driven methods. Three themes emerged: the value proposition of data, power, and social norms. A well-articulated value proposition for data encouraged business groups to change by communicating or demonstrating the potential value—and appeal—of data. The TechCo dashboarding group’s use of marketing employed a data use motivation in which a group identified and then communicated data’s possible value to workers in a customized manner. This marked a skill shift for TechCo’s central analytics groups.

Part of it was developing that relationship where users valued us first and foremost, where they want to hear us. We needed to provide value to them and then get that conversation back to us. By incorporating sales and marketing skills into our process, we have a customer-centric marketing view in how we approach the adoption of our data tools. (Business Analytics Manager, TechCo)

Power promoted change via the mandated adoption and use of analytics tools. Key performance indicators related to data use were introduced, in combination with a reporting mechanism for analytics usage metrics. For example, TechCo sales managers demanded that the salespeople reporting to them use the newly developed sales analytics platform. To monitor compliance, the sales analytics group tracked platform use and reported the usage figures to sales managers, who remediated accordingly. Also, leaders frequently communicated that data must be used in everyday work and for performance management, and they served as role models for this.

Finally, social norms motivated change by expressing employee expectations associated with the importance of data-driven decision-making, which came to be the most acceptable style of making decisions. Employees felt pressure to follow peers who were already applying analytics in their tasks, as exemplified by this comment: “We frequently hear from employees, ‘My peers seem excited about this; maybe I should be excited about this’ or, ‘Yeah, we’re discussing a common problem’” (Business Analytics Manager, TechCo).

## 4.2 Outcomes of Synergy Between Analytics and Business Groups

Here, we examine the two outcomes, emergence and convergence, representing synergies.

## 4.2.1 Emergence of Data-Driven Ways of Working

Data-driven work practices emerged through new, datainformed tools, mindsets, and behaviors. New tools emerged as custom artifacts were developed for various business domains. One example is the sales analytics platform, a tool crafted to meet needs specific to the sales group.

We developed a new sales-experience platform by bringing the seller insights and all the other information into one place. (Sales IT-Business Analyst, TechCo)

A new mindset emerged as the two groups developed new concepts and problem-solving approaches. For example, the sales analytics group and sellers jointly developed new definitions for common business concepts such as “lead,” “customer,” and “product categories.”

The adoption of newly developed analytics tools triggered new employee behaviors. For instance, the metrics presented in each of the sales data platform’s interfaces (e.g., showing the likelihood of closing the deal) were customized for each sales “persona” (e.g., for a sales manager). This inspired new behaviors, which, likewise, varied by persona. Sales managers started using the metrics to monitor their regional sales pipeline, while sales executives drew on in-depth reports and metrics to manage their overall sales portfolio.

## 4.2.2 Convergence of Data-Driven Ways of Working

The convergence of data-driven methods manifested itself in the creation of a shared mindset, shared tools, and shared behaviors that were generalizable and adaptable across the organization.

A shared mindset, representing similar attitudes among employees with regard to using analytics for decision-making, evolved at TechCo when common metrics and data definitions came to be valued and shared organization-wide. For instance, a metric specifically reflecting customer engagement (product use and sentiment) became popular across diverse business groups, while the ways of applying the metric were specific to each group.

Engineering and Marketing look at the same metrics. Marketing will come in to say, “Hey, here’s how we can increase the user base, and here’s how we get them more engaged,” and the engineering group will use the metrics to make the service more usable at feature level. (Marketing-Business Manager, TechCo)

Among the shared tools developed at both companies were common analytics artifacts with widespread use in multiple business domains. The set of dashboard templates prepared by the central dashboarding group for TechCo-wide use illustrates this concept. All other employees engaged in the company’s data-driven initiative contributed to the group’s inventory of common tools that represent opportunities for extension to additional business domains. At FinCo, meanwhile, the COE created a data-engine catalogue categorizing and listing semi-built data science models (e.g., for classification of customers or for credit scores) that could be reused in new projects. The catalogue streamlined searching through existing models.

Shared behavior, on the other hand, arose in how people developed common exploitation of dashboards, algorithms, and other analytics tools to improve local business and product performance. One noteworthy behavior that came to be shared by all TechCo workers was the use of dashboards for informed decisions throughout the organization. This was not confined to the leadership team; it extended to all employees, at every level in the organizational hierarchy. Employees gained competence in using self-service analytics tools to gain insight into business problems. They also learned how to interpret and make sense of algorithm-generated decisions. This configuration produced a novel energy-consumption solution to regulate building temperature purely on the basis of data; the solution was later deployed to save the company large amounts of money.

Configuration 2: Dedicated data groups. Configuration 2 relies on scoped connections between analytics and business groups, collaborative action, and power. Each scoped connection creates proximity between analytics and business groups, which facilitates collaborative action. Analytics groups acquire business domain knowledge firsthand, while business groups guide the development of analytics tools specific to their needs. Equipped with strong business knowledge, the dedicated analytics groups create increasingly customized and function-specific tools and processes that facilitate business users’ ability to draw on meaningful data when performing work tasks. In this configuration, analytics and business groups are incentivized to work together because of power and management mandates.

We found four cases displaying this configuration. The analytics groups showed scoped connections with their respective business groups and were responsible for helping create localized changes to work practices. For instance, marketing’s dedicated analytics group built demographic, attitudinal, and behavioral profiles of customers. The marketing analytics group reported to the chief marketing officer.

## 5.3 Explaining Convergence of Data-Driven Ways of Working

Our analysis revealed three configurations leading to convergence.

Configuration 3: Analytics institutionalization. The first convergence-oriented configuration relies on analytics education and power to encourage conformance. The analytics group form ties with business groups (scoped or scaled as per the intermediate solution) and leverage them to provide education and exert power to encourage workers’ use of the tools and cultivate the desired behavior. This configuration coalesces when senior managers and executives establish a clear, ambitious target of becoming data-driven and, correspondingly, employ power mechanisms to drive things forward. Configuration 3 involves institutionalizing the adoption and consumption of analytics tools across large groups such that the mindset and also the way of behaving converge.

In one of the two cases reflecting this configuration, TechCo’s sales leaders wanted thousands of sales workers to adopt the new sales analytics platform and devote more time to interacting with customers. The sales analytics group tracked salespeople’s platform use and reported back to the directors of sales so they could manage adoption and institutionalize usage.

Configuration 4: Analytics resource optimization. At its core, Configuration 4 optimizes analytics resources at the organization level. This comes about via a scaled connection, analytics education, and a value proposition that inspires a shared data-related mindset, shared behavior, and shared tools. The configuration provisions common analytics tools/techniques and shares good practices at scale. Although similar to Configuration 3 in its reliance on a scaled connection in which analytics education is provided to many business domain workers, analytics resource optimization inspires shared ways of working by demonstrating or otherwise communicating the value that data can provide.

We identified nine cases connected with this configuration. The central dashboarding group at TechCo illustrates the configuration well. TechCo’s central analytics groups, responsible for data governance, dashboarding, and data science, were established by the senior IT management to build and deploy organization-wide data-related services. These groups promoted the use of common data, reports, and algorithms throughout the company. For instance, the dashboarding group connected with many business groups, provided data training that any employee could access, and utilized marketing techniques to promote adoption.

The FinCo COE similarly engaged with all employees to deliver a common data platform and shared capabilities. One common capability was cultivated through the introduction of 34 purpose-built data engines, for use across all business units. The COE clearly categorized the data engines in accordance with common applications (producing credit scores, predicting the next likely action, etc.) so that COE leaders could more readily communicate their reuse potential company-wide. A data engine inventory was formalized whereby algorithms initially developed for a specific bank project (e.g., one designed for digitalmarketing investment analysis) were grouped under generic application categories (e.g., advertising optimization). The inventory, along with associated understandable examples, helped COE leaders better explain the applicability of the data science techniques to others.

Configuration 5: Social communities. The final configuration creates convergence in data-driven ways of working by using social communication channels to drive learning and influence business group members. This configuration coalesces around scaled connections, community engagement, and social norms. It connects individuals through physical social channels (e.g., conferences and other events) or virtual ones (e.g., collaboration or social-media platforms), and it exploits role models, gives access to thought leadership, and makes peer behavior and work practices transparent. This configuration inspires behavior change via peer influence and social norms.

We found two cases displaying the social communities configuration. The Yammer example illustrates it well. Through the central business intelligence group at TechCo, many members of data analytics and business groups participated in the Yammer exchanges, and their correspondence over time produced communitygenerated questions, answers, ideas, and examples that other members could then extend, reinforce, and reapply. Participants were motivated to interact with others via Yammer because company norms encouraged employees to use data in their work tasks.

In another example, the COE at FinCo invested heavily in employees’ reskilling to learn contemporary analytics techniques. This included the preparation of a 300-hour residential data science course after which participants were onboarded to a community-of-practice (CoP) platform by the COE and given free access to COE data and tools. The CoP activities included asynchronous knowledge sharing, virtual monthly community meetups, and annual in-person gatherings that featured presentations of technical best practices. The 2017 annual meet-up brought together 120 personnel, from 11 countries, to share developments in such areas as pricing optimization, churn models, and recommender systems. In July of the same year, the COE designated a “global community manager” to oversee the CoP efforts.

## 6 Discussion

The study fruitfully employed the concept of synergy to explore configurations for knowledge integration between analytics and business groups in data-driven organizational initiatives. Both the outputs (the configurations identified) and our approach and framing represent contributions to academic and practitioner understanding of how companies can gain value from data through undertaking a series of datadriven organizational initiatives.

## 6.1 Implications for Research

## 6.1.1 Organizing for Data-Driven Work

The KBV perspective helped us explore analytics and business domain knowledge integration in a variety of data-driven organizational initiatives. Such initiatives continuously draw together contemporary business and analytics knowledge to produce new and shared ways of working. With this understanding, we posit that enabling data-driven work consistently throughout an organization requires synergistic relationships between these two kinds of groups. Synergistic relationships entail the intertwining of analytics and business groups to such an extent that the tools, mindset, and behavior of members of both constituencies change, and the two merge. Consequently, their joint efforts and effects cannot be separated. Therefore, we argue that organizing for data-driven work requires a shift away from the traditional function-based approach of establishing a central unit toward serving multiple business units with their various analytics needs. A function-oriented approach generally leads to strained relations between analytics and business groups, since the former rarely can meet every demand of the latter (Peppard, 2019). Instead, data-driven work is rooted in a pervasiveness-focused approach in which analytics is woven into the fabric of the organization. This approach to organizing via between-domain bridging requires a collection of scoped and scaled connections, combined with learning activities and incentives, which can bring organization-wide change.

The configurational model we developed articulates the mechanics—the processes and outcomes of synergistic relationships between analytics and business groups— through which knowledge integration is achieved. We used the model, a contribution to scholarship in its own right, to identify specific configurations of developing group connectedness, exchanging knowledge, and incentivizing data use that advance data-driven initiatives. We examined scaled and scoped connections to formulate qualitative insight related to the nature of the holistic model’s connections (i.e., ties) between analytics and business groups. This marks a stark contrast to the usual network-oriented approach of exploring strong vs. weak ties or functional ties by using frequency and other quantitative metrics (Borgatti & Halgin, 2011). In consequence, our set of configurations communicates a rich, dynamic understanding of multiple forms of knowledge exchange (Argote & Greve, 2007)—analytics education, collaborative learning, and community engagement—in organizing pervasively for data-driven work. Similarly, the configurations illustrate the role of key motivation mechanisms (Ajzen, 1991) for assimilating data-driven work throughout organizations: social norms, value, and power. A configurational lens enabled us to draw together all of these distinct elements capturing causal complexity, drawing on several theoretical perspectives to create a single integrative theory.

We uncovered important patterns and distinctions, between and within sets of configurations. Two of them, multidisciplinary teams and dedicated data groups, use scoped connections and collaborative action to create novel data-driven ways of working (with emergence exemplified by the new sales analytics platform and the smart-building solution). In these configurations, a scoped connection helps groups from the two domains work closely together, actively combine and synthesize their knowledge, and collaboratively develop new solutions and data-driven methods. The two configurations, meanwhile, are mutually distinct with regard to motivational levers. The multidisciplinary teams configuration does not rely on formal controls or preexisting structures; rather, all parties self-organize to reach some goal with the aid of data. In contrast, the dedicated data groups configuration draws on formal work structures and responsibilities through which analytics groups and the designated business groups co-create value by adapting incumbent work practices. Both configurations have important roles in pervasive organizing for data-driven work in that they inspire profound transformation in how processes and products are redesigned via the aid of data in a bottom-up manner, yielding emergent data-driven ways of working that lead to differentiated value.

The remaining three configurations are instrumental for the convergence of data-driven work. The analytics institutionalization, analytics resource optimization, and social communities configurations typically use scaled connections to reach out to many groups and span organizational boundaries; these configurations build a toolbox of generalized analytics practices, and they come alive when groups contextualize common practices for their own localized needs. Analytics institutionalization uses education to train business groups, and power persuades them to adopt common analytics practices. Analytics resource optimization achieves similar outcomes by providing democratized access to contemporary analytics platforms, employing reusable, customizable analytics services that business groups can comprehend and exploit. Finally, the social communities configuration engages large audiences with between-group representation, renders audience members’ practices accessible and visible to the community, and encourages the reuse and adaptation of relevant ideas. These three configurations facilitate the pervasive organization of data-driven work because they scale data-driven methods through their creation of company-wide norms and shared ways of working (Dougherty, 2001).

Synthesis of our findings reveals that data-driven organizational initiatives continuously produce datadriven work practices (in the emergence of new ways of working) and share them organization-wide (in the convergence of these). We posit that entities committing to data-driven organizational initiatives must utilize multiple configurations, together producing both emergence and convergence. Emergence is necessary for a bottom-up approach wherein data inform the redefinition of processes, products, and services such that these constantly evolve on the basis of insight derived from the data. This differs fundamentally from the top-down implementations typifying technologies such as enterprise systems. However, organizations that focus purely on emergence end up repeatedly reinventing the wheel and ultimately fail to reap economies of scale from the reuse and extension of existing practices. Organizations that strive only for convergence merely adopt common industry practices that, being neither innovative nor context-sensitive, may fail to enable strategically important advances—or development distinct to the underlying or desired business model.

Further, we have preliminary evidence of a causal relationship between emergence and convergence. Most clearly, emergence can act as a force triggering convergence. For example, as new ways of working emerge from interactions between analytics and business groups, this can inspire the formation of other configurations, oriented toward convergence, to scale the new, data-driven methods. Equally, convergence can create an impetus for emergence. For instance, as convergence arises, workers organization-wide begin to utilize the shared tools and approaches; this widespread use can then expose gaps in the portfolio of data-driven practices available, allowing emergence-oriented configurations to coalesce. This was visible when TechCo’s shared-services team diffused the organization’s analytics tool across the company. As employees started using it, they provided feedback to the analytics team, which led to collaboration with the product group on innovation with the tool. Convergence, on the other hand, gained higher priority when TechCo bought a new humanrelations analytics company, complete with tools and approaches: the new analytics entity within TechCo immediately started to share, scale, and diffuse these approaches.

While we did not set out to study ambidexterity in organizations’ data-driven initiatives, the foregoing discussion of the relationship between emergence and convergence points to interdependence, with possibly complex two-way temporal and causal relations. This ties in with organizational learning theory and writings on ambidexterity (March, 1991) that portray successful organizations as effectively utilizing two opposing forces: one focused on the exploration of new knowledge and capabilities and the other on the exploitation of existing ones. Accordingly, our findings reveal that the essence of data-driven organizational initiatives is in possessing a capability to integrate analytics and business domain knowledge—firstly, to combine, recombine, and generate new knowledge and capabilities (i.e., perform analytics exploration) and, secondly, to contextualize, diffuse, and reuse existing analytics capabilities (i.e., perform analytics exploitation). The configurations we identified for the emergence and convergence of data-driven ways of working provide the causal recipes for achieving this beyond common paradoxical thinking and strategizing.

## 6.1.2 Knowledge Integration Through Synergies

Our central contribution to the knowledge-integration literature is the set of configurations that lead to datadriven change. Rather than consider knowledge integration from the angle of actors, processes, and tools as so many have done to date (e.g., Huang et al., 2001), we sought to explore knowledge integration by investigating patterns of interaction between analytics and business groups. We paved the way in modeling these interactions by means of the concept of synergy, the use of which has been largely confined to resourcebased IS research into how higher-order IT-enabled organizational resources emerge (Nevo & Wade, 2010, 2011). Our configurational approach to synergy yielded specific causal recipes that reflect how organizations can successfully implement data-driven initiatives.

A further contribution lies in unpacking the concept of synergy itself. We qualitatively specified three processes of a synergistic relationship (developing group connectedness, exchanging analytics and business domain knowledge, and incentivizing organizational data use) and two concepts related to outcomes of synergy (emergence and convergence of data-driven ways of working) specific to data-driven organizational initiatives. We find synergy to be a causally complex phenomenon in that a combination of processes, linked to different theoretical perspectives, is required for bringing it about. Through configurational analysis, we identified precisely such combinations of theoretical elements, and our configurations express the theoretical multiplicity inherent in the synergy concept. Thereby, our holistic approach enhances the theoretical understanding of synergy, heretofore articulated mainly in terms of statistical interaction between variables in endeavors to capture synergistic effects. Our configuration-based approach to achieving synergy is a qualitatively informed one that extends the scholarly work looking at enablers, sources (Tanriverdi, 2006; Tanriverdi & Venkatraman, 2005), and mechanisms of synergy (Venkatesh & Bala, 2012).

Finally, this paper contributes to the work on synergy by examining both emergent and convergent outcomes, whereas scholarship has thus far focused on emergent outcomes only. We argue that the success of data-driven organizational initiatives requires creating emergent and convergent outcomes concurrently. This is particularly true in light of the objectives set for it: most leaders of data-driven change efforts are intent not merely on innovating but also on scaling innovations across the organization: creating new standard ways of working.

## 6.2 Implications for Practice

One strength of the configurational approach lies in bridging the theory-practice gap. Our model offers causal recipes that translate into actionable templates, which organizations’ leaders can readily apply to nurture an organizational data capability through value-adding initiatives. We highlight four key ways in which our configurations can inform and guide management practice: enabling innovation and promoting the sharing of best practices, organizing for data-driven work via appropriate connecting structures, motivating engagement with data organization-wide, and enhancing the organization’s data literacy.

To take the first of these interrelated issues into account, executives responsible for data groups must prepare the organization (structurally etc.) to generate innovative analytics-based work practices and then maximize investments by sharing and scaling the best of the new practices across the company. Analytics is not a plug-and-play technology, and data-driven change involves far more than introducing a toolset to the organization. On the one hand, organizations need to cultivate analytics talent and encourage engagement with business groups to co-create new, data-driven work practices that solve compelling problems and address opportunities in situ. This need can be met by establishing two of our configurations, those involving multidisciplinary and dedicated data teams. On the other hand, organizations also have to share and scale data-driven practices so that employees and processes far and wide use data in ways that generate value (Someh et. al. 2017). Three of our configurations achieve this: analytics institutionalization, analytics resource optimization, and engaged communities. Via the prudent application of several suitable configurations, data executives can shape an organization that both enables innovation and promotes shared, contemporary data-driven ways of working.

Secondly, in awareness that structural divides are the main barrier to knowledge integration, data executives need to structure their organization for data-driven work by developing meaningful links between analytics groups and business groups. These connecting mechanisms must transcend domain boundaries and support the creation of new (emergent) and shared (convergent) data-driven ways of working. In contrast, organizations that extend a traditional, function-oriented way of arranging work to the data context typically establish a centralized entity in the form of an analytics center of excellence and/or create local decentralized groups such as a marketing analytics function. The observed lack of success and progress in organizational data-driven initiatives (Bean & Davenport, 2019; Davenport & Bean, 2018) suggests that such approaches require rethinking and refinement. Centralized efforts (e.g., COEs alone) are likely to fail to meet the numerous, highly varied needs of business groups or respond to all their nuances, while local, decentralized approaches fall short in terms of scalability. We have developed a new approach to this by uncovering aspects of how organizations can facilitate group connectedness by means of scoped and scaled connections. We highlight the value of both for practitioners: scoped connections accentuate deep, focused collaboration, and cultivating the scaled connections involves influencing large audiences of employees. Each type of connecting structure comes with supporting causal recipes that produce desirable outcomes for organizations.

At the same time, data executives need to motivate people to make full use of data. Traditionally, leaders establish power structures, accountability mechanisms, and performance metrics; however, our results show that such techniques must be accompanied by the identification and communication of a core value proposition related to data. This involves establishing a common way of talking about data; facilitating community discourse and engagement; and clarifying and articulating the benefits of data-driven actions, tools, and mindsets. Three of the configurations (multidisciplinary teams, analytics resource optimization, and engaged communities) favor value-proposition motivation over power-based techniques. In fact, through our casestudy work at FinCo, we identified configurations that operate on the basis of the value proposition and social norms, with very little recourse to power: FinCo established its analytics COE as a legal entity separate from the bank operations themselves for purposes of addressing regulatory requirements for data monetization; creating a safe haven for establishing and curating contemporary data-driven work practices; and attracting data science talent, which is in high demand. Accordingly, the COE did not have formal control, power, or reporting structures in place within the bank. Instead, its engagement with bank employees and units stemmed purely from an ability to demonstrate data’s value, by means of such activities as education programs, community offerings, and even endeavors for the social good. TechCo, in contrast, relied heavily on power to purposefully direct the success of efforts such as promoting the adoption of reengineered business process-based solutions, efforts fueled by data.

Finally, our findings confirm that success is conditioned on executives educating people throughout the organization about data. Data and analytics literacy is a prerequisite for developing datadriven initiatives and upskilling employees in ways that prepare them for the work tasks of the future. Both TechCo and FinCo invested in extensive training and education, which enabled the development of an analytics mindset. Both organizations segmented their staff by analytics needs and supplied training responsive to segment-specific requirements. Furthermore, communications about analytics at TechCo were personalized for specific employee audiences, to guarantee a clear understanding of messages about data and analytics. The analytics resource optimization configuration illustrates this well in that it points to a crucial role in the establishment of a common analytics education and literacy programs that can be offered at scale yet with an agenda and value proposition unique to a specific audience.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Work

There are several limitations to our study, which indicate multiple avenues for future research. First, our approach yielded preliminary findings in some areas that require further exploration. One of these is ambidexterity. While prior research into digital transformation has examined this, ambidexterity specific to organizations data-driven change initiatives remains unexplored. We believe that developing a deep understanding of the associated paradoxes and whether/how organizations manage tensions and competing demands in data-driven transformation merits further work. Future research could consider this perspective more directly, studying the push and pull between emergence and convergence configurations, alongside their causal and temporal relationship. Whereas our data set was insufficient for capturing this perspective, process-oriented theory development that applies qualitative data could be undertaken to examine data-driven change mechanisms.

Second, we did not purposely seek to collect extensive data related to failure cases. While we did meet QCA requirements for a threshold of 10% of cases reflecting failure, the research could have benefited from the identification and further analysis of more cases of failure. The analysis of failure-displaying cases could inform theoretical and practical understanding of what occurs when certain mechanisms do not work as intended or desired. It could also help to identify factors that act as definitive barriers to success.

As is appropriate for a starting point to conceptual work and theory development, we selected revelatory organizational settings: companies that were pervasively and aggressively pursuing data-driven organizational initiatives. While personal influence of some sort is inescapable in qualitative work and sometimes a single manager was responsible for leading multiple initiatives in the given setting, the patterns persisted between two, quite different organizations, each with ample opportunities for heterogeneity at various levels. That said, future research should test the applicability of the configurational model at scale, by means of large-N configurational analysis, and a larger number of cases would enable a higher case-frequency cutoff point (Park et al., 2017) in the truth-table analysis. Alongside addressing generalizability, such studies could delve into the performance effects of particular configurations to ascertain the systematic differences between them.

## About the Authors

Ida Someh is a senior lecturer in the Business Information Systems discipline at The University of Queensland Business School, and a research affiliate at the Center for Information Systems Research (CISR), MIT Sloan School of Management. Her research investigates the organizational and societal impact of data, analytics, and artificial intelligence. She completed her PhD at The University of Melbourne and was awarded the best PhD thesis at the Melbourne School of Engineering, and the Vice Chancellor's PhD Prize at The University of Melbourne. Her prior work has appeared in European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, MIS Quarterly Executive, and Sloan Management Review. In 2019, she won the Paul Gray Award for the most thought-provoking article published in Communications of the Association for Information Systems.

Barbara Wixom is a principal research scientist at the MIT Sloan Center for Information System Research. She leads the MIT CISR Data Research Advisory Board, comprised of one hundred data executives from CISR organizations. The board prioritizes, informs, and participates in research activities in ways that influence findings and insights at MIT CISR and help advance the data analytics field. Barbara has published research in such journals as Information Systems Research, MIT Sloan Management Review, MIS Quarterly, and MIS Quarterly Executive, and she regularly presents her work globally. Five of her cases have placed in the Society for Information Management Paper Awards competition, and in 2021, she received the Association for Information Systems AIS Outreach Practice Publication Award for her data monetization research. Prior to MIT CISR, Barbara was a tenured faculty member at the University of Virginia (UVA) where she twice earned the UVA All-University Teaching Award (2002, 2010), which recognizes teaching excellence in professors. In 2017, she was awarded the Teradata University Network Hugh J. Watson Award for contributions to the data and analytics academic community.

Michael Davern, BCom (Hons), PhD, FCPA, holds the Chair of Accounting and Business Information Systems at The University of Melbourne where he has held appointments in both business and engineering. With a focus on decision-making, his published work spans diverse topics including analytics, data governance, ethics, financial reporting, and IT Value. Michael is a Distinguished Member of AIS, a member of the CPA Australia Centre of Excellence in Digital Transformation, and co-founder of the AIS Cognitive Research SIG. His prior work has appeared in Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, The Data Base for Advances in Information Systems, Journal of Information Systems, and Communications of the ACM, among others. He is currently on the editorial board of the Journal of Information Systems, the International Journal of Accounting Information Systems, and AIS Transactions on Human-Computer Interaction. An industry-engaged scholar, with board-level experience in both the for-purpose and private sectors, Michael’s work has been supported by over \$2 million in research grants and direct industry funding.

Graeme Shanks is a professor in the Business Information Systems discipline at the UQ Business School, the University of Queensland, and a Professorial Fellow in the School of Computing and Information Systems, the University of Melbourne. He was previously the associate dean of research and a professor in the Faculty of Information Technology at Monash University. Before becoming an academic, Graeme worked for several private and government organizations as a programmer, systems analyst, and project leader. His research interests focus on the management and impact of information systems, data analytics, enterprise architecture, and conceptual modelling. He has published the results of his research widely in leading information systems journals, and conferences.
