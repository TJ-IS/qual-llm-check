# An electronic commerce strategic typology: insights from case studies

Grover S. Kearns

Information Systems, College of Business, University of South Florida St. Petersburg, St. Petersburg, FL 33701-5016, USA

Received 6 October 2003; received in revised form 8 September 2004; accepted 20 November 2004 Available online 23 February 2005

## Abstract

Growth in electronic commerce (EC) continues to exceed growth rates in traditional markets and continues to offer new and different opportunities than traditional businesses. Its failures, however, suggest that many firms are not following successful strategies and have failed to align their EC strategies with business strategies. Observing practices at 12 companies provided insight into the impact of EC alignment on organizational profitability. A framework for its strategy types, derived from the Miles and Snow typology, was found to be useful in examining the strategic practices of the companies and results were generally consistent with the ideal profiles. Data suggested that companies with medium to high alignment exhibited higher planning formality and were more profitable. Analyzers displayed the highest alignment and online commitment. Both analyzers and prospectors had high profitability but prospectors displayed low alignment. Reactors exhibited the lowest alignment and were the least profitable.

## 1. Introduction

Following the economic downturn from 2000 resulting in loss of venture capital and increased investor apprehension, management adopted a more rational and cautious perspective towards e-business investment. Despite tremendous losses, both businessto-consumer (B2C) and business-to-business (B2B) electronic commerce (EC) continue to expand at rates in excess of their traditional counterparts. The primary question facing managers today is not whether EC investments are worthwhile, but how to best manage the investments to increase returns.

In a recent analysis of CEO letters to shareholders, it was concluded that EC performance can be improved by emphasizing it in business strategies and that failure to integrate EC and business strategies decreased the likelihood of success [3]. In a survey conducted by the London Business School [4], twothirds of responding CEOs stated that their companies had separate EC strategies.

Aligning EC strategies with business strategies— that is, applying proven business approaches and achieving consistency in terms of future direction— has the potential to increase returns on EC investments. At the same time, the online business can reflect the unique opportunities offered by the electronic marketplace and create organizational synergies. There is general agreement that proper alignment is critical to strategic IT success, yet little is known about the implications of EC alignment. A recent review of the literature showed that less than 10% of 275 articles on EC-related topics took strategy into consideration and that none addressed alignment issues [22].

Three streams of research are available for deriving prescriptive approaches to strategic alignment: surveys of alignment practices; case studies; and conceptual studies [17]. Using a combination of the case and survey approaches, this study examined EC strategic behaviors adopted by various organizations. The theoretical background provided is that proposed by Miles and Snow [18]. The study attempted to answer two research questions:

1. How does EC alignment affect organizational profitability?

2. Are formal planning mechanisms more likely to support EC alignment?

Another purpose was to develop a framework of EC typologies that could be used to evaluate EC strategic behavior.

## 2. Theoretical background and propositions

## 2.1. Strategy

Goldsmith [9] was one of the first to suggest that information technology (IT) strategies should be developed using business strategy techniques. This is especially true when IT strategies are driven by business strategies, which are identified according to their textual, multivariate, or typological approaches [10,12,19]. A number of typologies have used broad, generic categorizations that encompass most industries to examine organizational strategies [1,23]. Of these, the best known is the Miles and Snow model, which has been empirically examined and shown to be rigorous [26]. Extensive and diverse use has established its codification and prediction strengths [13,29].

Their typology identifies four strategy types: prospector, defender, analyzer, or reactor. Firms select a particular strategy to match their conditions:

 Prospectors are considered innovators and market leaders; they accept higher risk levels and are more willing to invest in new technologies and to explore new markets. Their planning methods are broader and more exploratory, which matches their need to identify technologies and markets with greater potential for growth. This may entail disrupting markets and taking advantage of the resulting turbulence. Prospector success requires differentiation and flexible organizational structures that reflect changing markets. Informal planning mechanisms support the need for agility and adaptability.

 Defenders are associated with stable and established market niches. Relying on the trust and loyalty of a solid customer base, they emphasize quality and cost leadership by exploiting existing technologies and are not likely to invest in basic research or take risks. Defenders use formal planning mechanisms and are adept at modifying proven strategies and maintaining alignment.

 Analyzers combine the best qualities of prospectors and defenders. Considered opportunistic, they rely on research, quickly examine special opportunities, and accept risks that are commensurate with the expected outcomes of adopted strategies. Analyzers depend on well-formed strategies to minimize risk and to leverage existing technologies at the same time that they search for new ones. Stable product lines with acceptable quality–price ratios are exploited using formal planning approaches common to established markets. In turbulent markets, they use informal, adaptive strategies to create alignment.

 Reactors, generally considered the least successful, pursue strategies that actually impede business performance. They often recognize change but rely on formal planning methods that have failed to keep pace with evolving environments. Their failures are frequently tied to miscalculations of environmental change, the misuse of technology, or the inability to let go of older strategies that were once profitable.

The principal strength of the Miles and Snow typology is its ability to represent a complex view of an organization’s environmental processes, organizational structure, management, market, technology, and product characteristics [30]. Croteau and Bergeron [5], for example, found that the typology was useful in predicting the relationship between technological deployment and organizational performance. Hambrick used it to categorize 1452 firms as either defenders or prospectors. Results showed that defenders were marked by high capital intensity, high employee productivity, and low direct costs, while prospectors were marked by higher R&D investments and higher marketing expenses. In a study involving 47 electronic manufacturing firms, Smith et al. found that prospectors were characterized by a changing product mix, innovation, market disruption, and an aggressive growth strategy. Furthermore, they found that analyzers divided their attention between exploiting existing product lines and investing in new product designs; defender firms relied on advertising, maintaining production efficiencies, and offering competitive prices to protect their product lines; and reactor firms exhibited inconsistent approaches to organizational problems.

Although Miles and Snow recognized the defender, prospector, and analyzer positions as equally appropriate strategies, an argument can be made for the expected profitability of each in the online dimension. The defender strategy is cost-based competition; it places downward pressure on profits. However, greater environmental stability, brand recognition, and the ability to extract cost efficiencies from the virtual space will improve the defender’s profitability. Prospectors can create change, penetrate new markets via innovation, and enjoy first mover advantages but must trade cost efficiencies for flexibility. Maintaining a reputation as an innovator and first mover may even be more important to prospectors than short-term profitability. This restricts prospectors to less than high profits. Analyzers, by closely analyzing opportunities related to their product lines, can achieve higher profits by moving into markets opened by prospectors and realizing cost efficiencies simultaneously. Based on the belief that the Miles and Snow typology is appropriate for analysis of EC strategic practices, yields:

Proposition 1. EC strategy types will be consistent with those predicted by the Miles and Snow typology.

Proposition 2. EC firms that adopt an analyzer strategy will be the most profitable firms.

Proposition 3. EC firms that adopt a reactor strategy will be the least profitable firms.

## 2.2. Planning formality

Strategic planning improves the fit between organizational and external environments [8]. The extent of formal planning mechanisms in a firm’s planning approach may be expected to vary according to the turbulence in the external environment. Formal mechanisms include: steering committees, forecasting, planning meetings, preparation of planning documents, explicit strategy formulation, and monitoring activities. Formal approaches can be successful, particularly in stable environments [25,28]. In turbulent environments, however, they may be too rigid and reduce management agility [21]. The degree of formality in the planning approach is expected to be related to the strategic typology that the firm adopts. This yields:

Proposition 4. EC strategic typologies will be related to the extent of planning formality.

## 2.3. Alignment

Strategic IT alignment refers to the incorporation of the business mission, goals, and strategies into the IT strategy during the IT planning process [6,15]. Alignment between IT strategies and business strategies is of major concern of both CEOs and CIOs [32] and its importance has been well addressed in previous research [11]. Lederer and Mendelow [16] noted that alignment benefits the organization by assuring the development of applications most critical to the business. Failed IT investments are frequently caused by the lack of alignment between IT and business strategies. IT alignment can positively affect business performance [2] and is an important factor in achieving competitive advantage [16]. Misalignment between the business and EC strategies can also lead to failure of e-businesses. However, they offer unique opportunities that must be reflected in business strategies in order to realize the full value of EC [24]. These ideas are reflected in Fig. 1.

Without alignment, EC strategies might not reflect the overall direction of the company resulting in lower returns, marketplace confusion, and erosion of the firm’s competitive position. Sabherwal and Chan, for example, found that the ‘‘association between alignment and business success depends upon the business strategy’’ [27]. Furthermore, they found a high association between alignment and profitability for prospectors and analyzers but not for defenders, probably because they have less need to adopt new technologies in order to maintain alignment. Defenders, however, can more easily transfer successful customer relationship strategies to the Web and are more apt to create high online commitment.

In the development of EC strategies, however, explicit alignment between the online business and the physical business is rarely seen. Chang et al. contend that firms that fail to integrate EC ‘‘may not garner the appropriate resources in order to create the unique capabilities necessary to compete in the virtual world’’. Even for ‘pure-plays’ which have no physical counterpart, online strategy rarely reflects the full rigor of business planning. The absence of strategic EC alignment could result in an investment that does not support the direction intended by management or one that provides a less profitable return. Based on the belief that EC alignment has positive firm affects and is consistent with the firm’s approach to planning yields:

Proposition 5. EC firms will vary, by strategic typology, in the extent to which they align business and EC strategies.

Proposition 6. EC firms that use formal planning mechanisms will have greater alignment between business and EC strategies.

Proposition 7. EC firms that have medium or high alignment between business and EC strategies will be more profitable than those that have low alignment.

## 2.3.1. Electronic commerce and online commitment

EC firms might earn higher profits and be more successful if they adopt a strong online commitment and pursue strategies that reflect the unique opportunities of this market as opposed to simply using the Internet as another channel to promote their existing business. By aligning the business and EC strategies, there is a greater likelihood of adopting strategies that reflect EC strengths and opportunities.

Kalakota and Whinston [14] offered a classification of EC activities through four perspectives. The communications perspective includes the delivery of information products and services, or payments via electronic mechanisms and/or networks. The business process perspective uses online technology to automate transactions and workflow. The service perspective is customer-centric, adding value by cutting costs while increasing the quality of goods and speed of delivery. The online perspective provides customers with full retail or wholesale capabilities of selecting and purchasing goods and services online. The intensity with which these four perspectives are present can be used to measure online commitment.

E-businesses can vary in online commitment. Differing strategic approaches may contribute to the extent of the online presence and the underlying technology investment; e.g., defenders and analyzers operating in more stable environments can leverage brand trust and loyalty by marketing existing brands online. Barnes & Noble exploited the presence of a national chain of existing stores where buyers could return online purchases. Prospectors, having a greater need for flexibility in technology to support innovation, would fear becoming entrenched and would not be expected to invest in rigid, online systems and technologies that produce short-term efficiencies.

Defenders, on the other hand, have been shown to reap greater benefits from technological investments in both the short- and long-run [7]. Formal planning approaches, normally associated with lower market turbulence, would be expected to lead to higher fixed-cost investment in online technologies. This yields:

Proposition 8. EC firms that use formal planning mechanisms will have a stronger online commitment.

## 3. A framework for EC strategy types

Many companies report separate EC strategies. How they are aligned with business objectives, however, is not well understood. A framework was therefore created to present a strategic EC typology using the Miles and Snow theoretical background. The profiles for each strategic type were based on a set of criteria designed to reflect EC strategic behavior.

The criteria included Kalakota and Whinston’s four classifications of commitment to and reliance upon EC. Other criteria were business/EC alignment, and the extent of planning formality. The framework was completed by assigning classificatory levels for each of the criteria. Values assigned for planning formality were formal, informal, or mixed. The other criteria were assigned values of low, medium, or high.

## 3.1. Creating the framework

To complete the strategic typology, normalized strategic behaviors for each criteria had to be identified. Values were assigned to each of the criteria based upon the expected behavior for each of the strategic types. Defenders, for example, were expected to use IT to support and exploit a successful product line by maintaining quality and creating efficiencies. The high quality-to-cost ratio places downward pressure on profitability and growth was frequently limited because of a commitment to exploitation to a fixed product line. Operating mainly in stable environments, they therefore adhered to a formal planning approach. EC strategies mirrored proven business strategies making alignment high. Because successful business practices could be duplicated online and the defender could transfer the trust and loyalty of a known brand to the e-business, commitment to all four of the online perspectives was expected to be high. Barnes & Noble, Charles Schwab, and Wal-Mart have each created a successful online presence and exhibit high commitment to the online perspectives. Defenders may not have a choice: if they failed to move online, they risked loss of market to first-movers.

Prospectors were opportunistic and used IT to seek out innovative approaches, reduce uncertainty, and maintain agility. They used informal planning approaches to remain aligned with their turbulent environments but the need for agility made alignment of business and EC strategies difficult and resulted in varying commitment to the four online perspectives. Communications and service would be of medium intensity because the delivery and payment mechanisms, although present, would not have matured to a high level. Prospectors were too dynamic to focus on highly automated transactions and workflows, so the business perspective would range between low and medium. Infrastructure for online selection and purchase of goods would be emphasized in order to exploit the online marketplace and the online perspective would range between medium and high. Inability to use the Internet to its fullest potential placed barriers on growth and profitability.

Analyzers operated in mixed environments and used IT to support entrenched product lines and to explore new directions. Thus, planning approaches would incorporate both formal and informal aspects. Using the Internet to create an effective and stable online presence, they could exploit existing products and launch new products by leveraging the reputation of existing and respected brands. This advantage should have provided higher rates of growth and profitability than defenders or prospectors. Through a combination of formal and informal planning approaches, analyzers could achieve higher alignment between business and EC strategies and use best practices to improve value via online communications and sales. Because of the need for adaptability, online systems would be less refined than for the defender and the four online perspectives would reflect a position between the defender and prospector.

Reactors were expected to follow formal planning methods that were successful in past environments but were no longer competitive. Commitment to the four online perspectives would be erratic but would generally fall below that of the prospector because they failed to exploit the online channel.

## 4. Research method

The research approach combined aspects of case study method with a survey of the case study participants. The questionnaire contained both closedand open-ended questions. Responses were validated by observation and provided valuable information regarding opinions of the primary informant. The explanatory case approach provides for more objective data gathering and allows the observer to separate fact from opinion [20]. Combining two forms helps to identify areas of discrepancy between opinions and observed performance. Such discrepancies can provide further insights into the failure of executive actions in creating alignment.

The method adopted for case analysis followed an explanatory approach using multiple sources of evidence. This requires a priori knowledge of underlying theory and an examination of the underlying causal patterns that answer the research questions [31].

Several data collection methods are available and it has been recommended that two or more methods be used to provide for convergence of findings. The possible methods are: exploration of documents and memoranda; exploration of archived records; direct observation; interviews; participant-observation; and physical artifact. Exploration of records, direct observation, and interviews were the methods chosen here. A large number of companies were chosen to provide adequate data for a representative typology.

## 4.1. The companies

Twelve companies in a southern U.S. metropolitan area with a total population exceeding three million were selected for study. The companies were close to the researcher and management gave their consent for the investigator to collect data and to interview and observe management. A protocol, shown in Appendix A, was developed, tested, and used for initial collection of data during the first meeting. The initial interview, where survey data were collected, was with the senior IT manager, the president, or the CEO. Using this information, later meetings included direct observation, attendance at planning meetings, and involvement in the work activities of EC planning personnel. Information was recorded and studied later to determine whether the observed processes deviated from the material and statements given in the initial interview. For example, in one case the initial comments identified the planning process as highly informal whereas direct observation showed it to be formalized with little informal communication or knowledge sharing outside planned meetings.

Company size ranged from small businesses, which had been financially successful over a period of at least 5 years, to internationally recognized companies that retailed all their product line through e-business. Company descriptions, size in assets, and 3-year annual growth rates are summarized in Table 2.

Sales growth and profitability data were provided by each of the companies. The companies were further analyzed to determine the planning type (formal/ mixed/informal), planning emphasis, presence (or absence) of EC alignment, and extent of the four EC perspectives. Interview transcripts and observation notes were coded with labels that were later used to identify patterns of planning and alignment practices. For each company, the investigator made assignments for each of the criteria measuring strategy type. Assignments were based upon sufficiently clear evidence to provide a satisfactory degree of reliability. A determination was made as to strategy type using the proposed framework in Table 1. If the overall values were inconsistent with the normative values and did not clearly reflect a dominant underlying strategy type, a choice was made based upon the overall values; any inconsistency was noted in Table 3. Of the 96 assignments, there were 28 anomalies (indicated by an asterisk) representing behaviors that did not coincide with the ideal profile for that strategy type. Of these, only two deviated more than one step from the norm.

## 4.2. Prospector (company 9)

The prospector company is an international electronics manufacturer, primarily of computer and network equipment, that uses both B2B and B2C and has offices in 20 countries. Each business unit prepares a business plan and there is a centralized IT plan but no formal e-business plan; i.e., EC planning is not separated from IT planning. The prospector customizes business solutions for customers and provides detailed product and service information online. The B2C site has limited usage metrics. Design changes are made quickly, based upon management’s perception of market need, and usability testing is limited and often absent. Management does not view competition as a major threat and feels the high rate of customer retention reflects satisfaction with high quality service and reasonable prices. They see a lack of e-planning and lack of customer metrics as weaknesses that need to be addressed. Although they see explicit business strategies as important to EC decisions, they are rated low as drivers.

Their strengths include the ability to provide unique and quality products and the development of long-term relationships with customers. Proprietary software is provided free to customers to create switching costs. B2C retailing has strengthened their international market position and their site fully supports e-retailing. Major weaknesses include a lack of alignment between business and EC strategies, lack of customer metrics, inefficient site navigation, difficulty in maintaining online customer information, and lack of an effective process for EC managers in different countries to collaborate and exchange ideas.

Main threats include reliance on 40 major customers and a rapidly changing environment that makes it difficult to gauge customer’s changing needs. Planning is informal and ad hoc and there is feeling of being ‘‘always behind’’. Various planning approaches are used including: brainstorming, best practices, and gap analysis. Executives meet frequently to discuss customer needs. Because these change frequently, the business plan is not highly valued and there is not a separate EC plan. This prospector has been very good at identifying customer needs but only moderately so at providing an efficient mechanism for addressing them.

## 4.3. Defender (company 5)

The defender started as a large reseller of telephone systems over a tri-state area in the south but, with a well-executed entry into EC, quickly became a national retailer. They provide sales, service, and repair through both traditional and EC channels. The online site quickly doubled company revenues. All non-core functions are outsourced. They are price competitive and use a formal planning approach in which business strategies drive EC strategies. The online site uses a dynamic link to a database of all equipment, with pricing and sales information on the various systems, as well as a shopping cart and contact information. Because of the high cost of the phone systems, new customers usually make direct contact and collect more information prior to final purchase. Subsequent contacts are usually made by e-mail.

Defender strengths include good EC alignment, high sales and profit growth over the past 5 years, a high customer retention rate due to trust and loyalty, the experience and skills of employees, low overhead, and high quality service. Outsourced web design could be construed as either a strength or a weakness. Site success and the ability to reflect product changes argue for it as strength; however, design changes have been infrequent and the defender has complained about a delay in getting design changes reflected quickly.

Although the company offers a standard product they have differentiated themselves by adding full service warranty for each system and a buy-back program for customers wishing to upgrade; they often call and request a new system with few or no questions. Because customers can view the systems online, repeat business has increased. Major threats include competition and a rapidly evolving technology. Competition has been countered by superior marketing and EC strategies.

## 4.4. Analyzer (company 1)

The analyzer is a television-based shopping program with nationwide coverage. After several years of success, the company created a companion web site offering the same products online. The immediate success of this site led to an expanded product line with complete customer support, online service, shipment tracking, and various forms of electronic payments. The B2C site is dynamic and well conceived. The large product line is constantly updated requiring a retinue of skilled employees to ensure that the design is efficiently navigable and that customers can quickly find what they want. Collection of individual information allows the site to cater to repeat customers by dynamically providing them customized information about products that fit their profile and demographic segmentation. Originally conceived to relieve the burden of telephone sales, the site now has a higher rate of growth and profitability than the television channel.

EC strategies were originally driven by business strategies. EC now has a high degree of autonomy and uses both business strategies and customer information to evolve site designs which are targeted to increase sales, customer retention, and customer satisfaction. The customer-centric site is highly profitable and is now managed as a separate business unit. IT alignment is deemed as very important with both formal and informal processes of communication. An IT steering committee and weekly meetings of technical staff ensure that systems and EC changes are aligned with company direction. Planning is fluid, creating opportunities for knowledge sharing among employees and the agility to respond to changes in the market.

EC’s goal is to enhance and personalize the experience for the customer, to retain customers, and build loyalty. The infrastructure is kept simple to reduce the possibility of downtime, streamline ordering, and promote a high level of stickiness. EC strengths include a well designed site, strong sales growth, high customer satisfaction, alignment with business goals reflecting a proven business model, a dynamic process for customizing the customer experience, an architecture that allows adding higher numbers of products without causing degradation of service, and an established brand with trust and customer loyalty. The analyzer company’s only identified weakness is the potential for competition to erode the market, although entry cost is a formidable barrier.

## 4.5. Reactor (company 8)

This company develops customer intranet, network, and web applications for businesses that operate both regionally and nationally. Larger than most competitors, their functional areas are separated with limited internal communications outside of planned meetings. Everyone is considered an expert within his or her area and attention is directed at ‘‘doing, not thinking’’. Services are targeted at business clients who want to lower costs without compromising quality. The business strategy is to employ standardized architectures for intranet installations and modify existing web sites using templates. Rapid prototyping is used to determine if scope is correct and management teams meet to assemble a project plan using a standardized approach supported by software for tracking progress. Focus is on speed, cost, and scope.

New customers are the product of referrals and contacts from the reactor’s own EC site. Because sales growth has been high and management is reluctant to commit to hiring more technical staff, the reactor’s own web site is weak—a brochure to which product information, an FAQ, and graphics have been added haphazardly resulting in poor navigation. Customer communication support is by e-mail and telephone.

Online information is incomplete, requiring customers to make unnecessary contacts to the sales office. Despite these shortcomings, management acknowledges that the site is responsible for increased sales growth.

While the reactor depends on customer referrals, there is no way of tracking how new customers originate. Local competition is not considered a threat, but there is no assessment of national competition, nor are there plans to accommodate the higher growth, the firm is content to generate an acceptable profit. Formal planning exists and there is a tight planning process for bidding jobs. The company routinely initiates client satisfaction surveys but does little with the findings other than present them at a meeting. Strengths include a strong client base, highly skilled staff, high customer retention, good sales growth, partnerships with two industry software leaders, and a successful bidding process. Weaknesses include a lack of EC alignment, lack of internal communications, no written IT or EC plans, sole reliance on referrals for growth, and a weak EC site (scoring low on all of the four EC perspectives). Their business process is highly standardized with emphasis on the visual appeal and aesthetics of customer sites and fast installation of intranets and networks based upon standardized approaches that provide a high percent of the customer’s requirements without the cost of a completely customized installation.

A unique business strategy is that after the project is fully tested and placed into production, the reactor trains the customer to maintain the project in order to avoid the low-profit maintenance arena. The goal of ‘‘on time and under budget’’ is successful for most customers seeking value. The reactor, however, has failed to use its own web site as an example of the quality to which they aspire or to offer information online that would create sales growth and allow prospective clients to understand the benefits and tradeoffs of the services offered.

The typology was not necessarily consistent across all criteria. Some analyzers used a predominately formal approach to planning whereas they would have benefited by incorporating elements of an informal, adaptive approach. ## 5.1. Business/EC strategic alignment and profitability

The relationship between business and EC strategic alignment and profitability is shown by strategy type for all 12 companies in Fig. 2. Profitability was measured by the average return-on-assets (ROA) over a 3-year period with the exception of Company 10 for which the calculation was an estimated return-oninvestment. The three analyzers and one defender exhibited high alignment paired with a high ROA but two prospectors had low alignment paired with a high ROA. However, six of the companies with medium to high alignment also had medium to high profitability, indicating the existence of a positive association between alignment and profitability. The analysis also showed that reactors fared least well in terms of profitability: of the three reactors with low alignment, two had low ROAs and one had medium while the reactor with medium alignment had a medium ROA. One defender with medium alignment had low profitability while another with high alignment had high profitability.

Online commitment, as measured by the four online perspectives, was compared to the extent of formal planning to determine if a single planning approach was better for firms wishing to develop a strong online presence. ## 5.4. Limitations

Despite its contributions, the study has several limitations. First, many companies, as in this study, will not exhibit all of the characteristics for a particular strategy type. The analyst must decide which characteristics are most pronounced and assign weights according to the intended purpose to derive a specific strategy type. This does not make the framework less useful but simply reflects the failure of any typology to capture the uniqueness and richness of individual organizations. Second, this study is based on observations of only 12 companies and subjective interpretation of the author. Different observers may arrive at different conclusions. Finally, the companies varied in size. It is possible that this influences the strategy type of online companies: larger companies might benefit from greater planning capacity, which might improve the quality of environmental scanning and alignment.
