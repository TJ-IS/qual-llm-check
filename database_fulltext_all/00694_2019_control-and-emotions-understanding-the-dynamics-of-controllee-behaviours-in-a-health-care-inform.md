# Control and emotions: Understanding the dynamics of controllee behaviours in a health care information systems project

David Murungi | Martin Wiener | Marco Marabelli

Information and Process Management Department, Bentley University, Waltham, Massachusetts

Correspondence

David Murungi, Information and Process Management Department, Bentley University, 175 Forest Street, Waltham, MA 02452. Email: dmurungi@bentley.edu

## Abstract

The control of information systems (IS) projects is important for organizations seeking to realize the value‐creating capabilities of information technology (IT). However, their achievement has proven complex and has triggered research on the unique challenges related to IS project control. The existing research focuses primarily on the contextual antecedents and performance effects of control‐mode choices but largely neglects the dynamics of control activities, and particularly the socio‐emotional aspects of such activities. We conducted longitudinal fieldwork to evaluate the dynamic interplay between control activities and emotions in the context of a large‐scale, health care IS project. Our case analysis provides empirical evidence for the link between controllers' control styles and controllees' emotions and subsequent behaviours. For instance, our analysis results point to “suppressed” effects of negative emotions on control effectiveness. We find that while controllees' negative emotional appraisals of control activities do not necessarily translate into immediate resistance behaviours, they can give rise to delayed resistance behaviours triggered by shifting control styles.

## KEYWORDS

control dynamics, control styles, controllee adherence/resistance, emotions, health care information systems, IS project control

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

An enduring and prevalent theme in the information systems (IS) literature is the problem of IS project control or the task of ensuring that project stakeholders act in accordance with organizational objectives (Kirsch, 1996). Academic interest in this area has focused on the complexity associated with steering the behaviours of diverse stakeholders and ensuring their cooperation to achieve organizational goals (Chua, Lim, Soh, & Sia, 2012). This complexity is compounded by the distinctive features of IS projects, such as their highly abstract work processes and outcomes which are difficult to measure; their frequently changing goals and requirements, task characteristics, team composition, stakeholder involvement, and organizational context factors; and the uncertainty and difficulty associated wit their temporal nature and typically nonroutine and nonrecurring tasks (Remus, Wiener, Saunders, Mähring, & Kofler, 2016; Wiener, Mähring, Remus, & Saunders, 2016). Relatedly, prior studies found that IS project failures are ofte not attributable to the technology but rather to the complexities associated with stakeholder interactions and rela tionships (Kirsch, 2004)

To tackle this issue, the literature on IS project control has focused on the configuration of control portfolios in terms of control modes or a determination of “what” controls need to be in place to increase project performance (Wiener et al., 2016). The emphasis on determining “what” controls to adopt has largely been at the expense of questions related to the behavioural enactment of these controls; ie, “how” to put them into practice (Wiener et al., 2016) Put differently, while existing research has helped to unearth some of the core “ingredients” of effective control, the goal of achieving such control in practice remains elusive. Indeed, the focus on control modes has led to the prevalence of a mechanistic view of control that seems to obscure the humanistic aspects of control activities. This is a significant shortcoming because not only does IS project control have important ramifications in terms of organiza tional outcomes, but it is also an inescapably human process that has significant implications for the people involved

Control as a human process is complicated by the role of emotions. Emotions are responsible for creating and sustaining work motivation (Amabile, Barsade, Mueller, & Staw, 2005; Barsade & Gibson, 2007), and IS project control essentially is an attempt to motivate individuals to behave in manners consistent with organizationa objectives (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 1996). Therefore, it is expected that emotions influence IS project control activities, and vice versa. However, because of the scant exploration of emotions in the IS literature generall (eg, De Guinea & Markus, 2009; Stein, Newell, Wagner, & Galliers, 2015; Thompson, 2012) and of its particular impact on the exercise of control (eg, Cram, Brohman, & Gallupe, 2016; Wiener et al., 2016), the nature and dynamics of this interplay are unclear. For example, some scholars have linked the notion of control to negative emotions that can stifle creativity and decrease job satisfaction (eg, Gregory & Keil, 2014). Others have suggested that by articulat ing expectations and removing ambiguity with respect to the control situation, control can increase creativity satisfaction, and motivation (Boss, Kirsch, Angermeier, Shingler, & Boss, 2009; Roberts, Hann, & Slaughter, 2006).

To address this ambiguity and to help shed light on socio‐emotional aspects of control, we set forth to explore the impact of controllers' control styles (authoritative vs enabling) on controllee emotions, as well as how these emotions affect controllee adherence and resistance behaviours relative to the enacted controls. We place specia emphasis on the controllee side of the controller‐controllee dyad because the existing literature on IS control is mainly oriented towards the position of the controller and thus largely neglects the perspective of the controllee (Cram & Wiener, 2018; Wiener et al., 2016). This oversight is likely to be particularly acute in organizations with strong hierarchical command structures as these structures are prone to the discounting of the controllee perspective. Our research, consequently, addresses this imbalance by demonstrating the importance of the controllee perspective to control‐related processes and outcomes. To accomplish this, our study aims to answer the following questions: (1) How do IS project control activities affect the emotions of controllees and (2) how do these emotions influence controllee adherence or resistance behaviours relative to these controls over the course of an IS project? By exploring the dynamic interplay between control activities and emotions, we aim to identify the socio‐emotiona antecedents to and consequences of control activities and contribute to the control literature in the specific contex of IS projects.

Our paper is structured as follows: First, we review two complementary conceptualizations of IS project contro activities and provide an overview of the extant literature on IS project control dynamics and emotions. Next, we describe our research methodology and case study context. We then present and discuss the findings of our cas analysis and conclude by discussing implications for research and practice.

## 2 | BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | IS project control

Consistent with prior IS project control studies (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 1996) and related studies in contributing disciplines (Das & Teng, 1998; Ouchi, 1979), we define control as the attempt to motivate participants to behave in a manner that is in line with organizational goals. In this behavioural view of control, a control situation involves a controller who conducts control activities to influence the behaviour of a controllee or group of controllees (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). The literature provides two complementary conceptualizations of IS project contro activities.

The first (and broadly adopted) conceptualization views control activities as control modes (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1996), which can be divided further into formal and informal modes. Formal control modes (input, behaviour, and outcome control) rely on explicit controller prescriptions (eg, specified resource allocations, procedures, or outcomes), whereas informal control modes (clan and self‐control) rely on rather implicit determinants (eg, shared norms or intrinsic work ethic) of controllee behaviour (Jaworski, 1988; Ouchi, 1979). The second comple mentary conceptualization of IS project control activities focuses on how the controller enacts controls through her or his interactions with the controllee(s), thereby emphasizing the multidimensionality of such activities (Gregory, Beck, & Keil, 2013; Wiener et al., 2016). In this vein, Wiener et al. (2016) introduced the distinction between two “extreme” control styles: authoritative and enabling.<sup>1</sup> An authoritative control style relies on bureaucratic values and represents a top‐down control approach (Adler & Borys, 1996; Gregory et al., 2013; Gregory & Keil, 2014), which is designed to ensure, and if needed, enforce controllee compliance. In contrast, an enabling control style involves frequent interaction between controller and controllee (Gregory & Keil, 2014), promotes bilateral feedback (Adler, 1999; Gregory et al., 2013), and grants the controllee some flexibility when dealing with real‐work contingencies (Adler & Borys, 1996). There are two central features that distinguish an enabling control style from an authoritative control style: repair and transparency (Wiener et al., 2016; cf Adler & Borys, 1996). The repair feature of an enabling control style anticipates “breakdowns” in control processes (eg, a flaw in the design of a report template or an unnecessary step in a standard process) and provides capabilities for fixing them by appreciating controllee feedback about, and facilitating controllee responses to, real‐work contingencies (Adler & Borys, 1996; Wiener et al., 2016). The transparency feature of an enabling control style concerns the visibility of control and other project activities (internal transparency) as well as the visibility of the broader project organization and context (global transparency) In Appendix A, we provide examples of how the different control modes can be enacted in either an authoritative control style or an enabling control style.

Prior IS project control research points to the importance of extending extant research beyond examinations of the various control modes (or the “what” of project control) to considerations of the applied control style (or the “how” of project control) (Wiener et al., 2016). Yet empirical studies on this topic remain scarce (Remus, Wiener, Mähring, Saunders, & Cram, 2015). Considering the controller's control style consequently can be expected to further our understanding of control dynamics, which remains an under‐researched topic in the IS project contro literature (Wiener et al., 2016). Also, recent empirical research comparing the effects of control modes and contro styles point to the superior explanatory power of the latter (Cram & Wiener, 2018; Remus et al., 2016). Our study, therefore, focuses on examining IS project control activities through the lens of the two contrasting control styles introduced above.

## 2.2 | IS project control dynamics and emotions

As indicated earlier, IS proiect control is essentially an. “attempt to motivate individuals to behave in a manner consis tent with organizational objectives” (Kirsch, 2004, p. 374, emphasis in original). Emotions have been credited with creating and sustaining work motivation (Barsade & Gibson, 2007). Therefore, we propose that emotions are relevant to our understanding of control activities. The significance of emotion for IS project control is supported by prior literature. For example, Cram et al. (2016) found that the structural characteristics of the IS control mechanisms (eg, the categories and types of control activities, including the distinction between authoritative and enabling control styles) encourage intrinsic and extrinsic motivations and, thus, could influence individual participation. Moreover, structura control characteristics cultivate feelings of satisfaction (or positive emotions), which in turn can reinforce related controls and produce improved outcomes. Relatedly, Adler and Borys (1996) observed two antithetic perspectives on the relationship between control activities and emotional experience. One perspective suggests that control is associated with reduced creativity, satisfaction, and motivation (Gregory & Keil, 2014; Orlikowski, 1991). The other perspective suggests that by clearly articulating expectations and removing ambiguity with respect to the contro situation, creativity, satisfaction, and motivation increase (Boss et al., 2009; Roberts et al., 2006)

However, although emotions are important to the practice of IS project control, our understanding of the dynamic interplay between control activities and emotions remains very limited. To address this shortcoming, we need to understand how control activities trigger emotional responses and how these responses contribute to subse quent changes (ie, dynamics) in control activities (Cram et al., 2016; Wiener et al., 2016). Nevertheless, developing this understanding is difficult because of the variety and confusion of the different emotion‐related concepts (Stein et al., 2015). It is complicated also by the implicit tension between the rational, goal‐oriented attributes of the notion of control and the visceral, spontaneous traits of individual emotions. To address this challenge, our study focused on emotion as a discursive construct and not as an impulsive biological reaction that is present from birth (Tomkins 1980). We adopted Ratner's (2000) perspective on the inter‐linkage between cognition and emotion. Ratner (p. 6) suggested that “emotions are the feelings that accompany thinking. They are the feeling side of thoughts; thought filled feelings; thoughtful feelings.” These “reasoned” emotions (ie, emotions that are underpinned by cognitive processes) are apropos for the study of IS project control because they are more amenable to recalibration or manip ulation based on control measures than are spontaneous (or “uncontrolled”) manifestations of emotion.

Our study also builds on Zhang's (2013) affective response model that brings together a mélange of emotion related concepts. The model accomplishes this by establishing affect as “an umbrella term for a set of more specific concepts that includes emotions, moods and feelings” (p. 247). Further, Zhang proposed that stimulus and core affec are two fundamental concepts within this vast domain. Building on Zhang's work, Stein et al. (2015) showed how different characteristics of a stimulus event (or cue) interact to produce various types of affective (or emotional) responses, which in turn have an impact on patterns of information technology (IT) use. Stein and colleagues drew also on Beaudry and Pinsonneault (2010), who categorized emotional appraisal along two dimensions: (1) threat vs opportunity (relative to perceived consequences of cue) and (2) low vs high level of autonomy (relative to ability to influence or cope with consequences). On this basis, they offer four classes of emotional response (see also Table 1): loss (threat and low autonomy; eg, anger caused by the withdrawal of project resources that are difficult to substitute); deterrence (threat and high autonomy; eg, anxiety produced by a threat to withdraw project resources if a certain deadline is not met); achievement (opportunity and low autonomy; eg, happiness produced by the announcement of additional project resources to be allocated at another party's discretion); and challenge (opportunity and high autonomy; eg, excitement produced by an offer to increase resources in the event of meeting specific performance goals).

In addition, Stein et al. (2015) introduced the notion of an emotional cue, which is the distinguishing feature of an event that induces an emotional appraisal. In this context, Stein et al. recommended longitudinal analyses of emo tional cues, emotional responses, and behavioural patterns, because this method helps reveal the conditions unde which cues change over time, and the influence of these changes on resultant emotions and behaviours. Stein et al. proposed a dynamic model of emotions, which we have adapted to fit the specific context of the control of IS projects (see Figure 1).

Specifically, in our emotion‐centred model of IS project control dynamics, a control process starts with a control ler performing a control activity, referred to as “controller activity” (see again Figure 1). As noted above, such a control activity can be classified along two main dimensions: control mode (what) and control style (how; focus of this study). From a controllee perspective, the controller activity may contain an emotional cue and, eventually, trigger an emotional response from the controllee. For example, an IS project manager may perceive a top‐level manager's request to provide daily updates on the project progress as onerous (emotional appraisal: threat and low autonomy) and, thus, may feel despondent (emotional response: loss). The controllee's emotional response can then be expected to influence her or his behaviour, referred to as “controllee behavioural response” (eg, the extent to which the controllee adheres to the behaviours prescribed by the controller). We classified this behavioural response into two forms: adherence (ie, abiding by the stipulations of a control measure) and resistance (ie, attempts to subvert or circumvent a control measure). Further, we distinguished between two forms of resistance behaviours: active resistance (ie, deliberate attempts to subvert or overturn a control measure) and passive resistance (ie, an attempt to circumvent or evade the control measure) (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Marakas & Hornik, 1996). From the control ler's perspective, the controllee behaviour may then trigger control dynamics, which result in an adaptation of the controls enacted by the controller (see “controller activity” above). We apply this model of control dynamics to our longitudinal case study to show how the relationship between controller activities and controllee emotions influ enced behavioural responses over time.

## 3 | RESEARCH SETTING AND METHOD

## 3.1 | Study context

Our study examines a large‐scale IS project aimed at implementing a health care IS (HIS) that would facilitate the sharing of health‐related data at a large private health system located in Southeastern United States known as Southeastern Health System (SHS). We chose a health care domain for our study of the impact of control activities on emotions because of the domain's unique dealings with decisions related to “life and death,” which make it apposite for the investigation of emotional constructs. In addition, health systems are characterized by a complex organizational structure, which made SHS an ideal site for our study of control dynamics (the organizational chart is reported in Appendix B). SHS is composed of four acute care hospitals that operate in separate markets and have their own distinct group of affiliated clinics. While SHS's senior management team coordinates broad strategic objectives for the health system, SHS also has a federated governance structure. The federated structure gives eac hospital significant latitude to improvise and develop strategic imperatives for their specific markets.

Historically, each hospital had its own chief information officer (CIO) and developed its own market‐specific IT strategy and operations function, However, in September 2007, to improve the alignment across SHS. the hospitals IT departments were consolidated to the system level, and the hospital CIO positions were replaced by a single system‐wide CIO. Also, IT staff were physically relocated to a central location, leaving only a skeleton staff that included a director of IT operations to handle hospital‐specific tasks. Informed by this governance structure, our study focused on two primary control relationships: (1) between SHS's senior management team (controller) and the IS project management team and/or business department stakeholders located at the constituent hospitals/affiliated clinics (controllees) and (2) between the IS project management team (controller) and business department stakeholders (controllees). The two control relationships were linked since, in the second relationship, the IS project management team acted as a representative of the corporate senior management team. To examine these control relationships, we analysed core control events and phases that took place from the inception of the project in 2007 until its final implementation in 2011.

The HIS project was initially supported by a federal grant (June 2007) provided by the National Cancer Institute to facilitate the exchange of health data between one of SHS's constituent hospitals (Lakefront Regiona Center [LRC]) and an affiliated community cancer centre (Eagle Cancer Center [ECC]). Progress on the project, how ever, was stunted by what we identified as the first major control‐related event in this endeavour that occurred in September 2007 when SHS's senior management team decided to centralize the IT function across its four facilities to achieve economies of scale. The reorganization of SHS's IT department presented a major challenge for the project as it constituted a radical departure from established work processes. After the reorganization, IT requests (that for merly involved a call to a local representative) had to be channelled through SHS's newly created centralized project management office (PMO). About 2 years later, following very little progress, the HIS project was shelved

The proiect, however, received new life in March 2010 when it was incorporated into a larger initiative that was designed to meet the requirements of the 2009 Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act that was promulgated by the US federal government. As a consequence, the HIS project expanded from a relatively routine interface project to a broad health information exchange project that was a critical part of a \$10 million initiative. The initiative was tied to about \$30 million in incentive payments administered by the Federal Department of Health & Human Services (HHS). ## 3.2 | Data collection and analysis

We adopt a qualitative, in‐depth approach (Baskerville & Myers, 2002; Myers, 1997) to examine the dynamic interplay between IS proiect control activities and emotions. Following Fasterby-Smith, Golden-Biddle, and Locke (2o08) we chose a single case study to allow close observation of the organizational actors' everyday practices on the basis of fieldwork and the collection of supplementary data. The single case design was also well suited to our investigation of the role of emotions in the enactment of IS project control because it enabled the collection of rich data and facilitated an in‐depth analysis of the data (Sarker, Xiao, Beaulieu, & Lee, 2018; Walsham, 2006). The primary data used in this study were collected through 40 interviews conducted between November 2010 and October 2011 and aimed at understanding various (control‐related) dynamics within SHS's HIS project. Interview length varied but lasted about 1 hour on average.

Most subjects were interviewed only once. However, one project manager (from SHS's centralized PMO) was interviewed a total of 14 times to obtain regular updates on the project progress during the critical vendor‐selection process (Appendix C provides additional details about the individuals we interviewed). To recruit study participants, we also adopted a snowballing sampling technique as we used our regular meetings with PMO staff/project managers to determine who the relevant stakeholders were during the various iterations of the project. Informed by past research capturing work practices through interviews (Orlikowski & Scott, 2015; Scott & Orlikowski, 2014), we conducted open‐ended and semi‐structured interviews to collect as much detail as possible through careful listening to the “stories” of project participants of their direct experience, and personal perceptions and feeling during the various phases of the HIS project (Walsham, 1993, 2006). This allowed us to gather retrospectiv (2007‐2010) and longitudinal (2010‐2011) data.

All the interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed. Note taking during the interviews was minimal to make the interview feel more conversational than interrogative. To build trust, we also assured respondents of the confi dentiality of their responses (no individually identifiable responses would be reported without the express approva of the interviewee). Reflection and memo writing were conducted shortly after the interview to consolidate the insights derived from the interview. In addition to interviews, we collected documents on system demonstrations, listened in on conference calls, and examined archival data in the form of reports, news clippings, and other filed doc uments. We exchanged follow‐up emails with some of the study participants to clarify certain details. We conclude our interview round after vendor selection was completed and when details about the project became repetitive, ie after theoretical data saturation was reached (Bowen, 2008).

It should be noted that data collection was conducted as part of a wider proiect, and therefore, some of the inter: views were performed for purposes unrelated to the present study. However, given our interest in control activities and associated emotions, and because of the richness of this dataset on the aspects of interest (controls and emo: tions), we decided to undertake further data analysis. We believe this approach (analysing data through a lens that is divorced from the initial data collection purpose) represents a strength rather than a weakness of this work, as it facilitated the capturing of spontaneous expressions of emotion. Consequently, the details that emerged from ou case analysis were in no way driven by the interviewers and emerged from loosely structured conversations wit focal actors involved in the HIS project.

Our data analysis was performed using NVivo and built on previous coding using an open‐coding approach (Miles & Huberman, 1984). This approach is consistent with prior research aimed at interpreting and making sense of how phenomena unfold in practice (Nicolini, 2011; Orlikowski, 2002). In our research, we scanned the interview transcripts and the open‐coded data (approximately 5500 initial codes) to develop preliminary observations related to significant emotional events in the HIS project, and to identify overt links between these emotional events and IS project control activities.

To capture controllee emotions in the data, we used the Stein et al. (2015) approach, which supports the iden tification of the presence of affective data by identifying emotional terms (eg, pleased, worried, and angered) in interviewees' depictions of HIS project events. As noted above, we grouped these emotional responses into the four categories proposed in Stein et al. (loss, deterrence, achievement, and challenge) and analysed the emotional cues for these responses to understand how they were associated with the controller's specific control activities. T explore what aspects of a controller activity trigger emotional responses by the controllee, we assessed whether suc responses are linked to the unique features of two contrasting control styles (ie, authoritative and enabling)

To illustrate our coding process (which followed the Stein et al., 2015, approach to capturing emotions from semi‐structured interviews), we detail main steps associated with analysing the following depiction of the HIS projec by one of the controllees (medical director at LRC) when discussing the history of the initiative:

The genesis of the problem is related to IT trying to drive change … they [SHS's senior management team] did not realize how passionate stakeholders were about their particular strategic imperatives. The centralization was a unilateral attempt to organize around IT, but it should have been handled in reverse. Centralize the strategic function and then organize IT around the strategy. I get a little bit frustrated when I hear people talk about IT can actually be a change agent. My concept is that IT cannot ever be an effective change agent … It can assist change, but it is never effective as a change agent.

In the initial coding round, this depiction was coded as follows: problem genesis, role of IT in change management, passionate stakeholders, unilateral decision, conflicting strategic imperatives, IT‐business misalignment, philosophically induced frustration, and IT as change agent vs IT as tool for change. From these codes, “passionate stakeholders” and “philosophically induced frustration” were deduced as being the core emotional terms in the discourse, and they were placed in the deterrence category of emotional response, which was prompted by the controllee's appraisal of threat (negative view of decision) and high autonomy (maintained ownership of strategic imperatives in the wake of decision). The controllee's emotional response could be linked to the controller's use of an authoritative control style (emotional cue), since the reference to a “unilateral attempt” suggests a lack of repair and transparency of the centralization decision. Also, to examine the impact of the controllee's emotional response, we looked at the links between the response of deterrence and the controllee's subseguent behaviours (see also

Figure 1). As noted above, we are particularly interested in the impact of emotions on controllee behaviours in terms of adherence, active resistance, and passive resistance. In the above quote, the medical director's behavioura response to the emotion of deterrence was classified as passive resistance because the hospital hired a project manager to manage its strategic IT‐related initiatives (not mentioned in the quote) instead of going through the centralized PMO.

## 4 | CASE FINDINGS AND ANALYSIS

Our study addresses two research questions: (1) How do IS project control activities affect the emotions of controllees and (2) how do these emotions influence controllee adherence or resistance behaviours relative to these controls over the course of an IS project? To answer these questions, we identified key control‐related activities during the HIS projec and examined the impact of these activities (with a focus on the controller's control style) on controllees' emotiona appraisals and responses, and ultimately on controllees' behavioural responses to the enacted controls (in terms of adherence, active resistance, and passive resistance). In the following, we present our findings along the six major project phases outlined in Table 2.

## 4.1 | Phase 1: Centralization of IT department

## 4.1.1 | Controller activity (control style)

Not long after the onset of the HIS proiect in June 2007, SHS's senior management team made the executive deci sion to eliminate the CIO position at each of its constituent hospitals (including LRC) and to integrate the hospitals' IT functions into a corporate function. Serving all four SHS hospitals, the centralized IT department was managed by a single CIO and responsible for coordinating all IS projects across the health system. The senior management team's (controller) decision to centralize the IT function was characterized by an authoritative control style, as this was a uni lateral decision by the executive team and did not involve any consultation with the business departments or IT staf at the various hospitals (controllees). The vice president (VP) of marketing at LRC explained

I think [SHS's senior management team] is still paying a price for a very poorly executed centralization. I think they thought they were doing it really well, but that particular model that they used, which was kind of just napalmed across the organization, created a lot of hard, hard feelings, a lot of distrust …

The evidence of an authoritative control style is found in this controllee's observation that the decision was “napalmed across the organization” (lack of repair and transparency). This was corroborated by other controllees making similar observations related to the authoritative way this control activity was enacted. For example, the operations coordinator at LRC's managed care department described the decision to centralize the IT function in terms o a sudden (lack of transparency) and non‐negotiable edict (lack of repair) when he said:

It was a drastic change that dramatically altered our existing relationships with the IT department. There was no runway … It was just boom! Management issued the decree and IT was gone to corporate office.

## 4.1.2 | Controllee emotional appraisal and response

Controllees who manifested an emotional response to the centralization decision universally appraised this decision as threatening and exhibited either the emotional response of loss or deterrence. Those who exhibited loss (IT depart ment members) felt like they had no choice but to adhere with the control measure (ie, low autonomy). A senior IT architect at SHS explained

[The centralization of the IT function] produced a lot of consternation in our team. There was an edict given, but we did not know exactly what it entailed and there was no one to provide us with guidance. The decision involved a new reporting structure … a new accountability structure. The CIO was gone so we were just in limbo waiting to be told where to go and what to do.

On the other hand, those who demonstrated deterrence as their emotional response (LRC business stakeholders) manifested a sense of self‐sufficiency (ie, high autonomy) that enabled them to devise a means of mitigating against the perceived adverse impacts of the decision. This emerges from the words of the administrator of the cance department at LRC:

With the loss of our dedicated in‐house IT department, the National Cancer Institute project stalled. Instead of navigating the new bureaucracy, I used grant funds to hire a project manager that would be dedicated to this project. The project manager performed the tasks that would have been performed by our in‐house IT department. As you would imagine, with a change of this size, there would be a lot of upheaval … and it wasn't an opportune time … in the middle of this grant … to have a change of this magnitude. It was a difficult time for us in this department, but we adapted.

## 4.1.3 | Controllee behavioural response

Controllees who expressed deterrence demonstrated passive resistance, while those who experienced loss manifested grudging adherence to the control measure. An example of passive resistance was found in the behaviour exhibited by the administrator of LRC's cancer department. He circumvented the new structures by using National Cancer Institute grant funds to hire a dedicated project manager to manage the HIS project instead of going through the cen tralized PMO. The marketing department developed a similar workaround by developing a separate consulting arrangement with an external vendor that—while couched as a marketing endeavour—also included the development of a long‐term IS (and e‐health) strategy for the department.

In contrast, controllees who experienced loss reluctantly complied with the new structures (adherence). As one controllee—an IS system administrator who had fond reminiscence of the old structures—told us:

… there was a lot less bureaucracy, a lot less red tape. Things got done a lot faster. There was a lot less accountability without project management overseeing everything. A lot more meetings now than there used to be …

I am a nuts and bolts guy. I am not a decision maker so to speak. I may not like the decision, but hey, it is what it is. Would I have done it differently, like really understand how this would impact our existing processes and get some input from the technical team … But again, that's not my place.

## 4.2 | Phase 2: Pre–HITECH Act management of HIS project

## 4.2.1 | Controller activity (control style)

As indicated previously, in the wake of the decision to centralize the IT function, the administrator of LRC's cancer department used funds from the National Cancer Institute grant to hire a dedicated project manager to handle the execution of the HIS project instead of manoeuvring the new bureaucracy. Upon being hired, the LRC project man ager (controller) performed the tasks of creating a project steering committee, developing a project charter, and scheduling and conducting meetings with key project stakeholders, including LRC and ECC business and clinica employees (controllees).

The LRC project manager's control style was enabling in nature. In particular, as described in the following quote her control style involved an interactive planning process and a collaborative approach to attaining project objectives (repair). Also, she designed project meetings to get input from the users—and not to tell them what to do—and wa careful to ensure that individual responsibilities were clarified and well defined (transparency):

So, my job was to facilitate the project. Both parties had expressed a desire to make this happen and I was there to help them achieve their goals. Make sure that the project was well defined, clarifying who would do what and making sure the resources were available to get the job done … So, what I was trying to do … is put our docs and our nursing staff, our administrative staff into one room so that they could give us thei input. Just put them into one room so that we could say, “Look at this program and tell us what you think.”

## 4.2.2 | Controllee emotional appraisal and response

Interestingly, controllees manifested negative emotions in response to the LRC project manager's enabling approach to control the HIS project. The project manager (controller) observed

I experienced a lot of pushback from the clinicians. So, there was buy‐in at the overall concept level, but it's a whole new ball game when you walk into a clinic and say, “How can we change your practice? The pushback was related to this fear of novelty … The clinicians were also bombarded with all the othe initiatives, plus their clinic hours … they were kind of spinning their wheels. So, when I'd come around to get their feedback they'd kind of say, ‘let us push that off a bit.’”

Controllees, on the other hand, told us that they viewed the controller's enabling control style as an improper shift of responsibility from controller to controllees, as it placed additional demands on their already heavy workload When reflecting on LRC project manager's activities during this period, ECC's medical director told us:

So, they put on this big thing about how they were going to integrate data across these different databases, but they did not seem to have resolved all the issues related to restricting levels of access to different data. It did not look like a fully baked solution and I do not think it was fair to infringe on ou schedules to think through stuff they could have done on their own. I get a little impatient with these brainstorming meetings where the other party has not done their homework and is trying to manage on the fly.

Controllees thus seemed to express a desire to be provided with more direction and instructions as opposed t participating in a collaborative planning process. In terms of the controllees' emotional appraisal, the LRC project manager's control style was appraised in terms of threat (described as fear of novelty and an infringement of schedules), together with high autonomy (as the clinicians had broad latitude regarding the conduct of the project and possessed uncontested expertise and influence in the design of clinical workflows). These emotional appraisal (threat and high autonomy) consequently produced the emotional response of deterrence, which is captured by th fear of novelty and the pushback that was directed to the LRC project manager

Controllee behavioural response to the emotional response of deterrence was consistent with that of active resis tance to the control measures put in place by the LRC project manager. This active resistance was manifested in controllees not showing up for meetings, not providing necessary information, and generally lacking in cooperation as indicated by the project manager:

So yes, theoretically they were supportive of the endeavor, but when it came to the actual change process it's very much pushed back … So, we would have low attendance at meetings, delays in getting necessary information. Just lip‐service, but no real cooperation. It really hindered what we could get done with this project.

## 4.3 | Phase 3: Adoption of HIS project by SHS's centralized PMO

## 4.3.1 | Controller activity (control style)

Primarily because of the active resistance of controllees, the early attempt to establish a HIS was abandoned after it failed to make significant progress on its core objectives, other than the development of a shared imaging archive that could be used in both LRC and ECC settings. However, with the introduction of the 2009 HITECH Act, the HIS project was revived as it facilitated the exchange of health information between separate health care organizations, which was a key requirement of this Act. Consequently, the centre of control for the project move from LRC and its dedicated project manager to SHS's senior management team (controller) who oversaw the activities of SHS's centralized PMO (controllee) who was tasked with managing all HITECH‐related projects, including the HIS project.

Given its top‐down approach and bureaucratic handling of the HIS project, the senior management team's control style can be characterized as an authoritative one. This is reflected in the following comments by the senior project manager (controllee) who was assigned to the HIS project by the PMO:

I provide updates to the committee every month. They are not interested in the details of the project, they just care to make sure that we are on track with meeting the goals set forth in the initial charter. There was no slack in timelines and the deliverables … Other than providing oversight and issues directives they were pretty hands off.

## 4.3.2 | Controllee emotional appraisal and response

The senior project manager's emotional response to this authoritative control style was one of deterrence. The HIS project was viewed predominantly in terms of the ramifications of failure or threat (see quote below). However, the senior project manager still had high autonomy as she was given significant latitude in terms of how to respond to this threat and she expressed no reservations about her ability to meet these demands

And if you do that and only if you do that does the organization reap the benefits. All the projects were on this tight aggressive timeline. Very stressful for one individual to manage

Controllee behavioural response to the emotion of deterrence was one of adherence, with the senior project man ager focusing her attention on accomplishing the tasks that she was directed to perform.

## 4.4 | Phase 4: Early PMO management of revived HIS project

## 4.4.1 | Controller activity (control style)

Given the charge from SHS's senior management team regarding the performance of the HIS project to meet the provisions of the HITECH Act, SHS's PMO assumed control over the project and developed a new charter for it. The PMO also assumed responsibility for coordinating all interactions with project stakeholders from LRC and ECC. The control style adopted by the senior project manager (assigned by the PMO) was an authoritative one, as it did not involve much consultation with the business stakeholders (controllees). Reflecting on the lack of transparency that characterized the senior project manager's control style, the medical director at ECC observed:

There was broad agreement at the level of the senior management of the two organizations that this was a win‐win for both organizations, but in terms of how this is going to be accomplished we are pretty much in the dark … We are aware of the big picture goals, but do not have all the details. At this point, we are operating on faith.

Similarly, the controller's use of an authoritative control style did not afford controllees with much influence over the conduct of the HIS project. The IT director at ECC commented:

We support the initiative as it could benefit both organizations in the long‐run, but we do not own the process. I have no clout in the decision‐making process. It's the health system running the show … They are calling the shots.

## 4.4.2 | Controllee emotional appraisal and response

Interestingly, the emotional response to the senior project manager's authoritative control style was uniforml positive as controllees seemed comfortable with PMO ownership of the HIS project. This also emerges from an observation made by the ECC's IT director:

Well, if you talk to the doctors, they do not care how the stuff gets there, they just want it there, they just need it there. You get all that stuff in front of the doctor, the doctor's happy, he does not care how it got there. They are not involved in the set up [planning] and they are quite happy to be uninvolved.

The doctors positively appraised the project in terms of an opportunity, even though they were faced with a reduced ability to influence its outcomes (low autonomy). Their emotional response consequently could be characterized as one of achievement. This is supported by other comments from clinicians who manifested their overal approval of the project and general contentment with the control approach used by the senior project manager— though with a little wariness due to a lack of awareness of the project details.

## 4.4.3 | Controllee behavioural response

In response to the emotion of achievement, controllees uniformly exhibited adherence to the controls enacted by the controller and did not demonstrate any resistance behaviours.

## 4.5 | Phase 5: Early HIS vendor‐selection process

## 4.5.1 | Controller activity (control style)

Because of the expanded scope of the HIS project and its compressed time frame for implementation, SHS's PMO decided to outsource the project to an external vendor. The PMO also assigned a new project manager to the HIS project, which allowed the previous manager to focus on other aspects of the larger HITECH Act initiative. A key task during this project stage was selecting an appropriate vendor firm. Here, the PMO and the new project manager (con trollers) relied again on an authoritative control style, as they did not involve business stakeholders (controllees) in the vendor‐selection process. The VP of clinical IS commented on the isolated decision‐making process:

When we went to purchase the HIS, it was envisioned as a technical tool that would work in the background and not impact the user interface. It would simply provide an additional feature that the physicians could opt in. We did not see the need for broader stakeholder engagement.

Similarly, commenting on the new project manager's control style, a manager at LRC's marketing department confirmed the lack of stakeholder involvement (ie, lack of repair and transparency), when he told us that:

Ideally, they would work with the business owner and seek their vision … Ask what's on your menu of things that you say we must accomplish and then how do we help, how do we use technology to help you accomplish those goals. In this instance, they were imposing a solution on the business without seeking our input.

## 4.5.2 | Controllee emotional appraisal and response

Controllees' emotional appraisal and subsequent emotional response to the authoritative control style used in the early vendor‐selection process was mixed. Some controllees—the ECC clinicians in particular—continued their satisfaction with this control approach, even though it reduced their autonomy, or level of influence over the HIS project. More specifically, since at this point the project did not adversely affect existing workflows, the controllees wer content with viewing the vendor selection as a “technical” concern that did not require their input. The controllee consequently manifested the positive emotional response of achievement (opportunity and low autonomy).

In contrast, users in LRC's marketing department became quite alarmed when SHS's PMO announced its choic of vendor because this decision threatened an existing relationship that the department had established with an external vendor (as a workaround to the IT centralization decision that was made about 3 y ago). This threat was exacerbated by the PMO's use of an authoritative control style to enforce its vendor decision. Ultimately, thi resulted in the emotional response of deterrence (threat and high autonomy). This happened because the perceived synergies between the external vendor's strengths and LRC's local strategic imperatives helped buffer the marketing department against encroachments on the existing vendor relationship. Reflecting on the vendor‐selection decision the VP of marketing at LRC told us:

I do not think the IT department really understands our needs. I am willing for them to convince me that they do understand what we need. I guess that was part of my frustration, I really was open to hearing what they had to say and understand their choice. I was open to their explanation if they would speak to me as an equal.

Similarly, when reflecting on the tensions that had emerged in the organization during this project stage, the chief medical officer at LRC said:

So, what's happening now is that the marketing people and other folks have very strong feelings about the decision that has been made and how it will impact existing relationships that they have established with other vendors.

## 4.5.3 | Controllee behavioural response

Controllees who had positive emotional responses to the project manager's authoritative control style (ie, experienced the emotional response of achievement) adhered to the control measures and did not manifest any resistanc behaviours. In contrast, controllees who experienced the emotion of deterrence in response to the project manager's control style largely acquiesced to the vendor decision but also communicated their frustration to the PMO (passive resistance). For instance, the VP of marketing at LRC told us that:

I came to inquire about their vendor choice and they spoke to me like I was from a third world country …

And if you are from a third world country I am really sorry, but they talked to me like I was an idiot. I mean

I do have a decent relationship with the folk over there, but that was quite an unpleasant experience.

## 4.6 | Phase 6: Late HIS vendor‐selection process

## 4.6.1 | Controller activity (control style)

In the wake of the frustration with the vendor choice voiced by a key project stakeholder group (LRC's marketing department), the PMO and the designated project manager (controllers) switched to an enabling control style that would encourage stakeholder feedback. Relatedly, although the PMO had already announced the vendor of choice, they decided to hold off on signing the contract with this vendor until they had resolved the concerns raised by ke project stakeholders. In other words, the PMO sought to get buy‐in from these stakeholders in order to ease the project implementation process. Further, the PMO also embarked on a campaign to help educate controllees on the criteria used for selecting the vendor. The SHS's CIO pointed out:

Now my approach, which might not be the best way, is … I try to let people get there. And some of it is just by me asking questions. That's what I call my stupid question. I already know the answer, but I ask a trai of questions to help them get educated. You can make them think a different way. In my style of doing it, I know it's what I want to do, but I want to get a consensus if I can and hopefully they get there without me saying this is what we are going to do.

## 4.6.2 | Controllee emotional appraisal and response

There were no observed changes in the controllees' emotional appraisal and subsequent response to the shift from an authoritative to a more enabling control style, Controllees who were satisfied with the previous control style hac no evident changes in their emotional appraisals and response (achievement). This happened because the enabling style was particularly designed to address those who had expressed frustration with the unilateral approach to th vendor‐selection process (ie, the authoritative control style). Put differently, controllees who were comfortable wit this process were not impacted by the change in control style.

Interestingly, however, controllees who had experienced deterrence in the wake of the announcement of the vendor of choice also showed no change in their emotional appraisals and response and continued to manifest deterrence even after the controller had shifted to an enabling control style. This occurred because the controllee continued to interpret the controller's actions in terms of what they had done in the past and not solely on thei sudden openness to feedback. The VP of marketing commented:

The good news is that we are talking, at least I am talking with them and they are talking with me, but this has not resolved the issue. I think they are holding to a very narrow interpretation of this system that fits them, but does not fit me. I do not think they understand our needs

## 4.6.3 | Controllee behavioural response

Again, there was no change in the behavioural response of controllees who continued to manifest positive emotiona responses in the wake of project management's change in control style (ie, continued adherence to control measures). In contrast, controllees who maintained the emotional response of deterrence began to manifest active resistance behaviours that were geared to subvert the control measure. An example of this active resistance was a threat to reject (or not use) the PMO‐imposed solution based on its purported inadequacy in addressing the intricacies of LRC's marketing department. When, however, the project manager sought to determine the technical specifics of these inadequacies, it became apparent that the issue was not technical but the result of marketing's general distrust of the IT department. An enabling style therefore did not resolve the issue and may have even exacerbated the problem. For instance, the marketing VP indicated that the project manager made her feel like an idiot when he used technical jargon to try to explain the project (see above). This is also evident in the following quote from the PMO manager who was handling the HIS project:

One thing that I am on guard for are the different agenda's surrounding this project. I have tried to be straightforward in explaining the technical rationale for the decision because at the end of day we are not tied in to a particular vendor. I consider myself a rational person, if the vendor that marketing is seeking is the better solution we are fine with that. I can be persuaded, but it is beginning to look like some of the parties to this are out to settle some scores or gain some political leverage or whatever …

The conflict was finally resolved by what was perceived to be a sub‐optimal solution, at least from the perspective of SHS's senior management team: The marketing department kept its existing relationship with the other vendor on an interim basis, as it was discovered that this would not adversely impact SHS's ability to meet th requirement of the HITECH Act. Otherwise, this would have jeopardized the millions of dollars in incentive payments that were at stake depending on the successful implementation of the HIS project.

## 5 | CASE SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION

Table 3 summarizes the case findings along the six phases of the HIS project. On this basis, in the following, we high light and discuss key cross‐phase patterns that emerged from the case analysis

As Table 3 indicates, in the HIS project, controllee adherence to enacted controls was associated with the control ler's use of both authoritative (phases 1, 3, 4, and 5) and enabling (phase 6) control styles. An authoritative contro style, however, produced “grudging” adherence when associated with the emotional response of loss (as opposed to deterrence or achievement). In the single instance of adherence in response to an enabling control style, thi adherence was unqualified and associated with the emotional response of achievement.

Passive resistance, on the other hand, was only associated with an authoritative control style and only occurred when this control style evoked the emotional response of deterrence in controllees. In other words, passive resistance was the behavioural response of those who viewed the controls enacted in an authoritative control style to be threatening but who nevertheless maintained high levels of autonomy relative to their perceived abilities to man age the consequences of the enacted controls. For example, this autonomy was manifested in the development of workarounds that mitigated the consequences of the centralization of the IT department (phase 1), while in the event of the early vendor‐selection process (phase 5), this was manifested in vocal complaining that got the attention of senior management.

Similarly, active resistance was only associated with an enabling control style and only occurred when this contro style evoked the emotional response of deterrence in controllees. For instance, deterrence resulted in passive resis tance when it was evoked by an authoritative control style during the early HIS vendor‐selection process (phase 5) Interestingly, this behavioural response changed to active resistance when the control style shifted to an enabling one during the late vendor‐selection process (phase 6).

In answer to our research questions, we therefore found evidence that controllee resistance or adherence behav iours were influenced by the control style adopted by the controller. However, we also found that the two control styles did not, necessarily result, in different emotional responses by controllees. Eor example authoritative and enabling control styles produced instances of deterrence and achievement throughout the course of the HIS project The only clear difference in controllee emotional responses to the controller's control style was the manifestation of loss, which was exclusively observed in response to an authoritative control style. This also suggests that both an authoritative control style and an enabling control style could be viewed as threats or opportunities depending o the content and context of the enacted control.

For example, while it is counter‐intuitive to view an enabling style as threatening (given its attributes of repair and transparency), we found that, in the context of the pre–HITECH management of the HIS project (phase 2), this control style was considered threatening by controllees who viewed the increased level of controllee‐controller interactions as unnecessary for the tasks at hand and as a sign of controller incompetency. Interestingly, at a later phase o the project (phase 6), we found that the same controllees were content with an enabling control style, as in this instance (vendor‐selection process) the control content did not interfere with existing workflows. Similarly, while an authoritative control style could reasonably be associated with the notion of threat, we found that, in certain con: texts, controllees appraised the controller's use of this control style as an opportunity. For example, in phases 4 and 5 of the HIS project, at least some controllees viewed the use of an authoritative control style favourably as a sign of controller expertise or command of the project.

## 6 | CONTRIBUTIONS AND IMPLICATIONS

With this study, we contribute to the literature on IS project control by shedding light on the interplay between control activities and the emotional impulses that influence controllees' behavioural responses to these activities We accomplish this by deriving an emotion‐centred model of IS project control dynamics from prior literature and applying this model to longitudinal data on a large‐scale HIS implementation project. In particular, extant IS project control research has placed great emphasis on control effects at the project level of analysis and provided relatively little research on the socio‐emotional consequences of control at the individual level (eg, Remus et al., 2016; Wiener et al., 2016). Our study, on the other hand, focuses on the role of control‐induced emotions and even goes one ste further by linking controllee emotions to their subsequent adherence or resistance behaviours.

A related contribution of our study concerns the role of controllee emotions in fuelling control dynamics (ie, changes in control activities) over the lifespan of an IS project, which continues to be an under‐researched area (Wiener et al., 2016). More specifically, by adopting a dynamic perspective on the relationship between controllers control activities and controllees' emotional and behavioural responses, the study offers novel insights into the lim ited theorization of IS project control dynamics. In this regard, existing IS project control research has largely relied on three theorizing approaches for studying control dynamics, namely, phase‐based, learning‐based, and encounter based approaches (Wiener et al., 2016). Focusing on the project level of analysis, the phase‐based approach and the encounter‐based approach view changes in IS project control activities as being linked to the specific requirements of different project phases (eg, Kirsch, 2004) and the occurrence of critical project events (eg, Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003), respectively. The learning‐based approach links control dynamics to adaptive learning processes (eg, Mähring, 2002) and thus views controllers as the motors of change (Wiener et al., 2016). In contrast, our study acknowledges the important role of controllees, and their emotions, in triggering IS project control dynamics. Our study thus contributes to the existing body of knowledge by offering the perspective of emotional theory, which links the dynamics of control to controllee emotional appraisals and their associated emotional and behavioural responses.

Another study contribution rests on exposing the link between control‐style choices and control effectiveness Unlike prior research, which has typically been unequivocal in highlighting the benefits of enabling approaches to control (eg, Adler & Borys, 1996: Chapman & Kihn, 2009: Cram & Wiener, 2018: Remus et al., 2016), the results of our study add nuance to this view by highlighting instances of negative emotional response to this control style While, for example, Cram and Wiener (2018) found that controls enacted in a bilateral (ie, enabling) control style were perceived to be more legitimate by controllees than controls enacted in a unilateral (ie, authoritative) style, our study shows that these perceptions of legitimacy can be distorted by long‐held emotional grievances. Adding to this, our study suggests that if controllees are already “unhappy,” a shift to an enabling control style may make things worse, thereby pointing to a potential drawback of this control style.

Further, the findings of our study also suggest broader implications for theory and practice. In particular, our find ings imply that being aware of the “power of emotions” (Newell & Marabelli, 2016)—ie. the extent to which controllee emotions can positively or negatively influence the effectiveness and trajectory of control activities—is crucial for managers (controllers). In this context, prior studies of nonprescriptive types of control (Ouchi, 1979; Ouchi & Maguire, 1975) suggest that organizational culture, engagement, and leadership can lead to more effective clan control in which supervision is replaced by trust and empowerment. This leads to a relevant practical implication: While culture and leadership are widely applied in modern organization dynamics (eg, Bertels, Howard‐Grenville, & Pek, 2016; Groysberg, Lee, Price, & Cheng, 2018; Joshi, Lazarova, & Liao, 2009), managers might want to consider embracing a control style that accounts for the relevance of emotions in the workplace, which would allow these emotions to be leveraged. In doing so, however, controllers also need to be mindful of controllees' perception o authenticity (Ibarra, 2015), which, if lacking, can be expected to undermine control effectiveness

Relatedly, Stein, Newell, Wagner, and Galliers (2014) suggested that emotions are produced relationally (Thrift, 2008); that is, relations among actors (eg, controllers and controllers) produce not only emergent task‐relate outcomes but also human emotions, which can result in a felt quality or mood that characterizes our “being‐in the‐world” (Ciborra & Willcocks, 2006). Thus, emotions are experienced not simply as the follow‐up to an action that is produced by an individual's interpretation of a specific control‐related situation but rather as a psychosocial phe nomenon that emerges from collective action. In this regard, Dreyfus (1991) argued that mood is always present, shaping and being shaped by the individual's collective actions, and that this mood can generate collective energ (or its opposite—apathy). In practical settings, this can represent a powerful asset, which can be used by controllers (and paradoxically, also by controllees) to shape the ongoing dynamics of complex control systems

## 7 | LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

While we have highlighted the significance of our study for the development of theory and practice of IS project control, we would also like to note that the study contributions and implications should be interpreted with some limitations in mind. One such limitation stems from our approach to study emotions retrospectively on the basis of interviewee recollections of project events/phases rather than observation of their emotional appraisals as they occurred. We acknowledge that individual recollections of historical emotional experiences may not be identical to the lived experience and be skewed by subsequent emotional events (eg, an experience that seemed emotionally painful might be perceived as less so if succeeded by another painful experience). However, this problem is mitigated by the fact that these reflections—although they may not be accurate representations of historical reality—comprised an important part of the respondents' sensemaking and helped to shape their ongoing behavioural responses to IS project control activities. Further, in line with Giddens and Pierson (1998), we believe that people are knowledgeable and reflexive, and often know more about what they do than researchers might give them credit for (Orlikowski 2002). Also, our findings are the result of multiple accounts—including direct observations, retrospective data (eg, internal documents), and informal conversations with participants—which enriched the more formal interview data on long‐term exposure to unfolding events in the case organization (SHS).

Notwithstanding the above attempts to mitigate against the issue of studying emotions from retrospective interviews, we acknowledge that, to capture ongoing and evolving practices, it is important to observe rather than enquire about what the individual did (Bourdieu & Nice, 1977). Future studies based on a more deliberate design to capture real‐time expressions of emotion might yield additional insights by facilitating an exploration of broader aspects of the emotion construct that include latent or spontaneous expressions. We also acknowledge that our analysis of the relationship between controllees' emotional responses and their behavioural responses is based on a relatively small set of control activities and focused on the controllee side of the controller‐controllee dyad, which may con strain attempts to extrapolate from this study to other IS project control settings.

A future study therefore could be designed to include an evaluation of controller emotions with the goal of test ing our findings against a broader array of control activities. This would enable an evaluation of any linkages between controllee adherence/resistance behaviours and subsequent controller emotional appraisals and their corollary con trol responses. Further, our study could be extended to an examination of these constructs within an agile setting, which is likely to yield rich insights due to the frequency of controller‐controllee interactions (eg, sprint meetings) (cf Tripp, Riemenschneider, & Thatcher, 2016). An application of this research within an agile setting may also provide a valuable contrast to the hierarchical model of control that was prevalent in our study, and it would facilitate an examination of whether the relationships observed in this study can be extended to other contexts.

## 8 | CONCLUSION

The findings from our longitudinal case study offer first empirical evidence of the interplay between controller activities, controllee emotions, and controllee adherence or resistance behaviours. A key finding of our study concerns the persistence of controllees' emotional appraisals, which can lead to significant behavioural consequences in the long run, particularly if there is a shift in the controller's control style. Thus, our study contributes to, and extends, the literature on IS project control by adding new insights derived from a dynamic view of how control activities unfolded over the course of a large‐scale IS project and depicting the important role that controllee emotions played in this context. Our study also contributes to the literature by acknowledging the key role of emotional cues and responses for explaining the effectiveness of control activities. In conclusion, we hope that our study will draw increased research attention to the role of controllees and their emotions in IS projects, as well as inspire innovative research designs on how controllee emotions influence, and are influenced by, control activities in a variety of project settings and control relationships.
