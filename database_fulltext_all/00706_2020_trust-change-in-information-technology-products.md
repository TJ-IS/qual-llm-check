# Trust Change in Information Technology Products

D. Harrison McKnight <sup>a</sup>, Peng Liu <sup>b</sup>, and Brian T. Pentland <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Accounting and Information Systems, Eli Broad College of Business, Michigan State University, East Lansing, Michigan, USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems and Decision Sciences, College of Business and Economics, California State University, Fullerton, Fullerton, California, USA

## ABSTRACT

We examine why trust change occurs when potential users first encounter news about a specific technology. We propose personal perceptions and three cognitive outcomes—attention, sensemaking, and threshold—afect trust change in educated young adults surveyed regarding a technology product. We find the outcomes of attention, sensemaking, and threshold positively afect trust change, while most hypothesized personal perceptions of the technology (e.g., reputation) do not predict trust change. For research, this implies scholars should focus more on cognitive outcomes of mental news brief processing than on technology perceptions. Our results also imply that research should examine other key dependent variables the way we studied trust change (e.g., “intention-to-use change”—to produce a more dynamic picture of how people adopt a technology). For practice, our data imply that tech companies can counter initial bad news about a technology by quickly providing strong positive news items to repair trust in that technology.

## KEYWORDS

Trust change; trust in technology; initial trust; trust repair; cognitive attention; sensemaking; threshold; event; fragile; robust

## Introduction

When Samsung introduced its new Galaxy Note 7 smartphone, several consumers reported their new phones exploded or caught fire. This news spread quickly via social media and news media. Samsung quickly provided a replacement unit, but these also malfunctioned [10]. This news spread fast, too. A month later (mid-September), a survey found 34 percent of Note 7 users said they would no longer buy a Samsung smartphone [16]. In mid-October, the Federal Aviation Administration banned the Note 7 device from all flights [22]. Soon after, Samsung stopped manufacturing the unit. By then, Samsung’s stock had decreased 8 percent in value since August, or \$17 billion [10]. Throughout, issues of trust took center stage in the news. Soon Samsung repaired consumer trust, but during this period, trust took a nosedive.

This incident raises questions about how robust or stable technology trust remains in the face of negative or positive news events and how it changes over short timeframes. So we focus this study on:

1. How does trust in a specific technology change as news events impact potential users’ views (i.e., how fragile is it)? This question is important because if news events are influential in the beginning of a product life cycle, as in the Galaxy Note 7 case, then companies need to take proactive steps to repair the decreased trust that news events can cause. If trust in a specific technology is not fragile at first, then we need no intervention. Trust fragility in the light of early product news briefs is not yet well understood.

2. Which mental mechanisms influence peoples’ technology trust change as they process news events? This question is a key factor, because developing interventions to address news briefs will work better if one knows specifically what types of mental processes have the most efect on trust change. In this paper, we propose that attention, sensemaking, and threshold outcomes all afect trust change. But we hope to uncover which of these three afect trust more than the others, which should help us recommend which types of interventions are likely to be most efective.

3. Which situational personal perception factors afect trust change as potential users encounter news about the technology? It is possible that attention, sensemaking, and threshold are not the most influential reasons trust changes. For example, trust may change due to initial personal perceptions, such as perceived risk level of the technology. If risk is a factor, then one would need to address that risk. If risk is a stronger factor than the three mental processes, one would need to concentrate more on risk interventions than interventions that address the efects of the mental processes.

## “Initial” Defined

This paper is about changes in initial trust in a technology. “Initial technology trust” means trust during the period before one has significant personal experience with the technology [68]. Initial trust is like what influential trust research calls “swift trust” [37, 69]. Even before prospective Samsung phone users have much experience using a Samsung product, they already have positive or negative opinions about it. These opinions would be tentative, given they have just started using the product, and would be based on initial experience and what they have heard others say about the product—from friends, social media, or news media [68].

## Why Initial Technology Trust is Often Unstable, and Thus Trust Change is Vital to Study

Whatever news that people encounter about a product could change their trust in it. Initial opinions about a technology may form quickly—within seconds [94]. But initial opinions are especially likely to be fragile [41], as opposed to later, when they become more robust [36]. Experience becomes more predictive than initial impressions [44, 103], and experiential perceptions are harder to change. But trust in the initial period is flexible, so it is relatively likely to change when one encounters new stimuli. This makes the initial trust period crucial to study. Furthermore, initial trust change is important because it can influence how a person trusts later [48]. That is, initial technology trust may impact subsequent technology trust (and usage) [17]. And since trust can change rapidly, as with the Galaxy Note 7, it is vital to study trust change in a technology.

## Import of Studying Initial Trust in a Specific Technology

Trust in a specific technology is diferent from trust in a person. This paper focuses on a person’s initial trust in a specific technology [64]. By studying trust in a specific technology, we contribute to information systems (IS) research that focuses on the IT artifact [98]. In a technology-rich age, trust in a specific technology is becoming more important than ever. We trust technologies in many situations involving risk. We trust (or don’t trust) self-driving vehicles, advice given by recommendation technologies, and robot-assisted surgery. Such trust is vital to promoting technology usage. While trust has been studied thoroughly in electronic-commerce [30, 65] and virtual teams [37, 42, 11], less research examines trust in specific technologies (exceptions include [50, 99, 64]).<sup>2</sup>

## Trust Change as a Dependent Variable

This paper addresses a research need by studying the dependent variable of initial trust change instead of trust. Many IS papers study the causes of trust (e.g., [2, 30]) or the impacts of trust (e.g., [76]), and many comment on the fragility of trust (e.g., [48, 104]); but papers do not examine trust change as a distinct construct. Yet this is vital to study because as soon as trust forms it often changes. Initial trust can be robust (i.e., change slowly—[69]) or can be fragile (i.e., change quickly—[57, 68]). Some researchers have said how trust forms and changes is a “critical issue” [62, p. 730; 30, 42, 29, 11].

Yet neither trust change itself nor the mental mechanisms<sup>3</sup> leading to trust change have been adequately examined. Trust formation and change is crucial to vendors like Samsung who encounter IT product or service failures and negative news reports. It is crucial to companies like Google, whose new products like Google Glass and Waymo autos are covered widely by the press during beta testing, with each news article constituting an event that could help or hurt its future adoption.

## Adding Event-Related Causality

Trust-related events (e.g., news items) are crucial to examine because events can help change trust levels directly. Our study is the first to examine how the outcomes of three mental mechanisms—attention, sensemaking, and threshold—operate simultaneously to change initial trust as news events are encountered. Using Hierarchical Linear Modeling (HLM) methods, we examine both person-level factors and event-level factors (see Figure 1). The paper proceeds with theory development. We then describe the methods, report the results, and draw implications for future research.

## Theory Development

After defining trust and trust change, we review the literature that is most closely related to our main trust change predictors and to trust fragility. We focus on initial trust, since that is the phenomenon we studied. Longer term stage theories of trust (e.g. [57]) are briefly discussed in Online Supplemental Appendix A.

## Trust Concept Definitions

By trust, we mean that for whatever one needs from a technology, one has a willingness to depend on or be vulnerable to that technology (compare [62]). This definition is like the trusting intention definitions of [4] and [65], but for technology-related trust. We use trusting intention instead of trusting beliefs (ability/benevolence/integrity—e.g., [30]) for two reasons. First, willingness to depend is more action-oriented than trusting beliefs, since one who has willingness to depend is prepared to become vulnerable to the trustee instead of just believing in trustee attributes. Second, trusting intention predicts risk-laden constructs like website use intention and willingness to share information better than do trusting beliefs, per [4] and [66, 67], making it valuable when trust is most needed. In fact, trusting intention mediates most efects of trusting beliefs on important organizational outcomes like risk taking [61, 62].

By trust change, we mean the extent to which one’s willingness to depend on the technology increases or decreases over a given timeframe. We later expand this definition. Trust change is vital to IS research because technology trust relationships do change, and trust changes will afect vital trust outcomes [26]. Trust change per se has not previously been treated as a dependent variable. Yet initial trust fragility makes this vital. Therefore, our study represents a first attempt at understanding trust change.

## Literature on Trust Development

## Specific Mental Trust Development Mechanisms

Several IS scholars have theorized specific trust-related mechanisms. Some say trust develops through the mental mechanism of calculating penalties versus rewards; hence, it is termed calculus-based or deterrence-based trust [38, 30]. Jarvenpaa and Leidner [37] discuss how an in-group categorization mechanism can take place swiftly in virtual work teams. Komiak et al. [51] and Komiak and Benbasat [50] list a number of literature-based trust processes: attribution, bonding, calculative, expectation evaluation, illusions of control, prediction, and reputation categorization. The aforementioned research taught us that there are many mental and social mechanisms that may influence trust change. We focus on three mental mechanisms: Attention, Sensemaking, and Threshold.

## Attention

Attention refers to whether the trusting person actually notices or pays attention to a trust-related event, which several articles said was important to trust (e.g., [7, 37, 51, 71]). Biros et al. [7] talk about signal detection theory, which is based on paying attention (or not) to an indicator such as an alarm. Kovar et al. [52] discuss the importance—to e-commerce vendor trust— of paying attention to Website assurance seals. The importance to trust of attention to privacy/assurance seals also finds evidence in Kovar et al. [52]. For example, one must notice a privacy seal in order for that seal to be considered for raising one’s level of comfort with the Web site [52]. Only 56 percent of subjects noticed the WEBTRUST<sup>TM</sup> seal shown by Kovar and colleagues, and only 42 percent correctly said they saw the same seal in the Lala et al. [54] study. When an event attracts attention, this facilitates the trust-changing inferences that an attribution [56] or sensemaking [100] process produces. It is clear from the aforementioned that attention to a seal either takes place in the mind of the user or it does not. However, degrees of attention may also exist.

While some amount of research has been done on attention to Website seals, $3 ^ { \mathrm { r d } }$ party endorsements, and other B2C ecommerce assurances, very little has been researched regarding attention to trust-related signals about specific technologies, such as through news media events. As the Samsung vignette at the start of this paper suggests, news articles can quickly change trust. But like e-commerce signals, it is unclear whether attention to a news article afects trust change in a specific technology.

## Sensemaking (and Related Attribution Research)

Some stage model papers identify one important mental mechanism that should relate to trust change: attribution (e.g., [71, 72, 83]). Some research posits that trust is afected by attribution [60, 80, 105]). In general, “ … attributions appear to be centrally involved in the process of drawing inferences about others’ motives, intentions, and dispositions” [53, p. 219].<sup>4</sup> While one may or may not ascribe motives or dispositions to a computer system [55], one can and does ascribe them to the person(s) behind the system, and this may afect trust in the system. Note that people do treat technologies as having human attributes [82].

For computers, Lerch et al. [56] said that trust in computers is partly the result of an attribution process. Capturing subjects’ verbal protocols, Komiak and Benbasat [50] found trust attributions comprised 40 percent of the kinds of reasoning subjects used while employing a recommendation agent to shop for a laptop computer. Using verbal protocols provides strong evidence attribution often takes place in the user’s mind as they use a recommendation agent. Most of the time, subjects made attributions about the competence of the recommendation agent rather than its benevolence or integrity. Muir [71] and Muir and Moray [72] use the Rempel et al. [83] stage model to describe how trust in automated machines develops through three cognitive-emotional stages: predictability, dependability, and faith. Predictability involves observing trustee behavior so behavior can be predicted. Dependability involves attributing trustee dispositions. Faith involves: a) attribution of trustee motives and b) emotional attachment. Here, attribution means how a person makes sense of or appraises the event [35, 100] in terms of the other’s trustworthiness attributes. It involves ascribing underlying qualities or enduring motivations to the other based on evidence [80]. For example, one who sees a 3rd party seal on a Web site assesses what it implies about the site. One who is subject to computer monitoring (e.g., wearing location-monitoring activity badges) assesses the managerial intent behind that monitoring [32]. In the Rempel et al. [83] model, dependability beliefs form based on an attribution process in which the trustee is assessed [56].

Attributions can be made in the initial relationship period. People tend to make attributions even with inadequate information and to have excessive confidence in their prior judgments [92]. This is not necessarily harmful, because it enables people to feel that they can predict trustee behavior, even with a minimal amount of evidence. While theory exists about how sensemaking works, what the literature is missing is solid empirical evidence about how much attribution/sensemaking will afect technology product trust change over time during the initial relationship. Furthermore, we found no research on how sensemaking about a series of news events about a technology influences trust change in that technology. How often sensemaking changes trust is also a largely unanswered question. In general, attribution may produce trust changes on an infrequent basis. This agrees with Muir [71], who says that trust attribution is based on accumulated evidence, not one single event. If an event is strong, clear, unequivocal, and undeniably contradictory to one’s trust level [46], the attribution process will probably be enough to change trust. But not always; because changing one’s mind about a trustee on a core belief like trust is cognitively and emotionally costly [60]. It is very hard to admit: “I guess I was wrong about it.” Changing trust is cognitively costly because trust is a central, highly connected relationship concept [6], such that if you alter trust, you would have to change a number of other beliefs, such as liking and respect of the trustee. It costs less to hang onto existing beliefs and consider the event a quirk.

## Threshold

Some (but limited) research examines trust fragility in terms of thresholds beyond which trust may change. Luhmann [60] explains that trust is not threatened by every message or event. Kee and Knox [45] and Prietula and Carley [80] also mention trust thresholds briefly. Several other scholars mention thresholds, but provide limited information about their role. Fox [28] says that trust requires a minimum level or criterion of performance.

Fogg and Tseng [27] use the term “threshold” to describe one of three credibility evaluation models. Prietula and Carley [80] suggest that emotion impacts trust when afective responses exceed thresholds. Holmes [35, p. 84] says those with low trust arrived there after an accumulation of trust violations that exceeded “thresholds of tolerance.” Either during or after sensemaking, the trusting person assesses whether the attributed contradiction between an event’s implications and one’s trust level is serious enough to exceed the threshold cost of updating the trust level [45, 80]. If not, the trust level is typically not updated. If so, the trust level changes.

Luhmann [60] fleshes out the role of thresholds in trust development. Luhmann explains that not every message or event is likely to change trust. Rather, “The object of trust enjoys a certain credit which allows even unfavourable experiences to be efectively reinterpreted or absorbed … control is exercised by means of thresholds which may not be crossed without the withdrawal of trust” [60, p. 29]. This statement explains that passing a threshold is necessary for changing trust level. Luhmann theorizes that thresholds are simplifying cognitive mechanisms. “ … instead of being faced with an immense mass of slightly diferently shaded experiences, one is concerned with the substitute problem of recognizing the threshold, … And this problem is easier to master” ([60, p. 73]. Luhmann indicates further about thresholds: “ … [the mind] defines the criteria whose variation in the objective situation takes on the character of a threshold experience and suggests the change from trust to distrust or vice versa.” ([60, p. 74] It is clear from Luhmann’s detail about thresholds that he was convinced they play an important role in peoples’ minds as they consider how much trust to extend. We found the literature very sparse about trust thresholds, both in defining what constitutes a threshold and in empirical evidence that they afect trust. We know a minimal amount about how thresholds work. We know very little about how the threshold process and its outcome afect trust change.

## Trust: Fragile versus Robust

Since trust change relates to trust fragility, we also examined the trust fragility literature. Hung et al. [36] say early virtual team trust may be fragile because it is over-optimistically high. Hung et al. also argue fragility is due to trust forming based on thin cues (e.g., 3<sup>rd</sup> party information) or assumptions instead of experience. Feng et al. [25] find that mixed or contradictory messages may cause trust fragility. Kim et al. [48] create a model of trust building based on Zucker’s [105] three trust building modes (characteristic-based, institution-based, and process-based) and Doney and Cannon’s [23] five psychological processes (calculative, prediction, capability, intentionality, and transference). They posit and test hypotheses for potential and repeat customers in a cross-sectional study. They find, for repeat customers, afective customer satisfaction outweighs the efects of reputation and structural assurance on trust. But they find no diferences in trust-predicting strength for reputation and structural assurance between T1 and T2. They also suggest one can predict later trust via early trust. Hung et al. [36] propose that trust goes through a peripheral—a central—and then a habitual route stage. They theorize that trust will be most fragile during the initial/peripheral route stage, before one has solid evidence about the other. In this study, we are not interested in how trust moves from one broad stage or major time period to another. We are instead interested in fragility itself—in terms of what causes trust to change in quick succession during the initial trust period.

Some examine what efects trust in terms of several factors or conditions. Wang and Benbasat [99] produce several categories of trust factors: knowledge-based, interactive, calculative, and dispositional. Wang and Benbasat [99] also found both positive and negative trust factors. Bos et al. [8] study trust in virtual teams under four meeting conditions: face-to-face, video, audio, and text. They find trust was significantly more fragile in the video/audio/text conditions than in face-to-face. They examine both the number of defections and the magnitude of the related drop-ofs.

Others see trust as fragile because of events or actions related to the trustee that are processed mentally. Slovic [91] explains: “Trust is fragile. It is typically created rather slowly, but it can be destroyed in an instant—by a single mishap or mistake” [91, p. 697]. He says “Negative (trust-destroying) events” are more noticeable than positive events [91, p. 698], and the news media tend to “give greater weight and attention to negative events,” just as people do [91, p. 698]. Similarly, Crisp and Jarvenpaa [17] suggest swift trust in virtual teams is fragile because it is cognitive based (e.g., categorical). They hypothesize and find that while early (before project midpoint) trusting beliefs impact late (after midpoint) trusting beliefs, normative actions (setting and monitoring team performance norms) mediate that impact.

In the literature, there is plenty of room for more scholarly work on trust change, especially across more than two time periods. Several vital research problems remain. First, while significant research addresses how trust develops after one gains experience with the other party (e.g., [17]) or across broad stages (e.g., [36]), very little is known about what causes trust changes within the earliest stage—the initial trust time period. This is particularly true in terms of when potential technology users encounter positive and especially negative news events [91] about the technology, as illustrated by the Samsung cell phone vignette. Second, several mental processes (e.g., attention, sensemaking) have been studied with respect to trust; but it also seems crucial to know if these processes influence the more dynamic trust change (i.e., related to fragility) as opposed to trust itself. Third, several studies point to the influence of various situational factors on trust, such as reputation, structural assurance, afect, and initial trust in the technology [47]. But very little empirical work has tested which factors influence trust change (i.e., which factors make initial trust more/less fragile). Our trust change model builds on existing research by proposing that three mental mechanisms impact trust change in the initial relationship. Trust research mentions each of these mechanisms: attention [52], sensemaking (similar to attribution—e.g., [50]), and threshold attainment [60].

## Components of An Event-driven Theory

At the core of an event-driven theory is the story that connects the critical events [77]. Event-driven models typically explain “black box” mechanisms left unclear by other approaches. This study does not examine trust events over long time periods—just over eight short timeframes. Each short timeframe includes a news event. Monge [70] identifies two dimensions used to compare change over time: occurrence (i.e., rate) of change and magnitude of change (Figure 2). Occurrence and magnitude of change data can be used to test the predicted changes. In addition to the event-level predictors (attention, sensemaking, threshold), we also posit person/situation-level predictors.

We now delineate our above trust change definition using occurrence and magnitude. We base this on the Lee and Moray [55] article. Lee and Moray had subjects go through 60 sessions over three days as each controlled a simulated orange juice pasteurization plant. Each session is an event period. After each, subjects were asked, “Overall, how much do you trust the system?” Lee and Moray report initial trust averaged about 6 on a 1-10 scale. On trials 26 and 40, a fault was introduced as a treatment: a feedstock pump failure occurred. Over the first thirty-nine trials, the average trust level among subjects gradually increased to about 8. During this period, the system worked well except for the trial 26 fault. Average trust decreased significantly at trial 26, especially when the fault size was 35 percent (from 8 to 3.5). This showed us that trust change magnitude is important and can be measured. When continual recurring faults began at trial 40, trust for all fault size treatments decreased to about five. The smaller fault size groups continued level for about twelve trials and then started a gradual increase to about six. The 35 percent fault size group continued to decrease in trust to about 2.7 and then began a gradual rise to about four. When trust decreases significantly through a continuous fault, it does so over four to seven periods rather than immediately. This taught us that occurrence of trust change over time may be important to measure.

Figure 2 (see Monge [70]) shows that change occurrence (rate) and magnitude can be quantified in change units per time period. Occurrence (i.e., rate) of change means how often a variable changes (up or down) per unit of time. Across the four units of time illustrated in Figure 2 as “Rate [Occurrence] of Change” it means, ‘how often did trust change during the four time units?’ At a single unit-of-time level, which this study uses, occurrence means ‘whether or not trust changed during that time period’ (Yes = 1 or No = 0). (By time period, we mean during that particular event, not clock time.) We call this kind of trust change occurrence. In this paper, magnitude of change means the absolute value of the size of change (up or down) per unit of time. For a single unit of time, magnitude means, for example, ‘how much did trust change from time period one to period two?’ If trust was measured as 5 in period one and 7 in period two, then trust change magnitude was 2 (= |7-5|). If it was 5 in period one and 3 in period two, trust change magnitude is also 2 (= |3-5|). The occurrence and magnitude concepts will be built into our trust change hypotheses, although the current state of trust change research gives us no basis to diferentiate them.

## Trust Change Event Level Predictors Overview

We base the event portion of our trust change model on Weick [100] and Weiss and Cropanzano [101], who emphasize how events change mental models. To Weiss and Cropanzano, an event occurs after one’s initial afective state [101]. The person attends to the event, identifies and understands it, and then assesses its goal relevance. This is one example of how events and mental mechanisms work. We focus on three key mental mechanisms: attention, sensemaking, and threshold attainment.

## Attention

First, for an event to change trust in a product, it makes sense that the trustor must notice or pay attention to the event. The extent to which one notices or starts attending to the event is what we mean by attention. Weick says that a person may isolate or “bracket” an event “for closer attention” [100, p. 130]. Attention is not the closer examination itself (which we call sensemaking), but rather the bracketing in preparation for the examination. To Weick, attention means enactment—to directly engage one’s environment by extracting something for further consideration. Later, we define the event as a newsbrief; so attention means how much one attends to the newsbrief, in reading and thinking about its importance.

## Sensemaking

Second, for an event to change one’s trust, one would make sense of the product-related event by considering whether it is consistent with one’s current trust level. In Weick’s model, this is the selection mechanism that happens as one applies one’s cause maps to the current event to reduce event equivocality. Here one reasons about the meaning of the event. Applying Weick’s selection theory to trust, we define sensemaking as occurring when one interprets the event vis-a-vis one’s current trust level. For example, one who encountered news about the Galaxy Note 7 device catching on fire would think, “This does not sound like the kind of quality Samsung product I have heard about in the past. Previously, I have only heard things that gave me confidence in Samsung products. But this event has me wondering. Perhaps I am wrong about Samsung phones.” If the event contradicts one’s current trust level, one may consider whether one’s trust level is right or not. This event assessment is what we call sensemaking.

## Threshold Attainment

Third, even an event that is attended to and made sense about may not alter trust in the product until the trustor makes a decision to change that trust. In Weick’s model, this takes place during retention, “the storage of the products of successful sense-making.” [100, p. 131] That is, after sensemaking, one updates one’s cause maps if desired and needed, such that they remain in memory. Our threshold-met is similar to Weick’s retention, but not identical to it. After assessing whether the event contradicts trust enough to justify a trust change, one decides whether or not to make a trust change in the cause map. Kee and Knox [45] and Luhmann [60] support threshold as a key factor by referring to thresholds that must be passed before trust is altered. ## Attention to an Event about a Technology Product Afects Product Trust Change

By attention, we mean a person expending mental efort to bracket a product-related event for further processing instead of ignoring it. Attention is usually not a matter of yes or no about whether one notices something. Rather, it is a matter of degree: how much does one notice or pay attention to something? We define attention conceptually as the extent to which one spends mental efort on an event to determine its value for sensemaking. Attention does not mean one makes sense of the event—only that one starts focusing on it.

Per Cohen et al. [14], people do not attend to everything that happens. Bounded rationality assumes the mind only consciously processes a small percentage of the stimuli it encounters [58]. Only certain events engage people’s minds. Weick says new or extraordinary things tend to engage our attention [100, p. 130]. For example, 9/11 was so unusual that most of us can recall exactly what we were doing that morning, indicating we paid a lot of attention to it. As another example, in Kovar et al. [52], only 56 percent of study participants noticed a WEBTRUST seal put on a website to give users assurance. Those who noticed tended to increase their trust. Attention will relate positively to both trust change occurrence and magnitude (Figure 2). Occurrence and magnitude are simply two aspects of change. We found no theoretical or empirical basis to diferentiate between occurrence and magnitude of trust change in our hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1 (H1): The higher the attention level to a technology product event is, the higher the a) occurrence and b) magnitude of product trust change will be.

## Sensemaking Afects Trust Change

Weick’s model suggests information events come to us as puzzles to be solved. We want to understand them. Knowing what an event means is a mental process of comparing it to our causal maps of how things work [100]. The sensemaking process finds support in trust research that suggests people make mental attributions about the other [71, 83]. For example, if one hears that a GPS product directed a friend to the wrong place, and one attributes the fault to the GPS (not to its user), then one may feel this event contradicts one’s current high trust in the GPS and one may consider decreasing trust. In this study, we do not actually measure the sensemaking taking place. Rather, we are interested here about the outcome of sensemaking, which is that one feels their trust may be misplaced and needs to be reconsidered. To the extent that one considers revising one’s trust due to the event, sensemaking has taken place, because sensemaking is about making causal implications from, or drawing inferences from, an event [100]. For example, one who sees a 3rd party security seal on a website may assess whether or not it implies one can better trust the site; the related sensemaking outcome is that one feels like reconsidering one’s trust level. Although we argue that the outcome of sensemaking should afect trust change, we do so with caveats. First, cognitive scripts can become engrained [40] and second, people may use an attribution process to justify what they already believe [68]. Thus, a specific sensemaking episode may or may not change trust in the product; but it has the potential to do so. In spite of these two examples, we believe that, overall, the greater the sensemaking outcome, the more the trust change. That is, the more one experiences the outcome of sensemaking, the higher the likelihood that trust will change.

Hypothesis 2 (H2): The higher the sensemaking outcome regarding a technology product event is, the higher the a) occurrence and b) magnitude of the product trust change will be.

## Threshold Attainment Afects Trust Change

During or after sensemaking, a trustor decides whether a threshold is met for revising technology product trust levels. Revising trust involves deciding whether or not to update the trust aspect of one’s cause map [100]. It is easier not to make an update because it takes efort to change trust. Changing one’s trust level is mentally efortful because trust is a central, highly connected relationship concept [6]. If one alters trust, one may have to change many other beliefs, such as liking of and respect for the trustee, in order to be internally consistent. It takes less mental efort to maintain one’s existing beliefs and to consider the event an anomaly than to change one’s beliefs about the technology product trustee.

Like attention and attribution, threshold-met has a trust literature basis. Holmes [35, p. 84] says those with low trust arrived there after an accumulation of trust violations that exceeded “thresholds of tolerance.” That is, we often first give people the benefit of the doubt. But when they violate our trust enough, we decrease our trust level. Luhmann [60] explains that this is why trust is not threatened by every event. In spite of Luhmann’s theory, we found no empirical studies testing how trust thresholds work.

Our strategy was not to observe and measure the occurrence of the mental mechanism of a trust threshold judgment. Rather, we define an outcome measure that indicates that the threshold mechanism has occurred, so that the trustor is ready to change their trust level. We define it in terms of how well the threshold for trust change has been met and call the concept “threshold-met.” The threshold-met outcome means the extent to which one is ready or resolved to change one’s trust. This is a good indicator because one would not say one feels ready to change trust unless one is okay with the consequences of doing so. A high threshold-met outcome is not a guarantee one will change trust. Merely expressing a commitment to quit smoking does not mean one will actually quit; but it does increase the probability that one will. Similarly, with the threshold-met outcome: The more a trustor expresses the resolve to change trust, the more likely a trustor is to change it.

Hypothesis 3 (H3): The higher the threshold-met outcome level is about a technology product event, the higher the a) occurrence and b) magnitude of the product trust change will be.

On Figure 1, we place H1-3 in the “Event Level Predictors” of technology trust change. We do this not because our measures all capture the actual mental mechanisms taking place; one (attention) does, but the other two don’t. With attention, we measure the time they take reading the news brief and thinking about it as they answer two questions about it (Table 1, row 1). This gives us an objective assessment of the extent to which the attention mechanism is taking place. We do not do this for sensemaking and threshold. Instead, we capture survey outcome measures that indicate that the mental mechanisms have taken place. For sensemaking, when one makes sense of a news brief and compares it to one’s trust level in the technology, one who finds a discrepancy during sensemaking will reconsider their trust level. We measure sensemaking by the self-reported extent one is reconsidering one’s trust level (Table 1). This is an outcome measure, not a measure of the sensemaking process itself. Similarly, for threshold-met, we measure by self-report the extent to which one is ready or resolved to change one’s trust. This outcome indicates the extent to which they have completed the threshold-met process.

## Trust Change Robustness/Fragility Situation Factors Overview

## Trust Robustness Hypotheses

Trust robustness and fragility, which are opposites, relate easily to trust change. Here is how robust and fragile trust is defined: If trust changes frequently over time, it is said to be fragile. If it does not, it is robust. In terms of trust occurrence, if trust change occurs during a period, trust is fragile; if not, it is robust in that period. If trust change is high in magnitude, it is fragile; if trust change is low in magnitude, trust is robust. To develop person/situation level hypotheses, we relied on the IT trust literature and how it relates to trust fragility. Each variable we chose is about the situation, since in the initial timeframe, “situational factors” largely determine trust [36, p. 9]. First, we examine four time period zero variables that are likely to decrease trust change (i.e., keep trust robust): reputation of the technology, technology enjoyment, technology felt risk, and loyalty to the technology vendor.

## Reputational Influence

Reputation means one assigns good attributes (e.g., honesty, concern) to a specific technology based on second-hand information about it [34, 38]. McKnight et al. [68] posit that a good reputation is often built as people hear good news about the other. They propose good reputation will help initial trust be robust, since positive reputation beliefs make it hard for a negative event to reduce trust in the trustee. That is, one is influenced by the reputation one gleans from others that the technology product has positive attributes. This stabilizes one’s trust in the product, reducing the trust change that occurs.

Hypothesis 4 (H4): The higher the reputation<sub>0</sub> (i.e., reputation at time period zero) of the technology product, the lower the a) occurrence and b) magnitude of the product trust change will be.

## Enjoyment Influence

We use enjoyment as a trust change predictor because hedonic and afective variables influence technology adoption [97, 49] and trust [41, 48]. This is true even though people often adopt technology due to its usefulness [12]. Enjoyment means positive afect toward use of a technology. Enjoyment afects adoption because one’s initial exposure to the technology suggests how fun its use might be. Relating enjoyment to trust, one’s positive mood or emotion about a trustee may, from the start, “afect … immediate perceptions and judgments that the other party can be trusted” [41, p. 536]. Positive afect often correlates with trust [86]. For example, Jarvenpaa et al. [39] found attitude toward an Internet store correlated at .56-.58 with perceived trustworthiness of the store. If one anticipates enjoying a technology, one should also trust the technology.

But beyond influencing trust, afect should also make trust more resistant to change. We believe the higher the initial afect toward the technology, the more trust will be robust and not change. Murray and Holmes [73] found this in close relationships—both in dating and married couples. They measured peoples’ initial idealized attitudes toward each other, such as enjoyment, attraction, and love. Then they followed the couples 4 months and 12 months later. Those couples who had higher initial positive attitudes toward the other person tended to have firmer relationships in the later periods. Similarly, we believe that the higher peoples’ initial enjoyment beliefs about the technology are, the more these initial beliefs should cushion their trust in the product against change. Kelman argues “the existing attitude creates forces toward stability” [46, p. 128]. Specifically, people who expect to get pleasure out of a technology are more likely to ignore negative news about it and keep trusting it.

Hypothesis 5 (H5): The higher the enjoyment<sub>0</sub> (i.e., enjoyment at time period zero) of the technology product, the lower the a) occurrence and b) magnitude of the product trust change will be.

## Risk Influence

If one feels the technology is highly risky at time period zero (T ) with minimal or no experience with it, then one is being realistic or cautious about it. If one feels the technology has low risk, one may be overconfident or even naive about it. Realistic beliefs are more robust than overconfident beliefs. Still, news briefs may provide surprises that could result in one changing one’s trust level. However, if one initially expects it to be risky (i.e., is cautious and realistic at time period zero), then one is less likely to be surprised by a news brief and thus more likely to retain one’s trust level instead of changing it.

Hypothesis 6 (H6): The higher the felt risk<sub>0</sub> (i.e., felt risk at time period zero) of the technology product, the lower the a) occurrence and b) magnitude of the product trust change will be.

## Loyalty Influence

If one already has high loyalty to the technology vendor, then that loyalty should protect one against trust change. Loyalty means the extent to which one has decided to continue to purchase products from a particular vendor instead of its competitor. Vendor loyalty may have developed due to that technology or due to perceptions about other technologies the vendor provides. For example, one may not have used the Google Driverless car, but one has surely used other Google products, building loyalty to Google over time. One’s loyalty to Google may shield one from decreasing trust when a negative event about Google Driverless car is encountered. This is similar to what social scientists have found in committed romantic relationships—that loyalty causes one to idealize one’s partner and thus not to lose trust when the partner does something wrong [73, 35]. In efect, loyalty immunizes partners against bad news about the trustee. This may occur with a new technology by applying the loyalty one feels toward the tech vendor to trust in the vendor’s new product.

Hypothesis 7 (H7): The higher the loyalty (i.e., loyalty at time period zero) to the technology vendor, the lower the a) occurrence and b) magnitude of the product trust change will be.

We also acknowledge that it may not be loyalty that makes a diference to trust in a product but rather the investment one makes in getting to know the product’s features. We do not test this, but do list it, because it is certainly a plausible alternative to H7.

## Trust Fragility Hypotheses

Next, we examine two period zero variables that likely increase trust change (i.e., make trust fragile): trust in the technology product and structural assurance.

## Initial Period Trust Influence

Kim et al. [48, p. 413] suggest that initial trust afects trust at a later period, and that this be tested. Expectation Disconfirmation Theory (EDT) research posits that the higher the initial trust, the more likely it will be disconfirmed [75], because high initial trust may reflect overconfidence. This suggests the higher initial trust is, the more it is likely to be fragile. In our study, the initial trust period is Time period 0 $( \mathrm { T } _ { 0 } )$ —before the respondent sees any news briefs. EDT would suggest that high (overconfident) $\mathrm { { T _ { 0 } } }$ trust will be positively related to trust change. On the other hand, low $\mathrm { T } _ { 0 }$ trust may also be disconfirmed, which would mean that low trust would result in greater trust change. These two conditions of $\mathrm { T } _ { 0 }$ trust (high and low) would ofset each other. However, given disconfirmations to both, we believe it is easier for high trust to decrease than for low trust to increase. For example, Zand [104] found that attempts by the new leader in the low trust group to demonstrate his/her trustworthiness simply reinforced his/her subordinates’ distrust. This is because it is a dificult task to recover from a low trust situation, per those studying trust repair (e.g., [31]). The negative emotions involved are hard to overcome on the low trust side. And thus, trust at $\mathrm { T } _ { 0 }$ would tend to cause later trust to be more fragile than stable.

Hypothesis 8 (H8): The higher the trust<sub>0</sub> (i.e., trust at time period zero) in the technology product, the higher the a) occurrence and b) magnitude of the product trust change will be.

Structural assurance means the belief that success with the specific technology is likely because, regardless of the characteristics of the specific technology, one believes structural conditions like guarantees or other safeguards exist in the general type of technology that make success likely [13, 34, 65]. We believe structural assurance would likely follow the EDT pattern of trust<sub>0</sub> and support trust fragility. That is, due to overconfidence, high structural assurance is more likely to be negatively disconfirmed, while low structural assurance will not be likely to be positively disconfirmed, so that on net, structural assurance will relate positively to trust change.

Hypothesis 9 (H9): The higher the structural assurance<sub>0</sub> (i.e., structural assurance at period zero) of the technology product, the higher the a) occurrence and b) magnitude of the product trust change will be.

## Control Variables

Social science tries to eliminate plausible alternatives [15]. Therefore, we decided to include three news brief-related controls: news brief relevance, news brief negativity (from 1 = Strongly Negative to 7 = Strongly Positive), and news brief size (i.e., word count). These variables reflect repeated-event controls, since each relates to each news brief subjects saw. Instead of putting news brief negativity and relevance in the current questionnaire, we had another group of undergraduate students rate the news briefs on these attributes. We used the means of their responses.

We also include a number of person level controls. First, we captured one relevant demographic variable: gender (age was also measured, but had no efect in the model, since our sample was students; so it was removed). Second, using dummy variables, we controlled for: a) the technology assigned, and b) the positive/negative pattern $( \mathrm { e . g . , + - + - + - + - } )$ by which the news briefs were presented. Third, individual diferences may matter, so we controlled for risk propensity [90], cognitive rigidity [79], disposition to trust new-to-the-person technologies [64], trust in the news media, and situational normality [65], all measured at $\mathrm { T } _ { 0 } .$ Controlling for these may eliminate them as plausible alternative explanations.

## Methodology

Since our focus is on perceptual mechanisms and variables, we designed an online survey to test the hypotheses. In researching trust, perceptions are key factors, because the consensus is that trust is a “psychological state” [87, p. 395]. We assured respondents anonymity to reduce social desirability bias and common method bias [78]. We also varied some of the scale headers to reduce common method bias (see Online Supplemental Appendix B). The survey collects the trust a respondent has in one technology product: either current TomTom GPS, the Google driverless car, Ford SYNC’s in-car voice communication system, or the Apple Siri feature. These products were selected both because they are commented about in the news media and because they are probably interesting to our subjects. Also, each product is likely to have a reputation in the minds of our subjects, though this may be less true of the TomTom GPS. On average, it took just over 20 minutes to complete the survey. Survey participants are university students (average age = 22; 64 percent male) volunteering to participate. Respondents were from undergraduate business courses at four comprehensive North American universities, incented through course extra credit (0.4 percent to 1 percent of course credit); at one school students took the survey during class time. The response rate was 89 percent for a sample size of 2645. Because of the high response rate, nonresponse bias was considered unlikely, so we did not follow-up after the deadline. University students are often discriminating early adopters of new technologies, making this sample especially appropriate for evaluating trust in these technologies.

Most respondents had relatively little experience with the technologies. The technology with the most user experience was Siri, and even here respondents averaged only 1.9 on a 1-7 scale, meaning just less than one year of average experience. But some respondents had greater experience than others. To make sure the sample reflected only initial trust respondents, we removed 763 cases (29 percent) in which the respondent said they had experience with it, leaving 1882. Of these, 1684 who remained were those who had never used the technology (i.e., answered 1 = “Have not used at all” on duration of technology use) and 198 were those who had very limited experience with the technology (i.e., answered $2 ~ = ~ ^ { \alpha } \mathrm { I }$ Less than 1 year” on use duration and answered $2 \ = \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { R a r e l y } ^ { \prime \prime }$ on how often they use it.)

## Procedure

After giving consent, participants answered demographic, control variable, and time period zero items for H4-9. The survey system then assigned them randomly to one of the four technologies and one of the eight news brief patterns (explained in the following sections). After reading a brief Wikipedia excerpt about the technology, they were asked several of their initial impressions of it (felt technology risk, reputation, enjoyment, and trust) and were then shown a series of eight news briefs about that technology (four positive and four negative). After each news brief, respondents reported their attention, sensemaking, threshold-met, and trust in the technology. The news briefs were selected from recent months’ Nexis international print newspaper articles by searching on the technology name. They were shortened into news briefs to make them faster to read (average = 58 words). News briefs typically discuss the technology’s features or others’ experiences with it. Each respondent saw the same eight news briefs on that technology. As an example, see Online Supplemental Appendix B for a positive and a negative Google driverless car news brief.

To create more survey interest and to reduce social desirability bias, we told respondents that there were no right or wrong answers and that we were especially interested in their opinions. To further show felt interest in their opinions, we asked “Why? (please briefly explain:)” after each attention and sensemaking response. To create parity across subjects, we not only equalized the number of positive and negative news briefs (four each), but also equalized across technologies how negative or positive the news briefs were on average. To do this, we had a separate group of students rate a larger set of news briefs for each technology on a 7-point scale from Strongly Negative (1) to Strongly Positive (7) (midpoint 4). We selected news briefs used for a technology such that: a) four were positive and four were negative; b) the group of news briefs did not significantly difer in average negativity either from midpoint (4.0) or from that of other technologies. As a result, the positive news briefs completely balanced out the negative news briefs. To control for order of delivering the news briefs, the news briefs were presented in eight alternating positive/negative patterns nested within the technology treatments $[ + \textrm { - + - + - + } ] , [ - \textrm { + - + - + - } + ] , [ + \textrm { + -- } + \textrm { + - } ] , [ - \textrm { - } + \textrm { + - } + ]$ [+ + + + - - - -], [- - - - + + + +], [+ + - - - - + +], [+ + - + + - - -].

In the survey, we collected data on all the model variables in the hypotheses (see Figure 1). Questions on attention, sensemaking, threshold-met, and trust were answered (in this order) after they read each news brief. The first three measured the outcomes of the mental mechanisms occurring at the event level (for H1-H3). For attention, we also captured objective measures in terms of time spent by a subject reading the news brief and answering questions about it. First we measured the time respondents took to read each news brief. Next we measured the time it took to answer the attention questions—the scale question and the “why?” question. At the top of the attention questions page we placed the same news brief in case they wished to re-examine it while answering these questions. The more time one spends reading the news brief, the greater is one’s attention to the news brief. Time spent answering the attention questions is also an objective indicator of attention. Thus, we summed the time spent on these two pages to represent the attention measure (see Table 1, row 1). Before using this time measure, we first transformed the time scores into seven equivalent groups and from those into seven-point scales to make each numerically equivalent to the questionnaire measure.

Questions at the respondent level (for H4-H9) were answered at the beginning of the survey $\left( \mathrm { T } _ { 0 } \right)$ . See Online Supplemental Appendix B for survey items. In order to avoid demand efects, the questionnaire never defines “trust” for subjects. Neither does its header label any trust item (trust, disposition to trust, trust in media) as a question about trust. We used 7-point scales except for trust in the specific product, where we used an 11-point scale to capture smaller, incremental changes. Most of the items were adapted from existing scales referenced in Online Supplemental Appendix B. For example, trust is measured by an item adapted from McKnight et al. [65]: “For doing [actions that employ the technology], I feel I can depend on the [technology name].” This wording is central to trust because it suggests a willingness to depend on it.

## Measurement Scales

We used single-item scales for all the variables in the questionnaire due to instrument length. However, we first did a pilot study with multi-item scales to demonstrate construct validity. Online Supplemental Appendix C presents the multi-item scale pilot results, which were found to lie within generally accepted standards.

Here is our single-item scale rationale. Because we aimed to do eight measures of several of the study constructs, making the questionnaire extra-long, we consulted relevant literature. Russell et al. [88, p. 493] suggest multi-item scales may be “too time-consuming or too distracting” when using “quickly repeated observation.” They suggest that in “repeatedmeasures designs” multi-item scales may cause subjects to “become less conscientious or … drop out” [88, p. 493]. Russell et al. use a one-item scale (the Afect Grid) to measure afect. Their study concludes that the Afect Grid may be especially efective “when subjects are called on to make afective judgments in rapid succession or to make a large number of judgments … ” [88, p. 499]. Other one-item scales are used for such concepts as current anxiety [18], happiness [1], job satisfaction [74], trust [55], and self-esteem [85]. Due to survey length with eight news brief iterations, we decided to use 1 item scales because this would reduce respondent fatigue, increasing the validity of the results.

## Hierarchical Linear Modeling (HLM) Approach

Information systems researchers have applied HLM to study multi-level relationships (e.g. [20, 59, 102]). Given that our data and hypotheses are multilevel in nature (i.e., event-level and person-level), we chose to use hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) analysis, using HLM 7.01 software [9]. Our event level data are attention, sensemaking, threshold, and trust—measured across eight time frames. Our respondent level data are the independent variables related to H4-9 and some control variables—all measured at period 0. Thus, HLM allows one to test the efects on trust change of both the iterative event level variables and the respondent level variables. A significant portion (≥ 10-15 percent) of higher respondent level variance is a prerequisite for running HLM models [33]. The intraclass correlation of 25.3 percent for trust change magnitude indicates HLM is appropriate and necessary for a correct analysis.

We use a full maximum likelihood algorithm in parameter estimation in order to use likelihood ratio tests to compare diferent nested models [20], such as models 1 vs. 2, and 2 vs. 3 in Table 2. Models 1-3 predict trust change occurrence; models 4-6 predict trust change magnitude. Models 1 and 4 include the control variables only. Models 2 and 5 add the person-level variables used in H4-9 testing. Models 3 and 6 add the event-level variables for testing H1-3. The dependent variable magnitude of trust change was not quite normally distributed. Therefore, we used robust standard errors for all statistical tests, which overcome non-normality problems for large samples [93]. The other dependent variable (occurrence of trust change) is a binary variable: whether trust changes after an event or not (1 = change; 0 = no change). Hence, we use a logistic multi-level (Bernoulli) model for trust change occurrence. To reduce statistical estimate bias and increase eficiency, we use a Laplace transformation in the estimation [81]. Because trust change occurrence is a logistic model, no $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ is generated. To reduce the possibility of multicollinearity, we group mean centered all event-level predictors (per [81, 43]).

## Discussion

This paper adds a new lens—event-level predictors: attention, sensemaking, and threshold-met—to early trust change. It also tests how much outcomes of these three mechanisms simultaneously afect trust change. Scholars have proposed or tested various mechanisms by which trust grows or changes (e.g., [23, 50]). But no one has examined the way all three of these distinct mental mechanisms operate simultaneously to influence initial technology trust change. We also test (in Table 2, Models 3 and 6) whether or not period zero personal/situational trust factors afect trust change, finding they afect it only 2 out of 12 times.

## Study Contributions

Here are the main study contributions. First, we provide evidence of the key role of these three mental mechanism outcomes in trust change, which out-predicted the hypothesized person-level factors. Support for H1-3 was consistent across both the main model and the first two robustness tests—for a no-experience subsample and for a model that added mental mechanism outcome interactions. H1-3 were consistently supported 10 times out of 12 (83 percent of the time) for the third and fourth tests (alternative measures of attention time spent and sensemaking time spent). It was also consistent 71 percent of the time for the fifth robustness test (individual technology samples) (see Online Supplemental Appendix D). We conclude mental mechanism outcomes make a diference and should be included in future work on trust change/fragility. Second, by testing all three simultaneously, we identified which mechanism outcomes have the strongest efects. Threshold-met has the largest efect (see t-ratios in Table 2, models 3, 6). Sensemaking has the next greatest. Attention also has an efect, but it is much less. These findings help prioritize future work.

Third, the lack of support for person/situational trust change factors surprised us, with only structural assurance and perceived risk significantly afecting trust change magnitude (Model 6) and no factor afecting trust change occurrence in Model 3. But given that predicting trust change is a new venture, we feel it is key to know that variables that often predict trust—such as reputation and enjoyment—did not afect trust change. This demonstrates that predicting trust change is quite diferent from predicting trust itself and thus requires diferent antecedents. This should spur significant additional studies. Note that other factors may be more important to trust change than those chosen. For example, product usefulness may be more important to trust change than is enjoyment. The investment one makes in learning to use the product may be more important than loyalty.<sup>5</sup> These factors should be studied as possible trust change factors.

Fourth, while not hypothesized, three control variables—trust in the media, gender, and news brief relevance to trust—played a major role. None of the other control variables mattered, except news brief size (twice at p < .05) and disposition to trust (once at p < .05). Trust in the news media makes the news briefs more credible, enabling them to influence one’s trust. Relevance of the news brief to trust makes the news brief more applicable to trust, raising its level of influence. This finding supports Weiss and Cropanzano’s [101] conclusion that people react to events and events drive peoples’ mental states. Thus, the event side of trust change is especially important to examine. More event-related factors like relevance should be used in future trust change research. Also—and this is another new research direction—factors of attitude change, persuasion, and influence (e.g., [24]) should be used to predict trust change, such as those from Toulmin’s argumentation model (e.g., [47]).

## Two Other Research Implications: Change in Other Key Constructs; Factors Impacting Mechanisms

An obvious implication of this study is that one can examine the trust change phenomenon at a diferent level of specificity than before. We show that researchers should not only examine the personal/situational factors, but also the mental mechanisms by which trust change takes place. In medical research, disease prevention has progressed from examining the situation/person reasons one might get a disease to the specific mechanisms by which the disease is transmitted. Similarly, trust research can augment studying the factors of trust with studying the mental mechanisms leading to trust change. We think this is an important implication. However, beyond this, we suggest two further implications.

## Change in Other Key Constructs

First, we suggest that other important cognitive IS variables be examined the way we studied trust change. For example, behavioral intention to use a technology will also change over time and could be studied in the same manner (i.e., as intention-to-use change). As we did with trust change, one could examine both person/situation factors and mental mechanisms. This would produce a more dynamic view of how technology adoption occurs over the initial and later timeframes.

## Factors Impacting Mechanisms

Second, future trust change research can also study the factors that afect the mental mechanisms. For example, to increase the eficacy of sensemaking as a positive trust change mechanism, one can study what factors make sensemaking of a positive event more likely and more in-depth. The trust change mechanism type of study and the factors-behind-the-mechanisms type of study would be new endeavors that would advance trust research to create more efective interventions.

## Implications for Practice

The clear implication for practice from this study is that one needs to find ways to engage the attention, attribution, and threshold attainment mechanisms (especially threshold and attribution) that potentially increase trust. It is also clear from the lack of significance of four of the six person/situation trust factors (time period 0: reputation, loyalty, enjoyment, and trust) that one can largely ignore these and concentrate on other ways to change trust.

On the other hand, initial perceived risk and structural assurance, though not afecting trust change occurrence, both afected its magnitude. And their efects, as hypothesized, are opposite. Higher period zero perceived risk reduces trust change magnitude (i.e., stabilizes it), while higher period zero structural assurance increases it (i.e., makes it more fragile). This implies if one wants trust to be stable, one should disclose the product’s risks early so that a potential product user is inoculated from the efects of future news briefs that otherwise expose risks unexpectedly. And one should not overinflate assurance beliefs, leaving them open to deflation via negative news.

The next main general implication for practice is to create positive events and reduce negative ones. However, we see this in a more nuanced way in our data. For example, Figure 3 shows average trust levels upon providing subjects the eight news briefs using two patterns of news negativity (4 $[ - \textrm { -- } + \textrm { + -- } + \textrm { + } ]$ and $6 \ [ \mathrm { ~ -- ~ } + \mathrm { ~ + ~ } + \mathrm { ~ + ~ } ] )$ , both of which begin with negative news briefs. In these patterns, the first negative news brief produces either no average trust decrease (pattern 4) or just a slight decrease (pattern 6) (Figure 3). This indicates that on average, subjects give the tech product the benefit of the doubt and do not change trust on the first negative news item alone. The second negative news brief in a row, however, produces a relatively steep decline in average trust in the product in both patterns, indicating the subject is ready to lower trust when the second negative news brief confirms the first one: a news confirmation efect. In pattern 4, the third news brief was positive, producing a large increase in trust; similarly, a large trust increase occurs when the seventh news brief (a positive one) comes right after two negative ones. It is as though whenever the first positive news brief follows the negative ones, the subject jumps at the positive news and it increases their trust strongly. The same phenomenon occurs in pattern 6 when a positive news brief follows the first four (all negative) news briefs. It appears from this pattern that: a) people are unwilling to let one negative news item decrease their initial level of trust in the product; and b) when negative news gives way to positive news (at what we call a positive inflection point), they are quite willing to believe the good news and increase their trust in the technology. This indicates to practitioners that when customers or potential customers receive bad news about their product, supplying good news about it can powerfully turn it in a positive direction during the initial trust phase. This means sellers should watch carefully for bad news about their product and counter it with good news quickly, before consumers’ low trust becomes cement-like. These two patterns also show how much more powerful two bits of bad news in a row are than one in terms of lowering trust. With human cognitive limits operating, two points seem to define a trend.

After observing this “inflection point” where negative news changes to positive news (or vice versa), we decided to create a dummy variable to see if the inflection point had any systematic efect on trust change in our main model (Table 2, Models 3, 6). If, at a certain news brief, the news changed from negative to positive or from positive to negative, we set inflection point = 1; otherwise = 0. Inserting this variable in the model and leaving all else the same showed the variable was a very significant predictor of trust change (ocurrence: $\mathfrak { t } = 5 . 6 1 ; \mathfrak { p } < . 0 0 1$ ; and magnitude: $\mathrm { t } = 1 0 . 4 4 ; \mathrm { p } < . 0 0 1 )$ . In both cases, the efects of sensemaking and threshold-met remained at $\mathsf { p } < . 0 0 1$ in the equation. The strong significance of the inflection point variable indicates that during the initial relationship, one can very efectively change trust in a positive direction with the very first piece of positive news one provides a consumer. A similar test showed that the more positive an event (right after negative ones) is, the greater the magnitude of trust change it tends to cause. Therefore, positive events one creates should be positive enough to be convincing; the more positive the event, the more likely it will produce significant positive trust change.

And, similarly, after its Note 7 meltdown, Samsung took strong measures to counter consecutive negative news—and succeeded. ## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, for sensemaking and threshold-met, we used outcome measures rather than pure mechanism measures. Using outcome measures has been done in other mechanism-oriented research where the actual mechanisms are dificult to measure (e.g., [96]). However, since it would be very informative to use measures of the actual mental mechanisms, we recommend future research examine the actual mental mechanisms. Studies using fMRI technology [19, 84] would also shed additional light here. Second, the use of one-item scales is a limitation of this study, although we validated the measures with multi-item scales (Online Supplemental Appendix C). This was a necessary design tradeof (see [63]) that allowed us to collect eight periods of data without causing respondent fatigue. Third, this study examines trust in a specific technology product tested by showing potential users news briefs about the technology rather than by having the respondent evaluate their trust while using the technology. It is likely that technology usage will produce diferent trust-related reactions, and should be done. Fourth, we employed undergraduate U.S. students, which limits how far this study can be generalized (e.g., across cultures [5, 13, 98]. However, young adult students are an important testing group for trust in and adoption of new technologies. Students are also well-equipped to evaluate diferent new technologies, having already experienced frequent technical innovations during their lifetime. We suggest other samples be tested. Fifth, because our focus was trust in a technology product, our results may or may not apply to trust in people, but they may. Whether our model works for trust in a person remains an open empirical question.

## Conclusion

Our results show for the first time the simultaneous efects on trust change of three mental mechanisms—attention, sensemaking, and threshold. This study also opens new avenues for IS trust research. It initiates study of trust change as a dependent variable. It shows some established predictors of trust do not predict trust change. It shows that one can unpack the trust change “black box” to examine outcomes of the mental mechanisms behind it. Moreover, one could study not only the mental mechanism outcomes that produce initial trust change, but also the factors that afect those mechanism outcomes. Trust is a critical determinant of system usage, and end users are constantly confronted with news about hacking and other security threats, product failures, and technology risks. By hypothesizing and testing how these events afect initial trust change in four specific technologies, this study contributes to information systems research.

## About the Authors

D. Harrison McKnight (mcknig26@msu.edu; corresponding author) is a Professor Emeritus at Michigan State University. He received a Ph.D. from the University of Minnesota’s Carlson School of Management. His research interests include trust in technology products, initial trust, the humanness of technology, the meanings of trust, and trust change. He has published in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Academy of Management Review, and others.

Peng Liu (peliu@fullerton.edu) is an Associate Professor in the Department of Information Systems and Decision Sciences at California State University, Fullerton. His research interests include organizational routines and capabilities, board-level IT governance, and trust in technologies. He has published in MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Studies, Information Systems Management, and others.

Brian T. Pentland (pentland@broad.msu.edu) is the Main Street Capital Partners Endowed Professor in the Department of Accounting and Information Systems at Michigan State University. His research is focused on the analysis of repetitive patterns of action, such as organizational routines. His work has appeared in Academy of Management Review; Administrative Science Quarterly, Journal of Management Studies, Management Science, MIS Quarterly, Organization Science, and elsewhere.
