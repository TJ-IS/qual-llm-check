# How do industry-wide information infrastructures emerge? A life cycle approach Kai Reimers,\* Mingzhi Li,<sup>†</sup> Bin Xie<sup>‡</sup> & Xunhua Guo<sup>†</sup>

School of Business and Economics, RWTH Aachen University, 52064 Aachen, Germany email: reimers@wi.rwth-aachen.de, Research Center for Contemporary Management, School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084, China, <sup>†</sup>email:limzh@sem. tsinghua.edu.cn, and <sup>†</sup> email: guoxh@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn, and <sup>‡</sup>School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084, China, email:xieb@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn

Abstract. Industry-wide information infrastructures (IIIS) have recently gained increased attention by policy makers, especially in the healthcare sector where it is believed that IIIS can substantially contribute to the taming of exploding healthcare costs and dramatically improve service quality, e.g., by avoiding widespread medication errors. However, the emergence and evolution of IIIS is as yet poorly understood, partly because the information systems (IS) literature traditionally uses much smaller units of analysis such as projects, organizations or small networks of organization. In this paper, we propose a combined company-level and industry-level framework to shed light on the process of IIIS emergence. We demonstrate, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, that IIIS emergence is intricately linked to the industry life cycle for the case of fragmented industries. We also explore the relationship between the industry-level and company level life cycles in the process of IIIS emergence, and develop a novel proposition regarding this relationship. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that current policy initiatives to promote the development of IIIS have not adequately taken industry-level conditions into consideration.

Keywords: industry-wide information infrastructures, pharmaceutical distribution in dustry, industry life cycle, China, inter-organizational information systems, case study

## INTRODUCTION

Information infrastructures have only recently attracted broader academic attention in the IS literature (Tilson et al., 2010; Klein et al., 2012). Early treatments include the studies by Forster & King (1995), Monteiro & Hanseth (1995), and Star & Ruhleder (1996). A common theme in the limited literature on information infrastructures is the recognition that they are never built from scratch but are always constructed over an existing ‘installed base’ that they extend (Star & Ruhleder, 1996; Ciborra & Hanseth, 2000; Hanseth, 2000). Moreover, it seems doubtful whether ‘construction’ and ‘design’ are appropriate theoretical concepts for studying information infrastructures. Ciborra & Hanseth (2000) have argued that the notion of evolution might offer a better theoretical lens. Yet, few studies have addressed their long-term development (Robey et al., 2008; Reimers et al., 2013a; Hanseth et al., 2012a.).

One particularly interesting type of information infrastructures concerns those that develop at the level of whole industries (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010). From a practical perspective, such industry-wide information infrastructures (IIIS) have recently attracted intense government attention, especially with an eye to the modernization of the healthcare industry (Hillestad et al., 2005; Taylor et al., 2005; Braa et al., 2007; Greenhalgh et al., 2008; e-Business W@tch, 2008; Boonstra & van Offenbeek, 2010; Hanseth et al., 2012b; Currie, 2012). However, results have largely been disappointing so far (Greenhalgh et al., 2010; Hibberd et al., 2012; Kellermann & Jones, 2013). Even countries such as Finland and the Netherlands, often commended for their pioneering use of e-health technologies, are struggling to establish integrated e-health information infrastructures at the industry level (Reimers et al., 2013b). Thus, a better understanding of how IIIS emerge and evolve would seem to be practically helpful as well. We therefore ask, How do IIIS emerge? We focus on emergence rather than evolution because, in many countries, the healthcare industry, which interests us from a practical perspective, has yet to form a rudimentary information infrastructure, in contrast to other indus tries such as the automotive and the IT industry in which IIIS have emerged several decades ago (Reekers & Smithson, 1996; King & Lyytinen, 2005; Dedrick & Kraemer, 2005; Wells & Nieuwenhuis, 2012).

We conceptualize IIIS as an extension of inter-organizational information systems (IOIS) (a more formal de<sup>fi</sup>nition will be provided later). IIIS differ from IOIS in that they extend across a larger user base that potentially includes all organizations in an industry and cover a broader, in principle, unlimited functional range (Klein et al., 2012). Recently, researchers have called for studying IOIS at the industry level (Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld et al., 2005; Reimers et al., 2010). However, with only a few exceptions (Monteiro & Hanseth, 1995; Barrett & Walsham, 1995, 1999; Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998, 2001; Johnston & Gregor, 2000; Gregor & Johnston, 2001; Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld et al., 2005; Rodon et al., 2008, 2011; Rodon & Sesé, 2010; Reimers et al., 2013a; Hanseth et al., 2012a.), IOIS have generally been studied at the organizational level (Robey et al., 2008). Among the exceptions just mentioned, only a small subset actually studies IOIS at the industry level (Johnston & Gregor, 2000; Gregor & Johnston, 2001; Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld et al., 2005; Reimers et al., 2013a) as opposed to intermediate levels such as networks of organizations. trade associations and marketplaces. Johnston and Gregor (Johnston & Gregor, 2000; Gregor & Johnston, 2001) conceptualized industries as actors, whereas Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. (2005) proposed to use models of collective action to study the behaviour of whole industries.

To promote the study of IS phenomena at the industry level and to contribute to a better understanding of IIIS emergence, we adapt the model of the industry life cycle by combining it with life cycle-based theories of information systems development at the organizational level to formulate a hypothesis of how IIIS emerge. We argue that a life cycle-based approach is appropriate to our purpose, as we are interested in the phenomenon of IIIS emergence in fragmented industries, such as the healthcare industry, were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant structural and institutional change is expected to lead to large-scale consolidation over the next few years. Such patterns of industry consolidation have given rise to the concept of the industry life cycle that has been broadly supported through empirical research (Klepper & Graddy, 1990). Although the concept of the industry life cycle was developed for the case of manufacturing industries (Abernathy & Utterback, 1978; Anderson and Tushman, 1990), it has been adapted to the case of service industries, including pharmaceutical distribution (Fein, 1998). We therefore propose that a life cycle-based approach is, in principle, applicable to service industries and particularly promising for studying the emergence of information infrastructures in the healthcare sector. We selected China as a research site because of China’s rapid speed of economic development. This facilitates the study of development phenomena that usually extend over much longer periods.

We begin our argument by justifying in more detail our life cycle approach as a basis for our theoretical framework, which is developed subsequently. This framework is used to derive hypotheses regarding the process of IIIS emergence. After describing our research method, we construct a narrative of the development of the information infrastructure in China’s pharma ceutical distribution industry involving distributors, hospitals, government agencies, trade associations, e-commerce intermediaries and others, based on carefully coded data collected in 48 interviews, company visits, joint meetings and workshops with industry participants over a period of 9 years. This narrative is encapsulated in <sup>fi</sup>ve high-level descriptive summaries that we refer to as our main empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. These are used to evaluate our hypotheses. In this evaluation, we also discuss earlier <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the literature on IT use and adoption in China to establish the extent to which our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are possibly speci<sup>fi</sup>c to our case setting (a review of that literature appears separately in the Appendix). We conclude that, although the picture of IIIS emergence suggested by our original hypotheses was too simple, IIIS emergence is intricately linked to industry development as suggested by our theoretical approach. We thus contribute to the literature by empirically establishing a link between IIIS emergence and industry development, a link that has not previously been discussed or studied empirically. We also derive more speci<sup>fi</sup>c and detailed hypotheses to guide further research on the emergence of IIIS and discuss policy implications of our results should they stand up to further empirical scrutiny.

## STUDYING INFORMATION INFRASTRUCTURE’S EMERGENCE IN HEALTH CARE

In this section. we brie<sup>fl</sup>y de<sup>fi</sup>ne the concept of IIIS as a point of departure for justifying our theoretical approach to studying them.

Hanseth & Lyytinen (2010, p. 9) de<sup>fi</sup>ned information infrastructure ‘… as a shared, open, het erogeneous and evolving socio-technical system of Information Technology (IT) capabilities. Examples of IIs include the Internet, or industry-wide Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) networks. IIs are recursively composed of other infrastructures, platforms, applications and IT capabilities …’. Information infrastructures are thus

• open, i.e., not limited to a pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned user group;

• shared, i.e., jointly used by several user groups;

• heterogeneous, i.e., used by different kinds of user groups;

• evolving, i.e., in a state of continuous change;

• socio-technical systems of IT capabilities, i.e., consist of both social and technical components with the technical components comprising information technologies; and

• recursive, i.e., larger systems can be decomposed into smaller systems that constitute the larger system.

The recursive nature of information infrastructures implies that the de<sup>fi</sup>nition includes the systems that are interconnected through a network, for example enterprise systems for the case of an EDI network. Moreover, according to this de<sup>fi</sup>nition, information infrastructures also necessarily contain information technologies. Thus, although information infrastructures are never built from scratch (Monteiro & Hanseth, 1995; Star & Ruhleder, 1996), they can still emerge in the sense that, at some stage, information technology is used to automate business processes that is then gradually linked and shared across a larger, heterogeneous and open user base.

We reserve the term IIIS for information infrastructures that extend across several production and distribution stages in one industry. We use the notion of an industry in Porter’s (1990) sense that is simultaneously broader and narrower than the traditional (statistical) notion of an industry. It is broader in that it includes, in addition to competitors on a given production or distribution stage, their suppliers, customers, potential entrants and potential providers of substitutes. It is narrower in that it does not necessarily comprise all <sup>fi</sup>rms that engage in the same (productive) activity but requires that <sup>fi</sup>rms have an awareness of one another as competitors.

Porter (1990) saw an industry as an organized system rather than as a collection of atomic agents who meet occasionally. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, relationships between <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to be long term, giving rise to mutually stabilized expectations of role behaviour, rather similar to formal organi zations that are the subject of organization theory. Against this understanding, industrial dynamics may then be seen as a special case of organizational change. To motivate and justify ou theoretical approach, we therefore <sup>fi</sup>nd it useful to draw on a well-known classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme for organizational change theories proposed by Van de Ven & Poole (1995). These authors distinguish four types of theories based on the nature of the mechanism driving organizational change, called ‘motor’. (1) Teleological theories ascribe organizational change to a purposeful agent who sets goals, devises means to their achievement, observes deviations of actual events from intended outcomes and adjusts the chosen means accordingly. (2) Life cycle the ories assume that some inherent (genetic) programme drives organizational change. Organizational development thus proceeds through a pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned set of stages such as birth, growth, maturity and decline. (3) Dialectical theories explain organizational change as resulting from con<sup>fl</sup>icting forces such as juxtaposed interest groups. (4) Evolutionary theories model organizational change on the well-known biological process of variation, selection and retention.

All four types of theories have explanatory potential regarding industry development. How ever, as pointed out by Van de Ven & Poole (1995), each type of theory makes certain assumptions that must be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in view of the case at hand if that theory is to be usefully applied. For example, application of teleological change theories to industry development requires the existence of some agent, such as a ministry or an anti-trust authority, which monitors industria development and is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently resourceful to devise and implement measures to steer devel opment towards goals that it also de<sup>fi</sup>nes. These conditions seem to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled for centrally planned economies such as China’s. Dialectical theories require that some groups or other entities can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that actively oppose one another and thus drive industrial change.

For example, manufacturers and retailers may be seen as opposing groups, each vying to get the upper hand in the battle for appropriation of total value added in their industry (Porter, 1980). Regarding the other two types, life cycle and evolutionary theories, two elaborate industry-level instances of these have been developed, namely the industry life cycle theory and population ecology, respectively (Utterback & Abernathy, 1975; Hannan & Freeman, 1977; Abernathy & Utterback, 1978; Aldrich, 1999). Thus, all types of theories are promising in explaining industrial dynamics, if the conditions for their applicability can be assumed to prevail.

Usually, real cases of organizational change dynamics are best explained by a combination of two or more types of theories (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995). This logic also applies to industry dynamics. For example, the evolution of an industry may be usefully explained by population ecology, but regulatory action will usually be a factor that cannot be accounted for by population ecology. Thus, an overall approach may seek to combine evolutionary and teleological theories. A promising strategy for devising an overall theoretical approach appears to consist of selecting one type of theory that captures the main features of the phenomenon of interest and to complement that theory with other theories that cover aspects of the phenomenon that seem to be important for developing a convincing narrative.

The feature that interests us regarding industrial development is the large-scale change that comes with industry consolidation. We expect that the healthcare sector as a whole will experience such consolidation processes in the wake of regulatory measures to face increasing healthcare costs of an aging population as is the case in many countries. Such processes are described by the model of the industry life cycle, originally developed for manufacturing industries by Abernathy & Utterback (Utterback & Abernathy, 1975; Abernathy & Utterback 1978).<sup>1</sup> According to this model, a new industry is ‘born’ as a new technology becomes available that forms the basis for novel types of products. The initial phases are characterized by ongoing experimentation aiming to design a product appealing to customers, along with ongoing re<sup>fi</sup>nement of the technology itself. At some stage, the design stabilizes and innovative activity focuses on the manufacturing process in the quest for higher quality and lower costs. The emergence of such a stable design, the so-called dominant design, thus ushers in a new type of competition in which large-scale capital investments replace the competitive role of technical and aesthetical experimentation. Only large organizations can muster the <sup>fi</sup>nancial strength to support such investments so that the emergence of a dominant design also triggers a consolidation process.

The causal mechanism associated with the emergence of a dominant design results in a typical pattern of industry development in which the number of <sup>fi</sup>rms initially grows slowly then, as pro<sup>fi</sup>tability is reached, rises steeply, to fall sharply after the emergence of a dominan design until a steady state is reached. Apart from this pattern in the number of competitors, the model also provides statements about the change of horizontal and vertical relationships i.e., relationships among competitors and between these competitors and their customers and suppliers.

Fein (1998) had adapted the industry life cycle model to the case of service industries. He argued that although service industries display a similar development pattern as manufacturing industries, the shake-out phase in service industries is not triggered by the emergence of a dominant (product) design but the coming together of various practices and technologies that mutually stabilize each other. In analogy to the term ‘dominant design’, he called that set of practices and technologies a ‘dominant business model’. Moreover, he argued that service industries are not ‘born’ through the discovery of a new technology; however, he also did not specify an alternative mechanism. Indeed, he applied the concept to a rather old industry, pharmaceutical wholesaling in the USA, relating two hundred years of business history to identify the period between 1978 and 1996 as the industry’s shake-out phase. This is consistent with Porter & Rivkin’s (2000) argument that industries are actually never born but transformed. For example, the car industry evolved from the coach building trade. Thus, we conclude that the model of the industry life cycle is applicable to older service industries as well as to newly established manufacturing industries. In fact, we intend to apply the model to the evolution of the pharmaceutical distribution industry in China, taking Fein’s application to the same industry in the USA as a precedent.

Van de Ven & Poole (1995) warned that one risk of choosing a theoretical perspective for studying organizational change lies in seeing only what that theoretical perspective can render visible through its constructs so that important factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing organizational change of the case at hand may go unnoticed. To guard against this risk, we consider further possible factors driving industrial development. As we are speci<sup>fi</sup>cally interested in industrial evolution in China, we believe that government intervention plays an important role in that development, because of China’s legacy as a centrally planned economy. To allow for these in<sup>fl</sup>uences to emerge, we use an open coding approach, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally looking for possible in<sup>fl</sup>uences of government on industry development. We thus adopt a broad teleological orientation to capture possible government in<sup>fl</sup>uences rather than a particular theoretical model describing government behaviour with regard to industry development. Through this approach, we avoid the problem of combining two theories with potentially con<sup>fl</sup>icting theoretical assumptions. Because we do not adopt a fully grounded approach (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), we will not be able to generalize our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the open coding process. However, the main purpose of this approach is to discover possible limits to our life cycle-based approach. For example, it may be the case that the unfolding of the industry life cycle is severely constrained or even thwarted through government action, which would invalidate our theoretical approach.

## A MULTI-LEVEL, LIFE CYCLE -BASED THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

In this section, we build a theoretical framework for explaining the emergence of IIIS, based on the industry life cycle model. For this purpose, we elaborate the model in two ways to connect it with the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of information infrastructure given earlier. First, to account for the recursive nature of information infrastructures, we extend the model to comprise <sup>fi</sup>rm-level developments with regard to IT use, drawing on another life cycle concept known as the Stages Hypothesis; second, we synthesize statements about the change of horizontal (competitive) and vertical (supplier–customer) relationships as contained in various versions of the industry life cycle concept to explore the implications of that concept for the possibility of joint (collective) action for infrastructure development on the industry level.

Information infrastructures are recursive; i.e., the larger system can be decomposed into component systems that constitute the larger system. An IIIS can thus be conceived to consist of enterprise-wide systems that are connected to one another to form a larger system, an IIIS. Thus, information infrastructure emergence needs to be analyzed on these two levels, the <sup>fi</sup>rm and industry levels. We therefore seek to complement the industry life cycle model by a model that describes the development of enterprise-wide information systems. We consider it appropriate to do so by drawing on a <sup>fi</sup>rm-level life cycle model because then the underlying theoretical assumptions of both the industry-level and <sup>fi</sup>rm-level descriptions are likely to be compatible. A particularly well-known life cycle model describing ITuse on the <sup>fi</sup>rm level is the so-called Stages Hypothesis developed by Nolan and colleagues (Nolan, 1973; Nolan et al., 1993). A concise and balanced discussion of the Stages Hypothesis can be found in Marble (1992).

Nolan and colleagues argued that <sup>fi</sup>rms have to learn how to effectively use successive waves of new information technology and that this learning is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the growth of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT budget. Learning is conceived as an organizational process in which both managers and users adjust their behaviour to the opportunities and constraints offered and imposed by a new type of technology, Such learning proceeds through a series of pre-defined stages – hence the name ‘Stages Hypothesis’ – and ends when the new type of technology is fully integrated into organizational processes and structures. A new learning cycle is initiated when a new type of technology becomes available. The Stages Hypothesis is a life cycle theory in that it predicts that organizational absorption of a new type of technology occurs as a de<sup>fi</sup>ned succession of learning stages towards a <sup>fi</sup>nal state that is also pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned. It is, however, non-deterministic in that it does not predict that companies will necessarily proceed through these stages. For ex ample, a company may remain at one stage inde<sup>fi</sup>nitely or the learning cycle may be interrupted by the arrival of a new type of technology to start another learning cycle. In various versions of the Stages Hypothesis, Nolan had distinguished different numbers of stages; Nolan et al. (1993) consolidated these into four, as follows: initiation, contagion, control and integration.

Nolan (1973) proposed four indicators, so-called growth processes, to measure a company’s progression in its IT learning process: the structure of the application portfolio, the nature of the IT resource, the type of management control and the degree of user awareness. Nolan et al. (1993) further distinguished between three so-called eras characterized by three underlying technologies (in brackets): data processing era (mainframe computing), micro era (micro and personal computers) and network era (data communication networks). The arrival of a new type of technology characteristic of a new era is external to the theory; the era–concept functions as a trigger to usher in a new learning cycle driven by the four growth processes. The <sup>fi</sup>rst and the second of these growth processes, the structure of the application portfolio and the nature of the IT resources, composed of technologies and IT personnel, re<sup>fl</sup>ect a company’s progression regarding one of these three types of technology eras. Management control, the third growth process, is initially lax but becomes more pronounced as the spending on the new type of tech nology increases sharply to reach a balance between control and ‘slack’ in the integration stage. With regard to the fourth growth process, user awareness, users initially adopt a hands-off attitude towards the new technology, then develop a strong interest in it, which, however, is not yet based on a profound understanding of the technology’s possibilities and con straints, and, in the integration stage, become active and competent users of the technology.

Regarding the development of IIIS, we propose that <sup>fi</sup>rms must have mastered network era technologies as a precondition for IIIS emergence. Although the <sup>fi</sup>rst two eras, mainframe and PC era, are characterized by distinct technologies, as their names suggest, the network era is characterized by the dramatically falling costs of data communication networks as a result of various factors, including deregulation of the telecommunication industry and, later, the commercialization of the Internet, which allows for the integration of individual IT applications, whether mainframe-based or PC-based, across wide geographical distances. This, in turn, gave rise to a new class of applications, known as enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, that integrate all relevant business data and functions in a single database that is accessed remotely (Davenport, 1998). Our proposition now simply is that IIIS emerge as these enterprise systems further extend their reach by, for example, being connected to enterprise systems of suppliers and customers through EDI links or, alternatively, by giving suppliers and customers access to internal enterprise systems. Thus, IIIS emergence is conceived as a steady process of systems integration that <sup>fi</sup>rst extends across an entire enterprise and then stretches further to cover increasing sections of whole supply chains.

As justi<sup>fi</sup>ed earlier, we draw on the concept of the industry live cycle for our industry level analysis. Authors have used different ways to describe this pattern by dividing it into two, three and four phases. Here, we rely on the phase scheme proposed by Klepper & Graddy (1990), which distinguishes between three phases, namely growth, shake-out and stable stage. As described earlier, the industry life cycle concept has been adapted to service industries by Fein (1998). He argued that three modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of the concept are required to <sup>fi</sup>t the case of service industries: (1) in contrast to manufacturing industries, the shake-out stage is initiated by the emergence of a dominant business model rather than a dominant (product) design; (2) there is no <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage – i.e., older <sup>fi</sup>rms are not more likely to survive the shake-out phase than new entrants; and (3) exit – in the shake-out phase – occurs through acquisitions of weaker rivals rather than through bankruptcy. Based on this work, we argue that the characteristics of the three phases as summarized in Table 1 are also applicable to service industries provided that the concept of dominant business model is substituted for that of dominant design. As already mentioned, a dominant business model is conceived of as a mutually stabilizing set of practices and technologies. Fein (1998) used the innovation of electronic ordering in the pharmaceutical distribution industry to illustrate this idea. Electronic ordering, on its own, is a process innovation that is easily adopted across the whole industry (wholesale distributors) (Segars & Grover, 1995). Although there are some direct bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to the adoption of electronic ordering, the main effects are second-order effects. For example, the arrival of orders from hos: pitals and pharmacies in electronic format allows for radically changed practices in organizing the picking process of wholesalers that result in increased ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of warehouse operations. This, in turn, enables wholesalers to offer more frequent deliveries, which, in turn, enables retailers to reduce their inventories, freeing space for retail space in the case of community pharmacies and reducing capital costs related to the holding of inventory for hospital pharmacies.

As suggested by Table 1, the nature of relationships between <sup>fi</sup>rms changes signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly be tween phases. The industry life cycle concept distinguishes between two types of relationships, namely those between competitors and those between these <sup>fi</sup>rms and their customers and suppliers. We call the former horizontal and the latter vertical relationships. We propose that the nature of these relationships is crucial for the emergence of IIIS. Such infrastructures become important after a dominant design respectively a dominant business model has emerged because they are important for reducing costs and improving product and service quality. In contrast, the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase is characterized by widespread experimentation with regard to produc design and business model in which <sup>fi</sup>rms compete by offering different designs and service packages rather than by lowering costs or by increasing quality. Table 1 shows that, in the second phase, horizontal and vertical relationships are not conducive to cooperation among competitors and between customers and suppliers. Rather, competitors <sup>fi</sup>ght for survival and relationships between customers, and suppliers are also antagonistic as <sup>fi</sup>rms engage in a struggle over value added. In contrast, these conditions change to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more benign to collective action required to build a shared, open information infrastructure in the third stable stage as the relatively few remaining competitors engage in joint action in areas of common concern and vertical relationships are characterized by efforts to integrate processes across company bound aries to improve operations management of the whole supply chain. We therefore propose that an industry must have reached the stable third stage as a precondition for the emergence of IIIS.

In sum, our theoretical framework distinguishes between two levels, the <sup>fi</sup>rm and industry levels. We argue that, because of the recursive nature of information infrastructures, the right conditions must prevail on both these levels to facilitate IIIS emergence. On the <sup>fi</sup>rm level, companies must have mastered network era technologies, whereas on the industry level, the third, stable stage of the industry life cycle must have been reached. We draw on the theoretical constructs proposed by the Stages Hypothesis and the industry life cycle concept to measure the progression of <sup>fi</sup>rms and industries in their respective life cycles. ## METHOD

The case study research method is appropriate when the phenomenon of interest is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to untangle from its environment (Yin, 2009), which is true for our research where the phenomenon of interest, IIIS emergence, is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to separate from its industrial and regulatory environment.

We selected China as our research site because of its rapid industrial and regulatory change, which implies that development phenomena that typically extend across much longer periods unfold relatively more quickly so that they can be captured more easily through a longitudina study. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, China’s recent economic development is one of the fastest in human history (Zheng, 2005). It has moved from a centrally planned economy towards a capitalistic system, radically changing the organization of its industry as well as of its regulatory apparatus. The dominant form of economic organization just three decades ago was the so-called dan wei, an organizational unit that comprised and organized both the work and the social life of its members (Walder, 2000). These organizations were transformed into modern, commercia enterprises at break-neck pace (Park et al., 2006). Government agencies, used to run whole industries, were re-modelled into ministries whose main task is to supervise industries rather than to operate them (Walder, 2000). The institutional structure underpinning coordination within industries is also only recently emerging, including trade associations and other forms of company-to-company collaboration (Pei, 1998). At the same time, the government is strongly pushing the development of advanced information infrastructures in several industries, including the healthcare sector (Gao & Lyytinen, 2005; Chen, 2006). Thus, the phenomenon of IIIS emergence can be studied in China, and it is likely to happen in a relatively short period. For this reason, we submit that a single case study research design is justi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

We use two units of analysis, namely (1) China’s pharmaceutical distribution industry comprising two distribution stages, wholesale distribution and retailing, and (2) the practices of IT use in the individual companies making up the pharmaceutical distribution industry, distributors and hospitals. Both units are studied and observed over 9 years (from 2004 to 2012).

We used several methods for data collection: semi-structured interviews, company visits, joint meetings of two or three companies, industry workshops, and an internship. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst 5 years, we solely relied on interviews with industry participants. Beginning in 2009, we organized regular, bi-annual industry workshops that brought together many of the interviewees that were our main information source in the earlier years as well as further industry practitioners. Participants in these workshops increasingly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed themselves with this group, which we call a learning community, and began to interact with one another to promote mutual learning and common concerns in the industry. Some of these interactions, joint meetings and company visits were facilitated by us and became a further valuable source of information. Company visits provided a material context for soliciting more speci<sup>fi</sup>c information that usually concerned concrete procedures and practices related to pharmaceutical distribution. Joint meetings brought to the fore interaction patterns between companies located on adjacent supply chain stages. Finally, industry workshops allowed for discussing common themes and proved particularly useful for validating information collected previously through interviews. Finally, a 3-week internship by a member of the research team in a community hospital furnished us with detailed knowledge of the pharmaceutical logistics and dispensing practices in a hospital. In addition we systematically collected of<sup>fi</sup>cial documents regarding regulatory events relevant for the pharmaceutical distribution industry over the whole period of observation. The authors acted as independent researchers and were in no way engaged in commercial activities in the industry.

Interviewees covered all relevant actors in the industry; speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they included wholesaler– distributors, independent pharmacies, hospitals, manufacturers, e-commerce intermediaries, third party logistics service providers (3PLs), trade associations, a leading trade magazine, government agencies (including the Ministry of Health and the State Food and Drug Administration (SFDA), recently renamed China Food and Drug Administration), a government body responsible for the centralized bidding system in Beijing and a prominent individual in the industry who was instrumental in implementing an e-commerce initiative by central government. This person was interviewed every year from 2004 to 2007. Workshop participants were drawn from all supply chain stages, i.e., pharmaceutical manufacturers, distributors, wholesalers and 3PLs; on average, 17 people participated in our workshops.

All interviewees and participants in our learning community have extensive experience in the industry, ranging from up to 30 years for some wholesalers to several years for 3PLs that tended to be new players in the industry. In total, 48 data collection events took place comprising 37 interviews, 4 company visits, 3 joint meetings, 3 workshops and the internship. Initially, inter views were not tape recorded because of concerns of interviewees regarding the sensitive nature of many of the issues that we covered in our interviews, including the giving of bribes and kick-backs, a widespread practice in the industry. Later, as we became more familiar with the industry and as we gained some trust by our interviewees, interviewees mostly agreed to the interviews being tape recorded. We note that similar problems have been reported in the literature (Westrup & Liu, 2008). The tape recordings of all three workshops were transcribed; tape recordings of interviews, company visits and meetings were selectively transcribed after the need for transcribing particular sections of these recordings was discussed in the research team. The workshop transcriptions were translated into English; English memos were prepared for all other transcripts and notes of interviews that were not tape recorded. The intern has kep a diary in English to document her learning. Our data collection activities are summarized in Table 3.

Our data analysis proceeded in several steps. One researcher <sup>fi</sup>rst carefully read all transcripts and memos to extract descriptive codes – in essence interpreted quotations – that pertained to our several indicators regarding organizational and industry life cycles, and documented these in a spreadsheet <sup>fi</sup>le. All these descriptive codes were then reviewed by the othe researchers in the team, all ethnic Chinese, to ensure that they correctly captured the meaning of the original utterance that was recorded or otherwise documented. In many cases, the wording of these codes were changed as a result; these changes were also documented in the spreadsheet <sup>fi</sup>le. In all cases where the meaning of codes was contentious among the researchers, we referred back to the original recording or the <sup>fi</sup>eld notes to clarify the wording of our descriptive codes.

Regarding company-level indicators, we found that only those codes that concern the appli cation portfolio were suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently numerous and comprehensive to allow for the construction of a development narrative. In contrast, we could construct meaningful narratives for all industrylevel indicators speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in our theoretical framework from the coded material except for the number of <sup>fi</sup>rms. The number of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the distribution industry – likely to range between 6000 and 17 000 – has become a political <sup>fi</sup>gure because central government persistently attempted to reduce that number in order to facilitate supervision and reduce the degree of corruption notorious in the industry, whereas provincial governments often protect local industries and thus contribute to a <sup>fl</sup>at or even rising number of distributors. We therefore decided to base our analysis on the remaining four industry-level indicators (dominant business model, innovation focus, horizontal linkages and vertical linkages).

To capture the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the regulatory environment, we used an open coding process to identify and categorize codes that describe the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of government action on the industry, which were also recorded in our spreadsheet <sup>fi</sup>le. Although we relied on these codes to con struct a narrative about the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of government action on IIIS emergence, we validated all coded descriptions against a timeline of all relevant government actions that was developed on the basis of of<sup>fi</sup>cial government documents obtained through our desk research.

Next, all codes were grouped by observation period and further categories. The whole period of observation – 2004–2012 – was broken up into three periods of 3 years each. The main purpose of this way of organizing our longitudinal data was to make visible development processes while hid ing short-term changes. The approach may be compared with the use of moving averages in time series data. Codes were associated with each period according to the time at which the underlying data have been collected. For example, codes extracted from an interview conducted in 2004 were associated with the <sup>fi</sup>rst period of observation that extended from 2004 to 2006. All codes were further categorized according to the following scheme. Codes concerning the life cycle of industry development and company learning were categorized according to the types of companies involved in the pharmaceutical distribution industry (dominant business model and innovation focus of distributors and structure of application portfolio of hospitals and distributors, respectively) and to the type of relationship between companies (horizontal linkages between distributors and vertical linkages between distributors and hospitals). Codes regarding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of government action were grouped into in<sup>fl</sup>uence on standardization, in<sup>fl</sup>uence on industry structure, in<sup>fl</sup>uence on IT use and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of industry associations.

We then characterized each 3-year period in terms of these categories to provide a descrip tion of the situation regarding industry development and company-level learning that pertained to that particular period. Change thus occurred from one 3-year period to the next. In constructing our narrative, the descriptions for each period occasionally refer back and forth to the situation in the earlier or later periods or, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst 3-year period, to the historical baseline situation described at the outset of our narrative, to more clearly bring to the fore the changes that occurred over the whole period of observation.

As an additional device for summarizing our data, we calculated quantitative summary indica tors for each theoretical construct in each period. For this purpose, we associated all descriptive codes with a numerical value ( 1, 0, 1). These values re<sup>fl</sup>ect whether the code suggests that the maturity stage described by the industry-level and company-level life cycles was reached (‘1’) or whether the industry or the company was clearly in a pre-consolidation respectively a pre-network era stage (‘ 1’). If the code did not clearly and unambiguously suggest the arrival either at a mature stage or a situation characteristic of the pre-consolidation/network era stage, a ‘0’ was assigned to that code. By averaging all these values for each theoretical construct (e.g. ‘vertical linkages between distributors and hospitals’) in each period, a numerical summary statement ranging between ‘ 1’ and ‘1’ was constructed, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree of maturity at the company or at the industry level regarding that particular construct in that period.

For each 3-year period, we also provide all descriptive codes in tabular form. These tables include a column that registers the numerical values assigned to each code used to calculate the summary indicators just described. The coded data are presented both in narrative and in tabular form for two reasons: (1) to make available to the interested reader the full data set including codes not embedded in the narrative and (2) to contextualize the codes so that the codes become meaningful for the reader not familiar with the case. The codes are numbered consecutively across all tables. References to these tabulated codes in the narrative are given in square brackets. Company names mentioned in the codes have been replaced by pseudonyms.

## THE EMERGENCE OF AN INFORMATION INFRASTRUCTURE IN THE CHINESE PHARMACEUTICAL DISTRIBUTION INDUSTRY

## Historical context and precedents

Beginning in the late 1970s, economic reform in China not only gave rise to the development of commercial organizations and markets but also led to the deterioration of existing economic organizational structures. One characteristic of these previously dominating economic structures was the tight integration of work organizations and social services such as housing, education and health care. These integrated units – called dan wei – were not only internally integrated but, to a large extent, externally insulated. Workers would seldom leave the compounds on which all facilities required for everyday life existed (Walder, 2000).

Thus, the functional separation that came with the development of pro<sup>fi</sup>t-driven business organizations – as opposed to those integrated work organizations – implied that social services would have to be provided either as a commercial service as well or by the government. To some extent, both of these directions were pursued, especially with regard to health care. The government initially promoted the creation of decentralized, market-based and pro<sup>fi</sup>t-driven organizations but later on, beginning around the year 2000, reversed these policies by re-centralizing increasing portions of healthcare delivery and tightening regulatory requirements, culminating in a sweeping healthcare reform announced in 2009 (Ho, 2011). On the demand side, government steadily expanded the reach of the state health insurance system. A <sup>fi</sup>rst reform, in 1998, provided basic insurance coverage to workers in cities (Dou, 2003; IMS Health, 2004), whereas more recent efforts to also provide such services to the rural population have resulted in basic health insurance coverage for an estimated 95% of peasants (Guo, 2011).

On the supply side, the historical legacy consists of a tiered system for providing healthcare services. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, both hospitals and distributors of medical products, often called medical suppliers, are structured into a three-tier system related to the geographic organization of the country. Distributors were organized by provincial, county and municipal levels. Hospitals were equally organized into three tiers that were de<sup>fi</sup>ned differently for rural and urban areas: village station/street health station, township/community health centre and country/district hospita (Hsiao, 1995). As these players were released into a market economy, both state-owned distrib utors and hospitals became pro<sup>fi</sup>t-driven organizations, resulting in a rapid rise of drug-related expenses. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the old three-tier structure of distribution was maintained, causing multiple mark-ups as drugs passed from <sup>fi</sup>rst-tier to second-tier to third-tier distributors. At the same time, hospitals relied to a large extent on the sale of drugs to obtain the necessary funds for their operations, ranging from 50% to 90% of total revenues (Wang & Li, 2011). As government struggled to regain control over the industry, it consequently focused on eliminating these twin causes of dramatically increased healthcare spending. On the one hand, government attempted to consolidate the structure of the distribution industry aiming at establishing a small number of large national and provincial distributors who directly service hospitals. On the other hand, government attempted to affect prices, initially by imposing central procurement schemes based on public reverse auctions and later on by mandating that hospitals must sell drugs at the same price at which they have obtained them, the so-called 0% mark-up policy (Wang & Li, 2011).

An important intermediate development for our case is an early experiment with e-commerce undertaken in one province, Henan, in 1993. A government of<sup>fi</sup>cial was persuaded, through a visi to the USA, that e-commerce could become the basis on which drug distribution can be made more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and widespread corruption can be contained. Although this early experiment ulti mately failed, the central government later adopted the idea and mandated that provinces operate so-called e-bidding platforms to run reverse auctions for the procurement of drugs. The policy was implemented unevenly across the country (Li & Reimers, 2009) but is still continued to this day.

## Case narrative by period

First period (2004–2006): The old institutional structure still dominates the industry The situation of the industry at the beginning of our period of observation is still very much char acterized by the remnants of the old system of pharmaceutical distribution described earlier Distributors are regionally focused [1, 7; Table 4] so that they have to cooperate with other distributors in regions in which they are not present in order to extend their geographical scope of operation [14, 15, 16] while local competition is <sup>fi</sup>erce [17]. Moreover, the multi-tiered structure of the distribution system is still recognizable as <sup>fi</sup>rst-tier wholesalers sell mostly to second-tier distributors who, in turn, obtain half of their revenues by selling to third-tier distributors [5, 6]. Thus, distributors view themselves primarily as dealers rather than as logistical service organizations. This characterization is in line with the description of the industry’s core competence as consisting of knowledge about current prices [8]. Distributors increasingly offer pharmaceutical manufac turers information about sales to other distributors and hospitals [3]. Such information is very valuable for manufacturers because they usually do not know which hospitals <sup>fi</sup>nally buy their products, a result of the multi-tiered structure of the distribution industry. Therefore, such IT-based services reinforce the old business model and cannot be seen as indicators of a new business model based on logistical services, which is shaping up in the later periods.

Relationships with hospital customers are arms’ length. Hospitals use multiple distributors [10] as even the larger distributors carry only a relatively narrow range of products [2] and rely on their bargaining power to delay payments [12]. Although government-imposed e-commerce platforms are used for bidding and transmitting order information [10, 11], these are built and operated by third parties rather than by the trading parties themselves [17]. Hospitals are using internal information systems; these are, however, not integrated across the whole organization even for large, i.e., advanced, hospital users [18]

In sum, the change towards a service-oriented business model with a focus on logisticalbased and IT-based services apparent in later periods is not yet even noticeable. Instead, distributors view themselves predominantly as wholesalers with control of medical suppliers/ supplies being viewed as a critical competitive resource. The national market is regionally fragmented, and local markets do not show patterns of consolidation or market leadership. Ver tical relations are arms’ length, focused on prices and terms of payments. Although e-commerce platforms are used for ordering, their use is minimal and results from a government mandate rather than from a desire to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of supply chain operations. Horizontal coop eration is present to the extent that non-competing distributors combine their operations in order to extend their geographical scope. Otherwise, <sup>fi</sup>erce local competition prevents cooperation among distributors so that e-commerce platforms need to be operated by third parties. Overall, our qualitative characterization shows an industry in its early stages. This description is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the negative signs of all summary indicators for which data were available (Table 5; see the Methods section for details on the computation of these values).

Likewise, government has not yet developed a consistent and clearly visible strategy with re gard to regulating the industry. Rules regarding cross-provincial trading seem to be ambivalent [20, 22], and certi<sup>fi</sup>cation requirements are only weakly enforced [23]. At the same time, government is pro-active in terms of using e-commerce as a tool for regulation and industry oversight [24]. Such imposed use of e-commerce also speaks of the government's difficulties in regulating the industry, which may be a result of fragmented government structures [21] and explain the government’s desire to better understand the industry through organizing industry-leve discourses [19]. In these efforts, government cannot rely on industry associations for better information that are seen as weak [25].

## Second period (2007–2009): A new business model is shaping up

A new business model is shaping up in this second period. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, wholesalers begin to set up dedicated units for logistical operations [26, Table 6], whereas traditional so-called 3PLs are entering the industry [30]. This change is partly caused by government’s, at that time, current and planned policies such as the intended separation of <sup>fi</sup>nancial accounts of pharmaceutical operations within hospitals [33] and the imposition of a 0% mark-up policy [32]. Both these measures would severely affect the income of hospitals, as they could no longer earn revenues from the sale of drugs. Distributors have responded by adapting their business models. However, this change in orientation exposes some practices inherited from the old distribution system that are now seen as ‘weird’, namely the role o distributors as banks [27, 28, 29]. Because distributors are still mostly state-owned enterprises, they usually have access to loans by banks, whereas hospitals do not. As a consequence, hospitals traditionally relied on distributors for liquidity by deferring payments to distributors. In the same vein, the ability to ‘trace forward’ [31] is a remnant of the old distribution system, described earlier.

As the new, logistics-based business model is emerging, distributors begin to invest in logistica equipment and logistics-related IT [34, 35]. However, IT investments in the government-imposed e-commerce platforms did not, according to distributors, improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of logistical processes [36] because such systems prevent deeper systems integration between distributors and hospitals [37, 38]. Although such systems are nevertheless being used to support transactions be tween distributors and hospitals [45], distributors show increased activity and intention to directly connect their systems to those of hospitals [41, 43, 44]. Increasing degrees of forward integration [42] may also result from efforts to streamline logistical processes along the supply chain.

Competition among distributors is characterized by a heavy focus on relationships with hospitals [46] and highly variable (‘immature’) business processes [47], Thus, competition is not vet exerting its unifying and trimming forces that would be typical for an industry in the shake-out phase.

Regarding internal systems, larger distributors are moving towards more integrated and automated operations [50, 51], whereas many distributors still do not use any type of compute support [49].

Overall, the second period is a period of transition in terms of the industry-level and companylevel life cycle stages, as also shown by our summary indicators. The value of all indicators has increased with individual values being either zero or close to zero (Table 7).

Regarding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of government and regulation, the lack of standards, especially a lack of standard product codes, is increasingly seen as a problem for the industry. Although the industry looks to government for providing such standards [55, 56], government’s ability or willingness to actually set such standards is also doubted [53, 54, 57, 58]. Such inability is rooted in a larger problem of industry oversight because government appears as fragmented [59, 60, 65, 68, 71] and inconsistent [66, 73]. Although government is reluctant to impose standards, it actively and powerfully intervenes in other ways. First, government continuously raises barriers to entry by tightening formal licensing requirements [61, 62, 63, 69, 70]. These efforts are motivated by a desire to reduce the number of companies in the industry, as described earlier. Second, government directly shapes the use and type of distributors’ information systems [74]. Third, companies look to government rather than industry associations for speci<sup>fi</sup>c services and advice [79] as industry associations continue to be seen as weak [76, 80].

## Third period (2010–2012): The industry consolidates

The new logistics-based and IT-based business model is becoming clearly visible. Apart from continuing the trend towards the establishment of dedicated logistics and IT-services divisions [86, 87, 88, 90, 93, Table 8], distributors also offer novel rationales that support this trend, for example by arguing that online drug ordering strengthens the ‘3PL model’ [82] and that it is preferable for distributors to not own drugs [89]. Accordingly, distributors begin to offer fee-based services (rather than earning revenues from a gross margin between purchase and sales prices) [92, 95]. Another indication for the increased importance of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient logistical operations is the continuing trend towards vertical integration [84, 90]. The traditional role of distributors as funding agencies for hospitals continues as well, albeit with a twist. On the one hand, the two functions – funding and provisioning of logistical services – are now clearly separated [85]; on the other hand, the provisioning of IT services comprises support in <sup>fi</sup>nding funds to pay for novel IT systems [91]. Thus, the traditional funding role is being aligned with the new service-based business model. How ever, the new business model is not yet fully formed. On the one hand, distributors are still struggling to offer nationwide integrated services [83]; on the other hand, it is still an open question whether distributors will or even can offer warehouse operation services to hospitals on a broad scale [94].

Regarding the innovation focus of distributors, the role of IT to streamline logistical operations is becoming clearer as well [96, 97], which is cautiously suggestive of a shift towards process based innovation. However, how to improve operations is still seen as a problem by the largest distributor [98].

The change in vertical relationships between distributors and hospitals shows the clearest signs of the industry progressing through its life cycle. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, relations between distributors and hospitals become closer and are increasingly based on intense cooperation. Such cooperation takes on the form of adjusting practices such that information and goods <sup>fl</sup>ow more smoothly across organizational boundaries [99, 102, 103, 104, 112, 113, 114, 116, 117, 121, 123, 124, 126, 128, 130, 132, 135]. This assessment of increased vertical cooperation is not contradicted by the fact that, because of their bargaining power, hospitals often succeed in shifting the costs for such cooperation to distributors [117, 130, 132, 135]. Rather, pre-existing asymmetrical power relationships are only brought to the fore when companies engage in closer cooperative relationships. Cooperation does not imply equal sharing of costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. This is also born out by the increased IT consulting and development activities offered by distributors to hospitals, often for free and tailored to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs of a hospital [100, 101, 105, 106, 107, 108, 109, 115, 118, 129, 133, 134, 139, 140]. In offering such services, distributors have various motives, including hopes to increase future, rather than current, sales with a hospital based on an expectation of reciprocity [127, 133, 134, 141], to reduce logistics-related costs [106], and to affect industry structure by raising entry barriers [125]. In some cases, vertical cooperation extends right into the premises of hospitals when distributors take over the management of a hospital’s warehouse [110, 111, 119, 137, 138] or the construction of warehouses [118]. Overall, relations between distributors and hospitals are long term rather than arms’ length [131, 136].

Pre-existing relations occasionally seem to override results of formal tendering processes [122], which may be a remnant of the old business model or a more general characteristic of ‘relationship-based e-commerce’ (Martinsons, 2008). Further support for this latter interpretation comes from the observation that personal relationships built by distributors often cause political in<sup>fi</sup>ghting in hospitals [120] and that B2B platforms built by distributors are viewed as gifts intended to support personal relationships [133]. However, overall, the available evidence clearly points towards much stronger vertical cooperation based on integrated information systems and processes across organizational boundaries.

An important element in such vertical cooperation are the newly created B2B platforms [97]. These are mostly built by distributors – rather than third parties – [93] and given to hospitals, mostly free of charge [133, 134]. In contrast to the third-party e-commerce systems, these pri vately built platforms are increasingly and deeply linked into internal systems and operations [124, 160, 172, 190, 193, 194, 196], even though such integration is often dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and expen sive because there are no standards available for the task [102, 116, 117, 121, 123, 126, 130, 135]. Such integration also expands the functional scope of the B2B platforms, originally limited to order processing, by adding automatic barcode recognition functions [99] and connecting the platforms to internal inventory management systems [194] so that distributors can estimate the purchasing demand of hospitals [124].

Regarding horizontal relations, there are some indications pointing towards increased efforts of cooperation among competitors. Some instances of direct cooperation exist, are planned or are at least considered possible [145, 147, 149, 150, 152], albeit not always successful and generally mediated by hospitals [150, 151]. More importantly, indirect forms of cooperation have emerged such as opening the privately built B2B platforms to competitors [142, 153, 159, 160, 161, 162] to the extent that distributors undertake special efforts to make it easier for competitors to link to their platform [154, 158] even though it seems that, occasionally, these platforms are also used to increase a competitor’s costs [155]. There are two mechanisms that facilitate such indirect horizontal cooperation. On the one hand, distributors need to cooperate in order to offer nationwide service [143], as was the case in the earlier periods. This mechanism is indicative of the industry being in its early stages of development, as argued earlier. On the other hand, distributors are increasingly confronting situations in which they are forced to use the B2B platforms built by competitors; as this applies to all large distributors, the principle of reciprocity is seen to facilitate cooperation [156]; this is indicative of a more consolidated industry and thus of a more advanced life cycle stage. However, there are also forces that prevent effective horizontal cooperation, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally intense price competition resulting from high substitutability of services [146, 148], which points to the industry still being in its late shake-out stage. This assessment is corroborated by the characterization of industry members as being unwilling to share information required for joint projects [144] and an inability to jointly operate B2B platforms [157]. Yet, the focus of competition is shifting: while the privately built B2B platforms where initially seen as competitive weapons, effectively precluding cooperation in their construction and management, distributors are now more willing to cooperate because competition increasingly focuses on the quality of logistical services [162].

The application portfolio of distributors, according to our data, has regressed on the companylevel learning cycle. Whereas we found some, albeit very limited, evidence of distributors having entered the network era, most descriptive codes extracted from our interview material and work shop transcript indicate that distributors are not yet there [163, 164, 166, 168, 169, 170, 172]. For example, operations are still largely manual [170], or, if supported by computers, data exchange between computers is still manual [163, 169]. Also, the B2B platforms are simple systems mostly limited to information publishing (rather than processing) functions [164] that do not affect internal processes [166]. We will discuss this surprising result in more detail later.

Regarding the application portfolio of hospitals, we <sup>fi</sup>nd slightly more descriptive codes pointing to network era membership than those that are indicative of hospitals not yet having entered this era. For example, large hospitals have begun to integrate previously separate systems internally, mostly by implementing ERP systems [173. 174. 178, 181, 182, 185. 186, 197, 199, 201, 204, 206] although instances of external systems integration are also noticeable [175, 193, 196, 205]. However, there are also signs of non-integrated internal [183, 187, 188, 189, 191, 198, 207] and externa [190, 200] systems. A major weakness is seen in the management of hospital systems and hospi tals in general [176, 179, 192, 202]. A lack of internal and external systems integration, however, is also related to the absence of data standards [184] and data security concerns [195].

Overall, the evidence available regarding IT use on the industry level overwhelmingly points to the industry moving towards the stable, third stage, whereas the much more limited data available on the company level show an ambiguous picture. Although hospitals have made some progress towards more internally and externally integrated systems, the evidence available for ITuse levels in distributor companies suggests that these <sup>fi</sup>rms have actually regressed when compared with the situation in the second period. This characterization is in line with the development of our summary indicator values, which all increased, except those concerning the IT application portfolio of distributors (Table 9).

Regarding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of government on the development of required IT standards, the situation has not much changed as compared with the previous two periods. Government is still seen as in effective, incapable or unwilling to establish such standards [211, 212, 213, 214, 217], whereas the industry looks to government to actually provide such standards because the perceived ability of the industry to develop such standards on its own is also seen as de<sup>fi</sup>cient [216, 218, 219]. Government’s efforts to consolidate the industry are having mixed results. On the one hand, it is believed that government can reach its goal of creating three large national distributors [227]; on the other hand, fragmentation of government [223] and local protectionism are believed to stand in the way of successful industry consolidation [221, 224, 229, 230] and consistent policy formula tion [222]. Thus, the ambivalent picture of government’s clout over the industry that was apparent in the <sup>fi</sup>rst two periods continues to dominate in the third period.

Likewise, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of government on IT use is seen as rather critical. On the one hand, government pushes the adoption of third-party B2B platforms [232, 234]. This is seen to have negative effects such as crowding out similar industry-based initiatives [233], preventing IOIS integration [237] and adopting tested solutions because of monopoly concerns [236]. Also, gov ernment seems to lack the resources and the knowledge to actually build B2B platforms [235], and internal fragmentation prevents the integration of these platforms on a national scale [231].

Finally, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of industry associations continues to be rather limited. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it is not believed that industry associations will positively affect the development of much needed IT standards [240], whereas, surprisingly, industry associations are believed to possibly affect in dustry structure [239]. This may be related to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c history and structure of industry asso ciations in China, which are seen as closely related to government [238], albeit with much less clout over the industry [241]. They also seem to inherit the fragmentation of government [242], which hampers their ability to act collectively.

We have also extracted a single descriptive code that directly pertains to the possible in<sup>fl</sup>uence of national culture on IT use [243]. This statement re<sup>fl</sup>ects sentiments often voiced in the literature, according to which Chinese people are not used to following rules that hinder adoption of IT (Hsiao, 2001; Martinsons, 2004).

## The process of information infrastructure emergence was driven by top–down as well as bottom–up forces

The emerging information infrastructure resulted from two types of activities, which we characterize as top–down and bottom–up forces. Regarding top–down forces, government relied on enforced use of intra-organizational and inter-organizational information systems as a regulatory device. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it required that distributors and hospitals use third-party e-commerce platforms for transmitting orders; moreover, use of intra-organizational information systems became part of licensing requirements for distributors. With these measures, government attempted to increase transparency in the dealings between distributors and hospitals as well as to force inef<sup>fi</sup>cient distributors out of the market in order to modernize and consolidate the industry. Regarding bottom–up forces, distributors began to build B2B platforms to support data exchange with hospitals and helped hospitals connect their internal systems to these platforms. Moreover, they also supported hospitals in their efforts to integrate systems internally, sometimes to the extent that they adapted and con<sup>fi</sup>gured advanced business applications such as ERP systems for their hospital clients. These efforts were motivated by both concerns over the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of supply chain operations and a desire to build more cooperative relationships with hospitals.

## Top–down and bottom–up forces resulted in two types of systems – third-party e-commerce platforms and privately built B2B platforms – that were used in parallel to one another

Although the third-party e-commerce platforms continued to be used in a manual fashion – with hospitals uploading order information and distributors downloading such data without having the possibility to con<sup>fi</sup>rm or change order information – the B2B platforms built by distributors saw some integration with internal systems. For example, data dictionaries were maintained to facilitate the automatic mapping of proprietary product codes used by hospitals onto the codes used by distributors. Moreover, use of these platforms goes beyond simple transmission of order data by, e.g., giving distributors access to inventory data in hospital ERP systems. This difference in use resulted from three factors. On the one hand, third-party e-commerce platforms were perceived as in<sup>fl</sup>exible because the providers lacked the incentives to respond to user requirements, as their mandate to build and operate these platforms was given by government rather than by industry members. On the other hand, industry was mistrustful of the third-party operators, even though their technical capabilities were recognized as superior, because com panies feared that the third-party operators might establish a monopoly position. Finally, the B2B platforms allowed distributors to maintain and strengthen existing personal relationships, whereas the third-party e-commerce platforms would have enforced an arms’ length relationship between distributors and hospitals.<sup>2</sup>

## The activities of companies to build B2B platforms – the bottom–up forces – were contingent on the industry progressing in its life cycle

The creation of privately built B2B platforms coincided with the shift towards the new logistics based and IT-based business model of distributors, the development of close and cooperative relationships between distributors and hospitals, and cautious steps towards cooperation with competitors, in short, with the industry progressing towards a more mature stage in terms o the industry life cycle model. In observing this development, it is impossible to establish a defin: itive causal direction of in<sup>fl</sup>uence between IIIS emergence and industry life cycle progression. For example, a core element of the increasingly cooperative relations between distributors and hospitals consisted of distributors helping hospitals to integrate internal systems and to build for them the B2B platforms to which the hospitals’ internal systems could be connected. At the same time, vertical cooperation went much further than offering such IT-related services, for example by helping hospitals to streamline their logistical operations or even by taking over hospital warehouse operations. Thus, information systems were part of a much larger push towards closer relationships, while development of these systems has also helped open doors and build the data basis on which more advanced logistical services could be offered. Therefore, neither did building these systems precede – chronologically or logically – the establishment of closer vertical relationships nor did it follow on the establishment of such closer links Rather, these two aspects of the process unfolded hand in hand with one another. This change in vertical relationships also manifested itself in a new business model that emphasized the pro visioning of services and de-emphasized the distributors’ role as dealers. Again, the B2B plat forms were partly seen as a product offered to clients, partly as an enabler for more advanced services such as logistics consulting. Finally, the development of cautiously cooperative relations among competitors was, on the one hand, catalyzed by hospitals that demanded that competing distributors cooperate in the building and operation of B2B platforms; on the other hand, increasing consolidation in the industry confronted distributors with a situation in which competitors would be able to retaliate if they used B2B platforms to make life dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for one an other, i.e., a situation characterized by strategic interdependence. Therefore, they undertook to facilitate the linking of competitors’ systems to platforms built by themselves in the expectation that they will encounter situations in which they might bene<sup>fi</sup>t from similar behaviour by thei competitors. Thus, again, the structural change on the industry level facilitated the cooperative development of B2B platforms, while building such platforms also fostered a culture of cooper ation among competitors.

## The activities of government to impose IOIS on the industry – the top–down forces – began well before the industry had reached a mature stage and before companies had entered the network era

The third-party e-commerce platforms were established via government mandate in 2004, a the beginning of our period of observation. Their establishment followed on some experimentation in other provinces, which reach back to the early 1990s. At that time, the industry was still characterized by the multi-tiered structure inherited from the period of central economic planning. Distributors acted mostly as dealers and hospitals maintained arms’ length relations with distributors, aiming to buy high-value drugs cheaply and dealing with a large number of wholesaler–distributors with narrow product ranges and limited service offerings. Distributors competed <sup>fi</sup>ercely, mostly on price, and did not even consider the possibility or necessity of cooperating among each other. Internally, most distributors operated on a manual basis, i.e., did not use any kind of computerized information system, whereas hospitals used information systems for the management of patients regarding medication and other treatment related data, so-called hospital information systems (HIS). They also used systems for warehouse manage ment and logistical operations; however, such systems were not integrated with their HIS. As a result, both distributors and hospitals did not perceive the mandated use of third-party e-commerce platforms to be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to them and attempted to minimize their use.

## Intra-organizational systems integration seemed to lag behind external systems integration

The available evidence regarding internal systems development and integration points towards such development trailing external integration efforts. Whereas hospitals are well on their way to integrate previously separate ERP systems and HIS, distributors are struggling to standard ize and integrate systems internally. This may be related to the much larger geographical scope that distributors typically have to cope with. Alternatively, our data may paint a skewed picture because the development of internal systems integration was only addressed as a side topic in our interviews and discussions. Later, we will offer a third possible explanation.

## DISCUSSION

Based on this high-level descriptive summary of our case, we now come back to our research question: How do IIIS emerge? We start by addressing the question of whether and when an IIIS emerged within our period of observation.

There are two candidate systems around which an IIIS could have possibly emerged: the government imposed third-party e-commerce systems and the B2B platforms built by distributors. As shown by <sup>fi</sup>nding #2, both were used in parallel, with minimal integration be tween them. Although both are used by several and different kinds of user groups (distributor and hospital users) and both are socio-technical systems, use of the third-party systems is regulated by government so that the identity of their users is pre-determined to some extent. However, government may involve further user groups in the future, which would strengthen their character as information infrastructures. Yet, it is only the privately built B2B platforms that meet two further elements of our de<sup>fi</sup>nition: continuous evolution and recursive structure. Whereas the third-party e-commerce systems were limited to the original functions of bidding and order transmission, the functional scope of the B2B platforms continued to evolve throughout their short existence within the third period, e.g., by adding barcode reading capabilities and connecting the platforms to internal inventory management systems. Moreover, only the B2B platforms were integrated with internal information systems at all, whereas the third-party ecommerce systems continued to be used in a manual fashion throughout the period of observation. Thus, only the B2B platforms developed a recursive structure, i.e., are decomposable into smaller systems that constitute the larger system, which is a de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristics of information infrastructures according to our de<sup>fi</sup>nition. We therefore conclude that an IIIS emerged in the third period when the B2B platforms were created and integrated with internal IS of vertically related organizations as well as opened horizontally to competitors.

Regarding our research question, we now propose that the process of IIIS emergence is intricately linked to the process of industry consolidation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, these two processes mutually reinforce and drive each other. We offer two arguments in support of this claim. First, as described by <sup>fi</sup>nding #3, elements of the nascent information infrastructure, B2B platforms connected to interna information systems of distributors and hospitals, emerged as the structure of the industry, and modes of cooperation and competition within the industry changed to become more consolidated and integrated. In turn, as the industry consolidated, more opportunities for vertical and horizonta IT-based integration arose, while internal and external systems integration also became a major force in the process of industry consolidation and modernization.

Second, we observe that a strong push by government to mandate development of core components of a future IIIS failed in the sense that these components – the third-party e-commerce systems – were sidestepped by industry members. As described by <sup>fi</sup>nding #4, <sup>fi</sup>rms did not perceive a need to cooperate vertically or horizontally when these systems were introduced. Consequently, <sup>fi</sup>rms did not see any bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in integrating these systems into their existing, company-wide information infrastructures either. We therefore propose that, on the industry level, the conditions were not in place to foster the integration of these systems into existing information infrastructures. When that situation changed, private initiative became the main motor of building similar but competing systems. As described by <sup>fi</sup>nding #2, the two types of systems – government mandated e-commerce systems and privately built B2B platforms – were used in parallel. Although we do not want to further explore the reasons for that phenomenon – we have studied this in a separate paper, see Guo et al. (2013) – the fate of the government-imposed systems shows that, to become part of an IIIS, a more consolidated industry structure must prevail that fosters higher degrees of vertical and horizontal cooperation. Once the industry begins to consolidate, a mutually reinforcing mechanism is set in motion such that IIIS emergence is driven by industry consolidation, while the emerging information infrastructure also contributes to ongoing industry consolidation.

What does this mean with regard to our multi-level framework for explaining the emergence of IIIS? That framework postulates that, in order for IIIS to emerge, two conditions must exist: companies must have mastered network era technologies and industries must have reached the stable, mature stage of their life cycle. In contrast, our case study suggests that (1) companies have not yet mastered network era technologies, as described by <sup>fi</sup>nding #5, and (2) the industry had not <sup>fi</sup>rst entered the mature stable stage before companies undertook to build an IIIS. Thus, our model fails to explain our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and, consequently, would not accurately predict the process of IIIS emergence.

In what follows, we will discuss the possibility and desirability of modifying our model to more accurately account for our case <sup>fi</sup>ndings. We begin with the postulated relationship between IIIS emergence and industry development. Whereas our model states that industry consolidation is a precondition for IIIS emergence, we found that IIIS and industries co-evolve in the sense that both development processes reinforce one another. IIIS become a tool of industry consolidation as much as they are driven by industry consolidation. However, we believe that our model is still useful with regard to the claim that IIIS emergence and industry development are tightly linked but in a way different from our original proposition. That proposition viewed IIIS emergence as driven by a design logic wherein the IIIS is viewed primarily as an outcome of constructive activity. In contrast, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that IIIS become a force in the broader development of industry consolidation as well. They are a product of design activity as much as they are a driver of business strategy. Thus, while retaining the close link between IIIS emergence and industry development postulated by the original model, our revised model proposes that IIIS develop as the industry consolidates into a stable stage while that consolidation process is reinforced and possibly quickened by IIIS emergence.

Our case <sup>fi</sup>ndings also do not support the hypothesized link between IIIS emergence and company-wide ITadoption and absorption. Neither did the mastery of network era technologies on the company level precede IIIS emergence nor did it develop in lock-step with IIIS emergence. Instead, our data suggest that company-level learning with regard to network era technologies trailed the development of B2B platforms. The logic of the original proposition was that IIIS grow from the inside out; i.e., internal systems integration proceeds until it reaches organi zational boundaries from where the links extend towards other organizations to form IOIS which, by further extending their reach and scope, evolve into IIIS. If our <sup>fi</sup>ndings hold up to empirical scrutiny, they would suggest, in contrast, that internal systems integration may also start at the organizational periphery. But how could one account for this ‘reversed’ development process? One interesting proposition can be derived from our multi-level model, albeit in a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed form. Whereas that model, in its original formulation, saw a causal link pointing from the company to the industry level, by reversing the direction of that causal link, an interesting novel explanation comes to the fore. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the original model postulated that IIIS emerge on the industry level because companies extend their internal systems integration towards other organizations. For this to happen, the industry must have reached a stable stage. As discussed ear lier, that process is much more complex, involving IIIS not only as outcomes of design activities by companies but as active ‘players’ in the process of industry consolidation. Thus, external systems integration was seen largely as a result of company-level development activities with industry development playing the role of a passive enabler. However, industry development may have a much stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on company-level ITadoption and absorption. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, as the industry consolidates, the environment of companies becomes instable and even turbu lent. Many companies are exiting the industry, many are acquired by others or many merge to quickly reach a critical size needed for survival. Such conditions do not seem to be conducive to internal systems integration. For example, when companies merge, the degree of internal systems integration will likely drop as the newly merged companies typically will have different systems, data structures, data codes and so on. At the same time, the conditions for making large investment decisions are also unfavourable, as the quickly changing industry landscape makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to predict bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs of IT investments. Consequently, the degree of internal systems integration may drop as the industry consolidates, and industry development processes may become a main driving or rather dragging force for IT investment decisions. As the <sup>fi</sup>rst elements of a future IIIS appear, as described earlier, these may become the crystalli zation points around which systems integration proceeds. Thus, systems integration would be driven from the outside rather than from the inside, as seen from a company perspective. However, our data are far too patchy to support this conclusion. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we could rely on only one of the four indicators suggested by the Stages Hypothesis – the structure of the application portfolio – to construct a narrative of IT adoption and absorption at the company level because of a lack of suf<sup>fi</sup>cient data for the other three indicators. Yet, this seems to be an interesting proposition inspired by our case <sup>fi</sup>ndings, which would be exciting to further explore in future research.

Regarding the industry level, our proposed changes to the model are supported by our case <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Regarding the company level, our data are much less conclusive, and one likely explanation for our case <sup>fi</sup>ndings is simply that our data are wrong. Therefore, in what follows, we will focus on the industry level because we claim that our proposed modi<sup>fi</sup>ed model is well supported by our case <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Yet, we believe that our proposed changes to the model on the company level have a high degree of plausibility and therefore are worthwhile to be explored in future research.

To strengthen our interpretation regarding IIIS emergence, we want to explore possible rival explanations of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The obvious candidate for a rival explanation is that the processes we have observed are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the context in which we have studied IIIS emergence, which is characterized by heavy government involvement and a possibly peculiar national culture. Several studies have emphasized the leading role played by governments with regard to adoption of e-business technologies in Asian countries (Gibbs et al., 2003; Thatcher & Foster, 2003; Sarkar & El Sawy, 2003; Xu et al., 2004; see also the literature review in the Appendix on IT use and adoption in China). At the same time, several authors claim that national cultural characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption (Hempel & Kwong, 2001; van Everdingen & Waarts, 2003; Manske & Moon, 2003; Tan & Ouyang, 2004; Bagchi et al., 2004; Tan et al., 2007; Martinsons & Davison, 2007; Ge & Voß, 2009) as well as the type of e-business systems (Hsiao, 2001; Martinsons, 2004; Marble & Lu, 2007; Westrup & Liu, 2008; Martinsons, 2008) in countries in which the Chinese culture is dominant. Based on this literature, our case <sup>fi</sup>ndings might be explained in the following way. Although the government-imposed e-commerce sys tems are accepted by industry, albeit reluctantly, government policies were not very effective possibly because of the large size of the country, which would require more local adaptation. Actually, in another study, we found that the fate of government-imposed e-commerce systems varied signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly across provinces as did the design of these systems (Li & Reimers, 2009). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the attempt to use the e-commerce systems as a lever to <sup>fi</sup>ght corruption in the industry may have prevented more widespread and enthusiastic use. Cases of successful government intervention from Singapore and Taiwan (Sutanto et al., 2009; Thatcher & Foster, 2003; Chen, 2003; Parayil & Yeo, 2005) suggest that the failure of government-driven development of an IIIS in our case may be related to the particularities of government policies rather than to their timing, as would be suggested by our interpretation given earlier. Moreover, the emergence of parallel systems – the privately built B2B platforms – may be seen as a response to the ill-conceived government initiative, one that preserved existing cultural norms. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, the B2B platforms allowed distributors to maintain valued social relationships (Hsiao, 2001; Martinsons, 2004), and the way these systems were built re<sup>fl</sup>ects national cultural traits such as reliance on mediators (Martinsons, 2002) – cooperation among competing distributors was mediated by hospitals – and expectations of reciprocity (Hwang, 1987; Dunning & Kim, 2007; Davison et al., 2013) – distributors gave B2B platforms as a ‘gift’ to hospitals hoping to receive favourable treatment in the future, while distributors also made it easy for competitors to connect to their B2B platforms expecting similar treatment by their competitors. Consequently, the development of the B2B platforms as the core element of an emerging IIIS could also be explained by those situational factors that are generally described under the summary term of national culture.

Although we believe that this explanation is convincing and have actually elaborated a similar argument in a related study (Guo et al., 2013), we note that this explanation becomes a riva explanation only when posed in a strong form that claims that the relationship of IIIS emergence with the process of industry development is coincidental. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, such a strong form would claim that (1) the efforts of government to build an IIIS would have failed to the same extent had they been undertaken several years later when, according to our explanation, the conditions for IIIS emergence were in place, and (2) the B2B platforms built privately would have succeeded even when introduced a couple of years earlier when, according to our explanation, the conditions for IIIS emergence were not yet in place. Otherwise, i.e., in a weaker formulation, this explanation can be seen as complementary to our explanation offered earlier. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, (1) the timing of the government initiative as well as the design of the particular policies may have been ill-conceived, and (2) the nature of the privately built B2B platforms may re<sup>fl</sup>ect characteristics of national culture, while their successful introduction also depended on related changes on the industry level, namely a progression towards more consolidation and cooperative modes of interaction among both vertically and horizontally related organizations in the industry.

The strong variant of that alternative explanation, i.e., a true rival explanation, successfully addresses <sup>fi</sup>ndings #1 and #2 – the existence of two types of activities contributing to an emerging information infrastructure, namely by companies and government, which resulted in two types of systems that co-existed but where not integrated. Moreover, this explanation would consider <sup>fi</sup>nding #4 to be irrelevant because the timing of the government initiative was not important. However, such a strong formulation would be in con<sup>fl</sup>ict with <sup>fi</sup>nding #3, which states that the processes of IIIS emergence and industry development did not just coincide but mutually reinforced each other. We therefore propose that a strong formulation of the alternative explanation is not supported by our case, whereas a weak form can be seen to be complementary to the life cycle-based explanation offered earlier. Such an alternative explanation would mainly address the type or design of the emerging infrastruc ture as characterized by strengthening existing relationships and so forth, whereas the life cycle-based approach offered in the present paper mainly addresses the process of IIIS emergence.

A further rival explanation of our case <sup>fi</sup>ndings would emphasize the role of regulation in the process of IIIS emergence. Some authors suggest that, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in China, a stable regulatory environment is a necessary precondition for IIIS emergence (Oxley & Yeung, 2001; Martinsons, 2008; Chang et al., 2008). Our case may be seen to add weight to this claim. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it might be argued that, as the regulatory environment stabilized, efforts to build core components of an IIIS <sup>fi</sup>nally succeeded. However, regulatory activity occurring over the period of observation does not show a pattern of increasing stabilization. In contrast, government continued to vary licensing requirements and rules regarding the drug procurement process. Moreover, the possibly strongest effect of a more stable regulatory environment with regard to IIIS would be expected to be related to standards for product codes. However, in spite of increasing calls for a government-de<sup>fi</sup>ned standard for product codes by industry, government continued to avoid any such action. We therefore propose that regulatory stability does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly contribute to the explanation of our case <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

We have chosen China as a suitable context for studying IIIS emergence because industrial development processes and the process of IT adoption and absorption are much compressed because of China’s rapid economic development so that the potentially slow process of IIIS emergence is more easily studied empirically. This. however. also establishes a limit to the reach of our theoretical conclusions. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings cannot be usefully applied to contexts where IIIS emerge in mature industries. In such cases, other than life cycle-based approaches should be explored, such as evolutionary, dialectical and teleological theories of organizational change (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995). In fact, evolutionary and dialectical theories of IIIS emergence were found to be promising for a case of IIIS emergence in the Australian pharmaceutical distribution industry (Reimers et al., 2013a). Thus, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are useful only for cases in which industries have not yet begun to consolidate. Such industries, however, are plenty, also in developed countries. As a case in point, the healthcare industry currently <sup>fi</sup>nds itself to be the target of much government-sponsored IT-based modernization efforts. If our <sup>fi</sup>ndings hold up to further empirical scrutiny, the implications for such government-driven efforts would be far reaching. For example, the many large-scale efforts to sponsor the development of IIIS for sharing medical patient data to be observed in many Western countries (Taylor et al., 2005; Greenhalgh et al., 2008; e-Business W@tch, 2008; Hanseth et al., 2012b; Currie, 2012) may be similarly ill-conceived as was the effort to impose the third-party e-commerce systems in the pharmaceutical distribution industry in China, simply because the process of industry consolidation in the healthcare sector may not yet have set in. In many countries, the healthcare sector is similarly fragmented as was the pharmaceutical distribution industry at the beginning of our period of observation. Small clinics, health centres and GP practices still operate in an environment in which vertical and horizontal cooperative links are not perceived to be important. Actors rely on either government regulation or patients’ own efforts to ensure that medical data are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently integrated across various practices and organizations. In many cases, such hopes may turn out to be unfounded, as, increasingly, <sup>fi</sup>ndings are available that point to the huge and unaddressed risks through uncoordinated medication practices in many Western countries (Keers et al., 2013). However, programmes that use monetary incentives, as in the USA (Kellermann & Jones, 2013), or regulatory means, as, e.g., in the UK (Currie, 2012), are unlikely to succeed in creating an integrated IIIS for medication data if the healthcare sector is as fragmented as it seems to be and if our <sup>fi</sup>ndings hold up to further empirical scrutiny. We therefore propose that such increased scrutiny is well worth the effort and encourage further research to test and possibly modify or even reject our propositions.

A further possible limitation resulting from our choice of research site – China – concerns the availability of required IT products and services, i.e., the supply side rather than the use or demand side with regard to IT products and service. For example, internal and external systems integration may have been hampered by a lack of availability of adequate ERP implementation consultants and EDI specialists. In an earlier application of our multi-level approach (Reimers et al., 2004), we have concluded that the IT provider industry in China generally was able to offer network era products and services. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is corroborated by other studies reported in the literature (Hempel & Kwong, 2001; Tan & Ouyang, 2004; Martinsons, 2004; Burrows et al., 2005). We therefore submit that the IT provider industry did not represent a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bottleneck for IIIS emergence.

## CONCLUSIONS

Using a multi-level life cycle-based model of the emergence of IIIS, we have analyzed a case of IIIS emergence in the Chinese pharmaceutical distribution industry for which longitudinal data covering a period of 9 years were collected through a series of interviews, company visits, join meetings and workshops with industry participants. We found that IIIS emergence proceeds in lock-step with industry consolidation such that both processes mutually reinforce each other. Moreover, we developed a new proposition that IIIS grow from the organizational periphery rather than from the organizational centre under conditions of environmental turbulence characteristic of an industry in a transitory state. Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we conclude that further empirical scrutiny to test our <sup>fi</sup>ndings is warranted, as there would be quite far reaching implications for government policies aimed at developing IIIS.

We have contributed to the literature by empirically establishing a link between IIIS emergence and industry development. Although a few authors have called for industry-leve studies of IOIS, to the best of our knowledge, linking development of IOIS or IIIS to the industry life cycle has not yet been attempted. Although it turns out that our initial model depicted far too simple a link between these two development processes, it was helpful in focussing ou empirical work on that link. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have theoretical as well as practical implications. Theoretically, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings throw a new light on the issue of IT growth. Traditionally, IT growth is viewed as a process that begins at the centre of organizations and then gradually extends towards their periphery. As IT growth transcends organizational boundaries, IOIS and, by extension, IIIS emerge. Our case suggests an exciting alternative view, namely that information systems grow from the organizational periphery. Although that would seem counter-intuitive when viewed from an organizational perspective, adopting an industry-level perspective suggests a plausible mechanism that would account for such a process for the existence of which we found some empirical hints. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, industry consolidation increases environmental turbulence as seen from an organizational perspective, which may hamper or even upset intra-organizational information systems integration efforts. At the same time, industry consolidation proceeds hand in hand with the emergence of an IIIS. Consequently, the centre of information systems integration may move to the organizational periphery. We are not suggesting that this is a universal phenomenon. However, it might be the case that under speci<sup>fi</sup>c conditions, for example when IT adoption and absorption coincides with industry consolidation, such a pattern dominates. We therefore encourage further research on the relationship between internal and external systems integration with regard to the growth pattern of organizational IT.

In practical terms, we have contributed to the literature by proposing that conscious efforts to establish IIIS, as currently observed in the healthcare sector in both emerging economies and developed countries. should pay more attention to development dynamics on the industry level. Efforts to push or encourage IT adoption and absorption at the organizational level may fail to produce integrated IIIS, even if successful at the organizational level. A recent evaluation of the large-scale US programme to promote IT adoption in the healthcare sector (Kellermann & Jones, 2013) found that the expected savings were not achieved, largely because systems were not interoperable and processes were not aligned across healthcare organizations. However, the proposed remedies – improve interoperability of healthcare IT systems, change focus to patient centredness and improve ease of use – all operate at the organizational level. If ou <sup>fi</sup>ndings hold up to further empirical scrutiny, such measures are unlikely to succeed unless industry dynamics are also addressed. We therefore encourage further research on the link between IIIS emergence and industry development, a link that we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed empirically in this paper.

## APPENDIX: INTER-ORGANIZATIONAL AND INTRA-ORGANIZATIONAL IS ADOPTION, IMPLEMENTATION AND USE IN THE CHINESE CONTEXT

Over the last decade, a substantial body of knowledge on the development of information infrastructures in Chinese organizations has accumulated in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Lu & Heng, 2008). While this literature has focused on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of national culture on the development of information infrastructures, it has also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed other in<sup>fl</sup>uence factors not or only indirectly related to culture. For our literature analysis, we include all published work that reports empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings re garding implementation and use of large intra-organizational and inter-organizational information systems in a Chinese context. The main type of intra-organizational information systems studied are ERP systems; inter-organizational information systems are referred to as B2B e-commerce, e-business or electronic markets. Together, these systems (intra-organizational as well as inter organizational information systems) are important components of emerging IIIS. We include studies concerning IS use in the Chinese context broadly de<sup>fi</sup>ned as comprising, apart from China, Taiwan and Singapore.

A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of studies report empirical support for the claim that the Chinese culture in<sup>fl</sup>uences – often negatively – both intra-organizational and inter-organizational information systems adoption, implementation and use (Hempel & Kwong, 2001; van Everdingen & Waarts, 2003; Manske & Moon, 2003; Tan & Ouyang, 2004; Bagchi et al., 2004; Tan et al., 2007; Martinsons & Davison, 2007; Ge & Voß, 2009). Some studies argue that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of national culture is contingent upon the state of economic development – more important in developing than in developed countries – (Thatcher & Foster, 2003), the type of system – more important for B2C e-commerce than for B2B e-commerce – (Gibbs et al., 2003), ownership structure of companies (Newman & Zhao, 2008) or the level of IT use – important at the level of individual use but less important at the level of organizational or inter-organizational use – (Martinsons, 2008).

The literature has also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed other relevant national-level, industry-level and organization level in<sup>fl</sup>uence factors that sometimes work in addition to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of national culture and sometimes replace national culture as dominant in<sup>fl</sup>uence factors. These include a stable regulatory environment (Oxley & Yeung, 2001; Martinsons, 2008; Chang et al., 2008), a focus on intermediaries (Martinsons, 2002), global competition (Gibbs et al., 2003; Chen, 2003), heavy government involvement (Thatcher & Foster, 2003; Saxenian, 2003; Sarkar & El Sawy, 2003; Xu et al., 2004), various industry (Tan & Ouyang, 2004) and company characteristics (Zhu et al., 2003; Reimers, 2003; Sarkar & El Sawy, 2003; Xu et al., 2004; Chien et al., 2007), and technical infrastructure (Hempel & Kwong, 2001; Jennex et al., 2004; Martinsons, 2008).

Special attention has been paid to the role of speci<sup>fi</sup>c social relations (Guanxi) with some authors arguing that Western approaches are not suitable for the Chinese context because they do not recognize the importance of Guanxi in business affairs (Hsiao, 2001; Martinsons, 2004) and others arguing that Western systems need to be embedded in a thick layer of social relationships to function in the Chinese context (Marble & Lu, 2007; Westrup & Liu, 2008). The important role of social relations is attributed to a lack of a stable institutional environment rather than to national culture (Martinsons, 2008). In this vein, it has been claimed that Western standard-based approaches are not suitable for the business reality in developing countries (Braa et al., 2007).
