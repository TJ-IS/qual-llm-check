Promoting online learners’ continuance intention: An integrated flow framework

## Abstract

Despite the rapid growth and significant benefits of online learning, retaining students in online courses remains a key challenge. This study empirically tests an integrated model that simultaneously examines factors influencing students’ attainment of flow experience in online learning and the impacts of flow on their online learning continuance intention. The results identified telepresence as the most significant factor influencing students’ flow experience in online learning. Perceived hedonic value played the most important mediating role transmitting the effects of flow on students' continuance intention. The implications of the study for both research and practice are discussed.

Keywords: Continuance intention, Flow experience, Online learning, Perceived hedonic value, Perceived utilitarian value, Satisfaction, Telepresence

## 1. Introduction

Online learning has been a strategic element in many higher education institutions, with significant benefits for key stakeholders, including students, instructors, and the education sector itself [48]. Its popularity is demonstrated by the 36.5% compound annual growth rate of the global online learning market over recent years [122], a market expected to reach US \$230 billion by 2020 [41]. Despite the rapid growth of the market and the significant benefits online learning affords, the retention of students in online courses remains a significant challenge [89], with high dropout rates being a serious concern for both instructors and administrators [113,117]. Prior studies have shown that despite the greater autonomy that online courses provide in many learning aspects, many students report low motivation to continue their online courses [60]. The lack of motivation could be attributed to several factors. Specifically, the mediated virtual environment lacks social cues that may result in feelings of isolation or disconnection [97,117], causing low engagement with the environment. Furthermore, learning in an online environment lacks personal, face-to-face instructor guidance and attention (e.g., eye contact) [87]; consequently, students need to be more independent in keeping track of their learning states and in navigating any challenges that arise during the learning process. These issues may dampen students’ motivation to engage in online learning and may eventually lead them to drop out of online courses [102].

Thus, the challenge for online learning is to create an environment that engages students in ways that will maintain their high involvement, interest, and commitment throughout the duration of their learning, despite the aforementioned issues [23,91,102]. To this end, Csikszentmihalyi’s [20] flow theory, which highlights individuals’ optimal experience in the form of flow or a state in which people are “so focused that it amounts to absolute absorption in an activity” [20, p.1], is of direct relevance. Experiencing such a state of flow may help students overcome the isolated or disconnected feeling in an online learning environment (OLE) [97,117] and motivate them to continue their online learning. For instance, factors important for flow, such as immediate feedback, clear goals on tasks, and a balance between challenge and skill [20], are relevant in ensuring students engagement in an OLE that lacks face-to-face cues from the instructors and other learners and requires independent efforts from learners in navigating the environment and performing the learning task. Indeed, an individual’s optimal flow experience has been found to lead to online users’ continuance intention, as “experiencing flow encourages a person to persist at and return to an activity because of the experiential rewards it promises” [86, p.95]. Hence, cultivating a good flow experience in an OLE may promote learners’ continuance learning intention.

However, a review of empirical studies across various flow studies indicates that there is not a well-formed model of flow that can be applied directly to OLEs to add to our knowledge of what matters in cultivating such an optimal experience [91], particularly in regard to learners’ perception of the technology-mediated environment apart from traditional task-related pre-conditions of flow identified in the literature [23]. Given the challenging issues in current online education and the uniqueness of OLEs, a well-defined model of flow antecedents comprising both task- and technology-related factors to stimulate students to experience flow during online learning is deemed to be of value to instructors and learning management system (LMS) vendors.

In addition, although the importance of flow in promoting individuals’ positive learning experience and continuance intention is well recognized [51], prior research has fallen short in examining the underlying theoretical mechanisms that transform flow experience into an intention to continue with online learning. These mediating variables may be part of the causal chain, such that learners’ flow experience obtained from online learning generates all or part of its effect on learners’ continuance intention by first changing the mediating variables [120]. By integrating flow with a series of related consequential variables in one collective model, we may better understand both the impact of flow experience on learners' continuance intention as well as the specific path through which the impact is transmitted. Furthermore, through the identification of the specific path, we may also be able to understand the relative importance of key components that enhance the learners' overall online learning experience. Specifically, experiencing flow when performing an activity in an online environment has been suggested to result in positive value evaluations [54], and because online learning encompasses both utilitarian (learning desired knowledge) and hedonic value (obtaining enjoyment) [123], we expect that in this context, flow may lead to these two value perceptions, which in turn enhance learners’ satisfaction and continuance intention.

The objectives of this study are thus twofold. First, we seek to examine the key antecedents of flow in online learning by including not only Csikszentmihalyi's widely recognized flow pre-conditions, but also learners’ perception of the OLE to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of what contributes to flow experience in this context. Second, we aim to test the intermediate links between flow and online learning continuance intention in one collective model. Given the vast resources and expenses higher education providers devote to improving online learning quality, such understanding carries not only considerable theoretical but practical importance as well.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. First, the relevant theoretical perspectives, including flow concept and online learning characteristics, theory of telepresence, and the value-satisfaction-continuance intention (V-SAT-CI) model, are discussed. Next, we outline our research model and present our research hypotheses. This is followed by a description of the research method as well as the results of our data analysis. Finally, we conclude with a discussion focusing on the interpretation of the results as well as an examination of the theoretical and practical implications of the study.

## 2. Background and literature review

2.1. The Importance of flow experience in online learning continuance intention

Csikszentmihalyi [20] theorized how and when 'optimal experience' or a state of flow can occur. The basic premise is that if any activity or task is interesting and not difficult enough to cause frustration, there is a high likelihood of one’s total engagement. Csikszentmihalyi therefore suggests flow as the state that occurs when an individual is partaking in an activity for its own sake; an activity so satisfying that individuals want to repeat the activity continually [23]. Such an enjoyable state is mainly accompanied by an order of consciousness in which the individual experiences complete concentration, feelings of control, loss of self-consciousness, and a distorted sense of time [20]. Research informs us that individuals experiencing flow are more likely to be motivated to carry out further activities and attain positive outcomes [35]. Csikszentmihalyi identified nine flow dimensions: (1) clear goals, (2) immediate feedback, (3) balance between challenge and skill, (4) concentration on the task at hand, (5) loss of self-consciousness, (6) sense of control, (7) time distortion, (8) merging of action and awareness, and (9) an experience that becomes autotelic [23]. Among these dimensions, the first three are widely recognized as three essential pre-conditions for flow to occur [23]. Csikszentmihalyi’s work has influenced a large number of studies in education, marketing, psychology, and sport [106]. The concept of optimal experience has been found to be remarkably robust and theoretically useful, and it has been validated in numerous studies on various activities, such as shopping, playing instruments, rock climbing, and, in particular, learning [20,50].

Csikszentmihalyi [23] argues that educational institutions constitute a promising context in which to apply the flow concept, given that students spend considerable time here but are often in a state of boredom or anxiety (i.e., disengagement). In contrast, engaged students find learning to be intrinsically motivating and autotelic, as such a psychological state contains a powerful force to expand learners' energy, curiosity and creativity. He further argues that this type of learning consists of complete involvement with a challenging task, which is an avenue for personal growth, improvement, change, and happiness. Research has also argued that individuals participate in an activity with a much fuller sense of self-initiation if they find the activity to be interesting and engaging [28]. When this occurs, the individuals are likely to experience an optimal learning experience [26]. Research in the field of education [23] shows that such an optimal experience in learning is an important contributor to the learning process, conceptual understanding, satisfaction, and persistence [49,105].

The importance of flow in learning is likely to be particularly pertinent in the context of OLEs. An OLE refers to an environment “where the majority of instruction and interaction is accomplished via online resource, that is readily accessible via the Internet” [100, p.54]. Compared to traditional face-to-face courses, there is no doubt that the advantages that online courses offer to students, instructors, and educational institutions are manifold [44]. However, we should not disregard the challenges that online courses also bring to students’ learning. The shif from passive 'programmed instruction' to an active 'knowledge construction' by the students, which is often accompanied by a lack of structured learning materials, tends to require a higher level of self-discipline [72]. In addition, lacking or insufficient synchronous interpersonal, face-to-face interaction among instructors and

#

students highlights the absence of a ‘learning atmosphere’ and a higher demand on students to resolve on their own any issues that arise. These factors may in turn result in more frustrated communication and negative learning experiences [72]. Lack of a sense of community may also have a negative impact on the students, potentially making them feel lonely and isolated and thus resulting in emotional difficulties and less internal interest in the course. As a result, students may become even more disengaged with their learning tasks; in turn, this may affect their perseverance with respect to self-paced study and may have a negative impact on their level of sustained engagement, learning evaluations, and overall satisfaction [60,102]. Research indicates that the overall dropout rates associated with online courses are generally higher than that of traditional face-to-face courses [117], which can be attributed to a greater lack of engagement in online learning.

Although online learning may be prone to higher dropout rates and lower persistence for various reasons inherent in the environment, technology-mediated online learning has the potential to provide the tools and learning activities to promote optimal flow experience in the learning process [102,105]. Therefore, researchers have adopted Csikszentmihalyi's flow theory [20] as a guide for the design and implementation of online courses to create a learning environment that stimulates a student’s optimal experience. For instance, by adopting the theory of flow and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) in an e-learning environment, Davis and Wong [24] found that the necessary skills to overcome course-related challenges affect students’ flow experiences, which, in turn, affect their intention to use the online learning system. By synthesizing the expectation-confirmation model, TAM, Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and theory of flow, Lee [69] found that students’ concentration on their learning was positively associated with their continual e-learning intention. Using Csikszentmihalyi’s original flow model, Shin [104] tested a conceptual model of an online learner’s flow experience, composed of flow antecedents, flow experience, and flow consequences. Apart from finding the significant determining effect of online learners’ perceptions of skill and challenge in flow experiences, he also found that flow is a strong predictor of student satisfaction with online learning.

## 2.2. Antecedents and consequences of flow in OLEs

Although prior studies have validated the importance of flow in understanding student online learning continuance intention, there is a lack of a well-formed flow model elucidating what affects flow experience in online learning and what is the path through which students’ online learning continuance intention is formed. In this regard, our research first attempts to develop a more comprehensive flow model by considering learner perception of the OLE. Different from traditional face-to-face learning, online learners need to interact with the technology environment to perform learning activities, which adds to its complexity [35]. If not well-designed, the OLE may distract students from their learning tasks [91]. Thus, it is crucial that students attend to the OLE because such immersion will allow them to focus on the tasks at hand and feel in control of the environment,

#

leaving little attention to consider anything else [85]. In other words, elevating the feeling of telepresence, which refers to “the feeling of being a part of the phenomenal environment created by a medium” such that “the user of the medium considers the items in the mediated environment as unmediated and reacts directly to the items as if they are physically present objects” [62, p.8], in online learning may allow students to interact with the learning activities themselves, fully immersed in the online learning environment. Since telepresence was first introduced by Hoffman and Novak [50] as one of the flow pre-conditions in the Internet environment, by which Internet users’ perceptions about computer-mediated environments may be measured, it has been included in various studies to show that when technology users perceive a sense of telepresence in mediated environments, this can create a perceptual illusion of being present and highly engaged, thus allowing flow to occur [7,34]. However, few studies have incorporated telepresence into their flow models as a key antecedent within OLEs. Thus, this study will add telepresence as one technology-oriented pre-condition of flow beyond the three well-recognized task-related flow pre-conditions.

In addition, although the importance of flow in promoting individuals’ positive learning experience and continuance intention is well recognized [51], little is known about why students stay with online courses after they experience optimal flow. In other words, the mechanism underlying student continuance intention behavior has not yet been revealed. In this study, we argue that students continuing their online learning after experiencing optimal flow is because they perceive value through interacting with online learning systems, including technology, tasks, and other peers [8,67]. It is students’ perceived value of online learning that will affect their satisfaction, which, in turn, enhances their continuance intention [67]. In this sense, perceived value refers to the value that students perceive they receive or experience by using a course’s online learning systems [4]. According to the ‘value as experience’ perspective [8], students’ perceived value of online learning is created at the interaction between students and the activities they are doing online, and students’ desire is not the online course itself but the experiences the online learning can provide [8]. As Holbrook states [53, p.8], “value resides not in the product purchased, not in the brand chosen, not in the object possessed, but rather in the consumption experience derived there from”. Following this logic, we argue that when an optimal experience is fostered during their interaction with online learning activities, students will perceive the value they derive from the online courses. This verisimilitude will lead them to be satisfied with the online course and want to continue with it. Within the information systems (IS) field, continuance intention has often been investigated via the chained impacts of utilitarian value, hedonic value and user satisfaction [6,112]. We argue that the impacts of flow also operate via influencing the different value perceptions and satisfaction in an online learning environment. By viewing students’ perceived value and their satisfaction as intermediating variables between flow experience and continuance intention, and in turn constructing them into one collective model, we are able to examine students judgments simultaneously and compare and contrast their relative importance in relation to their online learning behavior, an interesting but often overlooked area of research.

## 2.3. Telepresence

Telepresence refers to “the feeling of being a part of the phenomenal environment created by a medium” [62, p.8]. While both flow and telepresence have gained increasing attention from IS researchers studying user behavior in Computer Mediated Environments (CMEs), there has been an ongoing debate regarding whether telepresence is an antecedent or a dimension of flow [35,85]. The relationships between flow and telepresence identified in the literature are inconsistent and inconclusive [85]. For instance, Skadberg and Kimmel [107] treated telepresence as the only additional characteristic in the model associated with flow. However, Chen et al. [12] found that telepresence was the only new pre-condition added to help web users achieve optimal experience when surfing the Web. When studying telepresence and flow in the 3D virtual world, Nah et al. [85] found that telepresence led to enjoyment in the 3D virtual world. Animesh et al. [3] also found that flow experience was a key mediator that transmitted the effects of telepresence and social presence on online consumers' intention to purchase. Similar results were reported by Phang and Kankanhalli [93] in a study on the impact of telepresence on flow in virtual worlds.

Although these two constructs share some common components, the key distinction between them lies in the objects that users immerse themselves into. When people are in flow, they are immersed in the activities and tasks they are engaged in. On the other hand, telepresence focuses on the technological characteristics of the virtual environment [119]; thus, the feeling of telepresence is "determined by how the environment assists or distracts the individuals" [101, p.231]. To disambiguate learning tasks students engage in and the technology that they use, researchers [34,51] have called for the inclusion of telepresence as a key determinant factor of flow in human computer interaction (HCI), indicating that telepresence is an essential factor for enabling the person to remain concentrated on the computer-based task [34]. If students are not paying attention to the computer-mediated learning environments, their attention may be elsewhere; thus, they will not be able to fully focus on the tasks at hand, and no meaningful learning will occur [34].

Pearce [91] argues that the artifacts used in online learning should be designed as a transparent layer between the students and the learning tasks, allowing students to interact with the learning activities themselves, fully immersed in the environment. This is the sense in which ‘the feeling of telepresence’ is critical to the fostering of optimal experience; to engage students in their learning tasks online, we must first learn how to create an online learning environment in which students feel immersed. Hoffman and Novak and other researchers describe telepresence as a compelling sense of being present in a mediated virtual environment, thus differentiating it from the time distortion feeling when people are in flow, where they have the perception of time passing rapidly while they are engaged in an activity [50,62,88].

In this study, our focus is on understanding how we can design an OLE that can foster an optimal experience and in turn enhance students’ continuance intention. When an OLE can afford a higher degree of telepresence (i.e., that feeling of being a part of the OLE), students will be better able to concentrate on the learning activity that they are performing, thus promoting an optimal learning experience [25]. Therefore, in line with Hoffman and Novak [50], we included telepresence as a pre-condition of flow in our model, as we believe that the uniqueness of the web-based OLE has made this an important factor to consider [50]. Specifically, we argue that telepresence is an important contributor for flow to occur within an online learning environment.

## 2.4. Value-satisfaction-continuance intention chain

In marketing research, consumers' perceived value is conceptualized as what consumers get for what they give [4]. This includes both perceived utilitarian value, a long focused utilitarian aspect of consumer behavior, and perceived hedonic value, which relates to the hedonic aspects of consumption behaviors [4]. Consumer satisfaction is defined as “an evaluation rendered that the (product) experience was at least as good as it was supposed to be” [56, p.459]. Within a marketing context, continuance intention refers to the consumer’s intention to continue using the artifact to perform a task [6]. Consumers’ perceived value is considered fundamental in understanding how consumers evaluate the consumption experience, which has been identified as an influential factor in determining consumers’ overall satisfaction and behavioral intention [39,115,121]. Satisfaction is widely indicated as one of the critical factors for establishing long-term customer relationships [90]. Therefore, it is not surprising that many studies that focus on technology use in online environments strive to study online users perceived value, satisfaction, and continuance intention within a chain framework [e.g. 29,98].

As the nature of information technologies (IT) has shifted dramatically from purely work purposes to work and/or entertainment, a sizable number of studies have recognized that in addition to utilitarian features, hedonic features of the IS play a significant role in improving user satisfaction and enhancing continuance intention [1,61,66,125]. In the IS literature, Kim et al. [61] proposed and empirically tested a balanced thinking and feeling framework of IS continuance. This framework emphasizes the overall consideration of both cognitive and affective aspects of individuals’ total experience, considering that the users play the dual role of technology user and service consumer in technology use behavior. On one hand, users use technologies for the benefits of utility (cognitive view of consumption), while on the other hand, they consume the services provided by the technologies, pursuing the benefits of feelings (hedonic view of consumption).

Although the flow construct has been widely adopted to explain users’ online continuance behavior, it is unclear how flow links to users’ perceived value, satisfaction, and ultimately their continuance intention simultaneously within OLEs. Given the enjoyable feeling students may experience when they are in flow, it may be that both perceived utilitarian and hedonic value are the intermediating variables in the relationship between flow and satisfaction and then their continuance intention. This is consistent with previous research that highlights the two as having pertinent value in online learning [123].

## 3. Theoretical foundation and hypotheses development

## 3.1. Overview of the research model

Figure 1 depicts our research model, which sets out a nomological net of students’ online learning flow experience. Flow antecedents included in this model are drawn from the theory of flow and the theory of telepresence. We then model the relationships between flow and continuance intention via the value-satisfactioncontinuance intention chain framework.

While flow is seen as a valuable construct for understanding students’ emotional states and subsequent behavioral intentions, it is often considered a too-broad and ill-defined construct due to the numerous ways flow has been conceptualized, operationalized, and measured [66], particularly in CMEs [51]. Prior research on flow [36,51,85] indicates that "there is no consistent approach to modeling relationships between elements of the experience: the same construct could be an indicator, an antecedent, or a consequence of flow, depending on an author's treatment" [95, p.612]. Although many studies make explicit reference to Csikszentmihalyi’s [20] model of flow, they do not utilize all of the nine original dimensions of flow in their models, suggesting that all dimensions need not simultaneously occur [36]. Furthermore, a number of studies have included other variables in their flow models, adding more complexity to its application in the CMEs compared to the offline context wherein the flow concept was originally developed. The differences in the flow variables used have been attributed to the nature of the activities in which the subjects were engaged [57].

In line with prior studies, we adopted Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory as the theoretical base for this study and have included all the original dimensions in the proposed research model with the exception of ‘merging of action and awareness', which refers to actions that are spontaneous and automatic and thus do not require active thought. Previous studies in the HCI space indicate that the flow dimension of ‘merging of action and awareness’ could be problematic [11,80]. For instance, Chen [11] found that ‘merging of action and awareness’ loaded across three factors suggested that it is in fact not a separable variable. Additionally, this dimension was found to have the smallest relative importance in Tenenbaum et al.'s [110] study. Thus, for the sake of parsimony, we do not include this construct in our research model. Furthermore, similar to Chen [11], we capture another flow dimension: autotelic experience via hedonic value as an immediate flow outcome (this will be discussed in Section 3.3.1).

## 3.2. Flow antecedents

In hypothesizing the antecedents of flow, we posit that for students to be ‘in flow’, it is not only the task design and the course delivery that are important (i.e., balance between challenges and skills of the task, clear goals on task, and immediate feedback on task) but also the setup of the technological environment such that it creates a feeling of ‘being there’, thereby making people feel that it is real (i.e., telepresence, as it is the key to engagement).

## 3.2.1. Balance betweenchallenges and skills of the task (BCKT)

One of the most consistent findings within the flow literature is the need for an optimal balance between task challenge and the skill level people possess in order for flow to occur [20]. Csikszentmihalyi [20] states that whether one is in flow depends on the individual’s perception about what the challenges and skills are. If the task can offer individuals challenges that are commensurate with their skills, the possibility of experiencing flow increases [21]; otherwise, people may experience either anxiety or boredom.

The idea that flow requires the balance between skill and challenge is also consistent with the innate human psychological need to be competent, which serves to drive optimal learning experiences [25]. When individuals have the opportunity to become more competent, in becoming increasingly capable and effective in completing the task at hand, they are more likely to feel that the activity is interesting and enjoyable. In turn, their performance and development will be maximized [27]. As an important predictor of flow, this construct has been consistently found to be one of the significant antecedents of flow across various contexts [see 36, for a metaanalysis].

In OLEs, the balance between challenges and skills can be viewed as being centered upon the ability of students to comprehend the tasks that they undertake. In other words, it is important to understand the balance between the challenges of the task (e.g., challenges students may encounter when involved in discussions on certain topics) and skills that students need to address these challenges. If the task presented to a student is overly challenging, they are likely to be overwhelmed because they will have to exert extra effort; if the students' skills exceed the challenges they face, they are likely to feel bored and lose interest [95]. Thus, in order for students to fully engage in and to commit to an online course, some degree of overall challenge (e.g., discussion topics) and skill (e.g., background information or knowledge) is required. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1a.The balance between challenges and skills of the task has a positive relationship with the flow that students experience during online learning.

## 3.2.2. Clear goals on task (CGT)

Many of Csikszentmihalyi’s flow elements relate to the actor’s interaction with the task, one of which is having clear goals– an instrumental condition of Csikszentmihalyi’s [21] flow theory. According to Csikszentmihalyi, when individuals have a clear goal for the tasks they are undertaking, they are guided by a clear direction and are able to focus on the essentials of the activity, leading to concentration. Without clear direction, people are more likely to get distracted by cues that may not be relevant [95]. Because students undertaking online courses have little or no opportunity for immediate communication with instructors to clarify any issues they may have for any given task, clarity of task becomes even more important. Otherwise, students may find it difficult to immerse themselves in the activity and may gradually lose interest in learning [102]. Research shows that clear goals on task enable students to focus on the activity [43], which in turn may make an optimal experience of flow more likely. Accordingly, we hypothesize that:

H1b.There is a positive relationship between clear goals on task and the flow that students experience during online learning.

## 3.2.3. Immediate feedback on tasks (IFT)

Immediate feedback on a task is another important factor for flow to occur [23]. When individuals receive immediate and clear feedback about the tasks they are performing, they can judge whether they have achieved their set goals – a condition necessary for flow to occur [57].

Studying online at one’s own pace can be a lonely and isolating process, and for some students it may lead to a loss of interest [68]. As the quantity of student interactions with others determines students’ psychological sense of distance in online learning [84], either no or delayed feedback about student performance from the instructors can add another level of frustration [108]. Thus, immediate interaction with instructors on student performance can provide students with an opportunity to actually learn as well as to obtain an emotional lift, which is even more important for online courses [37]. Frequent and open communication with students is the key to boosting student confidence and establishing a sense of mutual respect and belonging [68,102]. In turn, this can provide social support and promote their involvement and interest in the online activities [68]. Research shows that prompt, clear and unambiguous feedback from instructors and other participants helps to narrow the user’s focus onto the activity being performed and has a positive influence on achieving flow [124]. We therefore hypothesize that:

H1c. There is a positive relationship between the immediate feedback students receive on their task and the flow that they experience during online learning.

## 3.2.4. Telepresence (TP)

According to the definition of telepresence, almost any medium, such as newspapers, books, movies, and computers, can produce telepresence to some extent [103]. When individuals experience the feeling of telepresence, it is often characterized by the perception of ‘transparence’, ‘realness’, and ‘immersion’ [74]. In CMEs, telepresence is related to the extent to which one feels present in the mediated environment [109]. For instance, when online shoppers experience the feeling of telepresence, they may create a perceptual illusion of being inside a real store rather than a virtual one; or, when students taking an online course perceive a sense of telepresence, they may create a perceptual illusion of being in a physical classroom in which they learn and interact directly with others as if they were physically present [7]. This feeling has been widely studied in virtual environments, such as online games and online stores [3,85,88], and it has been identified as a powerful factor in channeling users’ attention on the activity conducted within the environment and in shaping their overall experience of presence in the virtual world.

The notion of 'being there’ and ‘real' perception is very important for online learning. As discussed earlier, unlike face-to-face courses, in which a minimal degree of presence is guaranteed through face-to-face interactions, students in online courses often feel isolated or disconnected due to a lack of real-time interaction and sense of community. If the online courses can provide students with a heightened sense of telepresence, it is more likely

#

that students will focus on their actions and feel in control of their environment, and in doing so attain an optimal experience in the environment (i.e. flow) [3]. For instance, Phang and Kankanhalli’s [93] empirical study demonstrated the important impact that telepresence played on students’ flow experience in an OLE. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H1d. There is a positive relationship between the telepresence that students perceive while online and the flow that they experience during online learning.

## 3.3. Flow consequences

## 3.3.1. Flow and perceived hedonic and utilitarian value

In this study, students’ perceived utilitarian value (PUV) refers to their cognitive evaluation of taking online courses in terms of purpose fulfillment and problem solving [4]. Students' perceived hedonic value (PHV) describes the potential entertainment and emotional worth of the online course rather than the achievement of any end goal [4]. Taken together, these two types of value represent a more comprehensive picture of the key outcome variables in a general model of consumption experiences [52].

Translating Kim et al.’s [61] balanced thinking and feeling framework of IS continuance into students’ online learning experience, students participating in online courses may gain cognitive rewards (utilitarian value), e.g., high grades, as well as more intrinsic, personal, and emotional rewards (hedonic value), e.g., enjoyment. Thus, in the current study, when we examine the impact of flow experience on students’ beliefs, we adopt a twodimensional framework for perceived value: the first, a hedonic dimension derived from the perspective of using online learning systems for fun; and the second, a utilitarian dimension resulting from completing online tasks. Thus, not only the utilitarian but also the hedonic dimensions are important [61,112] in influencing students continuance intention in online learning.

According to Csikszentmihalyi [21] and other researchers [11,74], an individual's feeling of enjoyment is one consequence of flow experience, particularly in CMEs. As described by Csikszentmihalyi [22, p.11], “when we are in flow, we do not usually feel happy, because we feel only what is relevant to the activity…. It is only after we get out of flow… that we might indulge in feeling happy”. According to the hedonic value definition [4], happiness with comfort and enjoyment is the hedonic outcome [85].The defining feature of this multifaceted optimal experience is the intrinsically rewarding experiential involvement that is accompanied by a positive experience quality. Following this line of reasoning, if the online courses can foster a sensation of optimal experience, they are likely to provide students with hedonic value. This is in line with prior studies demonstrating the important impact of flow on an individual’s hedonic beliefs pertaining to online systems [55]. Thus we propose that:

H2a. There is a positive relationship between the flow that students experience and perceived hedonic value.

Previous research [1] notes that a state of cognitive absorption, which is analogical to the flow construct, has a significant impact on technology users’ perceptions of usefulness due to the cognitive dissonance and selfjustification mechanisms [33]. Following the same logic, we expect a student’s optimal experience in OLEs to have an impact on both their affective beliefs as well as their cognitive beliefs. That is, when students experience an optimal state of flow during an online course, they are likely to come to perceive this online course as being useful, a utilitarian view of the system [1]. A similar finding was reported in Saadé and Bahli’s Internet learning study [99]. Some studies also validate the notion that the utilitarian and hedonic value perceived by web users can be derived from positive flow experience [66,115]. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H2b. There is a positive relationship between the flow that students experience and perceived utilitarian value.

## 3.3.2. Mediating impact of perceived value on student satisfaction (SAT) and continuance intention (CI)

In line with IS users' satisfaction with technology use, we describe online course students’ satisfaction in terms of their feeling of pleasure or disappointment as a result of comparing the online course’s perceived outcomes to their expectations [65]. Studies across various disciplines demonstrate the direct relationship between flow and satisfaction [e.g.,49,104], and within CMEs, the flow experience is shown to have a positive effect on satisfaction [49]. Shin’s [104] study found a moderately strong and positive correlation between the flow construct and satisfaction. Lee and Choi [70] found that the relationship between flow and online course retention was fully mediated by students’ satisfaction with online courses. However, in essence, customer satisfaction is closely related to perceived value [4]. According to the ‘broaden-and-build’ theory of positive emotion, experiencing positive emotions can promote one’s urge to explore and to learn [38]. As a result, students can experience more positive outcomes, including being open to more information, having greater enduring personal resources, and having more integrative thought patterns, which ultimately leads to satisfaction [38]. The strong relationship between perceived hedonic value and user satisfaction has been found in various settings, such as in shopping [32], e-services [10], and social virtual world services [125].

In addition, perceived utilitarian value is analogous to long-term perceived usefulness [16]. Thus, perceived utilitarian value represents students’ more instrumental reasons for engaging in a task, with task becoming a means to an end rather than an end itself [25]. It represents a post-adoption expectation in terms of utilitarian purpose [63]. Given that satisfaction refers to a consumer’s post-adoption evaluation and affective response to an overall product or experience, prior research within the fields of marketing and IS has demonstrated the significant impact of perceived utilitarian value/usefulness on satisfaction across a wide range of contexts, such as traditional retailers [58], e-learning systems [72], online banking websites [6], online book stores [66], mobile applications [13], and social virtual world service systems [125].

Given the direct impacts of flow on students’ perceived value of online courses, we propose that students flow experiences derived from online courses will indirectly lead to satisfaction via both perceived hedonic and utilitarian value. That is, as students’ flow experience increases the perceived value of the online course, this perception will in turn increase their satisfaction with their online course learning. More formally, we hypothesize the following:

H3a. There is a positive indirect relationship between flow and satisfaction through perceived hedonic value after students experience flow in their online learning.

H3b. There is a positive indirect relationship between flow and satisfaction through perceived utilitarian value after students experience flow in their online learning.

There is no doubt that IS continuance is one of the primary goals of all IT-enabled organizations, including universities. Applying the balanced thinking and feeling framework [61] to online learning, both perceived hedonic and utilitarian value should have an impact on students' online learning continuance intention. Indeed, perceived utilitarian value has long been identified as a major influence on users’ continuance intention [73,114,115]. In educational contexts, Chiu and Wang [16], Roca and Gagne [96], and Liaw [72] provide empirical support for the relationship between perceived utilitarian value/usefulness and continuance intention. On the other hand, hedonic value has also been found to play an important role in influencing online users' continuance intention [112]. In the context of online learning, both Chiu and Wang [16] and Liu et al. [73] indicate the important role hedonic value plays in promoting learners’ continuous intention. Students chose to learn online or intended to take more online courses if they perceived that the courses offered superior value [121]. The impact of flow on CI is realized progressively as students’ perceived value increases. Thus, we propose:

H3c. There is a positive indirect relationship between flow and continuance intention through the perceived hedonic value of students’ online courses.

H3d. There is a positive indirect relationship between flow and continuance intention through perceived utilitarian value of students’ online courses.

As per expectation confirmation theory, many researchers have found empirical evidence suggesting a positive relationship between consumer satisfaction and continuance intention [6,90]. Several studies have identified student satisfaction as a major factor contributing to online learning students’ decisions to drop out of courses [70,81]. Researchers have called for more effort in enhancing students’ satisfaction with online courses as a means of enabling students to successfully complete them [59]. If learners taking part in an online course are satisfied with their experience, they may be eager to continue undertaking online courses [89]. Thus, we predict that flow may indirectly affect students’ online learning continuance intention through perceived value and satisfaction. More formally:

H3e. There is a positive indirect relationship between flow and online learning continuance intention through perceived hedonic value and satisfaction.

H3f. There is a positive indirect relationship between flow and online learning continuance intention through perceived utilitarian value and satisfaction.

## 4. Research design

## 4.1. Sample and data collection

A total of 524 undergraduate students enrolled in one online course were invited to participate in the study during the tenth week of a thirteen-week semester. The online course was delivered via Blackboard, the university’s adopted LMS, and involved no face-to-face component. The course consisted of online lecture notes and weekly online tutorial discussions, conducted via an embedded online discussion forum. We obtained a total of 244 usable and valid responses for analysis, yielding a response rate of 47 percent. The majority of the respondents in the final sample were under the age of 25 (95.9%), and 52 percent were female (with three missing gender data). On average, respondents had studied more than 2 years at the university, providing a good indicator of their competence in using Blackboard. More than 75 percent of the respondents had previously completed at least one online course, and more than 65 percent of the respondents had more than one year of experience in using discussion forums in online courses. The respondents also reported a mean of 6 (out of 7) for their perceived level of Internet literacy (where $1 ~ = ~ \mathrm { ^ { 6 6 } N o t }$ at all literate” and 7 = “Completely literate”). The respondents’ Internet and computer accessibility also suggested that they had many opportunities to communicate through the use of technology and the Internet. We therefore considered all of the respondents to be experienced with the use of online learning technologies.

## 4.2. Instrument development

All measurement scales for constructs employed in this study were adapted from earlier studies in which they were validated. All constructs were measured with multiple items on a seven-point Likert scale, anchored between “strongly disagree” and “strongly agree”.

The measurement items for Csikszentmihalyi’s original flow constructs were directly adapted from Jackson’s Flow State Scale and were measured retrospectively<sup>1</sup> [63]. According to Csikszentmihalyi’s definition of flow [23] and consistent with prior studies about flow [66,73,116,118], flow<sup>2</sup> was measured as a reflective secondorder construct exhibited by four dimensions – namely, concentration on task at hand (CTH), loss of selfconsciousness (LSC), sense of control (SoC), and time distortion (TD). Telepresence was measured with multiple items developed by Kim and Biocca [62]. The measurement items for perceived hedonic and utilitarian value were adapted from Babin et al.'s [4] study. Satisfaction<sup>2</sup> was measured as a second-order reflective construct composed of the three separate yet correlated first-order measures, including perceived learning process satisfaction (LPS), perceived learning outcome satisfaction (LOS), and positive learning attitudes (PLA), which are defined as the students’ evaluative feelings about using technologies for online courses [17]. Based on the literature, these three sub-dimensions have been widely used in CME studies to capture participants’ collaborative learning process satisfaction and outcome satisfaction, in addition to their evaluative judgment about learning [17,45,46]. Continuance intention was measured using multiple items adapted from Mathieson’s behavioral intention scale [82]. Appendix A lists the scale items.

## 5. Results

Data analysis was undertaken in a holistic manner using partial least squares (PLS). This mode of analysis has the capacity to simultaneously estimate both the measurement and the structural models.

## 5.2. Common method bias

Given that we collected our data by means of a cross-sectional survey and that the respondent providing the measures of all variables is the same person, there is a potential for the response to be affected by common method bias. Following Podsakoff et al.’s [94] recommendations, we attempted to minimize the effects of this bias by protecting respondent anonymity and reducing evaluation apprehension by assuring respondents that there were no right or wrong answers. In addition, we performed two statistical analyses to assess whether common method bias was likely to be a serious concern in the study. First, we performed Harman’s single factor test in which measures representing the constructs in our model were loaded into an unrotated exploratory factor analysis [94]. The results from the factor analysis showed that the total constructs with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 accounted for 65.32% of the variance and that the first factor explained only 29.7% of the variance, indicating that common method bias was not substantially present in this study. We then followed the procedure outlined by Liang et al. [71] to implement the single unmeasured method factor design into our PLS model. We created a common method factor whose indicators included all the principal construct’s indicators and included it in our PLS model. We calculated each indicator's variance, substantively explained by the principal construct and by the method, as shown in Appendix C (CMV table). The results demonstrated that the average substantively explained variance of the indicators was significantly greater than that of the average method-based variance, with a ratio of substantive variance to method variance of approximately 52:1. Given that most method factor loadings were not significant, we are able to conclude that common method bias is unlikely to be a serious concern in this study [71].

## 6. Discussion

In general, the empirical results are encouraging and provide support for the two main objectives of the study. The first objective was to find empirical support for the theorized key determinants of flow in online learning. Consistent with prior studies, the findings from this study support the significant impact of BCKT (balance between challenges and skills of the task) and CGT (clear goals on task) on Flow, while the impact of IFT (immediate feedback on task) on Flow was not found to be significant. As described earlier, for the online course, only tutors replied to students’ queries on a weekly basis; it was therefore unlikely for students to expect their tutors or lecturer to provide immediate feedback on the tasks (IFT) they conducted. Although it had no direct impact on flow experience, IFT may have had an indirect impact on Flow through influences on both BCKT and CGT given the significant correlation (see Table 1). This is indeed an interesting finding, indicating that more complicated relationships might exist among pre-conditions of flow than were initially presumed in the original flow model [43].

More importantly, telepresence (TP) emerges as the most influential antecedent of Flow compared to Csikszentmihalyi's original three flow pre-conditions. The significant decrease in $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { R } } ^ { 2 }$ (from 66.4% to 16.8%) when TP was removed from the model is a good indicator of its substantive impact on Flow compared to other flow antecedents. This construct has been included in numerous CME studies since it was initially proposed by Hoffman and Novak in 1996 as one of the key flow antecedents [50]. Our results indicate that, in OLEs, telepresence is a key determinant for students to experience flow. This study’s findings confirm that when

technology users (in this case, students) can perceive a sense of being in a mediated environment, they are more able to concentrate on the computer-related tasks they are performing [34]. However, given the debate over telepresence and flow, as well as the cross-sectional nature of this study, future research is warranted to further validate these findings.

The second objective of this study was to identify the mechanisms through which flow affects students' online learning continuance intention. Based on the theoretical definitions of flow, perceived hedonic value, perceived utilitarian value, satisfaction and continuance intention, we proposed the immediate outcome of flow on students' perceived hedonic and utilitarian value of online learning; these two variables, in turn, would function as mediators to transmit flow impacts on student satisfaction and continuance intention. The posited theoretical relationships between Flow and PHV, Flow and PUV, and all proposed mediating paths were supported. By integrating all key constructs into one collective model, the effects of Flow on SAT were found to be fully mediated by PHV and PUV, and the effects of Flow on CI were fully mediated by PHV, PUV, and SAT. Although prior research found a significant direct effect of flow on student online learning satisfaction [70] and on student retention [59], these studies did not include students’ perceived online course value. This study’s findings suggest that the direct effect of flow on satisfaction and continuance intention may be fully mediated by the mediators of perceived value.

While PHV, PUV and SAT all played roles as mediators to transmit the effects of Flow on CI, PHV was the most important one: the indirect effect of Flow on CI via PHV (0.524) (Flow-PHV-CI + Flow-PHV-SAT-CI) was much higher than that of PUV (0.006) (Flow-PUV-CI + Flow-PUV-SAT-CI). These results show the value of increasing PHV in helping to enhance students' continuance intention. As a post hoc analysis, we re-ran the model by removing PHV to test the predictive power of PHV. The result showed that the $\mathsf { R } ^ { 2 }$ of CI decreased to 0.264, dropping by 49.2%, thereby further supporting the significant role of PHV in the determination of Flow on students' CI. This study’s findings are congruent with the results reported in prior research. For instance, both van der Heijden [112] and Mantymaki and Salo [75] found that perceived enjoyment was a much stronger predictor of behavioral use intention than perceived usefulness in the virtual world. When studying users’ continuance intention toward social virtual world services, Zhou et al. [125] found that the indirect impact of perceived hedonic value on continuance intention via satisfaction was stronger than that of the indirect impact of perceived utilitarian value on continuance intention via satisfaction. Chiu and Wang [16] also found that students’ perceived hedonic value was the most important predictor of their continuance intention to use web-based learning systems. Given that this study proposed the positive impact of optimal experience on students’ affective feelings and their behavioral intention, the significant and larger effects of PHV over PUV on CI is unsurprising. As suggested in the prior literature, the dominant design objective of hedonic features of the Information System is to encourage continued and prolonged use [112].

#

Contrary to prior literature, the relationship between PUV and CI was found to be significant but negative. Given the positive zero-order correlation between PUV and CI (0.21) and given that multicollinearity is not the cause of this significant relationship (see Endnote 4), a post hoc analysis revealed that PUV played the role of negative suppressor for SAT in relation to CI [18]. According to Conger [18, p.36], “a suppressor variable is defined to be a variable that increases the predictive validity of another variable (or set of variables) by its inclusion in a regression equation.” Variance of the independent variable can be partitioned into variance that is relevant and variance that is irrelevant to the dependent variable [111]. Tzeglov and Henik [111] note that suppression effects are more frequent in conditions of high correlations between independent variables, in which the suppressor variable has no (or few) elements in common with the dependent variable but shares with the other correlated independent variable(s) much information that is irrelevant to the dependent variable. The negative suppressor effect occurs when (1) the independent variables have a positive zero-order correlation with the dependent variable; (2) the independent variables correlate positively with each other; and (3) one of the independent variables (the negative suppressor) receives a negative regression weight after inclusion in the regression equation, reflecting the fact that the inclusion of the suppressor helps to partial out the irrelevant element from the predictor, which “purifies” the predictor (suppressed variable) and improves its prediction [18,76]. To detect suppressor and suppressed variables, we followed the procedure suggested in the literature [18,76] in which different regression models were performed with PUV and one of the other two antecedents of CI (PHV and SAT) served as a predictor variable. The path coefficient between PUV and CI became negative only when SAT was also included as a predictor of CI, but it was positive in all other regression model specifications. This result indicated that the addition of PUV, which shared many irrelevant predictive variances with SAT, into the model removed irrelevant predictive variances in SAT, thus enhancing its prediction on CI (path coefficient between SAT and CI increased) [18]. When the variance in CI due to SAT was accounted for, the remaining variance correlated with PUV revealed a negative relationship [18]. Maassen and Bakker [76] suggested that it is important to acknowledge the occurrence of a suppression situation in structural models and to consider it when interpreting the results. They also note that the negative path coefficient between PUV and CI does not imply that PUV has a negative relation with CI because the real relationship between the negative suppressor (in this case PUV) and the dependent variable (in this case CI) is concealed by the negative suppression effect [76]. The literature suggests that when a suppression effect occurs, it is important to combine both effects of the suppressor and suppressed variables when interpreting the results [76]. In our case, we can state that when SAT (which is a suppressed variable) is held constant, students who perceive low (high) PUV will tend to have a higher (lower) CI. One potential explanation for this is that students who are satisfied with their online learning experience yet believe they can accomplish more learning are those who yearn more to take more online courses. Clearly, this would be an interesting area to examine in future research.

## 7. Implications

This study makes a number of important contributions to both research and practice. One major theoretical contribution of this study is the development of a theoretical model explaining student online course continuance intention by integrating telepresence as one context-specific flow antecedent and by incorporating the valuesatisfaction-continuance intention as a chain of flow consequences. While prior IS research has examined the relationship between flow and telepresence and has investigated how flow relates to perceived hedonic value, perceived utilitarian value, satisfaction, and continuance intention, this study is among the first to simultaneously examine the interrelationships among these factors in online learning contexts. By linking flow with these factors, this study offers rich insights into how flow can be fostered during online learning and explicates the paths through which technology-use-behavior is manifested during students’ online learning

Specifically, this study extends the current understanding of flow by adding a meaningful antecedent to better explain what factors may influence flow in online learning environments. Furthermore, by introducing the antecedent of telepresence as another factor contributing to flow experience, this study demonstrates the significant importance of considering user perception of CMEs particularly OLEs. This finding indicates that creating a sense of ‘being there’ can be one of the potential key determinants in fostering an individual’s positive experience and persistence in the virtual environment, and particularly in an environment where immersion is generated through the use of technology.

Second, the significant predictive power of perceived hedonic value supports prior research findings regarding the role that hedonic value plays in the IS user continuance intention model [75,112]. Given that perceived utilitarian value loses its dominant predictive value in favor of perceived hedonic value when studying the relationships between flow and students' online learning continuance intention, this research suggests that if the focus of the study is about the hedonic aspects of the IS, then the predictive importance of the user continuance intention determinants may change, as reflected within the findings from van der Heijden's study [112]. As has been echoed by other researchers [112], this study's findings suggest that users' perceived hedonic value of the IS can play a pivotal role in determining their continuance intention and should be included in research models that attempt to explain and predict this phenomenon.

Third, the suppression effect of PUV for SAT in relation to CI indicates the complexity of interrelationships among them. Future research is needed for an alternative model to explain their relationships. In addition, the fully mediating roles of mediators on the relationship between flow and CI emphasize the importance of examining all related variables in one model simultaneously. This helps to achieve a more complete and accurate assessment of technology users' decision making criteria for online behavior so that the true relationship between

#

flow and CI is not masked or overstated due to omitted variable bias [19]. In general, the current research model offers the best overall fit and has the greatest explanatory power.

This study's findings also have a number of practical implications. First, because students’ online learning continuance intention takes place largely through a chain of telepresence, optimal learning experience, and then hedonic beliefs related to the online course, it is essential for course instructors to put great efforts into technology-mediated course design in order to produce and maximize a sense of telepresence. Through such a course of action, students may be kept more readily engaged throughout the course duration – a task that is especially challenging when the rest of the Web is just a click away. Research shows that advanced technologies, such as synchronous communication tools, multimedia techniques, or 3D virtual reality learning environments, can bring a real-time element into communicating online, increase the social presence of learners, and create interactive and stimulating learning environments. Thus, instructors delivering their course materials online should consider the creative use of these advanced technologies to reinforce the sensation of immersion in computer-generated virtual learning environments, thereby making the students’ overall learning experience more enjoyable [54,83]. For instance, Thrun and Norvig, who were among the first instructors to introduce Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) through their Artificial Intelligence course in 2011, added interactive components into their pre-recorded lecture materials to enhance student engagement, such as an online lecture peppered with probing questions that were auto-graded and then explained further [79]. Some instructors make use of 3D avatars to perform appropriate gestures, such as clapping to appreciate a good contribution or the appearance of a smiling face in the event of a correct answer, making the sense of telepresence in the environment more salient [93]. By doing so, students feel they are talking to the lecturers when they ask questions and receiving positive feedback for the contributions they make, increasing their feeling of engagement and thus their enjoyment and continuance intention. However, to minimize unnecessary distractions, course designers must ensure that advanced technologies used in online learning should be transparent, thereby serving to sustain student interest in tasks and avoid cognitive overload [85,91]. This aspect is also vital for LMS designers who strive to provide more advanced and comprehensive components in the learning system, making technology an enabler rather than a distracter and promoting both productive and prolonged system use [1,112].

Second, the significant role that perceived hedonic value plays in enhancing students’ continuance intention in taking online courses serves to stress the importance of this factor as a strategic objective for university OLEs. In OLEs, instructors should avoid a strictly utilitarian perspective on course design and delivery and should seek to employ ways to exploit students’ positive emotional characteristics to encourage engagement, positive feelings, satisfaction, and persistence [1,16]. In other words, when using primarily utilitarian feature-driven LMSs to design and deliver online courses, the course designers should integrate some hedonic features, such as surprising and challenging situations, collaboration, interactivity, and flexibility of learning tasks to promote students affective responses to the course materials and allow them to have fun as they pursue achievements. Given that today’s students are spending much of their time in virtual worlds, including social media and online games, instructors can create game- and/or simulation-based learning activities to meet students’ visual and cognitive needs, making the learning journey enjoyable, relevant, experimental, and persistent [1,16]. For LMS designers, they should greatly focus on enhancing the hedonic features of a system, such as graphics, interactions, and skillbuilding challenges, in order to reduce monotony and exploit students’ playful characteristics as well as meet today’s students’ technology use needs [44].

Finally, the significant direct and indirect impacts that flow has on students' online learning behavior indicate that a student’s decision-making process about their online learning behavior is both complex and comprehensive. For educators, this indicates that the best strategy to enhance students' online learning outcomes is to establish online learning initiatives that address and maximize the utility of all of the variables included in our model, i.e., universities should strive to foster positive learning environments for online courses in order to improve students perceived hedonic and utilitarian value of the online course and ensure their satisfaction, thereby in turn influencing their continuance intention. The need to address all of the variables helps to ensure that we do not overemphasize or underappreciate the importance of one or more of the variables in question [19].

## 8. Limitations and future research directions

This study has presented several interesting and novel findings; however, it is not without limitations. First, the sample consisted mainly of undergraduate students who were undertaking one online course as part of their degree requirement. Guo and Poole [43] suggest that different samples may reflect different insights. Thus, future studies should examine the model in different contexts, such as in the context of postgraduate studies. Second, the technology used in this study was Blackboard and embedded discussion forums, thus limiting the breadth of the conclusions. Future studies should re-examine this model in different technological contexts to investigate whether technology characteristics play a role in flow development and learners’ perceptions of online learning. Furthermore, the findings of this study were obtained based on self-reported data and cross-sectional design. In particular, flow was measured retrospectively [57]. To demonstrate that the perceived flow experience measured in our study was reliable and valid, we performed a follow-up laboratory experimental $\mathrm { s t u d y } ^ { 1 }$ to compare the relationships between flow measured immediately following the activity, as reported in Webster's [118] study, and flow measured retrospectively, as was performed in this and Chen et al.'s [12] study. Strong correlations were found between these two measures, indicating that flow measured in this study was effective and acceptable. Future research may conduct a longitudinal study to capture the flow state immediately following the activity, with beliefs, attitudes and intentions gathered at a later point in time [1]. Finally, SoC (Sense of Control) was not included in our final model, as it did not pass the assessment of several types of psychometric properties. Thus, this study is not able to make an assessment of the relative importance of SoC to flow. Future research is warranted to re-examine this model with a more reliable and valid SoC construct.

Finally, considering the significant impact of telepresence on flow, future research is needed to identify the most important causes of telepresence in order to maximize engagement, enjoyment, satisfaction and enhanced learning performance. Although this study recognized the importance of interactivity for telepresence, the actual impact of interactivity on telepresence was not examined per se. An empirical analysis was not included at this time, as we were primarily interested in discovering whether telepresence and other flow antecedents were key factors in improving users’ overall learning experience. A high priority for future research should be to further explore the elements contributing to telepresence in online learning environments and leading to the most instances of flow for students.

The thorough discussion about the choice of measurement perspective, i.e., whether a construct is reflective or formative, has been explicitly articulated in behavioral research and in the IS discipline [9,30,31,77]. The following four decision rules have been recommended to identify a construct as either reflective or formative: (1) The choice of measurement has always been based on theoretical considerations. If the items define the construct, they are formative. However, if the items are manifestations of the construct, they are reflective; (2) Measures for reflective constructs are required to covary with one another; thus, internal consistency or reliability is important in reflective measures, meaning that all of the items are measuring the same phenomenon and if the value for one of the measures changes, then all of the other values should move in the same direction. For formative constructs, though, measures do not need to covary; (3) Formative measures should not have strong correlations with one another because this suggests multicollinearity. While multicollinearity is desirable for reflective measures, excessive multicollinearity (greater than 3.3) [30] in formative measures can destabilize the construct and should be addressed by using one of the approaches outlined by Petter et al. [92]; and (4) Reflective measures are interchangeable; they therefore have the same antecedents and consequences because the measures are manifestations of the construct. In contrast, measures in formative constructs may have very different antecedents and/or consequences. For both Flow and SAT, apart from the theoretical justification demonstrated in the literature, the corresponding first-order dimensions demonstrated high correlations (see Table 1). Furthermore, the first-order dimensions for Flow and SAT all had similar antecedents and consequences, respectively, according to the literature. Thus, it was more appropriate to model them as reflective constructs.

We also ran the model with both Flow and SAT as second-order formative constructs. The explanatory power for CI remained the same (when rounded to two decimal places).
