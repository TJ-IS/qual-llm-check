# Understanding and attenuating decision bias in the use of model advice and other relevant information

Donald R. Jones <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Patrick Wheeler <sup>b,1</sup>, Radha Appan <sup>c</sup>, Naveed Saleem <sup>d,2</sup>

<sup>b</sup> School of Accountancy, University of Missouri-Columbia, 441 Cornell Hall, Columbia, Missouri 65203, USA

<sup>c</sup> Nance College of Business, Cleveland State University, 1860 E. 18th St., Cleveland, Ohio 44114, USA

Received 21 April 2005; received in revised form 23 January 2006; accepted 1 May 2006 Available online 11 July 2006

## Abstract

A human judge faced with model advice, modeled information (used by the model to compute the advice), and unmodeled information (known by the human but not included in the model) should use a “divide-and-conquer” strategy in which the human judge relies completely on the model to process the modeled information and focuses all energy on assessing and adjusting for the unmodeled information [D.R. Jones, D. Brown, The division of labor between human and computer in the Presence of Decision Support System Advice, Decision Support Systems 33 (2002) 375–388]. This paper extends Jones and Brown [D.R. Jones, D. Brown, The division of labor between human and computer in the Presence of Decision Support System Advice, Decision Support Systems 33 (2002) 375–388] in two studies. In Study 1, we find that, in lieu of the divide-and-conquer strategy, human judges give weight to all three types of inputs and that giving weight to the modeled information degrades performance. In Study 2, we find that (1) as strategies approach the divide-and-conquer strategy judgment performance improves, and (2) the divide-and-conquer strategy can be encouraged by a combination of instruction and a decision support feature. Application of these results could improve judgment in a variety of important contexts. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Decision making; Decision strategies; Judgment bias; Model advice

## 1. Introduction

How should a human, in forming a judgment, make use of model advice provided by a computer system? Prior research conducted over several decades indicates that the answer to this question depends on whether the human knows any additional information not incorporated by the model [24,28]. If the human brings no additional information to the judgment task, then the human should rely completely on the model, since using human intuition to combine the relevant modeled information is neither as accurate nor as consistent as using a mathematical formula or algorithm to combine the information to form a judgment [1,33]. If, on the other hand, the human also has relevant unmodeled information (information not incorporated by the model) then the human judge can feel justified in adjusting the model advice to reflect the unmodeled information [5,20].

In the latter situation, it is recommended that decision makers should execute a “divide-and-conquer” strategy [21]. The divide-and-conquer strategy reflects the correct division of labor between human and computer, in that it capitalizes on the strengths (and compensates for the weaknesses) of human and computer [20]. In the divide-and-conquer strategy decision makers delegate to the DSS the processing of information available to the DSS model while focusing their human effort on the information not modeled by the DSS to make adjustments to the model advice [[21], pp. 375–376]. Unfortunately, Jones and Brown [21] found that decision makers have no greater tendency to execute the divide-and-conquer strategy when the requisite conditions are present than when they are absent. In their experiment, decision makers often spent as much time looking at modeled information as unmodeled information and in some cases ignored model advice altogether. While Jones and Brown [21] imply that such strategies would have lower accuracy than the divideand-conquer strategy, they did not test this assertion.

This research extends the Jones and Brown [21] study by investigating two research questions. First, we investigate how decision makers acquire and weight information and hypothesize that decision making, in the presence of model advice and other relevant information, is subject to a bias (giving too much attention and weight to the modeled information) that harms decision performance. In our first experiment we find support for the bias and attendant performance degradation. Second, we investigate whether decision makers could be de-biased, via instructions and a decision support feature, and be encouraged to execute the divide-and-conquer strategy. Our second experiment finds process tracing and outcome results indicating that the divide-and-conquer strategy can be elicited and that doing so improves judgment performance.

The significance of this research stems from the ubiquity of the decision situation under study, the importance of accuracy in many types of decisions for which DSS provide model advice, and the danger of biased judgment. The decision situation occurs frequently in marketing [5], professional accounting [15], investment [45], and potentially in other fields that increasingly combine computer usage with complex decision making such as medicine, clinical psychology, and engineering. In such decisions large monetary amounts or even human lives are often at stake. Moreover, widespread Internet usage and the ease with which computerized decision support features can be implemented to ensure growth in both model advice and ready availability of unmodeled information. Furthermore, model builders strive to create parsimonious models and often create “generic” models that may omit variables important to a particular decision. After models are created, new relevant information may become available. For these reasons many decision makers may face model advice, the information on which the model was based, and available relevant information that was not included in the model. They would benefit from being enabled to use these decision inputs correctly.

In the following two sections, we describe two studies that address, respectively, the two research questions. Each section includes theoretical development, explanation of methodology, and results. In the final section, we discuss the contribution and implications of our study.

## 2. Study 1 — how underweighting the unmodeled information hurts judgment performance

## 2.1. Theoretical development

The judgment scenario of interest involves three types of information inputs to the human judge: model advice, modeled information, and unmodeled information suggestive of a deviation from the model advice. For example, in the Hoch and Schkade [20] experiment participants were asked to predict future credit ratings of commercial loan applicants based on several financial ratios (modeled information) and a mathematical model prediction based on the ratios. Unmodeled information provided was the geographic region of the loan applicant's business and a database list of other businesses' credit ratings and their geographic regions.

Model advice benefits the judgment process through an anchoring and adjustment mechanism in which the human judge's unassisted judgment (the anchor) is adjusted toward the model advice [20] (see also [19,37]). Since the model advice results from an accurately and consistently applied algorithm, its influence on the judgment typically reduces the effects of what might have been spurious or extreme intuitive judgments. This reduction in random error is, on average, beneficial. The benefits of unmodeled information derives from the human ability to view the model in a larger context [28] and at pattern matching [20,29]. The human judge can detect patterns in the unmodeled information and use these patterns to inform judgment. For example, decision makers used their pattern matching ability to notice the effects of geographic region in a “database list,” and adjust their predictions for whether the geographic region seemed favorable or unfavorable for business [20].

While prior research shows that decision makers are better off having model advice and modeled and unmodeled information than not having them, the way these information sources are used is subject to bias due to incorrectly weighting these decision inputs. Prior psychological research has widely found that when various information cues are provided they will all be attended to and given some weight. For example, when provided obviously irrelevant information (information that normatively should not be considered by the human judge), decision makers assign it weight in prediction tasks [17,45]. Indeed, the anchoring and adjustment mechanism was originally demonstrated as the assignment of weight to irrelevant information (a randomly generated “anchor”) [42]. Furthermore, decision makers in laboratory settings have been found to give weight to completely redundant information, which by definition adds no new information content to the decision setting [14].

Research indicates that decision makers provided model advice and only modeled information give weight to their judgmental assessment of the modeled information, reducing the weight assigned to the model advice [1,15]. This strategy or heuristic continues in use despite the near universal conclusion of research that model predictions outperform human prediction when both use the same inputs [24,28]. The superiority of models over the human ability to combine modeled information holds even when models are less than perfect [12]. Thus, while human judges would generally do well to replace their intuitive assessment of modeled information with model advice, they generally only supplement the intuitive assessment with the model advice [26]. This tendency leads to worse decision making than would occur by simply relying on the model advice alone [1– 3,5–7,11,15,22,23,31]. The aforementioned research indicates that decision makers give too little weight to model advice and too much weight to modeled information.

The allocation of weight to the available input cues by the decision maker is a zero-sum game. When aided decision makers view modeled information, unmodeled relevant information, and model advice, psychological theory (e.g., anchoring and adjustment) indicates that decision makers consider all of these types of available information in forming their judgments. Decision makers attend to unmodeled information, which allows it to have influence, and when the information is relevant, can contribute to improved judgment. Decision makers also give the model advice some weight, and this allows the benefits of model advice to have positive influence on the decision. The problem occurs when the decision makers separately assess the modeled information and assign weight to their assessments. This judgment policy is flawed in two ways: it gives too much weight to the most inferior judgmental component in this scenario, which is the human “intuitive” combination of modeled information, and gives too little weight to the superior judgmental components, which are the model advice and the human assessment of informative unmodeled information. This suboptimal weighting of the three types of input cues is expressed in the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1A. In the presence of modeled information and unmodeled information, human judges will give less weight to the information signal in unmodeled information when model advice is present than when it is absent.

Giving weight to all three types of inputs means that model advice has two contravening effects. Model advice gives the benefit of added accuracy in evaluating the modeled information. Model advice plus the redundant modeled information has the cost of reducing attention and weight given to the informative unmodeled information. Therefore, the net effect of model advice on judgment performance depends on the strength of the information signal in the unmodeled information. By strength of the information signal in unmodeled information we mean the extent to which the presence of the information would change the outcome (and hence, should change the prediction from that given in the model advice). If the unmodeled information signal is weak, the effect of providing model advice (relative to not providing it) is to give a large benefit (improved accuracy of assessing the modeled information) at a low cost (underweighting the weak unmodeled information signal, which would have had little effect on the judgment even if it had been given sufficient weight). If the unmodeled information signal is strong, the effect of providing model advice (relative to not providing it) is to give a small benefit (improved accuracy of assessing the modeled information) at a high cost (underweighting the strong unmodeled information signal, which would have more greatly modified the judgment had it been given sufficient weight). The combined effects on performance of providing model advice and the strength of the information signal in the unmodeled information is expressed in the following hypothesis.

Hypotheses 1B. In the presence of modeled information and unmodeled information, the effect of model advice on performance will depend on the strength of the information signal in the unmodeled information: model advice will help performance when the unmodeled information signal is weak but it will hurt performance when the signal is strong.

## 2.2. Experimental method

## 2.2.1. Procedure

We conducted an experiment to test Hypotheses 1A and 1B. One hundred sixteen participants (108 undergraduate and 8 graduate business majors) were asked to act as bank loan officers by processing commercial loan applications using computers. For each of ten trials, participants were asked to use provided information to predict the future credit rating in five years of a loan applicant. The information shown to all participants on their computer screens included three items of modeled information (debt ratio, cash flow, and revenue trend) and one item of unmodeled information (the location city of the business) for the loan applicant they were considering. For each trial, subjects were also provided a separate (trial specific) printed paper “database list” of data for other businesses showing financial ratios, location, and credit ratings, including businesses in the same location as the one being judged. Participants (except those in an unaided control condition) were shown “model advice” recommending a credit rating based on the financial ratios (but not location). A sample computer information display is shown in Fig. 1 and a sample printed paper database list is shown in Fig. 2. Businesses in the database list were sorted from highest to lowest credit rating, to enable participants to detect patterns in the predictor variables and the credit ratings. Participants were asked to give their credit rating prediction on a scale of 0 to 100, and were informed that 0 represents the lowest credit rating (D in Dunn and Bradstreet's rating) and 100 represents the highest credit rating (AAA).

## 2.2.2. Preparation of experiment

The experimental materials were created in the following manner. The variables were selected based on research in bankruptcy prediction [13,27] and the ranges correspond to actual data for small and medium size businesses [20]. The ten trials were created from ten simulated databases constructed as follows. First, 71 “businesses” were created by randomly generating the three financial ratios and a location “effect” of −e, 0, or +e, with a different value of e assigned to each database ranging from 4 to 40 (see also the note at the bottom of Fig. 2). Each of these numerical effects was assigned a unique city name displayed in the printout. The varied location effect represents variation in the “strength” of the unmodeled information signal expressed in Hypothesis 1B.

For each business a benchmark score was computed by (1) creating an equally weighted linear model of the three financial ratios, and (2) adjusting it for the planted location effect. The simulated credit rating for each business was created by adding to the benchmark a random adjustment drawn from a distribution with mean of zero and standard deviation of 7. From each of these ten simulated databases was randomly drawn one “case to be judged” with the remaining 70 cases constituting the “database list.” Each trial's model advice was computed on the case to be judged from a linear regression model prepared by regressing the three accounting ratios (but not the location city, which is unmodeled information) on the credit ratings in the database list. To help participants understand the meaning and effect of the variables participants were provided a statement about each variable that included its definition, range, and how it related to the bankruptcy prediction (e.g., “the higher the better”).

## 2.2.3. Experimental design

The experiment had two manipulations: the presence or absence of model advice (between participants) and the magnitude of the location effect planted in the ten database lists (a different list for each trial, within participant). Independent variables were judgment performance, the influence of the model advice, and the influence of unmodeled information. Performance was assessed by how close a participant's prediction came to the benchmark credit rating. The influence of model advice was measured as the mean absolute difference between participants' predictions and model advice over all ten trials. The influence of unmodeled information on each participant was measured as the Pearson correlation (over the ten trials) between (a) the planted location effect and (b) the inferred participant's response to the planted location effect (determined by subtracting model advice from the participant's judgment).

The experiment uses the same decision context as Hoch and Schkade [20] (i.e., using accounting information and location to predict future credit ratings), but the experiment was not a replication of that research. A critical distinction is that this experiment investigated the effect of varying the strength of the unmodeled information signal in the database over multiple trials, while their experiment did not.

To test Hypothesis 1B, we separately analyzed the five trials with the weakest planted location effects and the five trials with the strongest planted location effects (see Table 1). For the five trials with weakest planted location effects, providing model advice led to slightly better performance (error = 14.2) than not providing advice (error = 15.8); this difference is significant in a one-tailed F test $( p \mathrm { = } . 0 8 7$ as a two-tailed test: since H1B predicts direction, a one-tailed test results in $\begin{array} { r } { p { = } . 0 4 4 ) } \end{array}$ For the five trials with strongest location effects, however, providing model advice led to worse performance (error = 28.4) than not providing advice (error = 25.9) and this difference is significant $( p \mathrm { = } . 0 1 0 )$ . These results support Hypothesis 1B. When all ten trials are combined, the overall effect of model advice on judgment error (the mean absolute difference between participants' predictions and the benchmarks) was not significant. This is because in the two levels of location strength the presence of model advice has opposite effects on judgment that average each other out. The effect of model advice on judgment performance is moderated by the strength of the unmodeled information signal.

To summarize, model advice consistently had an influence on judgment performance by causing the decision maker to adjust toward the advice. Model advice influence, however, came at the cost of reducing the decision maker's attention to and weighting of the unmodeled information. Therefore, these results support our descriptive cue weighting theory. This cue weighting represents an alternative to the divide-and-conquer strategy, which will be addressed in the next study.

## 3. Study 2: encouraging the divide-and-conquer strategy

The divide-and-conquer strategy would attenuate the aforementioned bias because the human judges would explicitly (1) ignore the modeled information, relying on the model advice to have captured its extractable information value, and (2) expend their available limited cognitive resources on (a) processing the information signal in the unmodeled information and (b) deciding how much to adjust the model advice to allow for the unmodeled information [21]. Modeled information would not be attended to or assigned any weight, rather decision weight would only be assigned to the model advice and unmodeled information. Therefore, the divide-and-conquer strategy eliminates the dysfunctional cue weighting bias found in our first study and represents a suitable division of labor between the computer and human, capitalizing on each component's strength and compensating for its weakness. Thus, this strategy is normative in a bounded rationality sense because it is the best use of the computer and human components of judgment [21].

In a perfect execution of the divide-and-conquer strategy, the decision maker would not waste effort acquiring and processing the modeled information, but would expend all effort acquiring and processing unmodeled information. Accordingly, the more closely a particular judgment strategy approximates the divideand-conquer strategy, the greater will be the proportion of time, as indicating effort [[30], p. 155], spent looking at unmodeled information (in relation to total unmodeled and modeled information). Thus, the proportion of time spent looking at unmodeled information is a measure of the closeness of the strategy to the divideand-conquer strategy. This discussion leads us to our next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. In the presence of model advice, modeled information, and unmodeled information, the greater the proportion of time human judges spend attending to unmodeled information (relative to all information), the better will be the judgment performance.

Jones and Brown [21] found that providing critical information about which inputs were included and not included in the model advice did not prompt a redistribution of cognitive effort (time) as would be expected if a divide-and-conquer strategy were in use. This result is consistent with research about the use of advice which found that decision makers are better at assessing the quality of advice than at deciding how to use the advice [18]. Harvey [18] proposed that the use of advice imposes a heavier processing load than assessing the quality of the advice. Another study found that judges rely more on advisors they trust than those they do not trust [34]. In the current context, a model that ignores part of the evidence might be viewed as untrustworthy. A related study [16] found that human judges having knowledge of a special event and a time series forecast better utilized the forecast when they could request a forecast that incorporated the special event rather than adjusting the original forecast themselves. Furthermore, decision makers will not adopt the most accurate strategy if they deem it too difficult [4,10,30]. These studies and our Study 1 imply that the divide-and-conquer strategy needs to be encouraged in some fashion if it is to be executed.

The mechanisms we propose for encouraging use of the divide-and-conquer strategy are (1) simple instructions advising the decision maker of the advantages of using the divide-and-conquer strategy, and (2) a computerized decision support feature that allows the decision maker to enter an explicit adjustment to the model advice and computes the adjusted model advice.

Prior research has validated both of these general approaches for modifying strategies and improving performance in various contexts. For example, providing subjects “hints” was shown to improve problem solving processes and lead to faster solutions [25]. Suggesting to subjects that they follow the advice of a decision rule was also found to be influential and beneficial [1]. In a series of studies Todd and Benbasat [35–41] established that simple decision support features could encourage certain decision strategies by making them easier to execute. The effect of our “instruction” is to make decision makers aware of the divide-and-conquer strategy and point out its high quality (accuracy); the effect of our DSS feature is to make the divide-and-conquer strategy easier to execute. The joint effect of these two features is to make the divide-and-conquer strategy compete favorably in effort/accuracy tradeoff comparisons against other potential strategies, increasing the odds that the divide-and-conquer strategy is chosen. Our hypotheses concerning the effect of the instruction and DSS feature on the strategy and judgment performance are expressed as follows:

Hypothesis 3A. In the presence of model advice, modeled information, and unmodeled information, giving the decision maker strategy instructions and a decision support system feature (compared to not giving these features) will lead to strategies more consistent with the divide-and-conquer strategy as measured by the proportion of time spent looking at unmodeled information.

Hypothesis 3B. In the presence of model advice, modeled information, and unmodeled information, giving the decision maker strategy instructions and a decision support system feature (compared to not giving these features) will lead to better judgment performance.

## 3.1. Experimental method

In this experiment 154 participants (business information systems students at a state university) were asked to use a computer to perform ten judgment trials in which they viewed information about eight available apartments and judged which apartment's monthly rent was closest to a “target rent” amount. An example information display is shown in Fig. 3.

The computer displayed six attributes for each apartment, viz., number of bedrooms, distance to campus, whether furnished, room size, community features, and amenities. Definitions of the attributes were provided to participants.

The 80 apartments used in the experiment (10 trials with 8 apartments per trial) were selected from a large dataset of actual apartments in the local rental market. Regression analysis was used to verify that the attributes were predictive of rent and to generate the estimated rents used as the model advice in the experiment. Estimated rent (model advice) was calculated using only three of the six attributes: number of bedrooms, distance to campus, and whether furnished.

The remaining three factors served as the unmodeled information for experiment. The source of this regression model was explained to (aided) participants in the printed instructions.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of the five experimental conditions: (1) an unaided control group, (2) a group receiving model advice, (3) a group receiving model advice and a recommendation to use the divide-and-conquer strategy, (4) a group receiving model advice and a decision support system (DSS) feature that supported the divide-and-conquer strategy, and (5) a group receiving model advice, a recommendation to use the divide-and-conquer strategy, and the DSS feature. The order in which trials were performed was randomized within conditions and counterbalanced across conditions. As incentives, participants received course credit and could become eligible for a drawing for two \$100 prizes based on performance (for their experimental condition).

Participants received printed experimental procedures, which instructed them to use the six attributes to predict the rents and how to view these attributes on the computer since the software allowed them to access only one attribute at a time (as displayed in Fig. 3). The computer recorded participants' task decisions (the choice of apartment believed to have rent closest to the target), along with detailed logs of which attributes were viewed, when, and for how long—a data collection method extensively and successfully used in judgment and decision making research [30] and information systems studies [36–41]. A post-test questionnaire was used to gather information about each participant's demography, attitudes and perceptions concerning the experiment.

The computerized information displays and other instructional materials varied according to condition. Participants receiving model advice in conditions 2, 3, 4, and 5 had the model factors indicated on the computer screen (as in Fig. 3) and were given printed material explaining that the “regression advice” (the model advice) is a regression estimate of rent based on 3 of the 6 attributes. Participants receiving the divide-andconquer strategy recommendation in conditions 3 and 5 were provided printed material containing the following:

Using the regression advice and decision support feature. Most likely the best way to use the model advice is to follow a “divide-and-conquer strategy” where you take the model's advice as the best assessment of Number of Bedrooms, Distance to Campus, and whether the apartment is Furnished and spend your energy looking at Room Size, Community Features, and Amenities to decide whether (and by how much) the model advice should be adjusted. For this strategy to be the most effective, you would not even bother looking at the Number of Bedrooms, Distance to Campus, and whether the apartment is Furnished.

Participants receiving the DSS feature in conditions 4 and 5 were given material explaining how to enter their adjustment to regression advice for each apartment by entering numerical values (presumably based on the unmodeled information) into the DSS feature, which then recalculates the “adjusted prediction” shown on the screen. Participants could use these adjusted predictions to help them choose which apartment was closet to the target rent.

## 3.2. Results

We measured the closeness of each participant's judgment strategies to the divide-and-conquer strategy as the proportion of (a) time spent looking at unmodeled information to (b) total time examining at all available information. We measured judgment performance as accuracy (the number of times the participant selected the best alternative apartment) and error (the difference between the selected apartment's rent and the rent of the best alternative). Hypothesis 2 stated that the more closely judgment strategies approximated the divideand-conquer strategy, the better would be judgment performance. We tested Hypothesis 2 using conditions 2 through 5 because only participants in conditions with model advice have the potential of applying the divideand-conquer strategy. For conditions 2 through 5, the correlation between the proportion of time spent looking at unmodeled information and accuracy was r = .253, p = .005. The correlation between the proportion of time spent looking at unmodeled information and error was r=− .441, p =.0001. These results support Hypothesis 2.

The statistical testing of Hypotheses 3A and 3B is reported in Table 2. The mean process tracing results by condition are shown graphically in Fig. 4. ANOVA indicated that experimental condition significantly affected the time (seconds) spent looking at unmodeled attributes. Post hoc analysis indicated that the time spent looking at unmodeled attributes is greater in conditions 4 (model advice and DSS feature) and 5 (model advice, DSS feature, and strategy instruction) than in conditions 1 (unaided control group) or 2 (model advice only). ANOVA also indicated that the experimental condition significantly affected the proportion of time spent looking at unmodeled attributes relative to the total time spent looking at all attributes. Post hoc analysis indicated that participants in conditions 3 (model advice and strategy instruction) and 5 spent a larger proportion of time looking at unmodeled attributes than participants in conditions 1 and 2. The above results for condition 5 support Hypothesis 3A, that providing the instruction and DSS feature will lead to strategies more consistent with the divide-and-conquer strategy than not providing them.

Hypothesis 3B predicts that encouraging decision makers to apply a divide-and-conquer strategy while also providing them a DSS feature assisting their application of this strategy will improve performance. Mean performance measures by condition are shown graphically in Fig. 5. In ANOVA, experimental condition significantly affected the first performance measure, the mean number of times participants chose the best answer. Post hoc analysis (Bonferoni) indicated that this effect was driven by condition 5, which had significantly better performance than all other conditions. ANOVA also indicated that the experimental condition significantly affected the second performance measure (mean absolute difference between rent of the chosen apartment and the target rent). Post hoc analysis indicated that, while there were no significant performance differences among the first three conditions (unaided, model advice, model advice and strategy instruction), the last two conditions (model advice and DSS feature; modeled advice, DSS feature, and strategy instruction) had significantly better performance than did either of the first two conditions. Thus, the provision of model advice alone did not help performance. Providing model advice plus a DSS assisting feature or written encouragement to apply the divide-andconquer strategy was helpful under certain conditions, but not in others. However, providing all three assisting components was consistently beneficial. This result supports Hypothesis 3B.

Participants in conditions 1 and 2 spent approximately half their time (41% and 47%, respectively) looking at unmodeled information (30 and 29 s, respectively) out of the time spent looking at modeled and unmodeled information. In condition 5, however, the condition not only encouraged to use the divide-andconquer strategy but also provided a tool with which to do so, the proportion of time went up to 71%, to 58 s spent looking at unmodeled information, resulting in better performance. Thus, while we find no evidence of a natural tendency to use a divide-and-conquer strategy, we do find evidence that the strategy can be encouraged and assisted and that doing so leads to better judgment performance.

## 4. Discussion

We theorized that decision makers having model advice, modeled information, and unmodeled information would assign weight to all three types of information. While this approach often enables human judges to benefit from model advice and unmodeled information, the results of Study 1 indicate that this strategy is suboptimal because the weakest component of this judgment process (human intuitive combination of modeled information) is given weight at the cost of the two strongest components of the judgment process (model combination of modeled information and human processing of unmodeled information).

Theoretically, if human judges executed a divideand-conquer strategy, in which they relied on the model advice, ignored the modeled information and spent their cognitive effort assessing unmodeled information and adjusting the advice for it, then they should have better performance than the typical approach of attending to all types of information. The divide-and-conquer strategy is normative in accordance with bounded rationality in that a human judge would want to apply his or her limited cognitive resources to the part of the problem in which human intuition would yield the greatest contribution. It is also normative in that it avoids the least effective component of the judgment process, which is applying human intuition to the modeled information to secondguess the model.

Given the human tendency to process all available information, however, we anticipated that human judges would not apply a divide-and-conquer strategy without instruction and assistance. Indeed, Study 2 demonstrated that, left to their own devices, human judges spend a fair portion of their energy attempting to duplicate the model's data processing, energy which could be better spent in more carefully analyzing and assessing available unmodeled information.

Study 2 found that judgment strategies tending toward the divide-and-conquer strategy (as measured by the proportion of time spent looking at the unmodeled information) were associated with better judgment performance. Study 2 also found that providing instruction and a DSS feature to assist with the divide-and-conquer strategy led to strategies closer to the divide-and-conquer strategy and improved performance. Interestingly, instruction alone appeared to encourage attempting the divide-andconquer strategy (measured by an increase in proportion of time spent looking at unmodeled information) but the attempt did not improve performance. Only the combination of the strategy instruction with the DSS feature enabled the divide-andconquer strategy to result in better performance.

Our study suggests that the divide-and-conquer strategy can be easily facilitated with a simple instruction and DSS feature. It incorporates the goals recommended by Hoch and Schkade [20], i.e., designing decision support systems that use the computer where the computer is strong and the human is weak (computational processing of large amounts of data), and using the human where the human is strong and the computer is weak (pattern matching and assessing the import of new data). Our approach achieves these goals while alleviating the bias introduced by the human tendency to process all types of available salient information. The combination of the DSS feature and instruction is an example of deliberate “decisional guidance” in a DSS [32].

Several observations concerning the limitations of this study are in order. First, we are not inferring from study 1 of student participants the judgment behavior of practicing bank loan officers, but rather how human judges behave when making decisions about which they know something but are not experts. Accordingly, students are suitable subjects for this type of study. Nonetheless we point out that a self-perception of having greater expertise can lead to a greater tendency to reassess modeled information in lieu of relying on model advice [1].

Second, this study applies to situations where all three types of information are salient. Hoch and Schkade [20] showed that if unmodeled information is made salient (by being presented on the DSS computer screen), then decision makers can process the information using pattern matching to detect patterns and use this information to their advantage. Psychological studies have shown that information can be become “non-salient” for numerous reasons, e.g., changes in the figure–ground relationship can cause information to be hidden in the background [8], different ways of framing the problem can alter which data the decision maker considers most relevant [43], mismatches between the decision maker's problem representation or mental model and data may cause data to be ignored [29], and over-reliance on knowledge structures may determine which data are sought [9]. Thus, we offer the caution that for the divide-and-conquer strategy to be elicited, its input data must be salient.

Third, while our computerized process tracing method (in Study 2) could tell what information participants were looking at it could not clearly determine why they were looking at the information. They may have obtained modeled information (1) to process the information in lieu of using the advice or (2) to assess the quality of the regression advice as they decided whether to use it. Our finding in Study 2 that participants averaged more than half of the data acquisition time looking at modeled information over ten trials supports option 1, but we cannot rule out any processing for option 2. Further research will be required for this issue.

Fourth, this study applies to situations where the modeled factors are compensatory, so that an increase in one desirable factor offsets a corresponding decrease in another, and no unmodeled factor strongly interacts with single modeled factors, but rather with the whole set of modeled factors. We believe this would be the usual situation, since models are typically created by the model builders to be robust and parsimonious, and unmodeled information typically represents unique conditions or news that would change how the model advice should be understood.

It is possible that human judges are on the lookout for interactions among various factors in the information set and that this search is one reason they might consider all types of information. The human ability to process interactions among factors is questionable, as exemplified by the decision maker's shift to non-compensatory decision strategies in all but the smallest data sets [30]. The feeling that interactions are being successfully processed is probably a misperception based on processing one or two extreme factors, as human judges are prone to do. Judgments using only one or two strong factors often “feel right” but are also often wrong because of the evidence they ignore [31]. Moreover, given that model advice is an artifact not occurring in the natural world, it is reasonable that humans, who developed their decision strategies in the natural world, should want to consider all the evidence and look for such things as substitutable factors (to enable a factor to be ignored) and redundant factors (to increase the level of confidence in some factor). The introduction of model advice, however, changes all this, and these human tendencies that functioned rationally when model advice was absent become biased when model advice is present [44].

Finally, the human judge might want to satisfy two concerns before relying exclusively on the divide-andconquer strategy. The first is to make sure the model is valid, either by relying on the reputation of the model builder or by checking the model against modeled information. Probably decision makers are prone to do this; we note that even in the condition that most encouraged the divide-and-conquer strategy (condition 5 in experiment 2) participants still spent some of their time looking at modeled information. If a model could be validated “once and for all” perhaps decision makers could use the model to shift even closer to the divideand-conquer strategy. The second concern is to address the possibility of interactions between unmodeled factors and specific modeled factors. This could be accomplished in a DSS pre-screening to help the user decide whether the divide-and-conquer strategy is appropriate, or if interactions between modeled and unmodeled information should be considered instead. Alternatively, the interaction adjustments could be incorporated into the DSS itself, in a version only slightly modified from the one used in experiment 2 of this research. The decision maker could provide factor adjustments, rather than an overall adjustment to model advice.

## 5. Conclusion

Our experiments produced evidence that decision makers attend and give weight to all types of available information and that this attention and weighting can cause their judgment performance to fall short of its potential. Our hypothesis that the divide-and-conquer strategy can be encouraged through a combination of computerized decision support feature and simple instruction was also supported. Moreover, encouraging the divide-and-conquer strategy led to better performance.

The approach of dividing labor so that human and computer capitalize on their strengths and compensate for their weaknesses appears desirable, rational, and feasible. Nonetheless, our research has shown that merely providing model advice, modeled information, and unmodeled information without guidance or assistance is an approach to the human–computer relationship that fails considerably in enabling this relationship to realize its full potential because of bias due to the human tendency to weigh all cues rather than use the divide-and-conquer strategy. Our study also indicates that this bias can be significantly overcome by a simple instruction and DSS feature. Artifices that encourage the strategy are easy to implement and adapt for specific situations. Such artifices could be beneficially applied in a wide variety of important judgment contexts.

These findings have important implications for builders of models and computerized decision support systems, as well as for the decision makers who use them. Builders of models or decision support systems should consider adding features that encourage the divide-and-conquer strategy, such as computerized decision support features and instructions similar to those used in this research. Users of model advice should remember or be trained to know where the relative strengths and weaknesses of human and computer lie, and make a conscious effort to avoid replicating the work of the computer, spending their energy, instead, deciding whether and by how much model advice should be adjusted. Our research suggests that following these recommendations would lead to better decision making performance.
