## Introduction

Today, market competition forces firms to make quick moves and countermoves to gain first mover advantages or destroy and neutralize rivals’ competitive advantages (D’Aveni 1994). The ability to identify market opportunities and to mobilize or develop resources for effective exploitation of those opportunities is an essential prerequisite for gaining competitive advantage. This dynamic competitive firm behavior is observable and can be studied by examining the patterns of actions and reactions of firms over time (Miller and Chen 1996a, b; Grimm and Smith 1997; Ferrier 2001).

Competitive firm behavior has been found to be influenced by a number of organizational and strategic factors, for example, characteristics of top management teams (TMTs) (Hambrick et al. 1996, Ferrier 2001), firm size and industry experience (Miller and

Chen 1996b), firm resources (Miller and Chen 1994, Hambrick et al. 1996, Ferrier 2001, Ferrier et al. 2002), customer and competitor diversity in the market (Miller and Chen 1996b), and market growth (Miller and Chen 1996b). Recently, scholars have begun to examine how competitive firm behavior could be influenced by the relational context in which a firm is embedded (Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001). As the knowledge base of an industry becomes increasingly large and complex and expertise and resources needed to develop new products are broadly distributed (Powell et al. 1996), firms increasingly depend on external collaboration networks to obtain needed competencies and resources to compete against rivals. Because of this increased collaborative activity, firms are often embedded in a complex network of alliances.

Recent research suggests that the structure of alliance networks is an important determinant of firms’ potential to access valuable knowledge and resources which, in turn, enable them to outcompete rivals (Gulati 1998, Gulati et al. 2000). Some researchers emphasize the advantages of dense network structure in which all partners of a focal firm closely collaborate with one another. Dense network structure provides incentives for efficient exchange of information and other resources among network members by fostering trust and imposing sanctions against opportunistic behavior of network members (Coleman 1988). Alternatively, other researchers argue that a sparse network structure rich in structural holes is beneficial to the firms. Structural holes refer to the absence of ties between two network clusters. When a firm spans structural holes, it enjoys preferential access to unique information from disconnected parts of the network, which increases its ability to discover entrepreneurial opportunities. Empirical research has found support for both arguments. On the one hand, firms embedded in a dense network exhibit greater likelihood of survival (Uzzi 1997) and stronger innovative performance (Ahuja 2000). On the other hand, firms that span more structural holes enjoy higher revenue growth (Baum et al. 2000) and greater market share (Zaheer and Bell 2005).

In this paper, we examine how these two contrasting types of network structure affect competitive behavior of firms. We argue that both sparse network structure rich in structural holes (which we simply refer to as sparse network structure hereafter) and dense network structure provide firms with important strategic resources to compete aggressively. However, which type of network structure is more beneficial to a given firm will depend on the firm’s capability to effectively use IT. Over the last 20 years, firms have increasingly used IT to create and manage their interfirm networks. Many emergent digital systems enable firms in the interorganizational network to share data, automate transactions and business processes, and manage interdependencies among them. For example, Toyota Motor uses an Internetbased Web portal, Dealer Daily, to link its 1,300 dealers with Toyota factories, parts suppliers, and distributors for improving parts ordering, sales, and vehicle repair operations, as well as reducing production costs and cycle time to speed up new vehicles to market (Computerworld 2000). General Motors uses design collaboration, product life-cycle management, and graphics tools to tie as many as 10,000 tier-one, tier-two, and tier-three suppliers into one network to speed up product to market (InformationWeek 2002). These are just a few examples that illustrate the profound impact of interfirm networks, digital systems, and their interplay on competitive firm behavior and competitive dynamics in the market.

A few prior studies (e.g., Chi et al. 2007a, b, 2008) have explored the relationships among the structural properties of interorganizational networks (e.g., network centrality and structural equivalence), IT use, and competitive behavior. They have empirically demonstrated the importance of network structure, IT, and their interplay on competitive firm behavior. These prior studies have urged conduction of more research on the complex interplay between different types of network structure and IT and their effects on competitive behavior of firms. In this study, we focus on the sparse-versus-dense network structure of interorganizational networks and aim to theoretically and empirically investigate how these contrasting types of network structure interact with IT to influence firm competitive behavior. We extend prior research in three major aspects. First, we develop a comprehensive theory, using Chen’s (1996) awarenessmotivation-capability (AMC) framework as an integrative theoretical hook to link separate perspectives from social networks, information systems (IS), and competitive dynamics. We argue that sparse network structure affects competitive firm behavior primarily by increasing firms’ awareness of opportunities for developing new competitive actions, whereas dense network structure works mainly by increasing firms ability to actually undertake the competitive actions. We posit that the use of IT can complement each type of network structure, thus enhancing firms’ awareness, motivation, and capability to act. Second, our paper identifies the role of IT as an important moderator of the relationship between each type of network structure and competitive firm behavior. We argue that firms can benefit from both types of network structure, but the extent to which they exploit their network positions is contingent upon their use of IT. In doing so, our study adds insights into the debate on the beneficial effects of brokerage (Burt 1992) versus closure (Coleman 1988). Finally, our study provides an extensive empirical examination of the aforementioned relationships longitudinally, using a sample of

170 firm-year observations for 12 major automakers over 16 years (from 1988 to 2003).

## Theory and Hypotheses

Building on the “Austrian” economics’ conceptualization of the marketplace as a process through which firms continuously strive to outdo rivals (Hayek 1948, Mises 1949, Kirzner 1973), research in competitive dynamics has developed theory and methods for examining competitive actions and reactions of firms over time (Smith et al. 2001). Within this research stream, competitive action is defined as any externally oriented, specific, observable, market-oriented competitive move (such as new product introduction, creative advertising, product improvement, new market entry, price cuts, and sale promotions) that a firm undertakes to improve its market position (Smith et al. 2001). In highly competitive environments, performance differences across firms can be explained not so much by analyzing their existing market positions but rather by examining their competitive activity over time (D’Aveni 1994). Firms need to possess a dynamic ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure their internal and external competences (Teece et al. 1997) to more frequently attack rivals with a wide range of competitive actions. This intensive competitive activity can lead not only to frequent creation of first mover advantages but also can erode or neutralize rivals’ advantages (Grimm and Smith 1997). As a result, firms that introduce a wide range of competitive actions (more intensively than rivals) are able to frequently disrupt the status quo in the market and create a series of temporary competitive advantages (D’Aveni 1994, Ferrier et al. 1999). This pattern of aggressive competitive activity, in turn, leads to greater market share and profits (e.g., D’Aveni 1994, Young et al. 1996, Miller and Chen 1996b, Ferrier et al. 1999, Ferrier 2001).

Competitive dynamics research has developed theory and empirical methods that examine competitive actions generally at two distinct levels of analysis: (1) action-reaction dyad, which focuses on actions and responses of pairs of competing firms (Chen et al. 1992, Ferrier et al. 1999) and (2) action repertoire, which examines an entire set of competitive actions carried out by a firm over a finite time period, such as a repertoire year (Miller and Chen 1994, 1996a; Ferrier et al. 1999). In this paper, we are interested in examining how network structure and IT interact to affect firms’ action repertoire over time. Competitive dynamics research has examined three recognizable patterns of competitive actions: action volume, action complexity, and action heterogeneity.

Action volume denotes the total number of competitive actions carried out by a firm in a given year (Chen and Hambrick 1995) and is found to have the strongest and most consistent impact on firm performance (Smith et al. 1991; Chen and Hambrick 1995; Hambrick et al. 1996; Young et al. 1996; Ferrier et al. 1999, 2002; Ferrier 2001). For example, researchers have shown that a firm that initiates more competitive actions (action volume) than rivals enjoys greater market share and profits (e.g., Young et al. 1996, Ferrier et al. 1999). Action complexity reflects the extent to which a series of competitive actions carried out by a firm in a given year encompasses a wide range of different action types (Ferrier 2001). For example, researchers have shown that firms that carry out a wider range of competitive actions experience better performance than firms that compete with simple competitive repertoires (which are characterized by a narrow range of action types), especially in highly competitive environments (Miller and Chen 1996a, Ferrier et al. 1999). Finally, action heterogeneity reflects the extent to which the entire set of competitive actions carried out by a firm in a given year deviates from the industry norm (Miller and Chen 1996b) and is found to have a strong influence on changing market shares and shifting rules of competition (Ferrier et al. 1999, Gimeno 1999). For example, researchers have found that a firm can benefit from initiating actions that deviate from industry norms in markets with high competitor and customer diversity (Miller and Chen 1996b).

Given the importance of these findings for explaining firm performance differences, it is critical to understand why some firms and not others exhibit such patterns of competitive behavior. Researchers have explored several factors that enable firms to carry out more complex and heterogeneous competitive actions. For instance, firm size has been found to influence the number of actions firms undertake (Chen and Hambrick 1995, Young et al. 1996). Other studies have found that important characteristics of firms’ TMTs, such as TMT size, tenure, and educational and functional background heterogeneity, impact firms’ likelihood to initiate competitive actions (Hambrick et al. 1996) and also moderate the relationship between action complexity and performance (Ferrier and Lyon 2004).

Research at the dyad level analysis has extensively applied Chen’s AMC framework (1996) for explaining firms’ likelihood of attack and response. Because of researchers’ focus on predicting actions and responses between a pair of rivals, the main stimulus for a focal firm to act or react is assumed to be the expected behavior of a specific rival (Chen et al. 1992, Chen 1996). For example, a focal firm can expect rivals to react quickly when they are aware of the focal firm’s actions and are capable and motivated to react (Chen et al. 1992). Likewise, the speed of a focal firm’s response to rivals’ attacks is affected by the firm’s awareness of rivals’ actions and the firm’s capability and motivation to react (Yu and Cannella 2007). Our focus in this paper is not necessarily on how a pair of rivals will respond to each other but rather on how a focal firm’s access to network resources and use of IT systems increases its ability to develop and introduce new competitive actions. Thus we extend the AMC framework to the action repertoire level of analysis by examining how a firm’s network structure interacts with its use of IT systems to jointly influence the firm’s competitive behavior through increasing the firm’s awareness of opportunities for developing new competitive actions and its capability and motivation to act on the identified opportunities. Here, we view the three behavioral drivers as unobservable mechanisms that underlie competitive actions and use the AMC framework as a theoretical hook to integrate separate perspectives of social networks, IS, and competitive dynamics to explain why the two antecedents (i.e., network structure and IT) affect firms’ competitive behavior. ## Network Structure and Competitive Action

Sparse vs. Dense Network Structure. Research in interfirm networks has identified two types of network structure that increase firms’ potential for accessing external resources: sparse network structure (Burt 1992) and dense network structure (Coleman 1988). These two types of network structure affect firms’ performance through different mechanisms. Firms embedded in sparse networks rich in structural holes enjoy access to many brokerage opportunities (Burt 1992). An structural hole exists between two firms that are connected through another firm and thus do not have direct links with each other. An structural hole separates nonredundant sources of information, “sources that are more additive than overlapping” (Burt 2005). A firm can obtain the greatest network benefits when it positions itself in the structural hole between two closed networks. Information circulates more easily within the closed networks than across network clusters. Thus, firms that span distinct and disconnected networks are likely to access unique and novel information (Burt 1992). In addition, sparse networks rich in structural holes provide opportunities for accessing a wide variety of contacts that expose hole-spanning firms to diverse perspectives and encourage them to develop independent and innovative thoughts and actions (Granovetter 1973, 1985). As such, firms spanning structural holes are less susceptible to pressures to conform to the de facto norms (Homans 1950, Pfeffer and Salancik 1978, Powell 1991). However, the presence of structural holes in sparse networks creates long paths (through indirect ties) and makes the network less integrative. Coordination among multiple network partners who are not directly tied to each other may require significant effort. Thus, by spanning the structural hole a firm is able to create a wide network with a large number of relatively weak ties for information (Granovetter 1973, 1985; Burt 1992). Information that flows through this network is often explicit, broad, and diverse, for example, information that is highly scattered in the market.

Dense network structure, on the other hand, is characterized by a high degree of interconnectedness among network members (Coleman 1988). Dense networks tend to homogenize information by creating redundant paths among close-knit participants. Thus they may block participants from seeing new opportunities outside of their network (Gargiulo and Benassi 2000). However, dense networks provide incentives for developing strong norms and effective sanctions that prevent network members from violating the norms. Network members are connected through common third parties that serve as an incentive for conforming to the established norms and for presenting a cooperative image (Krackhardt 1998). In addition, dense networks facilitate effective coordination among partners. Prior studies (e.g., Krackhardt 1998) have shown that ties cemented through common third parties are significantly more stable than ties involving individuals lacking third-party connections. Familiarity with long-term partners breeds strong bonds of mutual understanding and trust that greatly facilitate cooperation and coordination and encourage joint problem solving (Gulati 1995). As a result, information that flows through this network is often tacit, focused, and complex, for example, information and knowledge shared in the product development process.

We argue that the two contrasting types of network structure entail different informational, normative, and coordinative implications for firms’ competitive behavior. Specifically, sparse networks provide significant informational advantages and thus help increase firms’ awareness of opportunities for developing new competitive actions, although they may not help strengthen firms’ capability to undertake competitive actions. In contrast, dense networks provide significant normative and coordinative benefits and thus help enhance firms’ capability for undertaking competitive actions, while they may not help increase firms awareness of new opportunities for conceiving competitive actions.

In the next section, we examine the role of IT in the interfirm network context. We argue that firms can use IT to complement both types of network structure to increase all three behavioral drivers for competitive actions. We first provide the rationale for the main effects of sparse versus dense network structure on competitive action and then examine the role of IT in moderating the effects of network structure on competitive action.

Sparse Network Structure and Competitive Action. Firms spanning structural holes are likely to be exposed to diverse perspectives, new ideas, and information that is highly scattered in the market. The more holes spanned, the richer the information benefits of the network (Burt 2000). Rich information, such as information about environmental changes, rivals’ moves in the market and their implications, opportunities for market growth, new possibilities for market-oriented competitive moves, and innovation opportunities for creating new products and services, provides value and is extremely useful (Daft and Lengel 1984). On the one hand, fast access to such information increases firms’ awareness of opportunities for developing and introducing new innovative competitive actions. Increased awareness of changes in the customer preferences and technological possibilities enables firms to discover opportunities for offering superior value to customers by initiating a wide range of competitive actions. On the other hand, awareness of action opportunities increases firms’ motivation to act on identified opportunities. Awareness of competitors’ moves increases firms’ motivation to compete aggressively and initiate many competitive actions. Firms that are well informed about rivals’ competitive moves are either motivated to act proactively and preempt rivals from gaining market share or to respond quickly to rivals’ actions and neutralize the impact of their actions. Thus we expect that firms that span more structural holes than those that do not will be likely to undertake a larger number of, a greater variety of, and more innovative competitive actions.

Hypothesis 1A (H1A). Access to structural holes in a firm’s network will have a positive relationship with action volume.

Hypothesis 1B (H1B). Access to structural holes in a firm’s network will have a positive relationship with action complexity.

Hypothesis 1C (H1C). Access to structural holes in a firm’s network will have a positive relationship with action heterogeneity.

Dense Network Structure and Competitive Action. Dense networks affect firms’ competitive behavior through a different mechanism. Dense networks, by having direct relationships among close-knit partners, allow information to flow fast, reliably, and accurately in the network. Fast and reliable information flows enhance firms’ execution speed of actions once they perceive opportunities. In addition, dense networks foster trust, shared understanding, and cooperation. When firms compete with modular products (such as automobiles) that consist of interrelated components manufactured and supplied by independent firms, the need for intensive communication and exchange of information and ideas among network members is crucial for continuous product improvements and updates. Dense networks increase firms’ capability of coordinating moves with partners to undertake many competitive actions (action volume) in a fast and efficient manner. In addition, because dense networks discourage opportunistic behavior of network members, partners are more willing to invest in transaction-specific assets (Williamson 1975) and share proprietary knowledge and resources. As a result, we expect that firms that are embedded in dense networks will be better able to orchestrate joined effort and deploy resources of their network members, thus having greater capability to initiate many different types of competitive actions (such as product improvements, new product versions, and capacity extensions). Greater capability is likely to further motivate firms in dense networks to carry out repertoires of competitive actions with greater volume and complexity.

Hypothesis 2A (H2A). Network density in a firm’s network will have a positive relationship with action volume.

Hypothesis 2B (H2B). Network density in a firm’s network will have a positive relationship with action complexity.

However, dense networks decrease firms’ motivation to undertake competitive actions in an innovative manner that deviates from industry norms or well-accepted conventions. Dense networks tend to homogenize information by having many network members directly connecting to each other and thus are likely to delimit the awareness of embedded firms from seeing new opportunities for innovative actions. Furthermore, because of the presence of strong norms and effective sanctions for violating the norms, firms that are embedded in dense networks are more likely to adhere to old conventions of behavior and undertake actions in a repeated manner, compared to firms embedded in relatively sparse networks. Additionally, the socializing influence of cohesive ties within the dense network tends to increase the firms’ dependence for resources and acceptance by their closeknit partners (Burt 1987, Erickson 1988). Thus, firms in dense networks tend to seek legitimacy by doing the same things that others do and will be less motivated to seek opportunities for undertaking actions that would depart markedly from others.

Hypothesis 2C (H2C). Network density in a firm’s network will have a negative relationship with action heterogeneity.

## Moderating Role of IT

A large body of work in IS has argued and demonstrated that IT is a strategic resource that can be leveraged by firms to create competitive advantage. Recent conceptual work has suggested that IT brings a set of firm capabilities that enhances firms’ ability to sense their environment and respond to opportunities and threats speedily (Malone et al. 1987, Weill et al. 2002, Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Overby et al. 2006). We refer to this set of firm capabilities as IT-enabled capability. Several technologies have been identified that, when adopted, provide firms the options to sense and respond.

A firm’s sensing capability is enhanced when it deploys IT to harness informational resources and convert them into knowledge assets (Overby et al. 2006). IT-enabled sensing capability achieved through using knowledge-oriented technologies such as groupware, electronic mail, video conferencing, instant messaging, and chat significantly increases communication frequency and enhances the effectiveness of socialization efforts among firms. Frequent communications facilitate the process of articulating and codifying tacit and personal knowledge and, therefore, may enhance firms’ ability to identify and interpret market trends and shifts. Moreover, in today’s digitized business environments, every activity leaves information as a by-product (Zuboff 1988) that can potentially be analyzed to gain insights about the business. This overabundance of information outstrips human ability to process it. IT-enabled sensing capability increases firms’ ability to harness this information and benefit from it. Furthermore, a firm’s sensing capability is enhanced when it uses digital systems to connect with external entities. Participation in electronic forums and marketplaces enables firms to easily identify and maintain a large number of contacts. In this regard, IT-enabled sensing capability increases firms’ ability to obtain timely information when this information is unevenly distributed among firms in the market (Uzzi and Lancaster 2003). For example,

American Airlines’ uses a decision support system in conjunction with its reservation system to increase a firm’s sensing capability, which enables American Airlines to develop co-specialized knowledge and expertise with its channel partners and enhances the firm’s market intelligence that can be acted on to improve performance (Christiaanse and Venkatraman 2002).

A firm’s responding capability is enhanced when it uses IT to support its business processes across business functions and manages interdependencies among business partners. IT-enabled responding capability enables firms to mobilize and deploy resources in support of strategic actions. Process-oriented technologies enable easy coordination and quick response because of information visibility and enhance the inherent flexibility of firms. For example, the use of computeraided manufacturing (CAM) has made manufacturing capacity flexible, and the use of automation tools for design (CAD) has made product development more modular and flexible (Sanchez 1995). Similarly, the use of inventory management systems has enabled firms to flexibly deploy their warehouse capacities, and the use of Internet-based sales systems has opened up new market channels that are inherently more flexible than traditional channels. Moreover, firms that have digitized their processes could increase their business degrees of freedom when confronted with market opportunities and threats (Gosain et al. 2004). For example, extensive business process digitization allows American Airlines to aggressively respond with price changes to specific routes when competitors announce promotional fares on routes served by American Airlines (Copeland and McKenney 1988). Digitization of customer boarding processes through the deployment of appropriate software allows Delta Airlines to be more responsive to customer service needs (Ross and Beath 2002). Similarly, Dell has the capacity to dynamically alter the mix of price, promotion, and products it offers based on the component inventory levels in its supply chain (Magretta 1998). Such a capacity to match demand to supply is, in large part, because of the digital systems that link Dell and its partners in a supply network. This capacity is determined by the extensive digitization of business processes that allows information sharing and coordination.

The above discussions suggest that IT-enabled capability may complement sparse-versus-dense network structures differently through IT-enabled sensing and responding capabilities to influence the behavioral drivers of competitive actions. We next elaborate on how IT-enabled capability interacts with network structure to jointly affect competitive actions.

IT-Enabled Capability, Sparse Network Structure, and Competitive Action. Firms spanning structural holes in sparse networks, by connecting to partners with heterogeneous knowledge domains, resources, and capabilities, are likely to face increased information flow and complexity in managing the interdependencies among diverse partners. Increased information flow increases firms’ awareness of entrepreneurial opportunities if harnessed effectively. Unless firms have the capacity to deal with this increased information flow and complexity, they are unlikely to attain the full benefits of spanning structural holes. Firms with a high IT-enabled sensing capability possess the capacity to deal with the volume and complexity of the information across their networks. For example, business intelligence applications greatly increase a firm’s ability to quickly analyze and interpret this information and gain insights about new market opportunities for new product and service offerings, new production possibilities for cost reduction and efficiency improvement, and intention behind and potential consequences of competitors’ actions. Electronic markets can connect many different buyers and suppliers through a central database and allow a firm to compare the offerings of many different potential suppliers quickly and inexpensively (Malone et al. 1987, Premkumar et al. 2005). They also enable firms to economically obtain timely information about price, availability, and product offerings of many suppliers in the marketplace. Thus, a firm’s IT-enabled sensing capability complements the awareness-enhancing potential of sparse networks and enables effective sense making of the myriad opportunities afforded by spanning structural holes, that are not yet easily identifiable without sophisticated analytical and visualization tools.

While IT-enabled sensing is beneficial, firms spanning structural holes might find IT-enabled responding capability to be critical, as it compensates for the coordination difficulties inherent in sparse networks. As discussed earlier, because sparse networks comprise relatively long paths with partners who are not connected to each other, coordination in sparse networks is challenging. For these reasons, process integration with partners across the network could be limited. IT-enabled responding capability is likely to increase firms’ capability to coordinate effectively among various partners and thus exploit more fully the opportunities available in sparse networks. Process digitization coupled with the use of process standards allows firms to develop a plug-and-play capability in which they can connect with diverse external partners as needed without elaborate integration effort (Oh et al. 2007). Such IT-enabled integration becomes necessary for coordination, especially when firms have a need to harness the resources of their partners connected through indirect ties (Basu and Blanning 2003, Zammuto et al. 2007). As such, IT-enabled responding capability benefits sparse network structure by increasing firms’ coordinative capabilities and, thus, firms’ capability to implement competitive actions.

Taken together, IT-enabled capability complements sparse network structure by further increasing firms’ awareness of opportunities and strengthening firms capability to undertake competitive actions. Increased awareness and capability further increase firms’ motivation to act.

Hypothesis 3A (H3A). The positive effect of a firm’s access to structural holes on action volume will be strengthened with a greater IT-enabled capability of the firm.

Hypothesis 3B (H3B). The positive effect of a firm’s access to structural holes on action complexity will be strengthened with a greater IT-enabled capability of the firm.

Hypothesis 3C (H3C). The positive effect of a firm’s access to structural holes on action heterogeneity will be strengthened with a greater IT-enabled capability of the firm.

IT-Enabled Capability, Dense Network Structure, and Competitive Action. Firms embedded in dense networks need to spend a great amount of time and effort in coordinating and managing interdependencies among partners in joint business activities and processes. IT-enabled responding capability is manifest in the process-oriented IT applications and the number and variety of business processes that are digitized and linked. Digitization enhances firms’ capacity to manage interdependencies between diverse partners. Diverse IT applications that support multiple functional areas provide firms the options to act on these information exchange opportunities without incurring undue costs (Tanriverdi 2006). For instance, process-oriented technologies (e.g., CAD/CAM, supply chain management systems) will greatly help tighten the integration and manage the interdependencies among partners and joint activities. CAD/CAM allows both design and manufacturing engineers to access and manipulate their respective data to test potential designs and to create products more acceptable to both sides and also speed up the product to market (Malone et al. 1987). Supply chain management systems (SCM) linking to suppliers’ inventory systems allow suppliers to replenish needed parts and components in time for production or sales and to reduce inventory costs. Thus, ITenabled responding capability complements the coordination capacity in dense networks and greatly increases firms’ capability for undertaking synergized moves with linked partners.

Furthermore, IT-enabled sensing capability enhances the awareness of firms in dense networks by compensating for the lack of exposure to diverse information inherent in these networks. The use of knowledge-oriented technologies not only increases firms’ access to information highly scattered in the market but also increases the speed of acquiring, processing, and interpreting this information at a low cost. For instance, the use of e-community of practice, Web 2.0 technology, knowledge repository, and business intelligence tools increases firms’ ability to scan and collect information broadly from the competitive environment, going beyond closely tied partners, and thus increases firms’ ability to discover new opportunities. Also, the use of data mining and simulation tools allows firms to systematically analyze and identify patterns in customer behavior, which leads to frequent discovery of new ways for satisfying customer needs and, thus, opportunities for various types of competitive actions. As a result, IT-enabled sensing capability mitigates the limitation of information homogenization in dense networks and thus increases firms’ awareness of new opportunities for competitive actions.

In addition, as the intensity of information exchanges among densely connected partners increases, the use of knowledge-oriented technologies not only facilitates intensified communications among partners but also provides rich media (e.g., visualization tools, groupware, video conferencing) to help firms cope with fine-grained information exchanges among close-knit partners. Thus, IT-enabled sensing capability facilitates joint effort in complex problem solving and encourages closely tied partners to experiment with new practices and product innovations while reducing the risks and coordination costs involved in joint activities (Uzzi and Lancaster 2003). As such, IT-enabled sensing capability provides the potential to increase firms’ awareness of new opportunities for carrying out innovative actions jointly with closeknit partners in dense networks. Taken together, ITenabled capability benefits firms in dense networks by increasing their awareness of new opportunities and the capability for undertaking competitive actions. Therefore we expect a positive interaction between dense network structure and IT-enabled capability on action volume and action complexity.

Hypothesis 4A (H4A). The positive effect of network density on action volume will be strengthened with greater IT-enabled capability.

Hypothesis 4B (H4B). The positive effect of network density on action complexity will be strengthened with greater IT-enabled capability.

The increased exposure to diverse information through the use of IT broadens the opportunity set firms in dense networks consider using. This exposure, coupled with firms in dense networks having the capacity to initiate joint actions effectively, could motivate firms to consider actions that typically may not have been part of their repertoire. As network density is expected to be negatively related to firms undertaking of innovative actions (H2C), IT-enabled capability is likely to negatively affect the relationship between network density and action heterogeneity by reducing the negative effect of dense networks on action heterogeneity.

Hypothesis 4C (H4C). The negative effect of network density on action heterogeneity will be mitigated with greater IT-enabled capability.

## Methods

## Sample Selection

We tested our hypotheses on a sample of firms from the global automobile industry (SIC 3711) that sell autos in the U.S. market. Following the sampling criteria used in prior competitive dynamics research (e.g., Smith et al. 1991, Chen et al. 1992), the automobile industry has clearly identifiable boundaries that provide an adequate setting both for comparable examination of the competitive activity of firms and for a clear specification of the boundaries of the interfirm network. The auto industry consists of very large, visible, and newsworthy firms and has long exhibited intense competitive interaction (using a wide range of competitive actions such as new product introductions, product improvements, clever advertising campaigns, predictable end-ofmodel-year price cuts, or flexible financing options). In addition, automakers extensively collaborate with suppliers, distributors, manufacturers, and competitors, and thus the structure of the alliance networks in which they are embedded is an important factor for understanding differences in firms’ competitive activity. Finally, automakers increasingly use various IT applications to reduce production costs and product life cycle and to improve the degree of coordination and exchange of information among network partners (Computerworld 2000; InformationWeek 1991).

We selected automakers that participate in the passenger cars segment. Compared with other segments such as Van/Minivan, Trucks, or SUV segments, the passenger car segment is characterized by a wide variety of vehicle makes, models, and model line extensions and spans a wide range of prices from \$12,000 to \$250,000. Our selection of this segment also provides a manageable scope for data collection using content analysis of published news articles related to each firm’s competitive actions.

## Competitive Action Data

Consistent with prior research in competitive dynamics (cf. Smith et al. 2001), we used structured content analysis to capture the competitive actions. This method comprises techniques for reducing qualitative text to a unit-by-variable matrix and enables researchers to quantitatively test hypotheses (Jauch et al. 1980, Denzin and Lincoln 2000). First, using the Kelly Blue Book (www.kbb.com), we identified the complete range of vehicle models in the passenger cars segment for each automaker in our sample. Then, we used Factiva to search for all published news articles and announcements about competitive actions and alliances that the 12 major automakers carried out over a 16-year time window (1988–2003).<sup>1</sup>

From the universe of news items in the Factiva database (www.Factiva.com), we applied the following search criteria within the Factiva search engine: auto company names, vehicle model names (all passenger car models listed in Kelly Blue Book), and panel time markers (i.e., date range from 01/01/1988 through 12/31/2003). This first-stage data collection process yielded an initial pool of more than 12,000 news items. Given that our study examines the relationship between alliances and competitive actions, we naturally excluded news reports that related to internal or corporate-level actions such as the formation of alliances (cf. Miller and Chen 1994, Offstein and Gnyawali 2005) and limited our definition of competitive actions to those that are externally directed and market based. Beyond this definitional constraint, we did not restrict our typology in terms of industry specificity or the number of different categories. With this in mind, we randomly selected 10% of the final set of news articles and, in consultation with two industry insiders/experts, used them to inductively and iteratively identify all possible categories of externally directed and market-based competitive actions.<sup>2</sup>

Our action coding process yielded the following six action categories: pricing (e.g., announcement of price cuts and sales incentives), marketing (e.g., announcement of new advertising and promotional campaign), product improvements (e.g., improved engine performances, redesigned interior), new product versions (e.g., new generation of an existing vehicle model), new product introductions (e.g., introduction of new vehicle model), and market expansion (e.g., entry into new international markets, building new manufacturing facility). Our typology is very similar to both classifications developed by Ferrier et al. (1999), which included six industry-indifferent action types and classifications developed by Yu and Cannella (2007), which included six industry-specific action types. We identified 4,832 competitive actions. Firms in our sample, on average, initiated 28 competitive actions per year (min 0 to max 112). Table A.1 shown in the appendix provides a complete list of our competitive action categories, as well as news examples of competitive action types and the earliest date of the news announcement. Two expert coders separately coded the news articles based on our defined coding typology. To test the reliability of our coding, we randomly selected 1% of the articles, which the two expert coders independently recoded. Using the procedure of Perreault and Leigh (1989) to estimate the reliability of our coding, we achieved a reliability index of 0.82, which exceeds the convention of 0.70 (Denzin and Lincoln 2000).

## Alliance Network Data

We collected complete alliance network data for the automakers in our sample. We used the SDC database (a product of Thomson Financial) as a main source for collecting alliance data. We used “Joint Ventures/ Strategic Alliances” section of the SDC database to access data on newly formed alliances. Compared with other similar data sources (e.g., MERIT-CATI, CORE, RECAP, or Bioscan), the SDC database is the most comprehensive (Schilling 2009). We first identified all alliances formed by our 12 automakers from 1988 to 2003. This initial search generated 1,471 unique partners. Then, we searched for alliances formed by these 1,471 partners for the same period. This additional search produced 37,520 alliances formed among both automakers and their partners. This approach enabled us to capture the extent to which an automaker’s partners are connected with one another, which is critical for reliable measurement of structural hole and network density.

We created matrices for each year for the period from 1988 to 2003. We followed prior research and used the moving-window approach to create matrices. This approach reduces the potential bias in the alliance data because of often-underreported data on alliance termination. In addition, the moving-window approach captures the lagged effect of network structure on the future competitive activity of firms. Prior research has used three- or five-year moving windows (e.g., Stuart 2000, Lavie 2007). In this paper, we chose the more conservative three-year moving window (assuming that the average alliance duration is three years). Thus, each matrix includes alliances formed during the previous three-year period. For example, the matrix for 1988 will include all alliances formed between 01/01/1986 and 12/31/1988; the matrix for 1989 will include alliances formed between 1987 and 1989, and so on. In each three-year period, firms in our sample, on average, maintained 65 alliances (min 1 to max 149).

## IT-Enabled Capability Data

We searched a number of popular computer journals, such as Computerworld, Networkworld, eWeek, ITweek, Infoworld, and InformationWeek, for information about specific IT initiatives of automakers. We gathered a total of 4,738 IT-related news reports from various data sources for the 12 automakers over a 16-year span from 1988 to 2003. We counted all IT technologies that were in use in each year. We assumed that any IT (hardware or software) implemented and used before a certain year would continue to be used unless this technology was reported to be upgraded, replaced, or discontinued in later news reports. We removed the duplicates and nonrelevant IT news announcements that did not directly report a target company’s IT technologies in use (e.g., an IT system that was planned to be implemented, unsuccessful IT investment, or trouble in rolling out an IT system).

We refined the coding schema for the IT variables after several rounds of discussions with experts. Table A.2 shown in the appendix illustrates sample technologies for coding IT news and also gives IT coding examples. To ensure clarity, accuracy, and completeness of categorization, all category characterizations were verified and fine tuned through independent discussions with several academic experts, as well as with IS managers and engineers at Ford, Nissan, and Toyota. The collected data were then coded by three expert coders independently. To check reliability of this coding, 1% of the news were randomly selected and re-coded. The coding reliability was tested using the reliability index of Perreault and Leigh (1989). This test yielded a value of 0.71 for IT data coding.

## Measures

Dependent Variables. Action volume is calculated as the total number of competitive actions initiated by an automaker in a given year. This measure was log-transformed to reduce positive skewness.

Action complexity refers to the extent to which a firm carries out a repertoire of actions that consists of a wide range of action types. We calculated this measure applying Blau’s index (1977):

$$
1 - \sum_ {\alpha} (N _ {\alpha} / \mathrm{NT}) ^ {2},
$$

where $N _ { \alpha }$ represents the number of competitive actions in the th category and NT is the total number of competitive actions across the six action categories discussed above. N /NT represents the proportion of competitive actions in the th action category. High scores indicate that a firm carries out a complex repertoire of competitive actions, whereas low scores indicate that a firm carries out a simple action repertoire (concentrated on a few action categories). To reduce negative skewness of this variable, we raised this variable to the third power.<sup>3</sup>

Action heterogeneity measures how a firm’s actions deviate from the industry norm in a given year. We use the Euclidean distance (Chi et al. 2007a) to compute

$$
S _ {t} (X _ {i}, \overline {{X}}) = \sqrt {\frac {(x _ {i 1} - \overline {{x}} _ {1}) ^ {2} (x _ {i 2} - \overline {{x}} _ {2}) ^ {2} + \cdots + (x _ {i , 6} - \overline {{x}} _ {6}) ^ {2}}{6}}.
$$

We then log-transformed this variable to reduce positive skewness. In the automobile industry, the Department of Transportation and National Highway Transportation Safety Administration publish regulations that always take effect or begin to phase in on September 1st, so most automakers start to produce the following year’s model at the beginning of September in the current year (based on one of the authors’ interviews with a Ford engineer, August 18, 2004). To sell out old car models and boost sales for new models, automakers tend to initiate marketing and sales campaigns and offer big incentives starting in the summer and through year’s end. Additionally, in each year, auto shows roll out regularly in certain months (e.g., the Detroit Auto Show in January, the New York Auto Show in April). As such, in the automobile industry, the industry norm defines a relatively stable or predictable action pattern in which certain action events (such as new model production, marketing campaign, sales promotion, and auto show) are repeated at certain times of each year (Chi et al. 2007a). The mean scores, which were calculated by taking the average of the 12 automakers’ actions across all six action categories in action repertoire, represent the industry norm of actions taken in each year. Thus, a high score of action heterogeneity indicates a highly distinct action pattern that deviates from the annual industry norm in an automaker’s repertoire of actions.

Independent Variables. All independent and control variables were lagged one year vis-à-vis dependent variables to increase our confidence in the causality of the effects.

Structural Holes. We used Burt’s network constraint index (1992) to measure a firm’s access to structural holes. Network constraint measures the extent to which a firm’s contacts are redundant (Burt 1992). We used the following formula to compute network constraint:

$$
C = \sum_ {j} (p _ {i j} + \sum_ {q} p _ {i q} p _ {q j}) ^ {2}, \quad q \neq i, j,
$$

where $p _ { i j }$ is the proportion of $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ direct relations invested in contact j and $\sum _ { q } p _ { i q } p _ { j q }$ indicates the extent to which another partner $q$ in which i has invested substantial time and resources is also connected to contact j. Thus, for a firm $i ,$ the contact j is redundant to the extent that another i’s contact q is also connected with $j .$ We used UCINET VI (Borgatti et al. 2002) to compute network constraint. Because we wanted to capture the extent to which a firm enjoys access to structural holes, we subtracted this index from one so that the higher values will indicate a firm’s access to more structural holes in its ego network (i.e., lack of constraint). This measure ranges from zero to one.

Network Density. Network density directly captures the extent to which a firm’s partners are interconnected (Scott 1991, Wasserman and Faust 1994) and thus reflects the degree of information exchange and the development of norms and expectations among network members. Although network density may not accurately capture the extent to which a firm bridges structural holes (as the network constraint index does), the two measures are highly correlated. For example, Burt (2000) showed that structural holes (reversed constraint) and network density were negatively related. To increase the confidence in our findings, we used both measures in testing our hypotheses. We again used UCINET VI (Borgatti et al. 2002) to compute network density. Network density is computed as the proportion of actual ties in the focal firm’s network divided by the total number of possible ties in the firm’s network, multiplied by 100. Network density ranges from a minimum of 0 (when all partners are disconnected) to a maximum of 100 (when all partners are interconnected).

IT-Enabled Capability. IT-enabled capability was conceptualized in terms of two dimensions: (1) IT-enabled sensing capability and (2) IT-enabled responding capability (Malone et al. 1987, Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Overby et al. 2006). IT-enabled sensing capability is captured by two indicators of knowledge-oriented IT applications and partner scope. Specifically, knowledgeoriented IT applications are IT systems used to connect with external partners, manage knowledge, and mine and interpret data. Examples of knowledge-oriented IT include electronic markets, groupware, data mining tools, business intelligence, simulation, and visualization tools. We counted the cumulative number of knowledge-oriented IT applications in each year to measure this variable.

The partner scope is calculated using Shannon’s entropy measure (1948) to reflect the diversity of different types of partners that are linked through the IT systems (Chi et al. 2007a):

$$
\text { Partner   scope } = - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {s} p _ {i} \{\ln (p _ {i}) \} - \frac {s - 1}{2 n},
$$

where s denotes eight partner types that are supported using knowledge-oriented IT applications, including parts suppliers or outsourcers, external design partners, logistics partners, auto dealers, customers, (financial) service providers, competitors (other automakers); $p _ { i } = n _ { i } / n$ denotes the extent to which partner $i ~ ( i =$ $1 , 2 , 3 , \ldots , 8 )$ is supported by IT; n <sub>=</sub> total number of IT applications; and $n _ { i } = \mathrm { n u m b e r }$ of IT applications linking partner i.

IT-responding capability is captured by two indicators: process-oriented IT applications and business function scope. Specifically, process-oriented IT applications are IT systems used to integrate business processes within the firm. Examples of process-oriented IT include CAD/CAM, ERP, SCM, and CRM. Based on past literature, we posit that the greater the number of business processes that are digitized and integrated, the greater the coordination capability of a firm (Ravichandran 2007). We counted the number of unique process-oriented IT applications in each year that have been deployed by automakers to measure this variable.

Business function scope was calculated using Shannon’s measure (1948) to reflect the extent to which different business functions within a firm are integrated through IT (Chi et al. 2007a):

$$
\text { Business   function   scope } = - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {s} p _ {i} \{\ln (p _ {i}) \} - \frac {s - 1}{2 n},
$$

where s denotes eight business functions that are supported by IT, including procurement, logistics, product development, production, marketing and sales, after-sale service, human resources, and finance and accounting; $p _ { i } =$ $n _ { i } / n$ denotes the extent to which business function i $( i = 1 , 2 , 3 , \dots , 8 )$ is supported by IT; n <sub>=</sub> total number of IT applications; and $n _ { i } = \mathrm { n u m b e r }$ of IT applications supporting business function i.

We conducted factor analysis of all four indicators to examine whether firms differ in their IT-enabled sensing and responding capabilities. Table A.3 shows the results of the factor analysis. As indicated in Table A.3, only one factor has an eigenvalue greater than 1. The scree plot indicated that the eigenvalue sharply drops after the second factor, suggesting that most of the variance is explained by one factor. Therefore, in this paper, we used IT-enabled capability as a single construct. We used the predicted factor scores of IT-enabled capability in the regression analysis.

Control Variables. Firm Size. We expected that larger firms would possess a larger stock of resources that may affect both firms’ capability to introduce new competitive actions and firms’ tendency to form new collaborative ties and develop new IT applications. We approximated firm size using the total revenues of a firm in a given year, as reported by Compustat and Mergent online databases. We logtransformed this variable to reduce positive skewness.

Number of Vehicle Models. We also controlled for the number of vehicle models that each firm positioned in the passenger car segment in a given year. This helped account for firms that were disproportionately represented in this segment so that their competitive activity in sports cars and subcompacts would not be inflated. On average, automakers were selling six vehicle models per year (min 0 to max 26).

Prior Firm Performance. We controlled for past firm performance because prior research in competitive dynamics found that it strongly influences the firm’s decisions to initiate competitive actions (Ferrier 2001, Ferrier et al. 2002). We used return on equity (ROE) as an approximation for firm performance.

Multimarket Contact. Prior research has found that multimarket contact can affect the competitive activity of firms (Gimeno and Woo 1996, Young et al. 2000, Yu and Cannella 2007). We constructed two measures of multimarket contact: market segment overlap and geographical market overlap. For market segment overlap, we counted the number of models that each automaker sold in each of the following five segments: sports car/luxury, subcompact/medium class, pickup/truck, SUV, and van/minivan. We then created a firm-by-segment matrix (12 5) and converted this matrix into a firm-by-firm (12 12) similarity matrix in which each cell represents a correlation between each two firms across five market segments. A higher correlation coefficient indicates greater market segment overlap. For each firm, we computed average market overlap by averaging similarity scores across all rivals. To compute geographical market overlap, we counted the number of new products and new product versions introduced in each of the following geographical markets in a given year: Australia/New Zealand, the United Kingdom, continental Europe, Japan, Asia (except China), South America, India, China, Canada, and the United States. We used the same procedure as that used to compute market segment overlap described above. High average geographical market overlap indicated that a focal firm had a high market commonality with rivals.

Network Size. We controlled for the firm’s total number of alliances in each three-year period to control for the possibility that it is simply the propensity of firms to form alliances, rather than network density or network constraint, that explains action volume, action complexity, and action heterogeneity in the next year. We log-transformed this variable to reduce positive skewness.

## Statistical Method

Although we controlled for several important confounding factors, it is possible that some unobservable firm-specific factors confound our results. When these factors are stable over time (e.g., specific industry location, managers’ dispositions, or entrepreneurial culture), fixed- or random-effects panel data models can be successfully applied to account for endogeneity issues (Wooldridge 2002)

We performed several tests for selecting between fixed-effects and random-effects model. From a practical standpoint, the random-effects model is preferable to the fixed-effects model because the latter causes a greater loss of degrees of freedom (Greene 2003). However, the random-effects model must satisfy one additional assumption: the unobserved individual effects should be uncorrelated with the other independent variables. Violation of this assumption produces inconsistent estimates. The Hausman specification test can be used to test for orthogonality of the random effects and the other independent variables. The Hausman test was insignificant for all regression models, indicating that the estimates of randomeffects model are consistent and more efficient than those of the fixed-effects model.

Furthermore, network structure, IT-enabled capability, and competitive action can also be affected by time-specific factors, such as abrupt changes in oil prices, government interventions, or economic downturn, recession and boom, and bust cycles in IT investments. One way to control for these time-related factors is to include dummy variables for each period. Hence we estimated the following random-effects model with time fixed effects:

$$
Y _ {i t} = \alpha + \beta X _ {i t - 1} + \varepsilon_ {i t},
$$

where subscript i represents firms (i <sub>=</sub> 1 to 12) and t represents years (t <sub>=</sub> 1 to 16), respectively. $\beta$ is the coefficient (slope) of the independent variables $X _ { i t - 1 }$ that is assumed to be constant across firms. Note that $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ represents the two-way error component disturbances $( \varepsilon _ { i t } = \mu _ { i } + \lambda _ { t } + \nu _ { i t } ) .$ , where $\mu _ { i }$ denotes the unobservable individual effect, $\lambda _ { t }$ denotes the time-specific effect, and $\nu _ { i t }$ represents the remaining stochastic disturbance term (Baltagi 2008). It is assumed that $X _ { i t }$ is independent of $\mu _ { i } , \lambda _ { t } ,$ and $\nu _ { i t }$ for all i and t and that $\mu _ { i } \sim I I D ( 0 , \sigma _ { \mu } ^ { 2 } ) , \lambda _ { t } \sim I I D ( 0 , \sigma _ { \lambda } ^ { 2 } ) .$ , and $\upsilon _ { i t } \sim I I D$ $( 0 , \sigma _ { \upsilon } ^ { 2 } )$ are independent of each other. The coefficients are estimated using feasible generalized least squares estimation technique.

## Discussion

In this study, we examined how two opposing types of alliance network structure—dense versus sparse— affect competitive behavior of firms. We argued that firms can benefit from both types of network structure, but each type of network structure affects firms’ competitive behavior through a different mechanism. Overall, our findings provide support for this argument. First, we have found that structure holes have a positive direct effect on automakers’ ability to compete aggressively by undertaking a greater volume and a broader range of competitive actions. Second, we have also found a positive effect of network density on action volume and action complexity when automakers have a high level of IT-enabled capability. Although our results showed that network density has a negative main effect on action volume and action complexity, this effect is contingent on the degree to which an automaker has invested in building IT-enabled capability. We have found that when automakers in dense networks possess a strong IT-enabled capability, they tend to carry out more and complex action repertoires. These results provide support for our contention that we can more fully understand the relationship between a firm’s network structure and its competitive behavior when we account for the moderating role of IT-enabled capability of firms.

## Substitutive Role of IT-Enabled Capability

Our results suggest that sparse network structure rich in structural holes and IT-enabled capability provide substitutive capabilities for developing and introducing competitive actions. Firms without advantageous access to brokerage opportunities (i.e., lack of structural holes) can still increase their ability to initiate competitive actions if they have developed a strong IT-enabled capability. As shown in Figures 2 and 3, we found a strong positive effect of IT-enabled capability on action volume and action complexity at a low level of access to structural holes. Note also that at a high level of access to structural holes, IT-enabled capability has no effect on competitive actions; firms already have a high level of action volume and action complexity owing to the high level of access to structural holes in their networks. These findings suggest that independent of structural holes, firms can alternatively use IT systems to effectively enhance their awareness of new opportunities and their capability for initiating competitive actions. As argued earlier, IT-enabled sensing capability, through the use of knowledge-oriented technologies such as groupware, video conferencing, or instant messaging, was expected to enhance the exchange of information and ideas across different departments and business units of the firm internally which, in turn, leads to increased entrepreneurial ability to discover and introduce new competitive actions. IT-enabled sensing capability was also expected to enhance firms’ ability to identify and interpret external information about both changes in the customer preferences and shifts in technological trends. This ability could, in turn, provide increased potential for discovery of innovative competitive actions.

However, contrary to our earlier contention, we did not find that IT-enabled capability led to increased competitive actions by automakers spanning structural holes. We reasoned that although IT-enabled capability could complement the sensing ability of automakers in sparse networks and compensate for their lack of coordinative capability, it possibly adds incremental benefits to automakers in sparse networks. IT-enabled capability may not significantly enhance automakers’ capability for undertaking competitive actions in sparse networks because of the high awareness already made feasible by the automakers’ access to structural holes. It is also possible that enhancing the capacity for coordination across business partners in sparse networks could require more than investments in IT. Other factors, such as establishment of strong business relationships, effective governance mechanisms, and incentives alignment, also need to be in place to reap the coordinative benefits from using IT. Thus it is possible that automakers spanning structural holes in sparse networks might have to adopt a more integrative approach of establishing appropriate relationships with members in the network before being able to exploit fully the coordinative benefits of IT.

## Complementary Role of IT-Enabled Capability

Our findings suggest that firms embedded in dense networks must develop a strong IT-enabled capability to increase their potential for competitive actions. Figures 4 and 5 illustrate the form of the interaction effects between IT-enabled capability and network density. Network density has a negative effect on automakers’ ability to introduce a large number and variety of competitive actions when automakers do not possess a strong IT-enabled capability. One explanation for this negative effect is that the disadvantages of dense network structure (e.g., information homogenization) become more pronounced when a firm does not possess a strong IT-enabled capability. Dense network structure filters out new information and knowledge and thus reduces the potential for discovering new opportunities for competitive actions. As a result, automakers in dense networks tend to be a victim of competitive inertia, which is reflected in the automakers’ tendency to initiate a few actions and a very narrow range of actions (Miller and Chen 1994). IT-enabled capability—especially IT-enabled sensing capability—can compensate for this deficiency and increase automakers’ awareness of new opportunities for introducing competitive actions.

Furthermore, in the automobile industry, coordinating the flow of parts and subassemblies (e.g., transmissions, chassis, and sheet metals) involves very complex and massive processes. The automakers need to spend significant time and effort in coordinating and managing interdependencies among close-tied partners and business processes. A high IT-enabled capability can increase operational efficiency by tightening integration and coordination with partners and can increase supply chain visibility by streamlining information flows among partners. This increased ability is crucial for automakers to compete aggressively by initiating more and complex actions that typically require leveraging both their internal resources and those of other partners in their networks.

The discussion above suggests that automakers need to optimize between maintaining network structure and developing IT-enabled capability. Figure 6 summarizes the role of IT in modifying the relationship between network structure and competitive action. As the connectivity among network partners increases (or as more partners of an automaker are interconnected), IT-enabled capability increases automakers’ ability to manage the increasing complexity, interdependencies, and information exchanges among an automaker and its partners. Thus the role of IT becomes increasingly salient to help automakers more fully tap into the network benefits (by leveraging partners resources) to compete aggressively.

While, on the other hand, as the connectivity among partners becomes low (or as few partners of the automaker are interconnected), the automaker may enjoy the brokerage benefits by connecting with nonredundant partners and obtaining new heterogeneous information and unique opportunities. Network position rich in structural holes may render the benefits from using IT less salient for identifying new information and obtaining information benefits, as automakers have already possessed high awareness afforded by spanning structural holes. Furthermore, in sparse networks, other factors beyond a strong ITenabled capability may be needed to realize the coordinative benefits from IT for increasing automakers capability to act upon the identified opportunities. As a result, IT may only add incremental benefits to automakers spanning structural holes in sparse networks. These incremental benefits may not significantly increase the automakers’ awareness and capability for undertaking competitive actions. However, when automakers are not positioned advantageously in the network by spanning structural holes, a high IT-enabled capability may effectively afford automakers similar informational benefits that they might get from hole spanning, thereby significantly increasing the automakers’ awareness of action opportunities and likelihood to undertake competitive actions.

We drew from the AMC framework and argued that increased awareness and enhanced capability increase the motivations for firms to act. Our findings highlight that because IT-enabled capability may not significantly increase automakers’ awareness nor compensate for their lack of coordinative capability in sparse networks for the reasons discussed earlier, automakers accessing structural holes in such networks and having IT-enabled capability need not necessarily be more motivated to act. On the other hand, when automakers in dense networks significantly increased their awareness and coordinative capability through IT, they were more motivated to act. Thus the unobserved behavioral drivers in the AMC framework provide a useful lens to interpret our results.

We failed to find support for the main effects and the interaction effects of IT-enabled capability and network structure on action heterogeneity. We reason that this result may be attributable to the strong norms and traditions of the U.S. automobile industry, wherein major automakers tend to adhere to well-established patterns of competitive behavior. Industry tradition has been argued to have a dampening effect on firms’ competitive nonconformity (Miller and Chen 1996b), which reduces the variation of action heterogeneity across firms. More specifically, the U.S. automobile industry is a highly mature and regulated industry that is dominated by a small group of large-sized, aging firms. The average firm age in our sample is 77 years, with the oldest firms, Ford and GM 106 and 101 years old, respectively. Even the youngest firms in our sample; namely, Mitsubishi Motors and Honda Motors, are in their 40s and 50s, whereas the others are in their 70s and 80s. Through a long history of continuity and stability of interactions among the same set of major competing automakers, the industry has established patterns of competitive behavior with a highly organized set of market events recurring each year. Moreover, many traditions in the automobile industry have remained unchanged even through periods of upheavals. In such an industry environment, the automakers are thus less motivated to disrupt established patterns of competing.

In addition, firm size may have a dampening effect on a firm’s action heterogeneity. While large organizations tend to adhere to convention simply because they are so visible to or so closely monitored by regulators, firm size may also encourage bureaucracy and inertia (Miller and Chen 1996b). Consistent with this reasoning, we found that automakers’ size had a negative impact on their action heterogeneity.

## Contributions

In this study, we focused on two important but distinct types of network structure that create benefits. We examined how IT-enabled capability modifies the effects of these two types of network structure on firms’ competitive behavior. Our results yield meaningful insights about how IT-enabled capability interacts with network structure, resulting in different competitive firm behavior. This research makes several important contributions to theory, methodology, and practice.

First, our study represents an early attempt to systematically explore the interplay between interfirm network and IT. As we begin to acknowledge that firm action is embedded in a network of social relations, both interfirm network and IT are important themes that deserve greater research attention. Issues such as designing a firm’s technology infrastructure to better exploit its external resources, as well as understanding how IT systems can be designed to support a firm’s network structure and enable the firm to more fully tap into the benefits afforded by the network (Schultze and Orlikowski 2004), are important aspects that deserve research attention yet remain underexplored. The theorizing and empirical work done here is perhaps a first attempt to explore this rich research domain. We hope that the results presented here will spur further empirical work in this domain.

Second, our findings about the moderating role of IT-enabled capability on the effects of network structure on firm action make an important contribution to the research on IT valuation. Early research in this area has focused on the performance effects of IT investments and has treated the intervening processes as a black box (Brynjolfsson and Yang 1996, Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996). Subsequent research has attempted to open this black box and shed light on the processes and mechanisms through which IT impacts firm performance (e.g., Sambamurthy and Zmud 1994, Soh and Markus 1995, Mooney et al. 1996). Our study adds to this body of research by focusing on the effects of IT on competitive action. We have found that IT interacts with network structure and subsequently shapes competitive action. Competitive action has been extensively studied in prior research and is found to have significant impact on firm performance. Our findings complement and support the arguments presented in the literature that IT, per se, may not have direct effects on performance; rather, it is how firms deploy and use IT that is likely to explain variance in performance (Kohli and Devaraj 2003; Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien 2005; Chi et al. 2007a, b, 2008). While past studies have focused on the complementarities that arise from IT use internally, our findings suggest that such complementarities can also be realized through the strategic use of IT to harness external firm resources. Moreover, our findings provide an empirical test to theoretical relationships posited in the IS literature that digital systems do impact competitive actions of firms (Sambamurthy et al. 2003). We also show that firms can increase their ability to initiate competitive actions when their IT configurations provide them with the capabilities to exploit the potential benefits offered by the firms’ network positions.

Third, despite the work of Smith et al. (2001) calling for more research on how network structure affects competitive interaction, the relationship between network structure and competitive behavior of firms is still relatively underexplored. Our study advances competitive dynamics research by showing how sparse-versus-dense network structure differentially affects competitive behavior of firms. In addition, our findings add valuable insights into the brokerage versus closure debate (Burt 2000) and thus extend research in the interfirm alliance network. We showed that whether dense-versus-sparse network structure is more beneficial for firms’ competitiveness depends on firms’ internally developed capabilities such as IT-enabled capability. Our findings suggest that both types of network structure provide benefits for the firms; however, firms can benefit more from dense network structure only when they develop a strong IT-enabled capability, whereas firms can benefit more from sparse network structure with relatively less focus on IT investment.

Fourth, our study examines firm competitive behavior by focusing on attributes and patterns of competitive action repertoire—a series of competitive actions carried out in a given year. We extend the AMC framework that identifies three internal drivers of competitive action as an integrative theoretical hook to examine how network structure and IT interact to jointly affect patterns of the competitive action repertoire. The AMC framework has been traditionally used to explain and predict competitive actions or responses of pairs of competing firms at the action-reaction dyadic level. Thus our extension of the AMC framework to explain and predict action repertoire adds value to competitive dynamics research by enriching the theoretical implication of the AMC framework.

Fifth, from a methodological standpoint, our study represents a pioneering effort at collecting secondary data about actual voluntary IT use. Prior studies of IT use largely rely on self-reported use. Although selfreported use measures offer one way of assessing IS use, they are susceptible to common method bias and the possibility that perceived use may not be consistent with actual use because of subjects’ difficulty in recalling past use (Kohli and Devaraj 2003). There is increasing recognition that actual system use provides better measures than self-reported use (DeLone and McLean 2003). In this study, we used secondary data sources to assess actual IT use. This methodology, which involves an extensive examination of news articles from multiple sources, provides us with relatively objective and rich information about actual IT use. Furthermore, this approach is perhaps the only feasible one where the data collection involves assessing IT use across multiple years because it is unrealistic to expect respondents to provide reliable information about system use over multiple years in the past. Thus, secondary data collection about actual voluntary IT use proposed in this study offers a fairly novel, but very useful, methodology with the potential for greater application in IS research.

Finally, our findings have important practical implications. Our results suggest that managers have to consider the network structure in which their firms are embedded when designing their technology infrastructure. Firms embedded in dense networks might have to target their IT investments toward systems that enhance their ability for facilitating unstructured information exchanges and managing coordinative actions among close-knit partners. On the other hand, firms should economize between spanning structural holes and their investments in IT, because having a high level of access to structural holes in sparse networks can substitute for the benefits from developing a strong IT-enabled capability, and vice versa. Given the general perception among practitioners that firms overspent on IT during the recent boom and that many firms have not realized the returns from their IT spending, an optimal balance between network structure and IT-enabled capability seems to be crucial.

## Conclusion and Future Research

Our work, as an initial and original effort, has opened up a wide array of research streams that are at the intersection of digital systems, competitive dynamics, and interfirm networks. A few of them are discussed in this section. We conceptually distinguished between IT-enabled sensing and IT-enabled responding capabilities. However, we were not able to empirically distinguish between these two capabilities. Theoretical and empirical distinction between these two capacities will enable us to develop nuanced and in-depth arguments about how IT-enable sensing and responding capabilities may differentially enhance firms’ ability to exploit the potential benefits provided by sparse network structure versus dense network structure. Future research should attempt to disentangle the effects of these two capabilities. More data collection about the IT applications may be needed to investigate this aspect. Additionally, a qualitative research approach (e.g., a comparative case study analysis, questionnairebased data, or ethnographic study) may provide alternative ways to reveal whether managers place uneven emphasis on each type of capability.

Future studies could also extend our model by examining the interplay between IT-enabled capability and other network structural properties such as centrality and structural equivalence. Future research could focus on action-reaction dyads and examine how IT-enabled capability affects competitive interaction between two partners at a dyad level of analysis. For example, two structurally equivalent firms (firms with a similar pattern of ties with other firms) may be strong rivals because they draw resources from similar partners. However, the use of IT to facilitate information exchange between these firms may reduce the degree of competitive tension between structurally equivalent firms. In addition, the use of IT among the partners of structurally equivalent firms may increase competitive similarity. This may increase firms’ propensity to collude and thus ease their level of rivalry. Thus, examining the interplay between IT, network structure, and competitive action at a dyadic level of analysis present an interesting venue to extend our quest to understand the competitive behavior of firms.
