# IT valuation in turbulent times

Dowan Kwon <sup>a,\*</sup>, Stephanie Watts <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> John Molson School of Business, Concordia University, 1455 de Maisonneuve Blvd. W., Montreal, Que., Canada H3G 1M8

<sup>b</sup> Boston University School of Management, 595 Commonwealth Ave., Boston, MA 02215, USA

Available online 14 September 2006

## Abstract

This paper investigates performance impacts of two types of IT value practices – eficiency and knowledge management (KM) – by taking two external environmental factors – dynamism and hos tility – into account. A survey of IT managers was conducted to assess the environmental conditions under which one mode of value practice or the other becomes more salient. Results suggest that despite the apparent value of knowledge management in the new economy, IT managers continue to emphasize the traditional orientation of eficiency. This tendency persists in highly dynamic and hostile industrial environments. Interestingly, however, when these environmental factors are controlled for, KM-based IT valuation clearly pays of for firm performance. - 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Knowledge management; Eficiency; IT value; IT value practice; External environment; Firm performance

## 1. Introduction

The question of how IT contributes to firm performance has been a crucial one in the IS research community, giving rise to the stream of research on the IT productivity paradox At the industry level of analysis, IS researchers have generated a broad pattern of increases in productivity attributable to IT (see Dedrick et al., 2003 for a review). At the firm level, however, the issue of how IT can optimally contribute to performance is far from resolved. Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1995) estimate that firm efects may account for as much as half of the IT-based productivity benefits identified in their earlier work. Clearly, business and IT executives are very interested in knowing whether their IT investments are paying of for their organizations. Yet studies thus far have failed to identify a relationship between IT investment and individual firm profitability (Dedrick et al., 2003).

This research investigates the relationship between IT investment and firm profitability in the context of the firm’s external environment. At the industry level, the efect of the external environment is manifest in productivity diferences across industries, and understanding the breadth of IT payofs across industries is one of the most important open questions of IT productivity research (Dedrick et al., 2003). At the firm level, IT executives take the external environment into account when they design IT initiatives for processing the information generated by that environment (Mendelson, 2000).

The dot.com era provides a clear example of the impact of the external environment on IT investment within the firm. As the dot.com era spawned a positive picture of IT productivity impacts, IT came to be associated with the growth of the new economy (Reingold and Stepanek, 2000). Soon thereafter, however, the dot.com bubble burst, the economy slowed, and many companies came to view IT spending as a strategic burden (Burrows, 2001). This example illustrates how IT spending is afected by executives’ perceptions of the external environment.

This research is based on the premise that we do not have suficient understanding of how managers wield IT in conjunction with broader economic shifts. It reports on a study designed to link the managerial imperative (George and King, 1991) to the broader industrial context within which managers work. In particular, it investigates how managers deploy IT when the environment is characterized by global competition, knowledge intensity, deregulation, volatility, and blurred industrial boundaries (Daft and Lewin, 1993; El Sawy et al., 1999; Prahalad, 1998). Such environments, generally termed ‘‘turbulent,’’ ‘‘dynamic,’’ or ‘‘hostile,’’ exhibit increased uncertainty, heightened volatility, and hypercompetition (Covin and Slevin, 1989; Mendelson and Pillai, 1998; Miller, 1987; Van den Bosch et al., 1999). Findings from this literature stream suggest that some organizational IT initiatives are likely to be more successful in these types of environments than others and that innovative IT investments provide distinctive positive impact on the market value of the firm (Dos Santos et al., 1993). Yet IT strategy is just beginning to incorporate competitive dynamics, particularly regarding sustainability of an advantage (Hidding, 2001). As IT managers and their corporate partners go about selecting IT initiatives to invest in, they need to understand the role that the environment can play in the success of these initiatives. This study contributes to this understanding by investigating linkages between the use of IT, the external context, and organizational performance. Fig. 1 depicts this conceptual space.

In order to conceptualize the performance impacts of IT, we utilize a framework for IT practice based on the two perspectives of IT for eficiency and IT for knowledge management (KM). We refer to them as ‘‘modes of valuation’’ and ‘‘value practices’’ because they reflect managers’ theories-in-use regarding how IT can best contribute to organizational value. This framework allows us to compare the more traditional perspective of IT as an enabler of cost reduction and automation, with the more recent view of IT as a driver of knowledge-based agility (Alavi and Leidner, 2001). This study relates these modes of value practice to the environmental characteristics of hostility and dynamism, and to organizational outcomes in the form of objective organizational performance. We also examine the conditions under which one mode of value practice or the other becomes more salient, shedding light on the extent that IT investments in KM have greater payofs in hostile and dynamic industrial contexts.

The following three research questions are addressed in this paper, with an emphasis on understanding the relationship between turbulent environments and IT value practice. (1) Do environmental hostility and dynamism influence IT value practices of top IT execu tives? (2) To what extent do the two modes of IT valuation afect firm performance? And lastly, (3) Do environmental hostility or dynamism afect the relationship between the mode of IT valuation and firm performance, and if so, how? We investigate these ques tions in terms of the theoretical framework and conceptual models developed below.

This paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we develop the theoretical underpinnings of our research model, beginning by elaborating on the role of the environment in IT planning and investment. We then discuss the two modes of IT value practice in terms of their relationships with both the environmental context and organizational performance. Next, we present the methodology used to investigate our hypotheses and the results of our empirical validation. Finally, we discuss the implications of valuing IT in these divergent ways, particularly for organizations facing turbulent external environments.

## 2. Conceptual background

Organizational theorists have long viewed the external environment as playing a critical role that underlies both organizational forms and change processes. For example, the per spective of organizations as open systems (Katz and Kahn, 1978) is based on the premise that organizational boundaries are permeable, hence the external environment can have a myriad of efects within the firm. Population ecology as applied to the firm (Hannan and Freeman, 1977) emphasizes the fundamental role played by the environment in influencing organizational survival and performance. Perhaps in reaction to the lack of determinism aforded to managers under these perspectives, other theories of organization stress managers’ ability to make heedful choices (Weick, 1979), responding to and afecting environmental change (Cyert and March, 1963). Other views tend to emphasize the importance of organizational resources, since wielding these efectively can serve to shield organizations from environmental changes. The debate over strategic choice versus environmental determinism has long been central to the discourse in organizational and strategic theory – the external environment is widely acknowledged as playing an undeniable role in organizational outcomes (Aldrich and Pfefer, 1976; Astley and Van de Ven, 1983; Leonard-Barton, 1992; Miller, 1987; Mintzberg, 1979; Selznick, 1957).

The need to understand the relationship between IT value and the external environment has been recognized in the context of global discontinuities of the competitive landscape (Prahalad, 1998) and the productivity implications of fluctuations of the external environment (Roach, 2003). The demand that the environment places on organizations for coordinating knowledge is increasingly receiving attention in the KM literature (Alavi and Leidner, 2001; Cohen and Levinthal, 1990). Despite the importance of the external environment to the needs of organizational information processing, IS research has had little to say on this topic. Two recent exceptions to this are the work of Quan et al. (2003) and Choe (2003). In their investigation of the efects of market structure on IT productivity, Quan et al. (2003) found that, in a duopoly market, returns on IT investments are contingent on market sensitivities to changes in the price and quality of product and service oferings. Thus eficiency-enhancing IT investments are likely to have greater payofs in markets that are price-sensitive than in those that are quality-sensitive. Choe (2003) investigated diferential environmental efects on the relationship of strategic IT to firm performance. He found that strategic IT contributes more to performance improvement in unstable environments than in stable ones. These two studies are forerunners in an emergent research stream that investigates the interaction between the industrial environment and IT investment strategies such that payofs at the firm level are afected.

The Internet bubble spurred a related stream of research investigating Web-based IT investment policies. For example, in a booming economy, Internet-dependent companies have higher returns, as measured by stock valuation, than companies that do not utilize an Internet-based strategy (Kamssu et al., 2003). In dynamic industries, Internet use is positively associated with profitability and sales growth in organizations that utilize autonomous decision-making (Anderson, 2001). Thus a stream of research is emerging that seeks to understand the efects of environmental diferences on the IT–firm productivity relationship. This study joins this stream to investigate how certain environmental characteristics can be optimally addressed by application of IT in particular ways.

Recognizing the importance of environmental factors, organization and strategy researchers have developed various environmental constructs, such as dynamism, uncertainty, hostility, heterogeneity, munificence, and complexity (e.g. Dess and Beard, 1984; Miller, 1987). Each of these constructs addresses a distinct environmental characteristic, and some are more suitable for certain research problems than others. Because we are interested in interaction efects between information technology investment paradigms and the environment, the environmental characteristics we investigate here have been chosen for their relevance to organizational information-processing capability: (1) hostility and (2) dynamism. These two environmental characteristics are essential for understand ing ‘‘turbulent’’ external environments (Dess and Beard, 1984; Gupta and Chin, 1993; Kraatz and Zajac, 2001), where ‘‘turbulent’’ refers to environments characterized by ‘‘hy per-competition’’ and ‘‘heightened volatility’’ (Daft and Lewin, 1993). Global transitions and competitive discontinuities (Prahalad, 1998) exacerbate these external situations through intensified competition and diverse competitive pressure.

The concept of dynamism pertains to shrinking product/service life cycles. Dynamic environments are characterized by fast industrial ‘‘clock speeds,’’ and firms facing them need greater information-processing capacity (Mendelson, 2000). In such environments, IT becomes an essential ‘‘machine tool’’ for meeting the demands of the information age (Mendelson and Pillai, 1998). Dynamism has also been associated with broader environmental dimensions such as unpredictability (Miller, 1987) and uncertainty (Bensaou and Venkatraman, 1995), but for the purposes of this study, we define environmental dynamism as the change rate of external elements such as product, customer, and technol ogy (Vitale et al., 1986).

Environmental dynamism is an important moderator of a wide variety of antecedents of corporate performance, such as strategic decision speed (Baum and Wally, 2003), outsourcing (Gilley et al., 2004), capital structure (Simerly and Li, 2000), social responsibility (Goll and Rasheed, 1997), discontinuous innovation (DeTienne and Koberg, 2002), strategic consensus (Homburg et al., 1999), and rational decision-making (Goll and Rasheed, 1997). Li and Ye (1999) found that IT investment and other factors have a stronger positive impact on firm performance under high levels of environmental dynamism. In their study of acute care hospitals, Walters and Bhuian (2004) theorized that elements that absorb complexity – a high degree of information acquisition being one of these – enable stronger performance as environmental dynamism increases. Their empirical results support this contention. Thus, dynamism is an important dimension of firm environments and is particularly relevant to investigations of information technology and processing capability.

The other environmental characteristic included in this study is hostility. Hostility is a fundamental environmental dimension (Dess and Beard, 1984; Slevin and Covin, 1997; Zahra, 1993) that reflects ongoing threat to firm viability. It has been conceived of in terms of both the rate of failure of firms in an industry and the competitive intensity of the industry. An industry is recognized as hostile when either the overall economic climate of the industry becomes unfavorable to firms/actors, or when so many firms/actors participate in the industry as to make it intensely competitive.

IT strategy incorporates little about environmental dynamics, particularly regarding sustainability of an advantage (Hidding, 2001). Yet the recognition of environmental challenges can pressure a firm to change its resource commitments (Kraatz and Zajac, 2001). In recent years, IT has been conceived of variously as both a powerful driving force of eco nomic productivity and as a strategic burden. Clearly, it is important to understand ways that variations in the external environment afect how firms deploy and practice IT value.

## 2.1. Eficiency and KM as modes of IT value practice

Thus far, our theoretical framework has encompassed the boundary between the firm and its environment. We now look within the firm at what we refer to as modes of IT value practice and introduce two contrasting valuation modes – eficiency versus knowledge management. IT performance is achieved on the basis of IT initiatives and practices in an organization (Cragg, 2002). And managerial understanding of how IT can contribute to firm performance frames the realm of its possible uses in the firm (Garud and Ahlstrom, 1997). This research aims to understand the diverse ways in which managers pursue such potential.

The general scope of the terms used in this study is depicted in Fig. 1. IT practice is a broad term which encompasses IT-related strategic and managerial practices, and implementation activities such as adoption, development, and use (Cragg, 2002). In comparison, ‘‘IT value practice’’ (or ‘‘IT valuation’’ – we use the terms interchangeably in this paper) is a much narrower term, defined as purposeful IT activities which are specifically aimed at achieving corresponding business and strategic outcomes. These value-based activities include eforts to reduce costs, to improve customer relationships, and to enhance KM capabilities, and are linked to the appropriation of technology and consequent business outcomes. Consequently, IT valuation practices do not necessarily include implementation practices, i.e. generic processes of technology adoption, management, and user support, although these have been important success concerns of technology implementation. For example, the value-adding potential of automating and linking primary and supportive business processes identified by Porter (1985) in his value chain model is a value-based IT practice. Holsapple and Singh (2001) further clarify the KM-based potential of the value chain model, emphasizing value-adding processes and the impact of KM conduct on bottom line performance. Because our notion of IT value practice mainly targets specific value outcomes, it represents purposive valueladen actions for the business and its IT infrastructure. The eficiency- and KM-based IT value practices introduced in this paper are other examples. This conceptual distinction – between IT value practices and IT practices more generally – is also important for ensuring that the scope of our measurement methods reflect the concept of IT value practices.

Companies that have a strong focus on the exploitation of existing competencies are practicing the eficiency mode of IT valuation (Van den Bosch et al., 1999). Such companies are typically in traditional industries characterized by mass production, machine-like metaphors, and pyramid-like organizational structures (Morgan, 1986; Nolan and Croson, 1995). This type of organizational form serves to enhance eficiency, enforce internal specialization, and reduce environmental uncertainty. In the IS field, system eficiency is generally treated as a tangible and directly quantifiable measure that underlies costcenter organizational approaches (Hamilton and Chervany, 1981; Luftman, 1996). These benefits accrue to organizations that are working to improve process eficiencies and automation – IT value practices that are central to our understanding of the ‘‘eficiency-based IT valuation’’ construct. Eficiency-based, cost-centered measures reflect tangible and quantifiable outcomes, typically making it easy for managers to use them to justify their investments.

In comparison, companies that emphasize KM-based value practices pay critical attention to factors that determine the nature of knowledge creation, management, valuation, and sharing (Holsapple and Singh, 2001; Nonaka, 1994). While such an emphasis is neither exclusive nor contradictory to the eficiency-based practices described above, KM-based IT valuation is distinct in its reliance on the provision of slack resources to support the knowledge management.

In the IS field, the development of telecommunications capabilities and platforms has led to the application of IT to KM problem areas such as knowledge transfer, coordina tion, and reuse (Sambamurthy and Subramani, 2005). IT can contribute to KM when it is designed to optimize knowledge transfer or provide repositories of organizational experience. Because pre-existing explicit knowledge is potentially limiting (Polanyi, 1966), the capabilities to generate new knowledge and transfer knowledge within and across units (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990) are important IT enablers. IT can even support the serendipitous processes underlying successful innovation and creative response to unanticipated environmental change (Bradley and Nolan, 1998a; Holsapple and Singh, 2001). These capabilities can provide enhanced firm-level benefits such as intensive expertise sharing and horizontal coordination and learning, and therefore reflect our KM-based IT valua tion construct. Note that these KM-based IT initiatives are less likely to be tangible and objectively measurable than those that are eficiency-based. Still, managers who are cognizant of the potential for KM-based IT value may make their IT investment decisions in terms of achieving these KM benefits.

## 2.2. IT value practice in a turbulent environment

As theorized, environmental hostility is a fundamental dimension of the organizational environment because it can significantly restrict the range of workable strategic alterna tives (Slevin and Covin, 1997). In hostile environments, firm failure rates tend to be high and profit margins are generally slim. Hostile environments pose constant threats to firms viability, creating a reality in which survival, rather than competitive excellence, is viewed as a noteworthy accomplishment (Slevin and Covin, 1997).

The fewer financial resources available to a firm, the more important it is for the firm to make IT investments on the basis of hard numbers. The following quote epitomizes this perspective: ‘‘As the (economic) downturn bites, more companies are judging information technology investments on the basis of how much money they can save’’ (Burrows, 2001). During the period 2001–2003, for example, the overall US economy experienced a downturn and the demand for IT followed suit. This hostile economic climate motivated managers to look for IT-based cost savings in the form of initiatives most likely to increase process eficiencies. The hypothesis below examines this relationship between environmental hostility and the IT valuation mode of eficiency:

Hypothesis 1a. IT managers faced with high levels of environmental hostility show a greater propensity to utilize eficiency-based modes of valuation.

We now turn to the relationship between environmental hostility and KM-based IT valuation. The literature ofers mixed prescriptions regarding this issue. The first trend emphasizes the importance of KM capabilities. Efective KM drives the learning and innovation necessary for sustainable competitive distinction (Grant, 1996; Nelson and Winter, 1982; Penrose, 1959; Spender, 1994). In environments where external hostility creates a complex competitive field, there is therefore a heightened need for organizational learning. Organizations focus here on the use of KM for improving how they acquire, interpret, distribute, and enculturate knowledge to facilitate organizational learning (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Senge, 1990). As Schumpeter (1942) (’s) notion of creative destruction suggests, organizations with flexible innovative capabilities have a greater chance of survival than those without these capabilities, since they possess superior means of coping with external challenges.

Alternatively, organizations in hostile environments may have additional demands for resources, which in turn may create pressure to make only those IT investments that are most likely to generate quantifiable, tangible profits. Thus, because the payofs from ITbased KM initiatives are often intangible and dificult to quantify, we do not expect to see a higher propensity to invest in KM during hostile times. This perspective suggests that KM may be viewed as an extra expense or optional resource commitment in times of economic slowdown. When it comes to making trade-ofs between the benefit of exploiting existing competencies and the need to improve adaptive capability, managers tend to sacrifice potential agility in order to conserve basic organizational values and capabilities (Kraatz and Zajac, 2001). Taking these conflicting perspectives together, the following hypothesis is a neutral one:

Hypothesis 1b. IT managers faced with high levels of environmental hostility do not show a greater propensity to utilize KM-based modes of valuation.

Now we turn our discussion to the relationship between environmental dynamism and the eficiency-based mode of IT practice. Traditionally, IT has been applied to reduce the costs of performing routine and repetitive jobs. IT plays an essential role in automation and the replacement of human labor, especially for routine operations. More recently, the pressure to reduce costs and respond to changing customer needs simultaneously have broadened the traditional definition of automation. Consequently, IT contributions to eficiency now include modularity and parts standardization for both production and service activities. For example, new IT applications are available that automate the process of meeting disparate customer requirements. Faced with dynamic external environments, firms are utilizing IT in the service of frequent and continuous cycles of automation that create highly eficient production cycles. Thus we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2a. IT managers faced with high levels of environmental dynamism show a greater propensity to utilize eficiency-based modes of valuation.

Our next hypothesis investigates the relationship between environmental dynamism and learning, as it is enabled by IT-based KM initiatives. Daft and Lewin (1993) suggest that ‘‘quantum changes in manufacturing and computer-mediated communication technologies have given managers radical new options for designing organizations, and the new paradigms demand flexible, learning organizations that continuously change and solve problems through interconnected coordinated processes.’’ In dynamically changing environments, mutual adjustment becomes the favored means of knowledge coordination (Van den Bosch et al., 1999). For developing firm absorptive capacity, Cohen and Levinthal (1990) stress the importance of cross-functional interfaces such as connections between functional areas, contacts across functions, and liaison roles in each unit. For creating, validating, coordinating, and transferring knowledge in dynamic environments, IT is increasingly viewed as a strategic tool (Thomas et al., 2001). Clearly, IT can facilitate organizational coordination and support the increased KM made necessary by dynamic environments. Thus, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 2b. IT managers faced with high levels of environmental dynamism show a greater propensity to utilize KM-based modes of valuation.

## 2.3. IT value practice and firm performance

The following hypotheses investigate the relationship between IT value practice and firm performance. Divergent ways of practicing IT internally in organizations can increase the chances of success and improved performance (Cragg, 2002). First, the role of IT in enhancing organizational eficiency through automation is undeniable. IT is highly efective for enforcing internal controls and exploiting the current set of organi zational competence (Burrows, 2001). These eforts achieve results, even in periods of substantial threat and hostility. Thus, not only would we expect organizations to engineer on eficiency and cost savings, but doing so is actually a prudent course of action in general. Ceteris paribus, organizations that emphasize eficiency-based IT valuation will perform better:

Hypothesis 3a. The utilization of eficiency-based IT value practices has a positive efect on firm performance.

Our next hypothesis concerns the linkage between KM-based IT practice and organizational performance outcomes. Organizations pursuing KM can create additional sources of value (Hedlund, 1994). Theorists have acknowledged the importance of historically valuable organizational knowledge for its subtlety and inimitability (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Penrose, 1959). However, enabling KM through IT often requires additional resources, and is thus risky and might even have negative consequences during periods of organizational turmoil. The benefits of investing in KM are often not realized in the short-term, a fact that may deter managers concerned about the bottom line from paying for it. The lead-time inherent in these types of IT investments is an important hidden ‘‘par adoxical’’ IT implication (Brynjolfsson, 1993).

However, companies that continue to invest in KM while competitors hesitate due to economic hostility often turn out to be great performers (Hamm et al., 2002). Firms need the capability to respond to external forces characterized by intense, diverse, and shifting competitive pressures (Covin and Slevin, 1989), and IT-based KM initiatives are an important means of doing so. Companies that invest during grueling economic times tend to grow when the slump is finally over. Sustainable competitive advantage can be derived from IT capabilities under this perspective, particularly when IT experience is combined with innovative applications (Dos Santos et al., 1993), other management skills and resources (Mata et al., 1995), and cross-unit knowledge synergies (Tanriverdi, 2005). This logic is not lost on astute managers searching for ways to maintain continuous innovative capability in the face of dynamic external forces. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3b. The utilization of KM-based IT value practices has a positive efect on firm performance.

## 2.4. The efect of environmental turbulence

The next set of hypotheses test the moderating efect of environmental turbulence on the IT–firm performance relationship. As global competition becomes the norm rather than the exception, external competitive pressures become increasingly intense and dynamic. Not many corporations are immune to this broad trend. Particularly during shrinking economic cycles, industry participants are under great pressure to exploit more, given their limited resource boundaries. Faced with shrinking market demand and hostile industrial climates, internal company criteria for investment justification are often tightened. Maintaining low expenditure levels becomes a strategic necessity. In such situations, IT presents a means for cutting costs from the existing pie rather than as a potential source of new revenue (Roach, 2003). Although eficiency-based IT valuation is efective for improving routine work and replacing repetitive labor, a broad trend in this direction may reduce positive performance impacts where IT is wielded inappropriately. Hence

Hypothesis 4a. Environmental hostility reduces the positive efect of eficiency-based IT value practices on firm performance.

On the contrary, we expect that the positive efects of KM-based IT practice will be strengthened in such hyper-competitive contexts. The knowledge and learning paradigm embraces the potential of factors such as values, meaning, symbols, and stories for stimulating innovation and creativity (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995). Complementary IT support for human capital and business learning can provide an important source of sustainable value-added (Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997). Moreover, because customers’ search costs tend to increase in heterogenous markets, IT can improve profit margins in such environments (Bakos, 1991). In these ways, environmental hostility is likely to amplify the efect of KM-based IT practice on organizational performance. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 4b. Environmental hostility increases the positive efect of KM-based IT value practices on firm performance.

Next we develop two hypotheses to test the moderating efects of environmental dynamism on the relationship between IT value practice and organizational performance. Dynamic markets tend to pressure firms to shorten their product life cycles and accelerate their operations. As environmental dynamism increases, efective information processing and quick response capabilities are becoming more critical (Mendelson, 2000; Mendelson and Pillai, 1998).

Eficiency-based IT practice often results in investment in applications designed to routinize and institutionalize change, particularly because this approach is easy to adopt. However, in dynamically changing environments, the consequences of doing so are not likely to be sustainable. Dynamism in the environment heightens the importance for organizations to develop adaptive capabilities. Thus, we would expect to find that the life spans of eficiency-based IT applications are shorter in dynamic environments. Hence, dynami cally changing environments will tend to reduce the positive efects of eficiency-based IT valuation.

Hypothesis 5a. Environmental dynamism reduces the positive efect of eficiency-based IT value practices on firm performance.

On the other hand, environmental dynamism demands organizational capabilities in order to make sense of external changes and to absorb changing external knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990). Although product and service volatility makes long-term organizational investments dificult, it is increasingly important for organizations to create the capability to sense and respond to diverse and changing customer requirements (Bradley and Nolan, 1998b; Mendelson and Pillai, 1998). Dynamic environments require that organizations adapt to the challenges they present, and KM-based IT practices ofer a clear path for enabling such agility (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Organizations that are at the forefront of creating KM capability will be those most likely to sustain performance. Thus,

Hypothesis 5b. Environmental dynamism increases the positive efect of KM-based IT value practices on firm performance.

The theoretical model developed above is shown in Fig. 3. It includes the two environmental factors of hostility and dynamism, and the two modes of IT value practice.

## 3. Method

A cross-sectional survey, in conjunction with an objective measure of firm profit, was used to test the hypotheses. The sample for the survey was drawn from companies in the US and Canada by using Standard & Poor’s COMPUSTAT database. The database from which we retrieved the names and contact information of senior IT managers was obtained from the Applied Computer Research, Inc., and the survey mails were directly sent to a single top computer executive at each organization. Via the survey, the top computer executives were prompted to address their corporate IT infrastructure in general, rather than any specific business unit or IT implementation.

The survey instrument was administered across two time periods, 2002 and 2003, respectively. In the first of these (February–June 2002), approximately 1100 questionnaires were sent to a top computer executive in US companies with net sales exceeding 100 million (USD). The second round of data collection was performed over 4 months (February– May 2003), and approximately 600 questionnaires were sent to top computer executives in Canadian companies with net sales exceeding 20 million Canadian dollars (CDN). Fiftyeight responses were received from the US sample, a response rate of 5.3%, and 67 responses were received from the Canadian sample, a response rate of 10.8%. Given the workload and high rank of these top computer executives, these response rates were not surprising. Recent studies utilizing responses from executive and senior levels have reported similar response rates (Enns et al., 2003).

A total of 125 returned surveys constituted the data. The US and Canadian data were combined to generate a sample of suficient size. In order to assess the potential for a sampling efect, equality tests were performed by testing both mean and variance diferences of all variables. Overall, the results did not show statistical diferences at the 0.05 alpha level (with the exception that variable EF1 exhibited a variance diference). Thus, we concluded that there were no systematic sample efects by country.

Descriptive characteristics showed that the respondents include 79 IT executives and 37 senior IT managers. These together constituted 92.8% of the sample. These respondents were thus assumed to be knowledgeable and reflective of IT value practices in the organization. The remainder were non-IT senior managers but were responsible for an IS/computer department in the organization. One respondent did not report his job position. Descriptive characteristics of the firm showed that companies in the sample were mostly large with annual net sales of \$ 100–5000 million (72.8%) or greater (12.8%). These firms were engaged in diverse industries including manufacturing (48%), retail/wholesale (12.8%), telecommunication and utility (12.8%), finance (9.6%), and software/service (9.6%). The company names of nine respondents (7.2%) were unknown.

In order to assess the possibility of respondent bias, we compared respondent companies with the population of US and Canadian companies that we had initially targeted using the COMPUSTAT database population. This method provides indirect evidence that the respondent firms are reflective of the target population (Sethi and King, 1994). Results did not reveal any significant diferences along various organizational measures of size (net sales, total asset, number of employees) and performance (return on asset,

EBIT margin). We concluded that the likelihood of sample bias was low despite the low response rate.

## 3.1. Measures

This study investigates the relationships between IT value practice, environmental characteristics, and company performance. The following four constructs are measured: eficiency-based IT practice, KM-based IT practice, hostility, and dynamism. Perceptions of IT executives are used to operationalize these theoretical concepts. This methodological choice reflects the fact that the perspective of high-level managers serves as a reasonable representation of organizational phenomena, since outcomes reflect the values and decisions of powerful organizational actors (Hambrick and Mason, 1984). With the exception of the KM-based IT practice, construct measures were adopted from previously validated studies. It is important to note that the perceptual measures are worded in a way that asks about prior IT value practices. This enabled us to identify associations between these prior practices and current organizational performance without using a panel design. The items were then averaged, resulting in each of the four independent constructs. The descriptive statistics among the variables used in the study are shown in Table 1.

Prior to the detailed analysis, all five constructs (including the dependent EBIT measure) in the model were subject to the standard test of normality. Skewness, kurtosis (2.0 threshold), and variance inflation factors VIF (10.0 threshold) were all within acceptable range, suggesting that the individual variables were normally distributed and multicollinearity was not likely to be present (Hair et al., 1998). In addition, White’s test (1980) of heteroskedasticity of residuals in the regression model was conducted and the results show absence of specific heteroskedastic patterns in the error terms. Based on these results, the data did not violate the basic assumptions required for multivariate data analysis using a regression model. In addition, the four independent constructs show reasonable internal reliability and discriminant validity. All values for Cronbach’s alpha were at or above 0.7. Regarding discriminant validity, four factors were identified in an exploratory factor analysis, using an unforced oblique rotation, and all of these factors loaded in a ‘‘simple’’ structure arrangement as expected, accounting for 56.7% of variance. For details regarding each scale item and the results of the factor analysis, please refer to Appendix A.

## 3.1.1. Hostility

Environmental hostility has been conceptualized in two ways in previous research. First, it has been based on hostile industrial conditions such as industrial threat, failure rate, and viability (Slevin and Covin, 1997). Alternatively, it has reflected market competition such as competitive intensity and market dynamics (Miller, 1987). Since our theoretical model of IT-related environmental hostility is tightly coupled with the first concept (e.g. impact of economic downturns), we adapt and use Slevin and Covin (1997) (’s) previously validated industrial hostility scale.

## 3.1.2. Dynamism

Dynamism has been operationalized in many previous studies (Dess and Beard, 1984; Miller, 1987), but consensus has not emerged around one standard measure. In this study, we adopt and use a set of items that have previously been applied to the IS domain (Vitale et al., 1986).

## 3.1.3. Eficiency

We operationalize the construct of eficiency-based IT practice by utilizing a previously validated instrument – the primary activity eficiency measure (PAE) from Sethi and King (1994). This construct consists of four items that these authors derived from the concept of Porter’s value chain (1985) and applied to the IT domain. We adopt this scale because PAE represents ways of reducing costs by using IT in diverse organizational activities such as logistics, operations, and marketing, and because it has been well validated (Sethi and King, 1994).

## 3.1.4. Knowledge management

In order to operationalize the KM-based IT practice, a five-item scale was developed. Previously validated items were not available for this theoretical concept. Similar instruments, e.g. the business resources scale (Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997), were reviewed but deemed inappropriate, thus it was necessary to develop items to measure this construct. Items were designed to represent the role of IT for cross-functional linkage and integration, since these are essential for the internal firm capacity of knowledge absorption (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990). Items were initially identified and reviewed by both academics and IT executives. An item-sorting exercise was carried out to improve the face validity of the construct and the scale was refined during a pilot test.

## 3.1.5. Objective firm performance (financial)

IT business value at the firm level has been assessed in previous studies by measuring the impacts of IT on company business performance. In our study, an objective firm profit measure, EBIT margin (earning before interest and tax divided by net sales), is used to measure firm performance. The EBIT margin represents the marginal profit of operational income, and is a typical measure of firm financial performance. It is an appropriate performance measure for this study because it excludes the interest and tax portions of earnings and thus assesses business-specific profitability, which may be more directly linked to the consequences of IT (Bharadwaj, 2000). Interest and tax are also factors dependent on the financial climate of the host country. As this study utilizes data from multiple countries, it is appropriate that such factors are excluded. In our analysis, it is also the closest financial measure to respondents’ perceptions of their firm performance. Since not every manager responding to our questionnaire identified their company, we had to drop nine responses from the original company sample to apply this measure. The findings reported below employ objective data and hence reflect a total sample of 116 rather than 125.

## 4. Findings

Results have been organized into three sections. First, the efects of the external environment on the two modes of IT value practice are investigated to test Hypotheses 1 and 2. Second, utilizing the firm profitability measure of EBIT margin as the dependent variable, we analyze the efects of IT value practices on performance (Hypothesis 3). In the third section, the moderating efects of the environment on the relationship between IT practice and firm performance are investigated (Hypotheses 4 and 5).

The following three OLS regression models are employed to test the first three Hypoth eses 1–3.

Model 1: Eficiency-based IT value practice $= a _ { 1 } + a _ { 2 } H + a _ { 3 } D + e$

Model 2: KM-based IT value practice $= b _ { 1 } + b _ { 2 } H + b _ { 3 } D + e$

Model 3: Firm performance (EBIT) $= c _ { 1 } + c _ { 2 } \mathbf { E F } + c _ { 3 } \mathbf { K M } + c _ { 4 } H + c _ { 5 } D + e$

where H is the environmental hostility, D is the environmental dynamism, EF is the efi ciency-based IT value practice, and KM is the KM-based IT value practice.

It shows that both hostile and dynamic external environments are significantly correlated with most of the scale items of eficiency-based practices. This result is consistent with the positive relationships implied in Hypotheses 1a and 2a. In contrast, it appears that KM-based IT practices are not correlated (except one item, KM3) with either hostile or dynamic environments. These results are not consistent with the positive association predicted in Hypothesis 2b, where we expected that dynamic external environments would be associated with greater KMbased IT practice in organizations.

This result afirms the notion that in hostile environments, the focus of IT is likely to be on cost reduction and eficiency. It also supports Hypothesis 2a – the positive impact of dynamism on eficiency-based practice. In the second OLS model in Table 3, KM-based practice is used as a dependent variable. Overall, the result does not reject the null hypothesis, indicating that significant impacts of external hostility and dynamism on KM-based IT practice are not supported. This result parallels the previous pattern of descriptive correlations (Table 2) and is consistent with Hypothesis 1b in suggesting a neutral impact of hostility on KM-based practice. However, the result does not support Hypothesis 2b – the positive impact of dynamism on KM-based practice.

In the third OLS model in Table 3, firm performance (EBIT margin) is used as a dependent variable and external environmental variables are used as control variables. The impact of IT value practice should be most evident when potential exogenous efects are controlled for, and thus the two environmental factors are treated as exogenous controls in this model. Overall, the model is significant $( p < . 0 1 )$ , but shows that the coeficient of eficiency-based IT practice is not significantly associated with firm performance. Thus, it does not support Hypothesis 3a – the positive impact of eficiency-based practice on firm performance. In contrast, KM-based IT practice is significantly associated $( p < . 0 5 )$ with firm performance in the result. Hence Hypothesis 3b is supported. This result is consistent with the general theoretical prediction that IT practices based on KM valuation will lead to positive firm performance outcomes (Tanriverdi, 2005). The two environmental variables of hostility and dynamism are also included in the model. While these external factors are not considered explicitly in the hypotheses, the inclusion allows us to control for direct influences of these external conditions and thus to focus on the main theoretical relationship between IT valuation and performance. We did, however, test these environmental efects. The result shows that the hostility is negatively associated with firm performance $( p < . 0 1 )$ , which is consistent with general intuition and previous studies (Covin and Slevin, 1989). Dynamism is not significantly associated with performance.

In addition, an SUR (seemingly unrelated regression) model was used to verify the regression estimates and its results are shown in the last column of Table 3. Since this technique provides a joint estimation of the three prior equations, allowing the error terms across the equations to be correlated, it thus verifies that error terms associated with the dependent variables (eficiency, KM-based IT valuation, EBIT margin) do not afect the separate regression results (Greene, 1993). The joint estimation in the SUR model shows that the standard Chi-square value is 20.97 $( p < . 0 1 )$ ), thus rejecting the null hypothesis of the model. Moreover, the coeficient estimates in SUR are nearly the same as those estimated in the third OLS. This result indicates that there is no significant efect after the error terms of the dependent variables are simultaneously correlated, and support the results of the OLS analysis presented below.

Next, potential moderation efects of external hostility and dynamism are assessed (Hypotheses 4 and 5). For this purpose, we followed the procedures recommended by Cohen and Cohen (1983), first performing a hierarchical moderated regression analysis (HMRA) and then a sub-group analysis. The results of the HMRA were examined by assessing the $R ^ { 2 }$ diference between the base regression model (the main efect model, OLS 3 above) and the model aggregated with additional moderation (interaction) terms. The results indicate an $R ^ { 2 }$ increase of 1.7%, which was explained by the inclusion of the hostility interaction terms – eficiency valuation · hostility and KM valuation · hostility. Regarding the potential moderation of dynamism, the ${ \dot { R } } ^ { 2 }$ increases by 2.7%, explained by the dynamism interaction terms – eficiency valuation · dynamism and KM valuation · dynamism. Both these increases exceeded the 1% rule of thumb (Evans, 1985). In addition, all the directions of the moderation efects were consistent with those anticipated in Hypotheses 4 and 5. These results suggest that the efects of moderation are substantial and support the hypothesized efect directions. However, a direct interpretation of the beta coeficients in the HMRA is prohibited because the coeficients can be influenced by the nature of scales used, i.e. other than ratio scales, and by the potential for linear transformation between independent variables (Carte and Russell, 2003). Thus, a sub-group analysis was undertaken, in order to avoid these problems.

Sub-group analysis is based on median splits of the moderating variables, in this case hostility and dynamism (Cohen and Cohen, 1983). Table 4 shows these results. Before the split, the original sample size was 116. After the split based on hostility, the size of the low hostility (benign) group is 54, and the size of the high hostility (hostile) group is 55. Seven responses which were exactly located at the median were eliminated. After the split based on dynamism, the size of the low dynamism (static) group was 50 and the size of the high dynamism (dynamic) group was 55. Eleven median responses were eliminated. We investigated moderation efects by assessing the extent to which the partial correlation coeficients were diferent between the sub-groups. The Fisher’s Z transformation was employed to test the diference (Hambrick and Lei, 1985; Cohen and Cohen, 1983). Although both type 1 and type 2 errors are possible, the relatively small cell sizes due to sub-group split raise the risk of type 2 error (failure to detect the true population relationship). We compensated for this limitation by setting a less stringent level of significance $( p < 0 . 1 )$ . Because doing so can reduce the rigor of any findings, the result should be interpreted in caution. We identify this issue as a potential limitation in the discussion section below.

Table 4 shows the results of this sub-group analysis on hostility (Hypothesis 4) and dynamism (Hypothesis 5). Again, all of the moderation efects are consistent with the directions anticipated in the hypotheses. First, the impact of eficiency-based IT practice tends to be reduced in high hostility environments, since it has less of an impact than in the benign (low hostility) sub-sample (Fisher’s Z, $p < . 1 )$ , thus Hypothesis 4a is supported. Hypothesis 4b is also supported, because the hostile environment strengthens the impact of the KM mode of IT practice on performance (Fisher’s $Z , p < . 1 )$ ). Hypothesis 5a is also supported, since the efect of eficiency-based IT practice on performance is attenuated in dynamic environments (Fisher’s $Z , p < . 1 )$ . Hypothesis 5b is not supported, because while the efect of KM-based IT practice is significant $( \gamma = . 2 8 7 , < . 0 5 )$ in the dynamic sub-group, the diference between the two sub-groups (Fisher’s Z) is not significant.

## 5. Discussion

This paper investigates two modes of IT value practice – eficiency and KM – within organizations and their potential performance implications under varying external contexts. This study was motivated in part by our observation that managerial awareness of potential IT value seems to vary over time, in ways that are consistent with the overall economic outlook. For example, the bursting of the dot.com bubble brought with it reduced expectations for the organizational value of IT. On the one hand, IT is viewed as a major potential enabler of KM, innovation, and creativity, but, on the other hand, it is perceived to be a strategic burden by managers. Thus the business value of IT seems to change depending on the external environmental context.

A summary of the hypothesis tests is provided in Table 5. The results reported here hold three theoretical implications in this regard. First, we found that environmental turbulence – in the form of hostility and dynamism – is significantly associated with IT value practice. This suggests that the reason for valuing IT in terms of eficiency or KM in practice is partly due to characteristics of the environment. Specifically, the first set of findings, Hypotheses 1 and 2, indicates that in both hostile and dynamic industrial environments, companies tend to practice IT in terms of eficiency and cost reduction rather than KM. Thus, despite widespread recognition of the importance of KM in turbulent knowledgebased economies, IT use for KM is minimally supported in our data. Managers do not see KM as crucial for their investment, particularly when they believe the survival of the organization to be at stake (i.e. in hostile environments). Instead, IT practice in both dynamic and hostile environments is conceived of in terms of traditional value domains such as eficiency and cost reduction.

IT managers, even those in highly dynamic industries, do not consider KM to be a top investment priority. This finding seems counter-intuitive given organizational theory prescribing KM for addressing environmental turbulence. However, it is consistent with the ever-practical dictum of using IT for cost control during dificult economic times. It may even suggest that IT managers do not take nuances of the external environment into account – such as the diference between fast moving, dynamic environments and hostile ones – when they make IT investment decisions.

This finding may also explain why managerial eforts to implement KM initiatives are lagging despite the positive potential of IT-based KM. Organizational and IS researchers have consistently favored KM practice over eficiency for meeting the demands of the new economy. Knowledge is recognized as an important source of inimitable and thus sustainable economic rents (Grant, 1996; Nelson and Winter, 1982; Penrose, 1959; Spender, 1994). IT is viewed as a critical component of an infrastructure that will support organizational flexibility and agility (Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Van den Bosch et al., 1999). The potential exists for achieving high profitability through the creation of such dynamic capabilities, but it seems that we must first shift practitioner paradigms toward KM-based IT valuation.

Second, analysis of Hypothesis 3 revealed associations between IT value practice and organizational performance. In order to test the impacts of IT practice, it was necessary to control for environmental conditions which might themselves be associated with firm performance. As we control for variations in environmental turbulence, KM-based IT practice is positively associated with firm performance. This finding is consistent with a recent study of the positive impact of KM competence on firm performance (Tanriverdi, 2005).

The finding that KM-based IT investment has positive performance implications if we compensate for the demands of the environment underscores the importance of the role played by IT managers. While managers facing hostile environments are likely to believe that IT investments ought to be eficiency-based, and typical companies might avoid KMbased IT investments, the results of this study identifies and clearly locates an opportunity for performance improvement: KM-based organizational initiatives – perhaps designed to address environmental hostility – payof in terms of bottom line performance.

The third set of findings, concerning Hypotheses 4 and 5, suggests that the turbulence of the external environment may moderate the efects of the IT value practice on organizational performance. The tests of Hypotheses 4a, 4b, 5a and 5b are compared horizontally in Table 5. Results indicate that the association between eficiency-based IT practice and performance is significantly reduced in both the hostile and dynamic environments in comparison to that in the benign and static environments. These results support Hypotheses 4a and 5a. Also, the association between KM-based IT practice and performance is somewhat significant $( p < . 1 )$ in the hostile environment relative to that in the benign environment, supporting Hypothesis 4b. However, the result does not support Hypothesis 5b, thus environmental dynamism does not moderate the relationship between KM value practice and performance.

The vertical dimension of Table 4 shows a surprising result of this analysis. In the vertical columns representing the benign and static environments, diferences between the efects of the two valuation modes is similar. However, in the columns presenting hostile and dynamic environments, there appear to be substantial diferences between the efects of the two modes. In particular, in hostile and dynamic environments, the associations between KM-based IT valuation and performance are both positive $( \gamma = . 3 6 1 , \gamma = . 2 8 7$ respectively) and significant $( p < . 0 5 )$ , whereas in these same environments, the associations between eficiency-based valuation and performance are negative $( \gamma = - . 1 2 2$ $\gamma = - . 1 5 3$ , respectively, non-significant). A possible explanation for this negative (or neutral) impact of eficiency-based IT practice is that the eficiency orientation may intensify competition and increase the cost of doing business in these already heavy-loaded industries and thus become additional burden to individual firms. This may in turn reduce the chances for realizing the potential of more innovative IT investments in these environments.

In all, our findings clearly support the growing consensus prescribing KM-based IT practices in fast-paced knowledge environments. It appears that IT managers tend to value IT for its eficiency potential, yet it is IT as an enabler of KM that has the greatest impact on performance outcomes in turbulent times. Findings suggest that, as the environment becomes ever more dynamic and hostile, practices that reap the value of IT for realizing KM are a most appropriate option. These findings contribute support the dawning of a new KM-based paradigm shift in the IS field (Sambamurthy and Subramani, 2005).

Our study is limited to the extent that we investigate the ‘‘general’’ patterns of the association between environmental contexts and IT value practices. The implications of this study need to be considered with caution. For instance, these general patterns do not provide specific explanations for why managers in organizations take the approaches found in this study. Future studies may pursue more qualitative data (e.g. field investigations) or investigate the psychological and behavioral reasons underlying why IT managers behave in a sub-optimal way (i.e. not following KM prescriptions) as they cope with external dificulties.

The R<sup>2</sup> in the models (OLS 1, OLS 2) explaining the value practices in this study are not high (.113 and .025, respectively), implying that there are other antecedent factors, in addition to the external environments, that explain the degree of eficiency and KM-based value practices. It is possible that the support of the neutral hypothesis (H1b) in the relationship between the hostility and KM-based value practice is due to the small sample size and lack of other antecedents. For instance, types of strategic orientation in the organization, such as defender versus prospector (Miles and Snow, 1978; Sabherwal and Chan, 2001), might have a diferential impact on the practices that organizations undertake to achieve value. Also, the model might be enhanced by considering various technological topologies and platforms. For instance, EDI and SCM (supply chain management) are likely to be associated with eficiency-based valuation such as faster inventory turnover, lower transaction costs, etc. Other types of IT such as groupware and cross-departmental applications are likely to be associated with the execution of KM activities (Holsapple and Singh, 2001). In addition, to further control firm-specific factors, future studies may include additional variables (e.g. firm size, business relatedness, IT ratio, R&D ratio) and industrial characteristics (e.g. service, manufacturing, high tech, and low tech).

The firm performance measure – EBIT margin – used in this study was chosen because it appropriately represents short-term performance. It indicates a firm’s operational superiority, since it excludes income earned by the firm from non-production sources such as interest incomes and taxes. IS researchers have devoted much attention to this issue of the impact of IT on bottom line firm performance, but their findings have not been always consistent. Kohli and Devaraj (2003) (’s) meta-analysis of the IT payof literature suggests that variations in the performance measures used may underlie many of these inconsistencies. For instance, studies which use productivity or output-based measures as dependent variables were more likely to report positive payofs than those that used profitabilitybased measures. While discussions of the IT productivity paradox assume that investments in IT may lead to productivity improvements which afect the output of a firm, Hitt and Brynjolfsson (1996) (’s) study emphasized the possibility that this increased productivity can coexist with unchanged firm profitability. According to basic assumptions of microeconomics, extraordinary profit margins will not be captured because the mechanism of market competition will result in value redistribution. Other potential accounting measures such as ROA and ROI can be used for profitability, but these measures are often distorted for many reasons, such as temporary disequilibrium efects, tax laws, and accounting conventions. Other longer-term alternative measures could also be utilized for the dependent variable here, such as Tobin’s Q, MB (market-to-book ratio), etc. (Bharadwaj et al., 1999). These can be used as a proxy for the firm potential, which can be captured in the stock price and presumed to signify a firm’s future operational growth. However, these longer-term measures were deemed inappropriate for this study since we were investigating the relatively shorter-term phenomenon of the impact of IT value practices. Thus, the pros and cons of all these measures should be understood and future studies should include the most appropriate measures for the specific purpose of the research.

The small size of the sub-group samples is one final concern. Due to broad recognition of the small efect sizes of moderation, relative to those of main efects, the design of moderation studies must balance the need to increase significance levels in order to prevent detecting a moderation efect where none exists (type 1 error) or relax significance levels in order to limit the possibility of not finding a true moderation efect (type 2 error). In order to minimize the possibility of type 2 error, and given the current sample size and sub-group split, we choose the less conservative level of significance, p < .1. However, this does increase the chance of false-positive findings, and results should be interpreted with this consideration. In addition, while it is standard practice to split sub-samples at the median (Cohen and Cohen, 1983), this could have given rise to a biased result. For instance, the sub-group analysis treats respondents who score 2.0 and 4.5 on dynamism (less than 4.75 median) as perceiving equally ‘‘static’’ environments when clearly they do not. Ideally, the best way to handle this limitation would be to enhance statistical power by increasing the sample size, and the results should be cross-validated in diferent settings, e.g. with second-hand objective measures, in the future. To the extent that both the sub-group analysis and the HMRA results confirm the basis tenets of the theoretical model, however, we feel confident that this exploratory validation has statistical conclu sion validity.

Despite these potential limitations, we believe that the construct of IT valuation developed in this study is useful for understanding IT practices and their impacts on businesses and organizations. In particular, KM-based valuation may underlie not only technological decisions, but also decisions afecting employees’ general knowledge and learning initiatives. Thus, part of the rationale for our model is that the process of IT investment valuation in today’s organizations is coupled with overall strategy determination (Holsapple and Singh, 2001; Sabherwal and Chan, 2001). Addi tional research is needed to understand how closely linked IT practices are with the business logic underlying organizational and strategic decisions (Sambamurthy and Zmud, 2000).

Overall, this study contributes to literature proposing the tripartite model of IT value practice, environmental turbulence, and firm performance. It suggests an important stream of future research, one that integrates the performance impacts of new IT paradigms such as KM with both the internal and external environments of the firm. Researchers are beginning to investigate market characteristics in the context of IT productivity (Choe, 2003; Kamssu et al., 2003; Quan et al., 2003). This study is the first to test the relationship between IT value practice and perceptions of environmental turbulence. We hope that future research will extend this stream and that the measures used in this study will be applied and further validated in additional research settings. There is clearly potential for validating the perceptual environmental constructs used here with objective measures. For instance, researchers in the fields of strategic management and organizational theory have demonstrated ways to characterize the external environment by formulating industrial indices such as market concentration ratio, industrial instability, complexity, munificence, etc. (Dess and Beard, 1984; Sutclife, 1994). These industrial indices can be constructed on the basis of various objective second-hand databases, and the opportunities for robust validation of our current findings. In the future, researchers should also continue to elaborate details of KM-based IT practices. In particular, there is opportunity to generate richer data through qualitative field-based research in order to better understand managers’ IT valuation processes. These data should reveal process-based strategies that inform theory and provide specific practitioner guidelines.

Organizational theorists continue to emphasize KM and knowledge-based organizational forms as optimal for the new economy (Grant, 1996; Van den Bosch et al., 1999). Yet generalizable research on how IT can enable organizations to meet the KM demands of the new economy is lacking. This study attempts to fill this gap by applying existing organizational theory to the IS domain and linking it to the phenomenon of IT value practice. As predicted by organizational theory, an optimal fit seems to exist between external environments characterized by hostility and dynamism, and KM-based IT practice. The evidence presented here contributes to the small but growing body of research that incorporates both the industrial context and managerial determinism into models of IT payofs. Such research challenges senior IT managers facing turbulent environments to move beyond traditional eficiency-based IT paradigms.
