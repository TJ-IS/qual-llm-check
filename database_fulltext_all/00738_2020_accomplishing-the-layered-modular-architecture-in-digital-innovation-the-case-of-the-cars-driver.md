# Accomplishing the layered modular architecture in digital innovation: The case of the car’s driver information module

Keywords: Digitalization Digital control system Layered modular architecture Mirroring Abduction

## A B S T R A C T

Architectural theory of digital innovation contends that, to enhance physical products with digital capabilities, a layered modular architecture is required. This architecture hybridizes hierarchically arranged components of physical products with modules of digital functionality configured into layers. Despite considerable research adopting this architectural perspective on digital innovation, questions of how this hybrid architecture is accomplished organizationally and technologically lack both conceptual clarity and empirical illustration. Noting pervasive tensions that characterize digital innovation eforts and the contradictions between hierarchical and layered modular configurations, this paper seeks to answer the following research question: Given that the layered modular architecture needs to hybridize modular arrangements with opposing logics, how is it accomplished?

Employing the concepts of digitalization, physical product hierarchy and digital control system to better theorize a product architecture’s movement from a modular to a layered modular architecture accompanied by organizational structures that enable this change, we abductively analyze the increasing digitization of a car’s Driver Information Module (DIM) over a 10 year period. We conclude by proposing three transformations through which the layered modular architecture is accomplished.

## Introduction

Much has been made of the disruptive nature of digital innovation (e.g., Lucas and Goh 2009; Soerensen and Landau, 2015). Entire sectors of the economy, including the newspaper (Ihlström and Henfridsson 2005), photography (Sandström 2011; Tripsas 2009), construction (Boland et al. 2007) and car industries (Hylving et al. 2012: Lee and Berente 2012). have been fundamentally transformed as bits replace atoms. In the car industry<sup>1</sup>, sensor-studded vehicles are rendered increasingly smart, alerting the driver to anomalies related to the vehicle (e.g., low tire pressure) and to traffic (e.g., automated braking). These not only affect safety (Sayvad et al. 2017), but also the driving experience (Reilhac et al. 2017) as the vehicle parks itself and alerts the driver when the car veers out of the lane.

Advancing an architectural theory of digital innovation, Yoo et al. (2010) argue that a layered modular architecture is a key indicator of an organization’s digital innovation<sup>2</sup> maturity and likelihood of success. A layered modular architecture is described as a “hybrid of the modular architecture of a physical product and the layered architecture of digital technology” (Yoo et al. 2010: 729). While prior research has shown that developing this architectural prerequisite of digital innovation is fraught with tensions and conflicts (Hylving et al. 2012; Svahn et al. 2017), it has failed to theorize their source.

We propose that a pervasive cause of these tensions and conflicts is inherent in the hybrid architecture. The modular architecture of physical products, which reflects a hierarchical configuration of modules (i.e., hierarchical modularity), and the layered architecture of virtual goods, implying a layered configuration of modules (i.e., layered modularity), represent contradictory logics (Henfridsson et al. 2014; Svahn and Henfridsson 2012). Specifically, hierarchical modularity relies on a top-down logic of decomposition, whereby components with high interdependence are clustered together and vertically separated from components with which they have fewer interactions. In contrast, a layered modularity applies a bottom-up logic, where universally used and relatively stable components form a platform on which components that enact more ephemeral functions then draw (Colfer and Baldwin 2016).

Motivated by Kallinikos’ (2012: 83) claim that “the progressive dissociation of function, form and matter from one another is the most remarkable attribute of technological evolution whose implications are poorly understood and … seldom investigated to a suficient degree,” we set out to investigate how digital innovation is achieved. Since digital innovation is a process of digitalization in which social and technological aspects of organizing are mutually implicated (Yoo et al. 2010). and organizational structures are correlated with product architectures (Cabigiosu and Camufo 2012), the need to integrate two contradictory types of modularity into a coherent architecture capable of generativity, presents us with a the following question: “Given that the layered modular architecture needs to hybridize modular arrangements with opposing logics, how is it accomplished?”

To answer this research question, we rely on a qualitative study of AutoInc’s<sup>3</sup> digitalization of its vehicles’ instrument cluster on the driver’s dashboard, i.e., the DIM (Driver Information Module). Focusing on a 10-year period (2005–2015), during this time the digitalization of AutoInc’s DIM consisted of three projects – each increasing the degree of digitization – we trace the transformation of the DIM’s architecture.

Our research makes a number of contributions. Firstly, we develop a theoretically-informed definition of the layered modular architecture. We do this by identifying the two types of modular arrangements – hierarchical and layered modularity – whose hybridization forms the layered modular architecture. Furthermore, we ofer a visualization of this hybrid structure (Fig. 2). As such, we significantly extend architectural theories of digital innovation.

Second, by describing and illustrating the interdependence of the social (e.g., organizational roles, allocation of decision rights) and the material (e.g., arrangements of modules) in digital innovation, we gain a more holistic and situated understanding of the complexity of digitalization (Yoo 2010), i.e., change in technology and practices. With few exceptions (Arakji and Lang 2007; Henfridsson and Bygstad 2013; Sandberg et al. Forthcoming), most prior digital innovation research prioritizes either its technical/ material (e.g., Huang et al. 2016; Ravichandran 2018; Scott et al. 2017) or its social/organizational aspects (e.g., Rolland et al. 2018; Tumbas et al. 2018; Yeow et al. 2018).

Third, we abductively develop a plausible explanation (Bamberger 2018) of how a layered modular architecture is accomplished, namely by means of three techno-organizational transformations: (A) uncoupling the digital control system from the physical product hierarchy, (B) layering the digital control system, and (C) continuously (re)connecting the two architectures. This conceptualization enriches prior research that adopts an architectural theory of digital innovation (e.g., Huang et al. 2016; Svahn et al. 2017)

Our paper proceeds as follows: we outline the architectural theory of digital innovation and, noting that the layered modular architecture represents the hybridization of hierarchical and layered modularity, we present their conflicting logics. An introduction to the mirroring hypothesis concludes our conceptual scafold for analyzing our empirical data. After describing the context of our case study, as well as our data collection and analysis strategies, we present our findings. We conclude the paper by answering our research question and identifying three transformations that constitute the process of digitalization (Yoo 2010).

## Digital innovation as evolving a layered modular architecture

Definitions of digital innovation range from “a product, process, or business model that is perceived as new, requires some significant changes on the part of adopters, and is embodied in or enabled by IT” (Fichman et al. 2014: 330), to “the carrying out of new combinations of digital and physical components to produce novel products” (Yoo et al. 2010: 276). These definitions accent diferent aspects of digitalization: while the former focuses on innovation as an outcome, the latter privileges its processual nature (also Kohli and Melville 2019). In this paper, we emphasize digital innovation’s processual quality, particularly the evolution of its architecture and organizing structures.

An architecture’s quality is defined by its flexibility and support for a wide variety of functions (Baldwin and Clark 2000). Both are afforded by the architecture's modularity. that is. the degree to which its functions are broken down into more or less independent components or modules (Glimsted 2018). A modular architecture maps product functionality (e.g., lines of code, physical components) such that a highly coherent and loosely coupled structure is created. This facilitates smooth and dynamically-enacted coordination among modules via well-defined, standardized interfaces (Sanchez and Mahoney 1997; Ulrich 1995). In addition to enhancing a system’s flexibility (Baldwin and Clark 2000), modularity afords specialization (Langlois 2002) such that people working on one component (e.g., car engine) need not have detailed knowledge of another (e.g., drive shaft) in order to do their work and innovate.

In the context of digital innovation, the layered architecture (Fig. 1), arranges modules in a way that core functionality that is universally used and relatively stable, is located at the bottom of the stack (Henfridsson et al. 2014). This creates a platform, a key enabler of generativity (Benkler 2006; Zittrain 2006). Specifically, the separation between hardware and software that is enacted through layered modularity ensures that digital and physical components can be mixed in new ways (Huang et al. 2016). For example, liberated from its physical moorings (e.g., cassette tape), digitized music can be streamed via a cloud service to myriad devices (e.g., phone, car radio), thereby creating new value.

A stylized view of the digital architecture stack reveals four layers: the bottom or device layer encompasses the physical components. In the case of a car’s DIM, this would include sensors on car components and the physical displays on which information is shown to the driver (Lee and Berente 2012). The network layer consists of physical network buses and communication protocols that aggregate and transport this data to the service layer, which consists of applications that manipulate and combine data into information to be displayed in the DIM. The top-most stratum of the stack, the content layer, includes not only the data that represents the status of a component (e.g., fuel level), but also the graphical elements (e.g., fonts and colors) used in the displays.

The Yoo et al. (2010) architectural theory of digital innovation posits that the development of a layered modular architecture, which is indicative of an organization’s movement along a continuum from the physical product’s hierarchical architecture towards its integration with the digital product’s layered architecture, is a critical success factor. Unfortunately, Yoo et al.’s (2010) nomenclature creates confusion as it implicitly positions modularity in opposition to layering, which relies on modularity also. “Hierarchical” as an indicator of how modules are arranged in a traditional product architecture represents a more appropriate opposing pole to “layered.”

Beyond the ambiguity inherent in Yoo et al.’s (2010) labeling of digital innovation’s hybrid architecture, we note three additiona challenges with their architectural theory: (i) the contradictions between hierarchical and layered configuration of modules are not theorized; (ii) neither a clear description nor a diagrammatic representation of the layered modular architecture is ofered, and (iii) digitalization, i.e., the interaction between the material/architectural transformation and the social/organizational changes, is not theorized. We elaborate on each.

## Contradictions between hierarchical and layered configuration of modules

While the modularity of both the hierarchical and layered architectures promise stability and flexibility by virtue of standardized interfaces that make loose coupling possible (Baldwin and Clark 20o0), the distinction between them revolves around how the modules are configured to form a coherent product architecture. As summarized in Table 1, hierarchical and layered configurations of modularity are at odds (Colfer and Baldwin 2016)

A hierarchical product architecture reflects a top-down logic of decomposition. Highly interdependent components are clustered into one branch of the hierarchy, so that conflict that arises during their coordination can be managed by decision/business rules that are controlled by one governing entity, e.g., manager (Sanchez and Mahoney 1997). In contrast, a layered architecture reflects a bottom-up, core-periphery logic (Colfer and Baldwin 2016), where components that do not change much (e.g., physical sensors) form the foundational layers at the bottom of the stack (i.e., device layer). This represents the core of the platform. Components that change frequently (e.g., the code that manipulates sensor-generated data) are clustered into the upper lavers (Henfridsson et al. 2014). The data and application layers, which deal with software, data and user interfaces, represent the periphery.

These logics have important implications for product innovation. A hierarchical modularity assumes that a product is suficiently knowgble in advance (i.e.. before designing the hierarchy of modules) and that the customer's product needs remain relatively stable over time (Colfer and Baldwin 2016). Only under these conditions will an a priori one-to-one mapping between functions and modules be possible (Henfridsson et al. 2014). In contrast, synonymous with platforms (e.g., Parker and Van Alstyne 2005), a layered modularity assumes that a product will remain incomplete and emergent, as the customer’s needs only become knowable over time. A layered arrangement of modules that supports the extension of the product’s core functionality by peripheral components, afords the flexibility required to evolve innovations even late in their life cycle (Ravichandran 2018).

The contradictions between these two configurations suggest that hybridizing them is fraught with tensions (e.g., Putnam et al. 2016). The dialectic nature of evolving this hybrid architecture is particularly pertinent in light of the fact that digitalization (Yoo 2010) entails the reciprocal shaping of the product architecture (material) and the organizational task structures (social). In particular, changes in decision rights, authoritativeness of knowledge, communication channels, problem-solving strategies and what is valued have been found to be deeply implicated in such architectural transformations (Henderson and Clark 1990; Sanchez and Mahoney 1997).

## Visualizing the layered modular architecture

Another concern with Yoo et al.’s (2010) architectural theory of digital innovation is that it fails to ofer a clear description or visualization of its central concept, namely the layered modular architecture. In our efort to conceptualize how the new hybrid architecture develops as digital innovation moves along the continuum from a modular (hierarchical modularity) to a layered modular (hybrid) architecture, we take inspiration from Lee and Berente’s (2012) visualization of digital innovation as the uncoupling of the digital control system from the physical product hierarchy. Together they form a “dual-hierarchy” (Lee and Berente 2012) in which elements of the physical product hierarchy and the digital control system overlap. With increased product complexity and increased digitization, a dual hierarchy is more and more likely to emerge as the information, rules and decisions that ensure the proper functioning of a product (e.g., displa options of an e-book) are decoupled from its physical components (e.g., e-reader hardware).

The digital control system’s role is to monitor and control the components/content of the product hierarchy, primarily by connecting components that are separated by the physicality of the product structure (Hobday 1998). With the capacity to cut across a vehicle’s physical product hierarchy’s siloed arrangements to achieve cross-component and cross-functional integration, the digita control system of a car can, for example, combine data from the fuel tank with a gyro sensor (located in another vertical in the product hierarchy) in order to estimate fuel consumption. This capability to connect to all components and subassemblies of a product hierarchy, as well as to mix and match the data they generate, lends the digital control system its generative value

Adapting Lee and Berente’s (2012: 1430) visualization of the dual hierarchy to incorporate the layered architecture (Fig. 1), we visualize the layered modular architecture (Fig. 2) as the hierarchical (physical) product architecture that is separated from and positioned orthogonally to the layered (digital) architecture. Nevertheless, the two architectures are connected to each other through shared materiality. For example, the sensors that are embedded in components of the product hierarchy simultaneously serve as the source of the data needed by the digital control system. As such. the sensors form part of the digital control system's device laver (shown by dotted lines in Fig. 2).

We consider the car DIM the user interface (UI) to the car’s digital control system, which is made up of numerous components and programs. For example, the car’s Electronic Control Unit (ECU) integrates all the sensor data generated by the car’s physical components and sub-assemblies to produce such functionality as lane departure alerts and automatic breaking. As the UI of the car’s digital control system, the DIM not only displays data related to the actions coordinated by the

ECU (e.g., automated braking), but also increasingly serves as an input device through which a driver can articulate her preferences to the ECU. For example, the ECU’s parameters for alerts are configured according to the thresholds and safety options chosen by the driver.

## Theorizing digitalization: The mirroring hypothesis

The third shortcoming of Yoo et al.’s (2010) architectural theory of digital innovation is that it fails to theorize digitalization (Yoo 2010), i.e., the organizational implications of digitizing products. We turn to the mirroring hypothesis (Colfer and Baldwin 2016) to provide a conceptual scafold for theorizing the techno-social mutuality that defines digital innovation (Barrett et al. 2015).

The mirroring hypothesis predicts that there is structural correspondence between the architecture of a complex product and the division of labor in the organization that builds and maintains it (Colfer and Baldwin 2016; Conway 1968). Baldwin and Clark (2000) refer to this relationship as the fundamental isomorphism between the design (the material product) and the task structure (the skills, practice and structures needed to accomplish a task). Given the challenge of communicating and problem-solving across increasingly specialized subgroups, the task and knowledge divisions that are instantiated through communication channels, collocation of workers and emplovee relations, are ideally mirrored in the product architecture (Baldwin and Clark 2000). As organizations that display fundamental isomorphism between tasks and product design conserve knowledge resources, limit coordination and create the conditions for effectively resolving conflict, they are expected to be effective and efficient (Cabigiosu and Camuffo 2012: Sanchez and Mahoney 1997). Indeed, Colfer and Baldwin (2016) find strong empirical support for not only the existence but also the eficacy of mirroring, both within and across firms.

However, they also note that digital technology reduces design and communication costs, the two key economic drivers of mirroring (Colfer and Baldwin 2016). Thus, they propose that design-task mirroring is likely to be relaxed in situations where digitization renders products more complex (also, Lee and Berente 2012). Thus, organizations engaged in digital innovation are likely to exhibit partial mirroring, which entails drawing the knowledge boundaries more broadly than the operational boundaries, i.e., explicit organizational structures (Colfer and Baldwin 2016). Partial mirroring takes the form of non-hierarchical organizationa arrangements (e.g., matrixed reporting relationships) and collaborative practices (e.g., agile development). System integrators also enact partial mirroring by virtue of their architectural knowledge (Lee and Berente 2012), i.e., their familiarity with the way modular components are combined and configured in a product design (Henderson and Clark 1990).

Mirror-breaking is another design-task arrangement emerging in increasingly digitized settings (Colfer and Baldwin 2016). It implies that the isomorphism is intentionally broken; e.g., dissolving organizational boundaries in order to engage in joint decision making and rich communication deemed necessary for developing new knowledge (Levinthal and March 1993).

Importantly, the mirroring hypothesis merely specifies correspondence, not causality. While Conway (1968) maintains that the structure of software is constrained by the communication structures among developers (task → design), Chandler (1977) proposes that the technical design is mirrored in the organizational structure (design → task). Baldwin and Clark (2000), in turn, highlight the bi-directionality of isomorphism.

In sum, migrating from a hierarchical product architecture to a lavered modular architecture conducive to digital innovation. implies transforming not only the material of the physical product (i.e., digitization), but also what tasks need to be done and by whom (i.e., digitalization). The mirroring hypothesis ofers us the conceptual infrastructure to theorizing the interaction between product architecture (material) and the task structure (social) in digital innovation. How organizations can accomplish a coherent architecture that melds competing modular configurations and do so in a sociomaterial manner, is the key question we seek to explore empirically.

## Method

## Case organization: AutoIn

AutoInc is a global car company that produces a range of passenger vehicles, including sedans, SUVs and hatchbacks. To learn about how the layered modular architecture is accomplished, we focus on the digitalization of AutoInc’s Driver Information Module (DIM) between 2005 and 2015. Not only is the car’s DIM the user interface of the car’s digital control system (Lee and Berente 2012), but it is also a source of strategic diferentiation and branding. Indeed, the DIM has become the focus of car manufacturers’ digita innovation eforts since more and more emphasis is placed on user experience (Stevenson 2017). This makes the digitalization of the DIM a well-suited empirical setting in which to seek answers to our research question.

In the early 2000s, more and more digital sensors that monitored both the car and its environment were incorporated into AutoInc’s automobiles. Not only existing components such as the fuel tank were equipped with either new or improved sensing devices, but also more advanced information-generating components such as cameras and GPSs were added to the vehicle. Leveraging such sensor data for strategic diferentiation, especially by developing new safety- related functionality, became a priorit for AutoInc.

For example, one of the functions AutoInc envisaged was adaptive cruise control (ACC), which relied on radar to determine the vehicle’s distance to the car ahead. Once the driver set the desired time interval between her vehicle and the car in front, the radar sensor monitored the follow distance. When the time interval to the car ahead became too small. the DIM alerted the driver and the car decelerated automatically. By relying on digital communication across multiple components and subassemblies in the product hierarchy, the car’s digital control system enacted ACC

Taking advantage of the new sensor data however proved challenging for AutoInc due to the limitations of its vehicles’ extant architecture. As one product design engineer explained, the electrical platform (i.e., the electrical control units and communication buses) had insufficient capacity to accommodate the increased data load:

“We finally outgrew [the electrical platform]; the buses were full with information, we didn’t have enough bandwidth to send signals.”

This constraint was one of reasons AutoInc embarked on a DIM re-platforming project in 2005.

## Increased digitization of the DIM

Each of the three DIM projects included in this study reflects an increase in digitization as measured by the amount of digital content displayed in the DIM and its technological sophistication (see Table 2 for summary). In the first DIM project, entitled Sensor, only a messaging service was available beyond the standard display of driver information (e.g., speed and engine revolutions). However, in the Connectivity project, which Internet-enabled the vehicle, the DIM was able to host third-party apps, thus ofering drivers virtually unlimited content and service choices. To support this development, numerous changes in the DIM platform (i.e., device and network layer) were needed. Not only did the number of digital devices increase (e.g., graphical processing units, RAM and flash memory), but their capacit was also expanded (e.g., color LCDs with higher pixel rates, higher speed and larger bandwidth communication buses).

## Data collection

To gain insight into the digitalization of AutoInc’s DIM, we relied on two phases of interviews with AutoInc employees that had worked on the three DIM projects. We also gathered documents (e.g., product specifications, organization charts). All interviews were conducted by the first author, who had previously worked for an AutoInc vendor specializing in HMI (Human-Machine Interaction) design. She thus had prior professional contact with AutoInc and some of the interviewees. The first round of 17 interviews employed the laddering technique (Reynolds and Gutman 1988), while the second round consisted of eight open-ended, follow-up interviews with various engineers and interaction designers who had expertise in the DIM’s architecture (Table 3).

For the first round of interviews (fall 2012 to winter 2013), the first author recruited people who had worked on all three DIM projects in some capacity. For example, one interviewee tested the production HMI in the first project, conducted prototype testing in the second and early testing of HMI in the last project. For these interviews, laddering (Reynolds and Gutman 1988), a targeted meaning elicitation technique (Schultze and Avital 2011), was employed. It ofered us insights into not only the similarities and diferences among the three DIM projects, but also the significance of project characteristics for the successful migration to a layered modular architecture.

During the laddering interviews, we relied on Kelly’s (1955) triadic sorting technique, asking interviewees to compare and contrast the three DIMs in order to identify as many meaningful distinctions as possible among them. A typical question the researcher asked during the interview was: “How are the Sensor and the Personalization projects similar to each other, yet diferent from the Connectivity project?” By repeatedly asking this question with diferent project combinations, key attributes of the DIMs digitalization were identified. These included both technical diferences (e.g., hardware and software changes), as well as social ones (e.g., shifts in responsibilities, redistribution of tasks across organizational units).

Even though laddering interviews typically include additional steps beyond the elicitation of a project’s unique features, we found that triadic sorting, which drew out rich descriptions of the three projects and ofered some insight into the significance of key features, was all that could reasonably be accomplished during a \~60 min interview. Instead of pressing the interviewees further, we filled the gaps in our understanding by conducting an additional round of interviews (winter 2013 to spring 2014) with people who had worked on at least one DIM project and were knowledgeable about the various DIM architectures and their evolution. Al interviews were recorded and transcribed

The first author’s ongoing engagement with AutoInc in other engaged scholarship projects (Van de Ven 2007), meant that informal data gathering techniques (e.g., email exchanges, conversations over the phone or lunch) with the interviewees allowed us to get feedback on our emergent theorizing. This informal contact also enabled us to get answers to questions that emerged during our theorizing.

## Data analysis

Our data analysis followed an abductive approach (Locke et al. 2008; Van de Ven and Poole 1995). Abductive reasoning typically takes the following form (Niiniluoto 1999): (i) a surprising empirical observation O is made, (ii) which would be a matter of course if claim C were true, (iii) leading us to suspect that claim C is true. This approach to theorizing thus develops plausible claims that may be true (Locke et al. 2008).

Abduction consists of three steps: (i) applying a theory as a sensitizing device, (ii) observing a surprise in the empirical phenomenon in light of this theory, and (iii) articulating a new theory that resolves the surprise (Alvesson and Karreman 2007). Architectural theories of digital innovation, especially Yoo et al. (2010) and Lee and Berente (2012), served as our sensitizing devices (Walsham 2006). Both informed our data collection strategy. A grounded analysis (Charmaz 2006) of our laddering data, which included data displays (Miles and Huberman 1994) in the form of mind maps, as well as rich descriptions (Rousseau and Fried 2001) of the three DIM projects, generated analytical categories such as “system specifications: paper based vs. simulations." These initial codes (Charmaz 2006) were refined over time into a total of 21 codes (e.g., “HW vs. SW” and “Product Architecture”).

Our grounded analysis of the data yielded a surprise, namely that, during the Sensor Project, AutoInc was unable to develop an architecture that aforded substantial innovation of the DIM. Given the size and efort of this initiative, it was surprising how little was accomplished on the DIM’s user interface. In contrast, the dramatic facelift of the DIM that the small, skunkworks- led Personalization Project was able to achieve, was remarkable. Instead of the movement along the architectural continuum (Yoo et al

2010) being continuous and relatively even, it struck us as punctuated and disjointed. This contrast prompted a number of questions, the most significant of which revolved around why it was so challenging for AutoInc to layer its digital control system.

In delving deeper into this challenge, we drew on theories of modularization (Sanchez and Mahoney 1997), mirroring (Colfer and Baldwin 2016), and imbrication (Leonardi 2011) to engage in a dialog with the data (Alvesson and Karreman 2007). A key insight we derived from Colfer and Baldwin (2016), revolved around the logics underlying the modular configurations of hierarchy and coreperiphery. When we conceptualized the latter in terms of layering (with the core representing the lowest layer of a software stack), we were able to formulate our research question.

In order to develop conjectures (Bamberger 2018) that answered our research question plausibly, we read and re-read our empirical material (i.e., our interview transcripts and documents) through the lens of the various theories. We also re-read the theory through the lens of the data. To ensure that both the technological and social aspects of AutoInc’s digitalization of the DIM – as well as their interactions – were accounted for, we completed an imbricational analysis (Leonardi 2011) of the DIM’s evolution (Appendix A)

Continuously cycling between the conceptual and the empirical (Van Maanen et al. 2007), as well as comparing and contrasting the three DIM project, the dimensions of our analysis eventually settled into a more-or-less coherent narrative of AutoInc’s DIM digitalization journey. Given that our goal was to identify how the layered modular architecture is accomplished, we sought to trace the movement of digital control system being detached from the physical product architecture, layered and (re)connected with the product hierarchy. This led to the insight that the layered modular architecture is accomplished through three transformations. The identification, visual representation and theorization of these transformations are among the key contributions of our research

## Empirical findings: Accomplishing the layered modular architecture

AutoInc’s digitalization journey comprised of three projects (Table 2): the Sensor Platform project, the Personalization project, and the Connectivity Platform project. Table 4 provides a summary of our analysis of each DIM.

## Sensor platform project

In 2005. AutoInc launched the Sensor Platform project to overhaul the entire electrical platform, as well as the driver interaction and infotainment system (i.e., center stack and DIM). While the center stack (located between the driver and the passenger) was mostly dedicated to infotainment (i.e., radio, telephone and navigation), the DIM focused on communicating information relevant to operating the car, e.g., speed and cruise control. In this study, we focus on the DIM only.

The Electrical and Electronics System Engineering group (EESE), which was housed within AutoInc’s R&D organization (Fig. 3), was put in charge of overhauling the electrical platform. This decision reflected the mirroring logic: the EESE organization included the Driver Interaction and Infotainment (DII) group, which was responsible for both the DIM and the center stack. DII owned both the physical components of the DIM (the device layer) and the UI software needed to display information (visual part of content layer)

DII housed three groups, two of which – Infotainment & Telematics (responsible for the center stack) and Driver Interaction & Comfort Electronics (responsible for the DIM) – were classified as PSS (Product System Structure). As verticals within AutoInc’s product hierarchy, PSSs were responsible for a significant subassembly, including its hardware and software. Each PSS also had its own budget with Profit and Loss responsibility.

In contrast, Vehicle HMI was a shared service organization responsible for ensuring that the rendering of the interactions between car and driver (e,g., colors used in speedometer display. font and size in menus) supported driver safety and the driver's cognitive capabilities. As a shared resource, Vehicle HMI lacked PSS status, limiting its decision rights regarding the vehicle design.

Indeed. Vehicle HMI was accorded little respect by the PSSs it served. While the function owners in the PSSs that made up the verticals of the product hierarchy, were almost exclusively engineers, the interaction designers in the HMI group typically had a background in the human sciences, especially cognitive psychology. Given the perceived superiority of the hard sciences in AutoInc, Vehicle HMI was mocked as “putting lipstick” on the vehicle’s product features.

The key DIM-related objective of the Sensor project was to expand the information about the car that could be presented to the driver. The previous DIM relied on lamps dedicated to single functions (e.g., low oil level warning) and a 16\*2-character black and white digital display for short text messages (e.g., “COOLANT LOW STOP ENGINE”). Adding two relatively high-resolution (42 DPI) 2.5”x 2.5” LCDs behind the physical dials of the speedometer and tachometer aforded the display of a larger variety of messages in both textual and graphical formats (see Fig. 4).

The need to develop and arrange new symbols and text messages in the DIM called for the active involvement of the Vehicle HMI group. Vehicle HMI was expected to collaborate with function owners in PSSs both within and outside of DII. By integrating the demands of various function owners in the DIM. Vehicle HMI fulfilled the role of a systems integrator

“We worked as an intermediary between groups [for example those responsible for back cameras and park assistance]. There are diferent groups working with similar things, but they don’t communicate. But both [the back camera group and the park assistance group] have interfaces towards HMI so we are in contact with both of them.” (Interaction Designer)

However, Vehicle HMI’s system integrator role (i.e., partial mirroring), which should have allowed them to gain architectura knowledge and expand their expertise beyond the operational boundaries delineated by the organizational structure, was limited by the fact that function owners had decision rights over a component in its entirety:

“If we take cruise control. In the sensor project, the person that belonged to chassis owned the whole function and he was responsible for HMI ... he was not an HMI person, but he could go to the HMI group and ask for recommendations on how he should specify (the HMI) and how it should work. It was his responsibility to specify it. HMI was more of an advisory unit.” (Software Engineer)

The dominance of the hierarchical arrangement implied that the local interests of function owners constrained the more cross. functional concerns of the HMI group as it sought to design a coherent DIM with novel functionality by mixing and matching diverse sensor data in innovative ways. ## Personalization project

During the course of the Sensor project it became apparent that AutoInc would not be able to meet its strategic objective of improving its competitive position with the digitized DIM. The display capabilities and user interfaces of consumer technologies (e.g., navigation devices) had already eclipsed the new design even prior to its implementation. Also, the two-display DIM was too expensive for AutoInc's smaller, more economical vehicles. Since ceasing the Sensor DIM midstream was infeasible, the manager of DIl and the manager of Vehicle HMI leveraged an ongoing initiative within HMI’s Research & Advanced Engineering to build a fundamentally diferent DIM in parallel using a skunkworks approach

The Research & Advanced Engineering group had been established within Vehicle HMI (Fig. 6) during the Sensor Project when the importance of the system integrator role became apparent. The group developed new functions that enhanced the driving experience and explored how emergent technologies (e.g., speech recognition and haptic touch screens) could be leveraged to present the driver with more information without compromising safety.

In part, the goal of the Personalization project was to demonstrate Vehicle HMI’s capacity to support a new competitive position for AutoInc:

“We realized that interfaces received a lot of critical comments now that more and more functions are implemented. … We saw the opportunity to develop a car that creates similar feelings as an iPhone, and that the user experience will make a great diference in the future, and that it might be a good way to diferentiate ourselves. There was no one [other car brand] that was standing out [on account of its user interface].” (Interaction Designer)

Central to the project objective of bringing the DIM’s user interface closer to the look and feel of consumer electronics was an $8 ^ { \prime \prime } \times 8 ^ { \prime \prime }$ digital color display with a resolution of 117 DPI, an almost threefold improvement over the previous displays. With the addition of a graphical processing unit and a bus with larger bandwidth (500 kb/s), the new DIM would be capable of advanced graphics and animations (like the water drops running down the screen as on the iPhone). Such graphics capabilities were critical to advanced functions like Parking Assistance, where the driver could watch an animated presentation of the car automatically man euvering itself into a parking space.

Instead of physical gauges and needles, the display of the Personalization DIM was entirely digital. The plastic frame that was mounted over the display nevertheless created an illusion of one round display in the middle with two oblong-shaped spaces on either side (see Fig. 7).

By operating as a skunkworks initiative, the Personalization project intentionally broke the mirror between AutoInc’s hierarchica product and task structures:

“My boss and I did what were included in our regular work tasks, but my boss developed an entire business case analysis and supplier negotiations [for the Personalization project], things that R&D usually doesn’t do. But our only chance to get [this Personalization project] through was to do it ourselves, to prove that there was a business case. So we violated all sorts of processes and went all in – and succeeded.” (Vehicle HMI manager)

By defying the hierarchical logic underlying AutoInc’s product development process, the managers responsible for the Personalization project expanded their knowledge far beyond the confines of the tasks for which their organizational units were responsible. Not only did they take ownership of all the DIM hardware decisions, but they also sought expert advice from the telecom industry, which was regarded as a leader in the design of mobile consumer electronics. Specifically, they needed help with developing a customizable DIM that allowed drivers to choose between a sporty, eco-friendly and classic theme. The sporty theme, which was intended to portray a performance- focused driving feel, washed the DIM in shades of red and the middle display presented the engine revolutions to give the driver a sense of how much more power was available to her.

Designing these complex themed DIMs demanded advanced HMI design practices, which did not exist within AutoInc. A manager

“The first people that came to AutoInc to work with interaction design knew nothing about, for example simulation tools. Inspired by the telecom industry's UX design practices, the skunkworks team adopted Motion Lab and Cascade⁴ (“Cascade"). Cascade separated the logical presentation laver from the visual presentation laver of the user interface. This is depicted in the bounding of the content layer in Fig. 8. The former defined the flow of the interaction (e.g., coded menu options), whereas the latter specified the graphical elements (e.g., radial dial vs. linear scale images) through which the data (e.g., speed) was rendered. In this way, the logical and visual elements of the HMI became distinct, afording designers the ability to change symbols, animations and colors without afecting the overall flow and logic of the DIM.

One of Cascade’s key afordances was simulation, which allowed the Personalization team to adopt a rapid prototyping approach. Vehicle HMI leveraged this ability to expand organizational participation in the design process. AutoInc’s senior management, including the CFO and the directors of Design and R&D, reviewed and commented on candidate DIM designs. This form of prototyping raised top management’s awareness of and interest in HMI in general, and elevated the Personalization project’s status from a skunkworks to a high-profile project.

Additionally, an internally-developed standardized interface, a protocol named “Public Attribute” (PA), started to emerge. As shown in Fig. 8, it separated the content and service layers of the DIM stack. Based on object-oriented programming logic, public attributes specified the input and output parameters of a given function. One software engineer explained:

![](/api/attachments/9GXGPGG3/fulltext/images/524cdec84571b669c3b583ecd39b21686e3270487ef3df5932f6bd70c51ba900.jpg)

“The person who develops the graphics and the logic of the HMI [content layer] is not interested in what my program specification [service layer] looks like…. But they [the HMI people and the software engineers] still have to communicate with each other, and that is where the public attribute comes in. It basically does a mapping of internal signals [generated by the service layer] and HMI [content layer].”

For example, ACC (Adaptive Cruise Control) used five public attributes: information about set speed, the time gap to the target vehicle (i.e., car in front), information about the target vehicle, the information display mode (e.g., temporary deactivation or standby mode), and the sounds used to communicate alerts to the driver. Each PA specified not only parameters (e.g., time gap to the target vehicle), but also flags to communicate states such as validity, availability, and status. These flags were used to instruct the DIM software (logical presentation in Fig. 8) to show a certain graphical indicator (visual presentation in Fig. 8) such as a triangle to show that the radar is blocked, in a certain color (e.g., red) and in a given display mode (e.g., flashing).

One key implication of the PA protocol was that the system engineers in the DII group who were tasked with developing applications (service layer) and who had previously had little to do with HMI, found themselves intermediating increasingly between the function owners (device layer) and the Vehicle HMI group (content layer). They thus became system integrators coordinating signals from multiple branches of the product hierarchy (Fig. 9), reflecting a logic of partial mirroring

By creating relationships with the disparate PSSs within AutoInc, the system engineers negotiated access to the raw sensor data, which allowed them to mix and match various data sources in new and innovative ways. Responsibility for manipulating and aggregating signals from the sensors, which had previously been under the function owners’ control in the device layer (Fig. 5), thus migrated to the DII-based software engineers (service layer in Fig. 8).

Increasingly Vehicle HMI’s organization began to mirror the DIM’s layered architecture. Whereas previously one HMI engineer had completed tasks that cut across the layers of the product stack (e.g., communicating with function owners, specifying the HMI logic and graphics, and testing the implementation against the specifications), HMI tasks now became more specialized. For example, interaction designers developed the graphical or visual presentation of the DIM; usability engineers were responsible for its logical presentation; and system engineers worked on applications that manipulated and aggregated data in the DIM architecture’s service layer.

This increased specialization was accompanied by the development of two separate DIM specifications: one that handled HMI issues (e.g., content layer questions of what data was to be presented and how), and another that dealt with the application logic (i.e., service layer) and the public attributes. One of the software engineers explained:

“The biggest issue for me was how to divide the specification, because the function owner wants one thing, the design department wants something else and then HMI want something diferent again. There has to be some kind of logic in between, a red thread. In the end we did the Public Attribute that basically is a protocol and divided the specification into two, where I owned one part and the HMI the other.'

The increasingly layered modularity of the DIM architecture meant that teams could work independently of and parallel to each other, leading to the project’s completion in record time:

“We established a whole new working process for HMI development, with new tools and everything. But it worked, I am most proud of that project out of everything I have done. I am proud of the product that came out of it and in addition we actually established a whole new work process.” (HMI manager)

Confronted with the success of the Peronalization project, AutoInc now faced a conflict of stakeholder needs; while satisfying the customer required a bold move towards learning new practices and technologies that had been pioneered in the consumer electronics space to increase product innovation and design flexibility, appeasing internal stakeholders entailed operating in ways that maintained controlled routines typical of the auto industry.

AutoInc resolved the conflict of stakeholder prioritization by elevating Vehicle HMI’s standing in the organizational hierarchy. It was given ownership over the physical displays (DIM and Center stack) and their respective system software (device layer). It also owned the content layer and parts of the service layers of the digital control system. Additionally, the group was renamed “Interaction Engineering” (IE), recasting its work as hard science. The group’s newly acquired independence and its ownership over the upper layers of the digita control system implied that Vehicle HMI was free to prioritize the needs of the customer. In contrast, the rest of the organization was able to maintain its focus on internal stakeholders and its established hierarchical structure and practices.

## Connectivity platform project

Motivated by the persistent competitive threat from consumer electronics (e.g., navigation services) and supported by an increasingly layered DIM architecture, a mirrored organizational structure and top management support, DII entered the next phase of DIM digitalization, namely the Connectivity Project. In 2012, AutoInc’s senior management oficially declared the design of the car’s user interface (i.e., DIM and center stack) a key competitive diferentiator; user experience (UX) had become a cornerstone in AutoInc’s branding. With this strategic mandate to develop a driver- centered solution that rivaled the graphical quality, information richness and rapid adaptability of consumer electronics, the Connectivity Project represented another platform redesign. A manager from the EESE group who had been the project manager for the Sensor Platform, explained the project’s technical imperative:

“[...] we have created a patchwork of technical solutions and it is starting to get very difficult to get an overview of it and understand the consequences of a change. If we pull one thread we don’t know where all the ends are. Because of that, we think we have to start from scratch and create a new structure, a new scafold to grow into.”

The lack of architectural integrity that was the result of porting the Personalization DIM onto the Sensor platform, highlighted that AutoInc was far from ofering drivers the capability to dynamically source third-party services (e.g., Spotify’s music streaming). Despite considerable concerns about AutoInc’s control over the vehicle’s integrity and safety, the Connectivity Platform project envisaged the car as a software platform, whose digital service offerings could continuously be extended through an ecosystem of 3rd party application developers. As an interaction designer in IE put it, when the car becomes a technology platform “the sky is the limit” of innovation.

To accommodate the anticipated explosion of infotainment services, a larger and more powerful display was needed. With a size of $1 2 . 3 ^ { \prime \prime } \times 1 2 . 3 ^ { \prime \prime } ,$ , a resolution of 125 DPI and full color, the new screen (see Fig. 10) could even show movies. To fully utilize the new display, larger- capacity communication buses (i.e., FlexRay and Apix2) were implemented. Also, a direct communication bus between the key component of the center stack and the DIM allowed navigation functionality to be displayed in three diferent places: the center stack, the DIM and a heads-up display (i.e., projection onto the windscreen). This, together with Internet connectivit enabling just-in-time app sourcing, further increased the customizability and functionality of the DIM.

![](/api/attachments/9GXGPGG3/fulltext/images/5ab5a90ee84b6ebc74582c16cd7704c4920a994aa5040bcf6767a0076cd85bf2.jpg)  
Fig. 10. DIM of the Connectivity Platform Project.

In order to transform the car into a platform, APIs (application programming interfaces) needed to be added to the DIM architecture. These APIs uncoupled the device and network layers (Fig. 11) in the DIM’s architecture from the more ephemeral aspects of the stack (i.e., service and content layers). As the owner of the car-as-platform, AutoInc exclusively controlled the layers below the APIs and relied on an ecosystem of app developers to complete the value of its platform.

To formalize the lines of ownership, the new Interaction Engineering (IE) group was elevated to PSS status in March 2012. With full responsibility for all HMI in the car, including the hardware and software required for the DIM, IE was now on a par with the product areas that it had previously served as a shared services organization. PSS status further implied that IE now had its own budget and deliverables on the car’s development schedule. As a result, IE had to comply with the product hierarchy’s R&D project governance, which entailed delivering set documentation and attending project planning meetings.

A key challenge for IE was that many of the component in the DIM were digital, making them dificult to document in the project governance forms, which had been designed for physical parts and sub-assemblies. For example, the document templates required specification for how a given component would be tested for dampness and crashworthiness, non-applicable to software:

“There are tons of boxes you have to fill in and many of them do not fit [for HMI]. It is probably 80% … at the very least, 60% are not relevant for HMI.” (HMI engineer)

This poor alignment between the governance process and HMI’s tasks highlights a conflict of work practice that arose from integrating the layered architecture of digital components into the hierarchical architecture of physical goods:

“Suddenly … they [R&D management] followed up on what HMI did. … The HMI engineers were supposed to deliver specifications and suddenly they were part of the oficial [development] process with gates and everything; “In this gate the specification should be 100% ready, …” It was a total change for the group. The manager responsible for the implementation of HMI burned out and was sick for weeks.” (SW Engineer)

The stage-gate-driven and specification-intensive approach to project governance (hierarchy logic) contradicted the platformbased approach to development, which prioritized flexibility and emergence (layered logic). AutoInc resolved the work practice conflict by institutionalizing feature teams. Feature teams represented new cross-functional arrangements that included HMI people, system engineers and function owners (Fig. 12).

The feature teams were led by members of IE, whose system integration roles put them in a position where they could mix and match data from disparate functions in order to create new user-focused (rather than function-driven) DIM “features.” For example, three distinct safety functions – all owned by diferent function owners, i.e., collision warning, brake assistant and auto- brake – were integrated into a single safety feature whose settings (i.e., earlier, normal. later auto- braking) could be customized by the driver who had to set only a single parameter. The Interaction designer who led one of the feature teams, described the new design practice as follows:

“All function owners that have some setting [relevant to the DIM] can come to our feature team meetings and request a specific setting. Then we will sit in the feature team and work with them: Do we need that setting or can we take it away? Can we simplify it? Sometimes you can merge two settings that come from diferent functional areas. The function owners frequently don’t know that the other settings existed.”

Internally, IE solidified the mirroring between the layered architecture (design) and its organizational arrangements (tasks). Its human resources clustered into four units:

The Advanced Research group experimented with new hardware and software to enable innovative interaction design, such as interactions through gestures and voice.

The Implementation group focused mainly on the content layer, working with the visual data and the logical presentation layers to prototype new HMIs.

• The System group worked primarily with the Public Attributes to clarify what signal statuses. flags, and variables were required for a feature. Some also worked with the aggregation of signals and application development. which was now located in the service laver. They also had close contact with the people developing API’s in order to map signals between the service and network layers.

• The Driver Information group was responsible for the DIM overall and had the final say on what was to be presented in it. With this responsibility they worked with both the physical display (device) and what to present on the display (service layer), but they were also involved in developing the content. Nevertheless, the Implementation group maintained overall responsibility for the DIM's user interface.

The shift in design responsibility from the function owners (i.e., hierarchical product hierarchy) to IE (i.e., layered digital control system) implies a reversal in power relations. Indeed, the power shifts towards the upper layers of the digital control system is indicative of hardware’s domestication by software (Kallinikos 2012).

Furthermore, relving on feature teams rendered ambiguous coordination of AutoInc's digitalization initiative, The interfaces inherent in the product's hierarchical and lavered architectures were no longer stable points of articulation. Instead, hand-offs between the various responsible parties were dynamic and required some knowledge and task overlap (i.e., partial mirroring):

“So if you run 4x400 meter relay, what is it that you have to practice? Yes, the handover. Because everyone can run, but it is the handover that makes the diference. …We have the same mantra here, practice the handover. … So, the optimal handover has an overlap. It is not a line that, for example, describes where a specific function ends and another one starts. We have to reach over to the other sides and follow through together.” (Manager)

While the work practice conflict was evident in the realm of both internal and external stakeholders, the discontinuities between AutoInc and its new non- automotive, 3rd party application suppliers who completed the value of the car-as-platform (layered logic) further revealed an identity conflict. The peer-based relationships that these vendors sought to enact were at odds with the contractin practices established with traditional automotive suppliers (hierarchy logic). A manager explained:

“We as a carmaker are traditionally top of the food-chain and we can put whatever pressure we want on our suppliers. But if we want to work with Google in the car, the power balance is the opposite. … we need a partnership … [and since] we don’t pay them, the relationship with them is … much more equal. … [With automotive suppliers] you want tough requirements regarding guarantees and things like that. But when you talk to Spotify you don’t really apply requirements regarding guarantees on a service like that.”

This identity conflict raised questions about which players AutoInc should identify with versus diferentiate itself from. While AutoInc had traditionally framed other auto makers as its primary competitors (implying that they needed to maintain suficient homogeneity with and distance from them), with their pursuit of a digital platform strategy they now found themselves in a new playing field. Increasingly digital platform providers (e.g., Apple, Google) whose customers overlapped considerably with AutoInc’s customers, leveraged these customer relationships to encroach on the consumer’s driving experience.

In response to this identity conflict, AutoInc formed another new organization in IE, namely the User Experience group, which soon became known as DUX (Digital User Experience). Composed of former members of the HMI group and pursuant of user centered design, this new organization was tasked with investigating how the AutoInc’s new digital platform strategy could be used to build innovating driver-car relationships. Furthermore, the DUX organization was externally focused; they started design projects with organizations outside of the automotive industry, e.g., Lego, and brought in new talent from the telecom industry. They were also tasked with sourcing digital content and apps from third parties.

Adopting new performance criteria, such as the “emotional attractiveness” of a UI, the DUX team sought to hybridize the governance practices anchored in the physical product. Specifically, in contrast to the boundedness and cohesive functionality of “features,” the concept of “emotional attractiveness” was intended to blur established definitions and performance criteria of functions. Additionally, however, DUX hybridized the practices of the feature teams by referring to themselves as “caretakers” of the driver experience. This served as a reminder that the feature teams had an obligation towards ensuring driver and passenger safety in addition to innovating the user interface.

## Discussion

Digital innovation, that is, enhancing physical artifacts by means of digital capabilities (Lyytinen et al. 2015; Yoo et al. 2010), has become a strategic imperative for many organizations (e.g., Tilson et al. 2010; Tripsas 2009). Despite garnering considerable attention in IS research (e.g., Bailey et al. 2012; Barrett et al. 2012; Loebbecke and Picot 2015; Lucas and Goh 2009), a detailed understanding of digital innovation from an architectural perspective (Lee and Berente 2012; Yoo et al. 2010) has remained elusive. It is unclear what the lavered modular architecture, which is advanced as the infrastructure of digitalization. looks like. Furthermore. there are questions about how this architecture, which hybridizes opposing modular arrangements (i.e., hierarchical and layered), is accomplished both organizationally and technologically. This paper thus focuses on answering the question, “Given that the layered modular architecture needs to hybridize modular arrangements with opposing logics, how is it accomplished?”

Drawing on our analysis of a 10-year window in AutoInc’s ongoing DIM digitalization efort, we identify three transformations through which the layered modular architecture was produced:

## Transformation A: Uncoupling the digital control system from the physical product hierarchy

Noting Lee and Berente’s (2012) definition of digital innovation as the uncoupling of the digital control system from the physica product hierarchy, the separation of a unified hierarchical architecture into two is a necessary condition for moving along the continuum from product innovation to digital innovation (Yoo et al. 2010). In AutoInc, this uncoupling was evident in the effort to integrate digital signals that originated from diferent verticals in the product hierarchy into a unified display capable of using the limited space on the DIM to communicate a wide variety of messages related to a diverse set of sensor-enabled components in the car. No longer was a direct connection between a physical component (e,g., fuel tank) and a lamp on the dashboard necessary. Instead, the digital control system mediated this connection.

However, this transformation did not generate the desired results of a more innovative DIM. As illustrated in Fig. 13, which depicts the outcome of this uncoupling of the physical and digital architectures in AutoInc's Sensor project, this apparent failure can be attributed to the lack of a layered digital control system (shown by dotted horizontal lines in Fig. 13). With a single requirements document that combined hardware, software and user interface specifications into one, as well as ownership over tranches of the stack residing with the organizational units that controlled the car component to which a given message pertained (shown by solid horizontal lines and links from the device layer only to sub-assemblies responsible for a given tranche), the ability to mix and match digital signals into novel communications was severely curtailed

For example, the signal aggregation, which should ideally be located in the service layer so that diferent programs can manipulate the raw data to fit the system requirements, was embedded in the device layer (see Fig. 5). This was because ownership over the sensors and the data they produced rested with the vertical in the physical product hierarchy that was responsible for the physica component (e.g., fuel tank) monitored by the sensors.

The mirroring between the organizational hierarchy and the digital control system meant that the vertical divisions in the digita control system were stronger than the horizontal ones (layers) and that HMI’s role as integrators, trying to tie signals from disparate parts of the product hierarchy together, proved exceedingly challenging. The incongruence between the vertical and layered logics found expression in an organizing conflict (Smith and Lewis 2011), which entailed a clash between the hierarchical structures and processes that maintaining stability and the layered configuration of the digital control system that sought to facilitate generativity and innovation. This conflict produced a disappointing DIM.

## Transformation B: Layering the digital control system

The poor results of the Sensor project created the conditions for an under-the-radar, mirror-breaking skunkworks initiative. By creating a self-suficient organization separate from the established hierarchy and engaging in reflective dissension (Henfridsson and Yoo 2013), the skunkworks organization produced a layered architecture for the digital control system by adopting tools and practices from the telecom industry. The DIM architecture’s layering (Fig. 14) was efective in that it aforded new practices, e.g., prototyping the UI with input from senior management, indicating that diferent parts of the design (material) and task (social) can “introduce a level of indeterminacy and unpredictability” (Lee and Berente, 2012: 1428).

While breaking the mirror between the hierarchical product organization and the DIM architecture resolved the organizational design conflict by forming an organization that operated parallel to the organizational hierarchy, the success of the Personalization DIM highlighted a tension between external and internal stakeholder, which Smith and Lewis (2011) label a performing conflict as a plurality of stakeholders pursue divergent definitions organizational success.

AutoInc resolved the performing conflict by building an organization that owned the digital control system and elevating its status within the organization. Vehicle HMI’s shift from a shared services organization to fully fledged business unit (i.e., PSS status with ownership over the DIM's hardware and software). strategically important (i.e., responsibility for the car's user experience) and respected (i.e., name change to Interaction Engineering (IE) framing their work as hard science), accomplished the separation of competing stakeholder needs. As a relatively independent business unit, Vehicle HMI was free to develop solutions that maximized driver experience and strategic diferentiation from other car manufacturers. Importantly, this transformation of the social structure was critical to achieving the lavered modular architecture at the material level.

During the course of the Personalization project, the organization of the Vehicle HMI group and the DIM’s architecture came to mirror each other, with diferent groups of professionals specializing in diferent layers. Interaction designers developed the graphical or visual presentation of the DIM, usability engineers were responsible for its logical presentation, and system engineers worked on applications that manipulated and aggregated data in the service layer. This transformation at the departmental level reflects the design-task isomorphism that afords innovation (Colfer and Baldwin 2016).

## Transformation C: (Re)Connecting the two architectures

A consequence of institutionalizing the ownership of the digital control system in an organizational unit that was integrated into AutoInc's extant hierarchical organizational structure, was that organizing conflicts (Smith and Lewis 2011) again arose. These revolved around disagreements about organizational structure and processes to coordinate the design of a new car. The stage-gatedriven and specification-intensive approach to project governance that was enacted in the physical product hierarchy, contradicted the platform-based approach to development, which prioritized creativity, flexibility and emergence (layered logic).

Transformation C, which entails building infrastructures capable of dynamically integrating the digital control system with the product hierarchy, addresses this conflict. Organizationally, IE connected the two architectures through their compliance with

AutoInc’s governance process (hierarchical structure) and the collaborative work of feature teams (layered structure). Interestingly, both of these connective mechanisms aforded stakeholders’ knowledge work beyond the operational boundaries as delineated by the task design (i.e., job roles). Specifically, the governance process was designed to develop standardized protocols that ensured nonredundant coordination and tight quality control across the verticals of the organizational hierarchy. Similarly, the objective of the feature teams was to reach across functions and facilitate collaboration, albeit in a more fluid and more redundant manner, so as to enable the generation of new design ideas (see Fig. 15).

The tensions between these two modes of coordination had implications for AutoInc’s identity. While it aligned itself with the auto manufacturers by maintaining its hierarchical governance process, in its development of the digital control system it seemed to identify more with the players in the consumer electronics industry. The blurring of industry boundaries, which is one of the efects of digital innovation (e.g., Tilson et al. 2010), thus manifests itself in conflicts of belonging (Smith and Lewis 2011), which revolye around an organization’s need to simultaneously identify with but also diferentiate itself from others in its industry. AutoInc’s creation of the DUX organization, which was tasked with sourcing digital content and services (i.e., apps) from digital platform providers, highlights its efort to manage this conflict over its identity.

## Contributions

Our study makes a number of contributions to IS research on digital innovation, particularly architectural theories of this phenomenon. First, we ofer a visualization of the layered modular architecture (Yoo et al. 2010) in Fig. 2, which is advanced as a critical success factor of digital innovation. Adopting Lee and Berente’s (2012) definition of digital innovation as the uncoupling of the digital control system from the physical product hierarchy, we present the layered modular architecture as the hybridization of the conventional product hierarchy and the layered architecture associated with digital goods. While de-coupled, these two architectures are nevertheless connected both materially (i.e., sensors in physical components generating digital signals that constitute the digital control system) and socially (i.e., feature teams developing novel DIM features through cross-functional collaboration). Our research highlights the role of the organizationa aspects of digital innovation, rendering Fig. 14 a more complete visualization of the layered modular architecture.

Second, we articulate that a fundamental contradiction underlying digital innovation derives from the hybridization of hierarchical and layered configurations of modules that is needed to accomplish the layered modular architecture. While Svahn and Henfridsson (2012) note that the tensions in digital innovation are the result of diferences in the development of physical and digita artifacts, we ofer a more nuanced and theoretically-grounded understanding of these tensions that have been reported in numerous empirical studies of digital innovation (Hylving et al. 2012; Sandberg et al. 2019). By articulating the ways in which the two modularity configurations are fundamentally at odds, e.g., the ability to specify the complete product ahead of time (hierarchica logic) vs. regarding the product as continuously-evolving (layered logic), we also extend Henfridsson et al. (2014) work on the discontinuities between a hierarchy-of-parts and a network- of-patterns. Furthermore, we demonstrate how the contradictions between hierarchical and layered modularity manifest themselves in conflicts of organizing, performing and belonging, which Smith and Lewis (2011) have identified as the prototypical dialectics of organizational life.

Third, by taking the materiality of technological architecture seriously (Orlikowski and Scott 2008), we highlight that Yoo et al. (2010) naming of the hybrid architecture is unfortunate, in that it equates modularity with hierarchy. However, since modules, i.e., relatively independent units that perform a coherent task (Baldwin and Clark 2000), can be arranged in layers also, future research might consider re-naming the layered modular architecture. Based on the opposing logics we identify in this paper, “layered hierarchical architecture” might be a more appropriate label.

Fourth, we identify three transformations that are necessary to accomplish a layered modular architecture: (A) uncoupling the digital control system from the physical product hierarchy, (B) layering the digital control system, and (C) (re)connecting the tw architectures to form a hybrid.

Importantly, these transformations highlight how deeply intertwined the social and technological dimensions of digital innovation are. Most prior research prioritizes either the technological (e,g., Huang et al. 2016: Ravichandran 2018: Scott et al. 2017) or the organizational aspects (e.g., Rolland et al. 2018; Tumbas et al. 2018; Yeow et al. 2018). Even though the importance of changes in processes to digital innovation are frequently mentioned (e.g., Huang et al. 2016), how the social and the material are intertwined has not been demonstrated thus far. Our study thus provides the conceptual groundwork for theorizing the social aspects in architectural theories of digital innovation.

In addition, our explicitly social-and-material presentation of the architectural theory of digital innovation, ofers a conceptual scafold for research on organizational agility, in which “digital platform capabilities” (Ravichandran 2018: 25) are deemed to play a central role. These capabilities are framed primarily in technological terms, but our study shows just how central the organizationa structures and practices are in developing and maintaining such platform capabilities.

Lastly, the three transformations advanced in this paper ofer guidance on how to accomplish a layered modular/hierarchica architecture to both practitioners and researchers alike. They outline the kinds of challenges and conflicts that organizations seeking to institutionalize platforms either as a technological infrastructure or an organizational form (Hinings et al. 2018). are likely to face and how they might efectively resolve them.

## Limitations

These contributions need to be qualified by our study’s limitations. By collecting and theorizing the empirical material derived from a single case study of digital innovation, claims of generalizability would be misplaced, despite the efectiveness of single cases for theory building (Eisenhardt and Graebner 2007). Our abductive theorizing, instead, leads us to advance the three transformations as a plausibl explanation for how organizations move along the continuum from a modular/hierarchical product architecture to a hybrid one.

However, despite the ordering of the three transformation that we signal with our alphabetical labeling (which reflect the sequence in which they occurred in AutoInc), we refrain from generalizing it to all digitalization eforts. While it seems reasonable, from a purely technical perspective, that uncoupling the digital control system from the product hierarchy (Transformation A) precedes its layering (Transformation B), our research highlights just how intertwined the social and material aspects of digita innovation are. And since the mirroring hypothesis posits a correlative rather than a causal relationship between product design and tasks, the sequence of transformations might be diferent in other empirical settings.

Even though abduction is deemed the weakest form of scientific reasoning (Behfar and Okhuysen 2018), the conjectural explanations it produces are nevertheless conducive to discovery of novel phenomena and the narrowing of possible explanations (Bamberger 2018). As such, our insight should be evaluated in terms of their potential to open up new lines of inquiry into the sociomaterial nature (Orlikowski and Scott 2008) of digital innovation. For example, identifying the diferent ways in which these three transformations unfold over time in a digital innovation initiative, under what conditions and with what efect, promises to be a productive stream of research.

Furthermore, while our interview sample is rather small, the richness of our interview data, which was generated using laddering, an efective meaning elicitation technique (Schultze and Avital 2011), ensured that detailed insights into the sociomaterial nature of digital innovation were produced. In abductive theorizing, data richness can compensate for a low-volume sample, as highly contextualized narratives form the basis for evaluating the plausibility of alternative explanations (Bruner 1990)

## Conclusion

While Yoo et al.’s (2010) architectural theory of digital innovation, which describes digitalization as a movement along a continuum from a modular (i.e., hierarchical) product architecture to a layered modular architecture, might conjure images of a smooth transition from one end of the continuum to the other, empirical research notes that digital innovation is fraught with discontinuities and conflict. In this paper, we theorize that the source of this conflict lies in the incongruity between hierarchical and layered configuration of modules, which need to be hybridized to form the layered modular architecture.

Furthermore, we highlight the interdependence between organizational structure and product architecture in digital innovation. In line with the mirroring hypothesis, our empirical material shows that a change in organizational structure (e.g., initiating a skunkworks project group) preceded architectural change (e.g., layering of the digital control system). At other times, organizationa structure (e.g., departmentalization of the Information Engineering group) was prompted by shifts in the product design (e.g., layers in the digital control system).

Finally, we answer our research question “Given that the layered modular architecture needs to hybridize modular arrangements with opposing logics, how is it accomplished?” as follows: accomplishing the layered modular architecture requires three transformations: (A) uncoupling the digital control system from the physical product hierarchy, (B) layering the digital control systems, and (C) (re)connecting the architectures continuously. Importantly. these transformations go bevond changes in the materiality of the product architecture; they are sociomaterial in nature.
