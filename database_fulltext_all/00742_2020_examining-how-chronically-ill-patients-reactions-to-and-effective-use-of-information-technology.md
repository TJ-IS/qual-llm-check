# EXAMINING HOW CHRONICALLY ILL PATIENTS’ REACTIONS TO AND EFFECTIVE USE OF INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY CAN INFLUENCE HOW WELL THEY SELF-MANAGE THEIR ILLNESS<sup>1</sup>

Azadeh Savoli IESEG School of Management, 3 rue de la Digue, 59000 Lille, FRANCE {a.savoli@ieseg.fr}

Henri Barki HEC Montréal, 3000 Côte-Sainte-Catherine Road, Montréal, Québec, CANADA H3T 2A7 {henri.barki@hec.ca}

Guy Paré HEC Montréal, 3000 Côte-Sainte-Catherine Road, Montréal, Québec, CANADA H3T 2A7 {guy.pare@hec.ca}

While chronically ill patients can significantly benefit from self-management (SM) information systems, they are also unlikely to perceive, use, and benefit from them in the same way, and past research has observed that many patients tend to not use such systems effectively. A key premise of the present study is that attributing the cause of one’s success or failure in self-managing one’s chronic disease to SM information systems is likely to influence how patients react to such systems, which in turn is likely to influence their usage behaviors and SM performance. Building upon attribution theory and learned helplessness theory, this paper examines how patients’ causal attributions of their success or failure in self-managing their chronic illness tends to influence the way they cognitively perceive, emotionally react to, and use an IT-based SM system. It also examines what constituted effective use in the SM context that was studied and how patients’ effective use of an IT-based SM system tended to influence their SM performance. Based on data collected from patients who were using a web-based asthma SM portal, the paper identifies three SM attributional styles and three patient views of SM information systems that help explain how chronically ill patients tend to interact with such systems, as well as the consequences of this interaction, and discusses the implications of the findings for research and practice.

Keywords: Chronic disease self-management, effective use, attribution styles, IT-based self-management systems, attribution theory, learned helplessness theory, mixed-method research

## Introduction

The development of chronic conditions, such as diabetes, asthma, and cardiovascular diseases, is largely associated with unhealthy lifestyles (Dufour et al. 2014; Fortin et al. 2014), and chronic illnesses have surpassed infectious diseases across the world as leading causes of mortality (WHO 2018). A key consequence of the paternalistic model of health care has been patients’ passive role in their health care decisions (WHO 2003), which in turn has been observed to be less effective for treating chronic conditions (Walker et al. 2004) than requiring patients to self-manage their diseases by choosing healthier lifestyle behaviors and using a skill set developed through information and support obtained from various resources (Bodenheimer et al. 2010; Lawn and Schoo 2010).

Patients’ involvement in the management of their own care is referred to as self-management (SM) and has been defined as

the individual’s ability to manage the symptoms, treatment, physical and psychosocial consequences and life style changes inherent in living with a chronic condition (Barlow et al. 2002, p. 178).

According to past research, effective SM is often difficult to achieve, as indicated by patients’ poor rates of adherence to treatment guidelines, poor psychological well-being, and reduced quality of life (e.g., Gamble et al. 2009; Turner et al. 1998). The recognition of such difficulties has led to the development of diverse disease-related education programs and community initiatives, often labeled as “SM interventions,” which target different aspects of how patients manage their chronic conditions. Successful SM interventions enable chronic patients to make informed decisions about their own care and become responsible members of a healthcare team (Anderson et al. 1991), as well as enhancing a patient’s selfefficacy (i.e., his/her confidence that he/she has the capacity to undertake specific behaviors, such as eating healthy foods and exercising regularly), which in turn can improve their health conditions (Bandura 1977). While past research has examined a variety of SM interventions, their effectiveness has also been observed to be mixed (Barlow et al. 2002; Schulmann-Green et al. 2012), which suggests that further research is needed in order to examine the potential barriers and facilitators of SM interventions (Mackey et al. 2016).

Interestingly, IT-based systems, such as telehealth applications and patient portals, are being increasingly used in the SM of several chronic diseases (Or et al. 2011; Kitsiou et al. 2015; Kitsiou et al. 2017; Tieu et al. 2015), as they are thought to make it easier to provide integrated patient support by enabling continuous patient monitoring and feedback, as well as by facilitating two-way communication and information exchange between patients and healthcare providers. Providing chronic patients with timely access to their health information and personalized alerts, especially when there is a need for action, is also thought to be an effective approach for empowering them in self-managing their illnesses (Ahmed et al. 2016).

While chronic patients are thought to potentially significantly benefit from IT-based SM systems, past research has also observed inconsistent findings regarding how such systems tend to influence patients’ SM capacity, their health outcomes, and the consumption of healthcare resources ( Kitsiou et al. 2017; Kruse et al. 2015; Osborn et al. 2010). As far as reactions to such systems are concerned, researchers have also observed that chronic patients are unlikely to use them in the same ways or to the same extent. For example, some chronic patients were observed not to use IT-based SM systems in expected or effective ways, with some of them keeping information from healthcare providers (Nicolini 2007), while others got frustrated and abruptly stopped to use them (Hess et al. 2007). Even though examining which patients can benefit most from IT-based SM systems has not been the goal of many studies, some researchers have tentatively suggested that the beneficial effects of such systems are likely to be observed mainly in patients who have a severe chronic condition (Trappenberg et al. 2008), play an active role in managing their chronic illness (Madsen et al. 2008), and have sufficient health literacy or knowledge of their chronic condition (Tieu et al. 2015). In essence, considering that all chronic patients are unlikely to similarly perceive, use, and benefit from ITbased SM systems, or do so to the same extent, this paper attempts to improve our understanding of the phenomenon of IT-based SM systems so that chronic patients who are likely to react positively to, and benefit most from, such systems can be successfully targeted.

Based on this objective, and by drawing on attribution theory (AT) (Weiner 1985) and learned helplessness theory (LHT) (Abramson et al. 1978), the paper develops the concept of SM attributional style and explores its synergistic influences on patients’ cognitive, emotional, and behavioral reactions to an IT-based SM system, and ultimately on their SM performance. Research across different domains of human activity has highlighted that an individual’s attributional style, defined as a trait-like stable pattern of causal attributions to events or outcomes, can significantly influence his/her reactions and actions (Bellu and Sherman 1995; Bridges 2001; Proudfoot et al. 2001). According to Mamonov and Koufaris (2018), extensive research on employee behaviors in organizational contexts has identified attributional style as an important predictor of whether an employee will tend to behave in a constructive manner or be unproductive when facing work challenges. Based on a similar reasoning, this study examined how patients’ causal attributions regarding their SM performance tend to influence how they cognitively perceive, emotionally react to, and use an IT-based SM system. In addition, by drawing from Burton-Jones and Grange (2013) and Burton-Jones and Volkoff (2017), the paper examines what constituted effective use in this context and how patients’ effective use of an IT-based SM system tended to influence their SM performance.

In order to attain the above objectives, the paper examines qualitative and quantitative data that had been collected from a group of 32 asthma patients who had used a web-based SM portal for several weeks in the context of a clinical trial. The data first indicated that asthma patients tended to attribute their SM performance to a wide variety of causes, such as exercising, medications, environmental conditions, healthcare providers, their knowledge of asthma, meditation and positive thinking, the severity of their condition, and luck. An indepth analysis of the data led to the identification of three distinct SM attributional styles (labeled autonomous, engaged, and reliant), which were observed to strongly influence how patients cognitively and emotionally reacted to the portal. In essence, autonomous patients tended to perceive the portal as an imposer, engaged patients mostly viewed it as a facilitator, and reliant patients saw it mainly as a protector. In addition, patients’ patterns of causal attributions were also observed to be influenced by their attitude toward their illness, as well as their perspective regarding how it should be managed and by whom, with each cluster of cognitive and emotional reactions leading to a different set of portal usage behaviors. Finally, highly effective usage of the portal, mainly reflected through reflection-in-action, was also found to be a necessary condition for patients to achieve higher levels of SM performance.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. The next section provides the key theoretical foundations that guided the study and, following Popper’s (1963) epistemology, a series of conjectures are proposed. Next, a detailed explanation of the mixed-methods approach that was used is provided, followed by its findings. Finally, the study’s implications for research and practice, as well as its limitations and directions for future research are discussed.

## Theoretical Background

The roots of AT can be traced to Heider (1958) who noted that people seek to make sense of positive and negative events in their lives, and developed the concepts of social perception and causal attribution in interpersonal relationships. The process of sense-making involves assigning causes to the outcomes one observes, which over time leads people to develop stable attributional patterns. As noted by Peterson (1991), individuals can have different attributional styles (i.e., different ways in which they tend to habitually explain the causes of good or bad outcomes). The concept of attributional style was first introduced by Abramson et al. (1978) in LHT and was developed by analyzing the patterns of how people explained the events they experienced. Past research has examined the concept of attributional style along four dimensions: locus, controllability, globality, and stability (Anderson et al. 1991; Mamonov and Koufaris 2018; Peterson et al. 1982; Weiner 2000). Locus refers to whether one views the cause of an outcome or event to be located within oneself (internal) or outside (external); controllability refers to the extent to which a cause is viewed to be under one’s volitional control; globality refers to one’s perception that causal responsibility for outcomes is always present in different contexts; and stability refers to one’s perception of how stable a cause is across time.

Based on these dimensions, LHT identified two attributional styles (i.e., pessimistic and optimistic) in order to explain individual differences in helplessness. The pessimistic style is characterized by one’s tendency to attribute negative outcomes to one’s personal characteristics (i.e., “I caused the bad event”), which are stable (i.e., “the cause is going to last for a long time”) and global (i.e., “the cause is going to challenge many areas of one’s life”), and to attribute positive outcomes to external causes, such as good fortune (i.e., external locus, not stable, and specific) (Abramson et al. 1978). On the other hand, the optimistic style is characterized by a tendency to attribute positive outcomes to one’s personal characteristics, which are stable and global, while attributing negative outcomes to external causes, which are unstable and specific.

Early studies on attributional styles focused on general attributions related to life events, with later studies focusing on domain-specific attributional styles (Ashforth and Fugate 2006; Proudfoot et al. 2001). Given its objective to examine how chronic patients’ attributional styles may affect their reactions to an IT-based SM system, and ultimately their SM performance, the present study also focused on a contextspecific attributional style, labeling it SM attributional style, and based on existing definitions of general attributional style (Proudfoot et al. 2009), it defined it as a trait-like characteristic that describes how chronic patients attribute the causes of their good and/or bad SM performance episodes.

According to past research, an optimistic SM attributional style would reflect a perception of personal responsibility for one’s outcomes, which is an attribution pattern related to proactive behaviors, and has been observed among employees, athletes, and students (Hanrahan and Cerin 2009; Peterson and Barrett 1987; Seligman and Schulman 1986). As noted earlier, using IT-based SM systems provides chronic patients with opportunities to learn more about their conditions and develop abilities to better self-manage their illnesses. Hence, consistent with the effects of internal attributions that have been observed in other contexts, the following conjecture can be suggested: the tendency to ascribe causal responsibility for episodes of good SM performance to oneself (e.g., personal effort) while ascribing causal responsibility of bad SM performance episodes to external causes (e.g., bad luck)—that is, an optimistic style—is likely to be associated with positive cognitive and emotional reactions to IT-based SM systems which, in turn, are likely to lead to their effective use (conjecture #1). On the other hand, a pessimistic SM attributional style would be likely to reflect a lack of motivation to take responsibility for managing one’s own health and for adopting proactive behaviors. In the chronic disease SM context, this would imply the propensity to ascribe the causal role for bad SM performance episodes to one’s personal characteristics (e.g., lack of ability), which are stable and global, and to attribute causal responsibility for good SM performance episodes to external and unstable factors (e.g., environmental conditions). As such, the pessimistic style is likely to be associated with negative cognitive and emotional reactions to IT-based SM systems which, in turn, would not be likely to lead to their effective use (conjecture #2).

It is also important to note that, while a substantial body of work has examined the links between one’s attributional style and general behavioral reactions, very little is presently known about how one’s attributional style is related to one’s cognitive and emotional reactions to IT-based SM systems. The present study contributes to the extant literature on health IT by investigating how patients’ cognitive and emotional reactions to an IT-based SM system (i.e., the portal) tends to mediate the relationships between their SM attributional styles and behavioral reactions to the portal. Further, while this paper focuses on chronic patients’ SM attributional styles, it is possible that other individual factors (e.g., perceived severity of one’s chronic condition, experience with asthma condition, health literacy) may also help explain chronic patients’ reactions to IT-based SM systems (and their performance with it), or moderate the links between a patient’s SM attributional style and his/her individual cognitions, emotions, and behaviors (conjecture #3).

Following Burton-Jones and Volkoff’s (2017) recommendation, this paper also examined effective use (which can be viewed as a form of behavioral reaction) of an IT-based SM system in terms of the actions and interactions that constitute it. Specifically, it was examined in terms of the practices that patients undertook as they interacted with the portal to achieve system goals. Effective use (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013) was assumed to help patients attain better SM performance levels, while it may not necessarily guarantee it (conjecture #4). In other words, following Volkoff and Strong’s (2013) view that the attainment of any outcome is the product of one or more underlying mechanisms, the present paper assumes that effective use of IT-based SM systems offers one potential mechanism for achieving high levels of SM performance. It is important to note that, as the present paper’s aim is theory building and not theory testing, the above conjectures and the conceptual framework presented in

Figure 1 were used as a starting point in examining a data set that had been collected without these conjectures and conceptual framework in mind. Following Eisenhardt’s (1989) recommendation, Figure 1 and the abovementioned conjectures were expected to help make sense of the occurrences observed in the data set, ensure that important issues were not overlooked, provide an initial set of constructs to be investigated, and guide the present paper’s interpretation and focus.

## Methodology

## Research Setting and Participants

The present paper’s data was collected in an in-depth study of a group of asthma patients who were using a web-based SM portal<sup>2</sup> in the context of a clinical trial. The portal was developed by members of a Canadian university’s Clinical and Health Informatics research group who designed its core features with the goal of improving patients’ SM by enhancing their problem-solving and decision-making skills (i.e., their ability to identify problematic patterns and environmental triggers, which could help them think about solutions and actions that might reduce the potential negative effects of their illness), as well as by helping them improve their knowledge about asthma and ability to find and use resources and to form partnerships with healthcare providers. Patients action plans, which were initially created by the treating physicians, were updated by a case manager whenever appropriate (following physicians’ approval). It is important to note that action plans help chronically ill patients develop problem solving skills and a personal plan for learning a new behavior (Coleman and Newton 2005; Von Korff et al. 1997).

A registered nurse was recruited as a case manager for the clinical trial and she used the portal on a daily basis to monitor the health status of all participating patients and take appropriate actions when needed. A key portal functionality was asking patients about their symptoms, medication compliance, and physical activities. Each patient was expected to respond to this asthma questionnaire at least once a week. Based on the data he/she entered, the portal provided automatic and personal feedback to each patient via a graphical interface (labeled Asthma Diagram and Historical Graphs). These functionalities were designed with the objective of enabling patients to self-monitor their symptoms, medication adherence, and physical activities, as well as to help them better understand how such factors tend to be related. Alerts were sent to a patient via the portal or personal e-mails under certain conditions, including when (1) the system identified a patient’s asthma control as being suboptimal and the patient did not initiate activities outlined in his/her action plan; (2) patient’s physician updated his/her action plan; or (3) a patient did not log in to the portal for at least seven days. Patients participating in the clinical trial were given an opportunity to act on these alerts, and if they undertook no action for 48 hours, their alert status was escalated by notifying the registered nurse. Patients had access to general information about asthma via links to specific educational websites that were available on the portal’s information page.

Participants were recruited from pulmonary clinics of two Canadian tertiary care hospitals. To be eligible, participants had to be over 18, have a physician’s diagnosis of asthma, be prescribed at least one rescue medication, be categorized as having poor asthma control at the time of recruitment by their treating physician, have access to the Internet, and smoke less than 20 pack-years. Patients who had been diagnosed with other serious medical issues, such as lung cancer, or those who had severe mobility issues that prevented them from leaving home, were excluded from the clinical trial. After being recruited to participate in the trial, 47 patients used the portal for several weeks, with five of them dropping out before the end of the trial period. Among the 42 patients who stayed until the end of the clinical trial, 33 (79%) agreed to be interviewed face-to-face. However, as the data collected from one of the participants was also excluded from the study because she had severe pneumonia during the trial period, which prevented her from using the portal on a regular basis, the final study sample consisted of 32 patients and their profiles are provided in Table 2. During data collection, 16 participants had used the portal for 24 weeks while the other half (n = 16) had used it for 14 weeks. A potential study limitation may stem from the possibility that patients’ cognitive and emotional reactions to the portal might be affected by how long they used it. However, the collected data suggests that this is unlikely to have occurred or affected the key findings of the study as none of the 16 patients who used the portal for 24 weeks exhibited any significant variation in their cognitive and emotional reactions to the portal or changes in their usage levels after their first 14 weeks, suggesting that once these characteristics developed during the first few weeks, they tended to remain relatively stable.

## Data Collection

The study data was collected by interviewing each patient for approximately one hour with an interview protocol (Appendix A) and open-ended questions that asked them to describe their portal experience, perceptions of its capabilities and constraints, as well as their asthma SM behaviors (with and with out the portal). During the same period, a second protocol was used to interview the nurse who rated each patient’s SM performance (0 = low; 1 = average; 2 = high) based on how well she thought the patient had managed his/her symptoms and treatments, as well as the physical and psychosocial consequences of living with asthma. Also, data from a governmental database was used to examine the medications patients had been prescribed by their physicians, the medications that were dispensed, as well as each patient’s emergency visits, enabling an objective identification of in-control and out-ofcontrol patients.

In addition, data on each patient’s weekly usage of portal functionalities was obtained from computer logs, including how many times each patient (1) updated the asthma questionnaire, (2) consulted the Asthma Diagram and Historical Graph pages, (3) consulted the information (learning center) page, (4) sent or received an e-mail to/from the registered nurse, and (5) logged into the portal. This data was also used during face-to-face interviews in order to ask participants how and why they used the portal the way they did. The present study was approved by the research ethics committees of the university and of the two hospitals.

## Stage 1. Coding Qualitative Data

The interviews and e-mail exchanges were coded based on the key concepts included in the study’s conceptual framework presented in Figure 1. To do so, each interview was first transcribed verbatim and the transcripts (686 pages), as well as the patient-nurse e-mail exchanges (n = 301) were coded with NVivo software (version 11), by focusing on two main objectives. The first was to understand how patients had been selfmanaging their asthma and their perceptions of the underlying reason(s) of their good or bad SM performance. Hence, the data was coded by identifying how each patient perceived his/ her SM performance and its causes, the particular attributions of the causes he/she perceived along the four dimensions (locus, controllability, stability, and globality), and his/her SM behaviors. This was achieved by conventional content analysis (Hsieh and Shannon 2005), that is, by inductively deriving codes from the data during the analysis. Following Miles and Huberman’s (1994) recommendation, a table was developed (not included in the paper) listing each patient’s perceived cause(s) of success (or failure) in self-managing his/her asthma, along with his/her attributional dimensions.

The second objective was to understand how each patient reacted to, and interacted with, the portal during the trial period. Hence, his/her view of the portal (cognitive reactions), emotions or feelings toward the portal, and general emotions or feelings that were evoked in the patient as a consequence of his/her usage of the portal (emotional reactions), as well as the extent to which and how he/she interacted with the portal (behavioral reactions) were identified. Each patient’s cognitive and behavioral reactions were coded via conventional content analysis, and his/her emotional reactions were coded via direct content analysis (Hsieh and Shannon 2005), based on Weiner et al.’s (1978) and Martinko et al.’s (1996) lists. However, as other emotions (such as joy, assurance, security, and hope), which are not in these lists were also observed, they were also coded.

## Stage 2. Comparing Patients’ Causes for Good/Bad SM Episodes and Deriving SM Attributional Styles

Several tables (not included in the paper) were built in an iterative manner in order to compare patients in terms of the causes they identified for their good/bad SM performance and their corresponding attributional dimensions. As explained in the next section, three distinct SM attributional styles were identified with each patient being assigned to one style. Interestingly, patients with the same style also tended to have similar beliefs and attitudes toward their chronic disease and how they managed it.

## Stage 3. Examining Patients’ Discourses to Identify Their Levels of Effective Use

Following Burton-Jones and Volkoff’s (2017) definition of effective use, instances of effective usage were identified in patients’ explanations of their usage behavior. A patient’s usage was categorized as being highly effective if it corresponded to reflection-in-action behavior of using the portal to reach its goals. It was categorized as moderately effective if the patient partly followed system goals, but did not reflect a mindful behavior of using the system to self-manage his/her illness. Finally, it was categorized as ineffective if the patient’s usage behavior did not help him/her reach the SM system’s goals. Thus, each patient’s usage of the portal was categorized as being highly effective, moderately effective, or ineffective.

## Stage 4. Examining Relationships Between Patients’ SM Attributional Styles, Reactions to the Portal, and SM Performance

A key question examined in the present study is whether a patient’s SM attributional style tends to influence his/her cognitive and emotional reactions to the portal and, if so, how (conjectures #1 and #2). Hence, how patients cognitively and emotionally reacted to the portal was examined by comparing their reactions across each SM style. These variables were initially coded inductively by one researcher based on a personal interpretation of the interview data. As noted earlier, this initial coding led to the identification of three overarching SM styles and three distinct sets of reactions to the portal. For each patient, the principal investigator identified two segments that best represented (1) the patient’s SM attributional style and (2) how the patient cognitively and emotionally reacted to the portal. Once acquainted with the coding scheme, a second researcher coded the selected segments (n = 64) in order to assess inter-rater reliability. Coders’ percentage of agreement (81%) and Cohen’s kappa coefficient (78%) suggested a strong agreement and their few disagreements were resolved in a team meeting and with the help of a third researcher.

Multiple data sources (qualitative and quantitative) were then combined in order to examine how patients’ cognitive and emotional reactions were related to their portal usage (behavioral reactions), and ultimately to their SM performance (conjecture #4). First, patients’ SM attributional styles, cognitive, emotional, and behavioral reactions to the portal, and SM performance, were synthesized into individual case stories (an example is provided in Appendix B). Second, based on patients’ “average # of logins/week,” “average # of updates of the asthma questionnaire/week,” and “average # of visits of the Asthma Diagram and Historical Graph pages,” a series of two-step cluster analyses, a data analysis approach that can help identify classes of objects that have similar characteristics, was performed (with SPSS v24 software). It is important to note that updating the asthma questionnaire was considered to reflect a patient’s interest in communicating his/her data to healthcare providers, and visiting the Asthma Diagram and Historical Graph pages was considered to indicate his/her interest in receiving automatic feedback from the portal. In order to ensure the accuracy of these analyses, the possibility of a “curiosity effect” or “learning effect” was also checked for the first few weeks of the trial. A significant difference existed $( t = 5 . 6 2 ; \mathfrak { p } < . 0 0 1 )$ between the logins that all patients made during the first two weeks of the trial (average = 4.3 logins/week) and after (average = 1.6 logins/week), suggesting that most patients went through a learning period during the first couple of weeks of the trial, after which their portal usage patterns became more stable.

## Stage 5. Examining Potential Moderators and Selection Bias

In order to examine the possibility of having moderating factors of links between study concepts (conjecture #3), each patient was also characterized based on his/her age, technology experience, perception of portal’s quality, perceived asthma severity, and experience with asthma, followed by an assessment of the similarities and differences that existed between patients who followed the suggested association between concepts and those who did not. Also, to test for selection bias, demographic data and usage patterns of five patients who dropped from the clinical trial and nine patients who declined to be interviewed were compared to the data of the 32 participants. Demographic data and usage patterns were also examined in order to see if they were specific to any of the three SM styles, or if they reflected a SM style different from those three.

## Results

In this section, the three identified SM styles, as well as patients’ cognitive and emotional reactions to the portal, are first described. Then, patterns of associations observed between these constructs are explained. Next, how patients’ reactions to the portal were related to their effective use, and consequently to their SM performance, are explained. Finally, potential moderators are discussed.

## Patients’ SM Styles

In order to assess how patients attributed the causes of their SM performance, their discourses were analyzed to identify their perceived causes of their good and/or bad SM episodes, and each cause’s attributional dimensions of locus, controllability, and stability. The dimension of globality could not be identified because, when the interviews were conducted, attributional styles had not been part of the data collection process, which had focused on patients’ asthma SM performance and not on their general health condition.

The interview data indicated that patients attributed their success and/or failure in self-managing their asthma to a wide variety of causes, such as exercising, medications, environmental triggers, the portal, healthcare providers, knowledge and experience about asthma, meditation, positive thinking, severity of their asthma, and luck. Thus, a patient could attribute the cause of his/her success to his/her efforts (e.g., exercising, taking medications regularly, avoiding external conditions), or to having access to high quality resources (e.g., healthcare providers, effective medications, the portal), and attribute the cause of his/her failure to his/her lack of effort, external conditions (e.g., environmental triggers), severity of his/her asthma, insufficient resources, or bad luck. Causal attributions of each patient were synthetized in a table with that patient’s characteristics (e.g., his/her attitude toward the disease, beliefs about his/her role in managing it). Following LHT, the attributional dimensions of the above causes were compared across patients and patterns in the selection of causes were identified and, similar to Peterson and Seligman (1984), these patterns were labeled attributional styles. As the context of the present study was chronic disease SM and the attributed outcome was SM performance, these styles were labeled SM attributional styles, and hereafter will be referred to as SM styles. The three distinct SM styles that were identified were labeled engaged, autonomous, and reliant. As noted earlier, the causes of asthma SM success/failure that patients identified varied in terms of locus, controllability, and stability. The present paper defines SM style as an attributional style that reflects a chronic patient’s tendency to attribute the causes of good or bad SM episodes to internal (versus external), controllable (versus uncontrollable), and stable (versus unstable) factors (Table 5). This definition is consistent with the notion of attributional style in LHT (Peterson and Seligman 1984) according to which “styles are the tendency to choose certain kinds of explanations for bad versus good events” (p. 349). In the present study, a good outcome for patients was having their asthma under control, and a bad outcome was not having it under control. It is important to note that a patient could experience both good and bad SM episodes.

Nine patients attributed the causes of their successful SM episodes mainly to themselves and to their skills and experience (i.e., internal locus) and not to an external resource, such as the portal or healthcare providers. For example, P2 believed that she already knew a lot about asthma: “I don’t see the point in me learning about asthma … I have a pretty good understanding of my symptoms and when it could develop in an asthma crisis … I don’t consider myself sick, like if I have asthma I’m not a sick person per se.” Likewise, P13 who was a nurse stated: “I’m a nurse so I know about asthma, I know what I need to do, I know what triggers it, I know what helps it.” Also, some of these patients felt that taking medication was not the best way to manage asthma. For example, P30 stated, “Me, I have problems in taking medications, for me medication is not good for health.” Similarly, P3 thought that, to manage his asthma he had to use methods that do not depend much on healthcare services, such as positive thinking and meditation: “I want to better understand myself and the way I react to things that happen to me that will prevent having [asthma attacks].” Thus, for these patients, the locus of causality for successful SM episodes was internal and they felt that they were under their control (high controllability), even though they believed that such factors could sometimes be stable or unstable across time. For instance, they felt that their knowledge about asthma could increase, but that the effect of certain factors, such as meditation, would be stable over time (high or low stability). These patients were quite confident that they had the knowledge and skills needed for controlling their asthma independently of healthcare providers. They were not very concerned about their asthma and tended to perceive that they were generally healthy, believing that they were always in control of their illness. They also tended to perceive themselves as being successful in self-managing their asthma. In a few rare cases they mentioned their failures, attributing them mostly to external and uncontrollable conditions, such as environmental triggers and stress, which they also perceived to be unstable. In essence, these patients perceived the causes of their unsuccessful SM episodes to be external, unstable, and not under their control, and hence they were labeled autonomous patients.

Nine other patients tended to attribute their good and/or bad SM performances to their personal efforts (e.g., exercising regularly, analyzing their symptoms for better decision making, and avoiding environmental triggers) and, to a lesser extent, to the help of healthcare providers. As such, their locus of causality for success and failure was both internal and external. They believed that they had to be responsible about their disease, and as they also felt that they had minimal knowledge and skills for adequately managing their condition, they thought that they needed to mindfully use external resources, such as healthcare providers or tools like the portal, in order to better manage their condition. They were generally very concerned about their asthma, felt that they had an important role in managing their condition, and felt responsible when they could not manage it well. They viewed the health care providers as being there to help them and as partners in their SM. For example, P7 stated: “Having the impression that others should manage your asthma is wrong. This is not the physician who treats you. It is not him who cures you like a priest .… You are also responsible for your health, to take care of it.” In essence, this group of patients tended to perceive the causes of their success or failure to be under their control. However, similar to patients with the autonomous style, they also believed that the causes that influenced their good performance could be both stable (e.g., medications) and unstable (e.g., their level of effort), and the causes that influenced their bad performance were unstable and would not be permanent. Hence, they were labeled engaged patients.

The remaining 14 participants tended to attribute the cause of success in self-managing their asthma episodes to external factors (i.e., healthcare providers, medications) that controlled their situation. For example, P17 managed her asthma just by taking medications, and had no interest in exercising: “I don’t exercise because I’m not a person who likes exercising.” She was also reluctant to assess her asthma situation by analyzing her symptoms and was mostly relying on a nurse or doctor to assess her condition: “It’s hard to assess your own self, so you need somebody there all the time to ask questions … because you feel more confident when you ask somebody. It’s not like only you assessing your own self.” In cases of successful SM, this group of patients tended to attribute their success to factors which they perceived as being unstable. Some of them had a feeling of insecurity that they might not have access to their healthcare providers when needed or that the medication they take might not be effective in the future. For example, P6 mentioned: “But people like me like to be in contact with a doctor or a nurse just in case. I’m not thinking negatively and I’m not saying it’s going to happen, but there’s always a what-if in any situation where anybody is on medication has to be monitored .… What if I woke up at two o’clock in the morning and I couldn’t breathe?”

In cases of failure in self-managing their asthma (i.e., when their asthma was not under control), this group of patients tended to attribute its causes to external factors, such as bad luck, not getting enough medication, or not having immediate access to healthcare providers (i.e., external locus), which they perceived to be either stable (e.g., bad luck) or unstable (lack of access to healthcare providers). While they were very concerned about their condition, they did not see their role in managing their asthma as being very important because they thought that they did not have the necessary knowledge and skills for self-managing their illness. Hence, they tended to play a passive role, rarely exercised, and were dependent on healthcare providers for taking care of them. Moreover, they rarely perceived themselves to be in control of their asthma condition, and hence were labeled reliant patients.

## Patients’ Reactions to the Portal

Patients with different SM styles tended to react to the portal differently, assigning to it different, but specific roles, and had different emotional reactions to it. The emotional reactions of some were toward the portal (e.g., loving it or being angry at it), while in other cases they were toward themselves and in response to their portal usage (e.g., feeling calm and secure). As explained below, each patient’s view of the portal was categorized as imposer, facilitator, or protector.

## Reactions of Patients Who Saw the Portal as an Imposer

Some patients viewed the portal as an entity that acted against their desires and forced them to engage in activities they were not willing to undertake. For example, some noted that the portal treated them like a child, telling them what to do and what not to do: “It’s like taking a child by the hand, and saying ‘Look, you're not able to feel yourself. We'll show you’” (P15). Some felt that the portal criticized them severely, forcing them to manage their asthma in ways they did not find appropriate or credible: “graphs just bang me on the head that I’m not doing enough … using that reinforces medications and I don’t want to do that” (P3). Some thought that the portal encouraged them to take more medications, but they did not want to do it. Hence, patients in this group tended to have negative emotions toward using the portal, including anger and frustration, and they saw the portal as not being beneficial for them. For example, P13 who was a nurse believed that she already knew a lot about asthma and thought that the portal did not seem to be a good tool for her: “for managing my asthma this [the portal] wouldn’t be the right tool because I know pretty much how to deal with it.”

## Reactions of Patients Who Saw the Portal as a Facilitator

Some patients thought that the portal made self-managing their asthma easier. For example, P1 referred to the portal as something she could use to analyze her symptoms: “I could pretty much look at that information [graphs] at a glance and realize that oh, okay, this combination of factors makes my asthma worse or better.” Similarly, P8 saw it as a tool that could help her track her asthma management activities and their effects on her health: “It was interesting to be able to track and see when I compared medication use versus my physical activity and to be able to see the relationship on the chart.” Patients in this group saw the portal mainly as an additional resource they could use for better managing their asthma. For example, P1 stated: “I liked the portal a lot because it gave me a way to not only understand my asthma better, but it gave me several resources with which to manage it.” They also believed that they were responsible for managing their asthma, and that the portal was essentially like an assistant that facilitated their actions: “In the end, it’s [the portal] an assistant. It’s like a virtual assistant” (P11).

Some patients of this group described the portal as a tool that motivated them to do more, like a coach in a fitness center. For example, P7 stated “It is like all physical education things, there is a small coach on the side that tells you … a little more, a little more, you see. That’s it. It is the same principle as a gym instructor finally.“ Viewing the portal as a facilitator seemed to result in positive emotions toward it and patients tended to feel joy and hope when using it.

## Reactions of Patients Who Saw the Portal as a Protector

Some patients tended to view the portal as an entity that took care of them and provided them with the help and support they needed. For example, P5 referred to it as “a mother who took care of her sick child.” This group of patients felt that healthcare providers were continuously monitoring their health status via the portal and would notify them if there were any problems, which might even save their lives. In essence, they viewed the portal as an extension of healthcare providers that controlled their situation. For example, P28 stated: “I believed in it …. confidence and security knowing that the asthma portal site is there.” Similarly, P17 stated: “if you’re feeling sometimes stressed … it [the portal] would make you more confident doing everything in your life because oh, there’s somebody backing up with this, in case I have these kinds of symptoms, oh, it will improve me. So you may feel secure or safe, especially if you have some insecurities … I don’t want to bother my doctors all the time.

With the portal, I have more confidence because I have a plan B in my life.”

Patients in this group also tended to have very positive emotions toward the portal, and they liked, approved, and appreciated it. For example, they expressed their interest with comments such as “I found it very pleasant” (P9) or “Many thanks for linking me up to this computer program! I love it!” (P6). Overall, they felt calm and secure because of the portal. ## Patterns of Association Between Patients’ SM Styles and Their Reactions to the Portal

In order to identify how SM styles were linked to patients reactions to the portal, their SM styles and portal views were mapped along two axes. As shown in Figure 2, the vertical axis represents patients’ SM styles, and the horizontal axis represents their portal views. As visual displays provide a powerful approach for discovering connections between data segments in qualitative research (Paré 2004), patients’ SM styles and views of the portal were displayed on a graph in order to grasp the study data as a whole and to render the relationship between the study concepts more visible. Hence, each patient was located on the graph where his/her style and view of the portal intersected, helping reveal three main patterns of associations (clusters). In essence, all autonomous patients tended to see the portal as an imposer (cluster 1), most of the engaged patients tended to view it as a facilitator (cluster 2), and most of the reliant patients tended to see it as a protector (cluster 3). Hence,

Proposition 1: Chronic patients are likely to react differently, both cognitively and emotionally, to ITbased SM systems, depending on their causal attributions to their SM performance.

## Cluster 1

All nine autonomous SM style patients viewed the portal as an imposer. They attributed their positive SM performance to internal/controllable causes which can be either stable or unstable (i.e., their knowledge and ability), and attributed their negative performances to causes that are external/uncontrollable/unstable (i.e., environmental triggers and stress). While they did not attribute their failure in self-managing their asthma to the portal or its functionalities, they also perceived it as a barrier or hurdle in managing their asthma in a manner they desired. Moreover, they did not attribute their success to the portal as they generally felt independent of the tools and resources the healthcare system provided them. It is likely that the autonomous SM style and its characteristics, such as patients’ over confidence in their personal knowledge and expertise about asthma and its management, and their feeling that such systems interfere with their personal approach to managing their illness, influenced them in seeing the portal as an imposer. As they believed that they had enough knowledge for managing their asthma independently, their negative feelings, such as anger toward the portal, are likely to have stemmed from viewing it as an object that tried to control them and interfered with their independence. According to AT, anger is linked to a cause’s controllability (Weiner 1985). When a negative, self-related outcome/event occurs, attributional antecedents of anger are factors that others control (Weiner 1985), which can explain why autonomous patients saw the portal as intrusive, since it made them feel that they were not in control of their situation.

In fact, when asked about their perception of the portal’s quality, most of them assessed it as being good or acceptable. In essence, autonomous patients typically felt that the portal was a worthless software that prevented them from managing their asthma the way they wanted to do it. However, as they had agreed to participate in the trial, they felt obliged to use the portal for the specified period, but they also felt that they had little control over the situation (i.e., they could not modify or change the portal), and tended to feel negative emotions when using the portal, such as anger, frustration, and disappointment. ## Cluster 2

Eight patients who had an engaged SM style viewed the portal as a facilitator. As they tended to attribute their SM success or failure’s causes mainly to themselves and assumed responsibility, they tended to see themselves as the drivers of change. Hence, while engaged patients considered the portal to have contributed to their SM success, they did not assume that it could help them independently of their will and effort (i.e., they saw the portal as a cause that was controllable). Also, they never blamed or attributed their poor SM episodes to the portal. As such, from the perspective of engaged patients, an IT-based SM system is a tool that is a facilitator for their disease management and better control of their illness. Further, engaged patients considered the portal’s effect on their SM to be stable since they were not expecting any changes to the portal during the trial period. Hence, perceiving the portal as a facilitator can be linked to their SM style and how they attributed the cause of their SM performance to themselves and to the portal (Table 6). Since they viewed it as a trustworthy and useful resource that can help or assist them in their personal efforts to self-manage their chronic illness, engaged patients believed that its usage would positively influence their asthma control. ## Cluster 3

The 11 patients who had a reliant SM style tended to view the portal as a protector and an entity that provided them with needed support. ## Effective Use of the Portal

In order to identify patients’ levels of effective use of the portal, their discourses and usage logs were examined. Analysis of interview transcripts regarding how and why they used the portal suggested that patients used it differently and that their usage behavior could be categorized as being either highly effective, moderately effective, or ineffective. Highly effective usage of the portal can be defined as using it in a way that helps attain system goals (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013), which in the context of the present study is SM of asthma. As discussed earlier, the portal was designed with the goal of improving patients’ SM performance by enhancing their problem solving and decision making skills, improving their knowledge about asthma and their asthma status, which eventually would help them communicate better with their healthcare providers. Hence, a patient’s portal usage behavior was categorized as being highly effective when they used the main functions of the portal to reach system goals. Patient discourses indicated that not all of them used the portal for making better decisions about their asthma and learning about it. Some of them used it mainly to follow their physician’s advice, without assuming any active role in decision making and problem solving. This type of portal usage can be labeled as compliance behavior that occurs when a patient follows the regimen prescribed by healthcare providers. Hence, a patient’s compliance behavior which the portal facilitated was categorized as “moderately effective” usage, since it partially helped patients to reach SM goals. Finally, portal usage behaviors that did not help patients in self-managing their asthma were categorized as ineffective usage, as it did not help them attain system goals. Patients’ discourses were analyzed based on these characteristics, and as shown in Table 7, each patient’s portal usage was categorized as being highly effective, moderately effective, or ineffective.

As shown in Appendix E, patients who were in the three clusters of Figure 2 were then compared in terms of their level of effective usage of the portal (based on their discourses). Patients who perceived it as an Imposer (cluster 1) exhibited low levels of portal usage throughout the study, and kept using it mainly because they had agreed to participate in the trial. Their lack of regularity in using the portal seemed to be a reaction to their view of the portal as being intrusive and to their negative emotions toward it (e.g., frustration and anger). Patients in this group blamed the portal for preventing them from managing their asthma the way they wanted, that is, they felt the portal was antithetical to their values on how to selfmanage. For example, P3 who stopped using the portal during the last weeks of the trial stated: “I stopped using it … because I don’t want to reinforce that I want to, I’d rather do things, healthier things, than taking the medications.” Most patients who viewed the portal as an imposer also had no intention to continue using it after the trial period.

Patients who viewed the portal as a protector (cluster 3) used it quite frequently. However, their discourses suggested that their usage was either moderately effective or ineffective. Some of them used the portal mainly to update their health data and to provide a better picture of themselves to healthcare providers, but they were not interested to act on the feedback they were provided. For example, P5 who had a reliant SM style and a protector view of the portal stated: “I want the people who are looking after me to know as much about me as they need to know. I don’t want them to be guessing what I might be doing. I’ll tell them everything.” This group of patients showed very little interest in using the portal’s historical graphs to analyze the causes of their symptoms. Other patients in this group used the portal somewhat effectively as it ensured their compliance with medication and exercising. These patients rarely looked at the historical graphs to analyze their symptoms and to make decisions regarding their asthma management. They believed that such decisions should be made by physicians and a patient’s role or responsibility was to do what he/she was asked to do (i.e., to be a good patient). Since many of these patients viewed the portal as an extension of healthcare providers, what mattered most to them was to follow what the portal asked them to do in terms of exercising and medication usage. For example, P20 who was a reliant patient stated: “It’s like having a nurse as your neighbor next to your house and they can ask the questions…it is like having two doctors.” In essence, this group of patients’ communication exchanges with the nurse case manager was to seek assurance and help. Their ineffective or moderately effective portal usage can be explained by their view of it as a protector which, in turn, is explained by their SM style: they attributed their asthma control to external factors, like to healthcare providers or to the portal which they perceived as being an extension of healthcare providers. And since they did not see themselves as the main actors of their asthma SM, they tended to adopt a passive role (ineffective usage), or reactive role (compliance behavior). It is important to note that these patients used the portal very frequently, which can be associated with their intense positive emotions toward it, and feeling safe and secure because of it. Hence, they exhibited a certain level of dependency on the portal, and wondered how they were going to manage their asthma without the portal after the trial period. They were very eager to continue using it.

Engaged patients with a facilitator view of the portal (cluster 2) used it not only to comply with the regimen advised by healthcare providers (e.g., taking their medications on time, exercising), but to engage in reflection-in-action behaviors (Burton-Jones and Volkoff 2017). These actions reflect mindful behaviors of patients in using the portal, such as analyzing their symptoms to make informed decisions, acquiring knowledge about asthma in general or specifically on their asthma condition (e.g., by contacting the nurse case manager, or by consulting the Learning Center page), and getting aware about their asthma condition. As discussed earlier, such usage behaviors were labeled as being highly effective. For example, when describing the portal, P27 stated: “It also helps you to realize what might be triggering your asthma that you’re not really aware of, but by answering the questions, they can see, oh, you know, this is where it starts or you should do this before it gets to that point.” Similarly, P11 explained how the portal made her aware about her asthma and described such an effect as being long lasting, even without the portal: “It just makes me more aware that I have to continue to take my medication, to exercise. I have to not only when I’m doing the portal study that I should be concerned about my asthma, I have to be concerned about it all the time.” Patients’ level of effective usage of the portal and their regularity in using it can be associated with their view of the portal as a facilitator, and their positive emotions when using it. Perceiving the portal as a facilitator, they clearly believed in their role in self-managing their asthma, attributed their good/bad SM outcomes mainly to their efforts, and saw the portal as a valuable resource that facilitated their actions. Thus, the active roles they played in their asthma SM and the portal’s enabling characteristics seem to have led to their relatively highly effective use of the portal.

In order to complement the analysis of patients’ discourses, their usage logs were also examined to see if different usage patterns existed among them via a series of two-step cluster analyses that tried to identify clusters in three variables: (1) average number of logins per week, (2) average number of asthma questionnaire updates per week, and (3) average number of visits to the Asthma Diagram and Historical Graphs pages per week. The results indicated three optimal numbers of clusters for each variable which were labeled as high, moderate, and low (cluster analysis details are provided in Appendix D). The cluster analysis helped identify a usage pattern for each patient (provided in Appendix E). For example, P1’s number of logins/week, updates/week, and feedback page visits/week were all moderate (i.e., a moderate, moderate, moderate pattern). Comparing the portal usage patterns of all patients suggested that each patient’s usage pattern corresponded to one of the five following patterns (based on their # of logins/week, # of updates/week, and # of feedback visits/week: {Low, Low, Low}, {Moderate, Moderate, Moderate}, {High, High, Moderate}, {High or Moderate, High or Moderate, High}, and {High or Moderate, High or Moderate, Low}. As shown in Figure 3, each pattern was associated with highly effective, moderately effective, or ineffective usage. As discussed earlier, patients who used the portal highly effectively had a tendency to use the feedback provided to them more frequently that those who used it moderately effectively or ineffectively. Hence, the number of feedback page visits was treated as a proxy for identifying a patient’s level of effective usage. That is, a patient’s usage level was categorized as being highly effective if he/she visited the feedbacks provided to him/her either the same number of times, or more than the number of times he/she updated his/her information, as long as his/her number of logins was moderate or high. Thus, {High/Moderate, High/ Moderate, High} (B1) and {Moderate, Moderate, Moderate} (B2) patterns were categorized as highly effective because a patient who had this pattern visited the feedback provided to him/her at least as frequently as updating his/her information. On the other hand, {High, High, Moderate} (C1), and {High/Moderate, High/Moderate, Low} (C2) patterns were categorized as being moderately effective usage since the number of feedback visits were less than the number of times their information was updated. Finally, the {Low, Low, Low} (A) pattern was categorized as being ineffective usage as all portal functions were used at very low levels.

As can be seen in Appendix E, eight of the nine patients in the Autonomous/Imposer cluster used the portal mainly in Pattern A (i.e., compared to the other styles, they tended to use the portal at very low levels). Autonomous/imposer patients were less willing to log in and update their asthma questionnaire (compared to patients of the other two clusters), which is understandable since they all had negative emotions toward the portal and felt that it was rather intrusive and irritating. This result is very consistent with their discourses.

On the other hand, portal usage data of six of the eight patients in the engaged/facilitator cluster mainly followed Pattern B. That is, patients who viewed the portal as a facilitator used it (logged into it and updated their health data) moderately to highly frequently, and checked the feedback they were provided more frequently than patients of the other clusters. They attributed their success and/or failure in selfmanaging their asthma to themselves and assumed responsibility for their health, and hence consulted the portal’s automatic feedback functionality more often than patients with the other two styles, in order to be able to make informed decisions. This observation is also consistent with patients discourses in which they stated that they used the portal in order to act mindfully regarding their disease and make better decisions for self-managing their chronic condition.

Given the characteristics of each style, this observation is plausible since patients who viewed the portal as a protector tended to seek support and help from it, and liked to provide a clear picture of themselves to healthcare providers. As noted above, the main concern of patients who viewed the portal as a protector was to provide complete information to healthcare providers because they viewed them as being the main cause of successful asthma control. They were not active participants in their disease management process, and hence were generally more reluctant than other patients to check their feedback, and analyze it, leaving this responsibility essentially to the healthcare team. Proposition 2a: A patient who views an IT-based SM system as an imposer will tend to have negative emotions toward the system, and not use it frequently and effectively.

Proposition 2c: A patient who views an IT-based SM system as a protector will tend to have positive emotions toward the system, and use it frequently, but ineffectively.

## SM Performance

As noted earlier, the nurse case manager evaluated each patient’s SM performance at the end of the trial by assessing his/her asthma SM performance as low, medium, or high: “I assessed patients’ self-management performance asking myself if they are able to accomplish their regular activities without difficulties … if they do have more symptoms, can they recognize it and what actions they take to rectify it … well to me an indicator that a patient was able to self-manage was a patient that had modified their medications before the portal prompted them to do it.” In essence, she observed whether or not patients were able to recognize their symptoms and react to them before the portal told them to do so. Patients’ SM styles identified in the present study and the nurse’s assessment of their SM performance are summarized in Appendix E.

In order to verify and strengthen the nurse’s assessments of patients’ SM performance levels, two objective measures were also examined: patients’ ER visits and their dosage of fast acting bronchodilators (FABA) medications. According to Ahmed et al. (2011), a patient’s asthma control can be assessed by examining his/her overuse of FABA in the last six months, combined with the number of his/her emergency visits for respiratory problems. A patient’s asthma was considered to be out of control if (1) he/she had one or more emergency room visits for respiratory problems during the three-to-nine month period after starting to use the portal or (2) the total FABA dispensed to the patient exceeded 500 doses in the six months following his/her usage of the portal. As can be seen in Appendix E, in most cases, the nurse’s assessment was consistent with data found in patients medical records.

## Potential Moderators

While the present study’s observations suggest that patients perceptions of the portal either as an imposer, a facilitator, or a protector tended to stem from their differing SM styles, it is possible that other factors may have also influenced how they perceived the role of the portal in self-managing their disease (conjecture #3). As can be seen in Figure 2, four patients did not fit into any of the three clusters. P15 (an engaged patient) and P19 (a reliant patient) perceived the portal’s quality as being low and not very professional, hence they did not trust it and saw it as an imposer. In addition, P1 and P8 who were reliant patients, viewed the portal as a facilitator. Looking at their profiles (Appendix E), it can be seen that their asthma condition was severe and can help explain their very high level of dependence on healthcare providers (reliant style), even though they clearly knew that their role in managing their asthma was very important, which in turn made them view the portal as a facilitator. Hence, trust in the system and its perceived quality can be viewed as factors that can moderate the relationship between SM styles and patients reactions to the portal.

Moreover, computer literacy, accessibility on mobile phones, and patients’ life routines are also likely to moderate the relationship between patients’ portal views and usage. For example, P27 and P29, who were both engaged and had the facilitator view of the portal, used it ineffectively because, in the case of P29, the portal was inaccessible from her mobile phone and, considering her busy schedule, it made it difficult for her to use it. As for P27, she was not computer literate and hence making sense of the graphs seemed very difficult for her. Moreover, P17 and P20 who were reliant patients with a protector view of the portal did not use it often because using the portal interfered with their busy life schedules and, for P17 who was not computer literate, the portal seemed too difficult to use. Hence, given the complexity of the context of SM of chronic diseases, various factors are likely to act as moderators of the relationships depicted in Figure 1 and need to be examined in future research.

## Discussion

The present study makes three key contributions. A first contribution is its conceptualization of three SM styles in the context of chronic disease management and its observations regarding their usefulness in better understanding such patients’ cognitive and emotional reactions to IT that are designed to support the SM of their illnesses. As noted earlier, past research in social psychology has conceptualized and studied two main attributional styles (i.e., pessimistic and optimistic) (Abramson et al. 1978). Conjectures #1 and #2 suggested that it might be possible to link these styles to patients’ cognitive and emotional reactions to IT-based SM systems, but the present study’s observations suggested that the autonomous, reliant, and engaged styles tended to better capture patients’ cognitive and emotional reactions to the portal than the optimistic and pessimistic styles.

The three SM styles proposed here have dimensional differences with optimistic/pessimistic styles. Although the locus of causality is an important factor in defining optimism and pessimism, it is not evident how an optimist patient might attribute causal roles to healthcare providers, or to IT-based SM systems. An optimist patient might feel independent of IT-based SM systems (similar to the autonomous style), or might think that such systems are useful tools that can help them in self-managing their disease (similar to the engaged style). Moreover, being an optimist does not help explain a patient’s dependence on IT based-SM systems (similar to reliant style). A similar argument can also be made for the pessimistic style’s inadequacy in fully explaining chronically ill patients’ perceptions and behaviors. For example, an engaged patient tends to partly assume responsibility for his/her SM failure (attributing negative outcomes to himself/ herself), which is not a characteristic that indicates pessimism.

Contrary to the optimistic/pessimistic styles in which the controllability dimension does not play a major role, in the context of chronic disease SM, controllability tends to be a key dimension, as it reflects a patient’s beliefs about his/her self-efficacy and how much control he/she has in selfmanaging his/her health condition. Self-efficacy reflects one’s confidence and beliefs about his/her capacity to undertake specific behaviors which can lead to desired outcomes, including improved health (Bandura 1977). The usefulness of increasing patients’ self-efficacy in order to enhance their health behaviors is well established in the extant literature, where greater self-efficacy has been linked to various disease management behaviors, such as using medications as prescribed, doing adequate exercises, managing stress, and following recommended diets (Bandura 1997; Clark and Dodge 1999). Past research on asthma patients has also found that enhancing self-efficacy via SM programs can improve their health-related quality of life (Barton et al. 2005; Mancuso et al. 2001).

The stability of a cause is also an important dimension in the context of chronic disease SM because attributing one’s success and failure to stable or unstable causes is thought to influence one’s expectations about future outcomes (Kovenklioglu and Greenhaus 1978). Similar to social psychology research, where the instability of a good cause has been observed to play an important role in explaining depression (Peterson and Steen 2002), or lack of classroom motivation (Weiner 1979), stability can help explain asthma patients general feeling of being insecure, which eventually can influence their views of an IT-based SM system and their emotional reactions to it. The perceived instability of access to healthcare providers can influence patients’ view of an ITbased SM system, which they essentially view as an extension of healthcare providers and a tool that replaces them. Hence, viewing IT-based SM systems as being stable and always accessible can evoke in patients feelings of being secure. As such, compared to optimistic/pessimistic styles, the three SM styles can be more useful for explaining how chronically ill patients tend to cognitively and emotionally react to IT-based SM systems.

A second contribution of the present study stems from its explanations of the effectiveness with which patients tend to use IT-based SM systems. While information systems such as the portal have been developed in order to encourage and help patients become more engaged in the management of their chronic condition, some patients appear to benefit from such systems more than others and use them more effectively than others. In general, SM interventions tend to ask patients to follow a certain structure in their disease SM, such as requiring them to report their symptoms, take their medications regularly, and exercise. A similar disciplinary and surveillance structure was also present in the portal. Engaged patients were observed to be receptive to such a structure. This observation is consistent with extant research according to which chronic patients can mostly benefit from SM programs when they feel responsible for their own care and want to play an active role in the management of their illness (e.g., DelliFraine and Dansky 2008; Madsen et al. 2008). In contrast to engaged patients, reliant patients did not see themselves as active partners in the healthcare process. However, some of them significantly benefited from the portal by being motivated to engage in compliance behaviors. That is, the surveillance structure of the portal helped encourage their adherence to the prescribed regimen. Adherence to medication and physical activities has been a challenge for the healthcare system and a lack of this type of adherence tends to have significant negative consequences on both patients and the health system, such as loss of treatment effectiveness and increases in healthcare costs (Ingersoll and Cohen 2008). The present study contributes to past research on this issue by observing that IT-based SM systems can be beneficial both for engaged and reliant patients by helping them adhere to their regimen. However, reliant patients appear to benefit from such a structure a little less because, contrary to engaged patients, they do not engage in mindful adherence, which can have a very short lived effect. Moreover, the present study also observed that IT-based SM systems may not be a good fit for autonomous patients. Autonomous patients felt that the portal and its accompanying disciplinary and surveillance structure was very intrusive, which tended to make them see themselves as non-active partners in their disease management process. As such, they tended to perceive the portal as a barrier to managing their asthma the way they wanted. In sum, a clear understanding of the fit between a patient’s SM style and the IT-based SM system he/she uses is likely to be useful for a successful uptake and use of such systems.

The present study’s third contribution is to the health IT literature by providing evidence that high effective usage of IT-based SM systems can be linked to patients’ better SM performance levels. Although these systems are expected to positively impact patients’ SM performance, extant research concludes that there is presently insufficient convincing evidence regarding these systems’ positive influence on patients’ SM (Peeters et al. 2013). The present study’s findings suggest that high effective usage of SM systems can be useful to chronic patients by engaging them in a mindful SM of their condition. High effective use of an IT-based SM system can increase a patient’s awareness of his/her condition and help him/her pay more attention to it. It also is likely to trigger a thinking process which can help a patient associate his/her symptoms with his/her SM behaviors, which eventually can help him/her to learn how to manage his/her condition more effectively. As such, IT-based SM systems can go beyond being just a traditional knowledge-based patient education system, by also including processes that develop reflection-inaction behaviors, which supports the application of knowledge in real-life situations that matter to patients. High effective usage of such systems are likely to have a long lasting impact on patients and, after using them for a while, patients will tend to be independent and continue their effective SM even without using the technology, a possibility that future research can examine. Moreover, moderate effective use can also impact patients’ SM performance since, as described earlier, it can help improve their adherence to a prescribed regimen. However, such an effect is likely to be temporary and without technology it might not last for long, which also needs to be investigated in future research. The present study also observed that an SM system, such as the portal, can help some chronically ill patients in terms of reducing their stress and anxiety by providing them the assurance and security that they are not alone in the care process.

## Study Limitations

The fact that 9 of the 47 contacted patients declined to be interviewed, and 5 of the 38 patients who accepted the invitation to participate dropped out of the trial before finishing the use of the portal is a potential limitation of the present study as it might have caused selection bias. While it was not possible to investigate the characteristics of non-participants, their demographic information and frequency of usage of the portal’s functionalities were available, and were compared to those of the 32 patients who participated in the study. As can be seen in Table 9, the usage frequencies and patterns of the nine patients who declined the final interview mostly followed pattern A. As such, their frequency of accessing the portal and usage of its key functionalities appear to be similar to those of the autonomous/imposer participants. Hence, it is possible that those who declined the interview might have been autonomous patients who saw the portal as an imposer. Moreover, there is also the possibility that other SM styles exist which, due to the limited sample of this study, could not be identified. As the present study was exploratory in nature and did not try to confirm theory, it would be useful to test its suggested propositions with a larger and more representative sample of chronic patients in future research.

Another potential limitation of the present study is that the globality dimension, which was initially used in LHT (Abramson et al. 1978), could not be assessed. As the present study’s focus was on asthma SM, it was not possible to specify whether the causes that patients identified were global or specific for them. As such, it can be useful in future research to investigate how the globality of a cause can influence patients’ attitudes regarding their SM of their chronic illness and views of IT-based SM systems. Moreover, although patients attributed their good or bad SM outcomes to stable and unstable causes, they viewed the portal as a stable cause during the trial period. They were unlikely to have expected significant changes to be made to the portal during the trial period, and its usage during a relatively short term may have also influenced the stability that they perceived. As the study patients knew that their usage of the portal would be temporary, it is unlikely that they took into account its effect on their future SM episodes. This limitation stems from the fact that the present study’s data was based on a trial usage of the portal and it is possible that collecting data from patients who use an IT-based SM system during a long period in an actual setting where IT-based systems can be expected to go through changes may be useful in future studies for better assessing the potential importance of the Stability dimension on patients’ cognitive, emotional, and behavioral reactions. Another limitation of the present study is that patients’ SM performance measures prior to the trial period were not accessible to the research team, and therefore, it was not possible to assess whether the portal improved their ability to self-manage their asthma.

## Study Implications and Future Research

The present study developed a context-specific theory which can be useful both for research and practice ( Burton-Jones and Volkoff 2017; Ramiller and Pentland 2009). The three attributional styles proposed in this paper capture tendencies of chronically ill patients to perceive IT-based SM tools in certain ways. As SM styles are habitual, patients with a specific style are likely to behave similarly in similar contexts. As such, being able to assess a patient’s SM style can also be useful in selecting the right patients to use an IT-based SM system, while accepting that some patients might benefit from it more than others. As the findings of the present study suggest, no tool fits everyone. Hence, it would be useful to examine whether IT-based SM interventions are suitable for all chronic patients. As some asthma patients appeared to benefit from the portal and the SM approach more than others, the present study’s results suggest that such interventions may not be suitable for all patients. Specifically, its findings suggest that high effective use and SM performance with the portal essentially happened among Engaged patients who wanted to play an active role in the management of their disease and were interested in using the portal to do so. Hence, when determining eligibility criteria for IT-based SM systems, clinicians might benefit from being aware of patients’ SM styles and their dimensions. For example, reliant patients are more likely to experience better adherence and emotional benefits than a better SM performance per se. While the present study’s results do not allow a definitive conclusion regarding how beneficial IT-based SM systems are for reliant patients, there is a possibility that they might become dependent on such tools, and it would be interesting to examine in future studies the potentially positive and negative consequences of such a dependence.

It is important to note that a patient’s SM style can also be changed by influencing his/her perceptions. Although some researchers view attribution styles as a personality characteristic (Abramson and Martin 1981), it is more likely that an individual’s tendency toward certain attributions becomes “habituated” over a period (Peterson and Seligman 1984). As such, an “attribution retraining” technique (Weiner and Sierad 1975) can help chronic patients understand their attributional styles which eventually may help them improve their SM performance. Attribution retraining is essentially based on the idea that, when individuals make maladaptive attributions to performance, it makes them disposed to failure. However, as maladaptive patterns can also be modified, educational programs that help patients understand their attributional styles and interventions that induce internal and controllable causes for a patient’s SM failure when using an IT-based SM system (rather than inducing uncontrollable causes), are likely to help patients feel more responsible over their situation. This can also help them become active partners in their care process, which can eventually lead to more effective usage of IT-based SM systems. Such interventions can be performed before using an IT-based SM system, in order to help a patient use it more effectively, provided that the patient’s attribution style has been identified. Moreover, IT-based SM systems can be designed in a way to include online educational programs (similar to the Learning Center feature included in the present study’s portal), in order to first identify patients’ SM styles, and then help them to modify their maladaptive patterns.

The present study’s findings suggest that conditions that can help successful SM interventions are likely to be related to patients’ characteristics and the IT-based systems that are used. In order to encourage more effective usage of SM systems, it is likely to be useful to design these tools with soft disciplinary and surveillance characteristics so that they can be perceived to be less intrusive from the patients’ perspective. Moreover, other factors, such as a patient’s computer literacy, his/her life routines, or portal accessibility through mobile phones, can also help explain the way they used the portal. Hence, such factors also need to be considered when designing and putting into practice IT-based SM systems.

While the three attributional styles and their consequent views of IT-based SM systems were identified in the particular context of asthma SM, they are also likely to apply to other chronic diseases. As such, it would be interesting to study them with patients who have more complex chronic conditions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and chronic heart failure. Future research could also develop and test an instrument for measuring SM attributional styles, along similar lines to the Attributional Style Questionnaire (ASQ) (Peterson et al. 1982), in order to enable a more detailed examination of the relationship between SM styles, the use of IT-based systems that might be developed for different chronic conditions, and their effects. Moreover, patients’ SM styles can be identified before they start using a system so that proper system configurations or training sessions can be applied in order to increase the effective use of these systems, with the ultimate objective of improving SM performance. Last, the present study suggests a plausible explanation of how chronic patients’ SM styles can be linked to their cognitive, emotional and behavioral reactions to IT-based SM systems, as well as to their SM performance. While the present study developed explanations of patients’ cognitive and behavioral reactions, it briefly discussed their emotional reactions. Hence, it would be important in future research to study patients’ emotional reactions to IT-based SM systems for examining questions such as, how their emotions are linked to their SM performance, or how their emotions toward their chronic disease can influence their emotions toward IT.

## Conclusion

Chronic diseases are common and expected to increase in the future and in many countries. It is also generally accepted that successful management of a chronic disease depends, among other factors, on the active involvement of the patient (Holman and Lorig 2000; Michie et al. 2003). As such, there is a pressing need for more SM interventions that place greater emphasis on collaborative relationships with patients and on supporting patients’ efforts toward becoming active and engaged in managing their illness and its consequences (Novak et al. 2013). While chronic patients can significantly benefit from IT-based SM systems for self-managing their condition, all of them are unlikely to similarly perceive, use and benefit from these systems or to the same extent. Hence, it can be useful to better understand which patients can most benefit from such IT-based systems and why. The main objective of the present paper was to shed some light on this important topic by proposing a new conceptualization that (1) suggests three distinct SM styles (i.e., autonomous, engaged, and reliant), which reflect how chronic patients tend to attribute causes to their good and bad SM episodes; (2) suggests three distinct views of IT-based SM systems (i.e., facilitator, imposer, and protector), which reflect how chronic patients tend to portray or characterize an SM technology, and emotionally react to it, and (3) proposes a contingency explanation of how patients’ views of technology and their emotional reactions toward an IT-based SM system can be associated with their effective usage of, and actual SM performance with, the system. It is hoped that the proposed conceptualizations can be useful for other researchers, as well as for practitioners in their efforts to continue expanding our understanding of patient-centric IT systems, how they support chronic disease SM, and under what circumstances they can best accomplish what they are designed to so.

## Cluster Analysis

The mean values of the three clusters observed in “number of logins” are 0.98 logins/week (low cluster), 1.74 logins/week (moderate cluster), and 3.00 logins/week (high cluster). The mean values of the three clusters observed in “number of updates” are 0.56 updates/week (low cluster), 1.26 updates/week (moderate cluster), and 2.75 updates/week (high cluster). The mean values of the three clusters observed in “number of visits” are 0.44 visit/week (low cluster), 1.47 visit/week (moderate cluster), and 2.95 visits/week (high cluster). The silhouette values for the study analyses are the following: 0.8 (number of logins), 0.7 (number of updates), and 0.7 (number of visits), indicating the validity of the cluster solutions (Norusis 2008). Moreover, the result of the following ANOVA analysis also confirms that the clusters were optimally separated: average logs/week: F = 110.5, p <.001; average updates/week: F = 112.8, p < .001; average visits/week: F = 106.0, p < .001.
