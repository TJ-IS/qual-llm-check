## 1. Introduction

The practice of sourcing business services from offshore providers to benefit from wage arbitrage—often referred to as business process outsourcing (BPO)— continues to grow. A recent report by the research firm Gartner (2014) forecasted that global BPO would grow at 5.6% (compound annual growth rate) from 2012 through 2017, with a growth of 6.2% in 2014. However, researchers in the business press have noted that offshore BPO may not deliver the gains of wage arbitrage if the risk of suboptimal quality of output cannot be managed (Bloch and Jans 2005). Aron and Singh (2005) identified several kinds of risks associated with offshore BPO, all of which result in suboptimal quality of output.

Given the size and wide prevalence of this phenomenon, offshore BPO has attracted a lot of media attention, but relatively few academic studies have specifically looked at it. Researchers in the information science (IS) field have only recently started to investigate the factors that have an impact on the performance of BPO contracts. Indeed, the paucity of research on BPOs is recognized by Rouse and Corbitt (2006, p. 586), who noted that decision makers looking to outsource processes to BPO service providers must make “decisions based on faith” because there is so little independent empirical research into the phenomenon. In this study, we develop a theoretical model of effects that explains the impact of various factors on the quality of output of business processes executed offshore.

To be specific, we empirically test—based on primary data collected from offshore BPO service providers and their clients—the impact of two different sets of control mechanisms on output quality at the process level. The first set of control mechanisms has to do with “behavior control” of offshore agents by both the client and the provider, including monitoring of work and exerting direct managerial control over offshore workers. We term this dual managerial control exerted on the offshore agents within the bilateral BPO contract the dual governance mechanism. The second set of control mechanisms has to do with outcome controls implemented through incentives— a combination of penalties and rewards—for quality of output built into the contract at the process level.

Another key construct that we address in this study is process codifiability. We investigate how process codifiability affects output quality through its direct impact on knowledge transfer between firms and its indirect impact as a moderator of the effectiveness of the dual governance mechanism. We summarize below the principal findings of our study.

First, we find that for business processes outsourced offshore, contractual incentives have a positive impact on output quality. Second, process codifiability has a direct impact on the quality of output—the higher the codifiability, the higher the output quality. Third, the dual governance mechanism does have a positive impact on output quality. Fourth, the nature and extent of benefit from the dual governance mechanism is driven by the moderating effect of process codifiability. We find that where output quality is considered, the client and provider firms’ managerial efforts are complementary for processes of low codifiability and substitutes for processes of high codifiability. Fifth, we find that the use of process specific interorganizational information systems to monitor and track the work of offshore agents has a strong and positive impact on the quality of output. We discuss below the contributions of this study.

The contributions of this study are fourfold. First, we add a dimension to the theory of bilateral interfirm alliances by investigating how a dual governance mechanism—in which the managers of clients and providers both exert direct control over the offshore agents’ behavior—impact on output quality. While extant research in information systems and organizational theory has focused on modularity (Tiwana 2008), creating a common ground (Srikanth and Puranam 2011), and contract structures (Bajari and Tadelis 2001, Susarla et al. 2010), no study that we know of has investigated the phenomenon of the dual governance mechanism. We develop theoretical frameworks to explain the moderating effect of process codifiability on the dual governance mechanism and identify the conditions under which the efforts of the client’s and provider’s managers complement or substitute each other.

Second, we deconstruct the working of the dual governance mechanism and show that under conditions of greater task uncertainty (low process codifiability), the client enjoys an advantage over the provider. The client’s managerial efforts at behavior control have significantly higher impact on the quality of output as compared to the provider’s. We show that in order to build a more complete theoretical model that explains the functioning of interfirm alliances in offshore BPO, we must combine the contract structure with the dual governance mechanism and the codifiability of services executed offshore.

Third, we theorize on the basis of the directly observed values of key constructs. This is the only study that, to the best of our knowledge, that is based on the directly observed quality of output and captures the varying mix of (actual) efforts made by the client and provider in each period. Other studies either use self-reported proxies for output quality or assume the beneficial impacts of bilateral offshore alliances.<sup>1</sup> We develop a theoretical model of effects to explain the functioning of the dual governance mechanism and its impact on the actual quality of output— thus the central results of our model are not exposed to the threats of construct validity.

Fourth, we extend the body of theories in extant IS research around the use of interorganizational information systems in bilateral interfirm alliances. We show that the use of interorganizational information systems at the process level to monitor the work of the provider’s agents and to exchange information between firms has a strong, positive impact on output quality. This suggests that interorganizational information systems extend the scope of bilateral governance in offshore BPO.

## 2. Theory and Hypotheses

Offshore BPO as a domain of research meets the criteria laid out by Agarwal and Lucas (2005), wherein they observe that the key to successful future research in the IS field lies in researchers undertaking research into business domains wherein information technology (IT) has a transformational impact. IT certainly plays a transformational role in offshore BPO by both enabling offshore BPO and in expanding its scope. Developing theories to better understand the factors that influence the functioning of offshore BPO is thus central to expanding the theoretical horizons of the field of information systems. In the sections that follow, we draw on multiple research domains including IS, management, strategy, organizational theory (OT), operations management (OM), and economics to develop theoretical frameworks that explain how the key constructs in this study impact on the output quality of offshore service providers.

## 2.1. Process Codifiability

Researchers in the field of OT and strategy have approached the problem of coordination between the client and vendor by viewing it through the theoretical prism of modularity strategy. Srikanth and Puranam (2011) observe that the essence of the modularization strategy is to create interfaces between the client and vendor so as to economize on the need for communication between the two parties. Tiwana (2008, p. 771) also observes that modularity is achieved by “codifying the interface specifications for how a subsystem connects with a larger system.” Other researchers in strategy have argued that the objective of modularity strategy is to decompose a system of activities into modules (or subsystems) such that there are clearly specified interfaces between modules and little or no dependencies between activities in different modules (Baldwin and Clark 2000, Langlois 2002, Ulrich and Eppinger 1999). Although there are similarities between process codifiability and modularity, the differences between the two constructs are significant as recent research in IS has shown.

The role of process codifiability in offshoring business processes has of late attracted the attention of researchers in IS as well. Mithas and Whitaker (2007)

observe that process codifiability (and work standardizability) makes it easier to decouple processes from one location and produce them in another location. Mani et al. (2012) describe the concept of process codifiability as process analyzability and observe that process analyzability (codifiability) has a significant impact on the ease and accuracy of process execution in BPO. Thus, process codifiability is not about interfaces between the client and vendor—rather, it is about creating a set of task execution rules for how a process is to be executed (offshore) by a provider. Mithas and Whitaker (2007, p. 240) define process codifiability as “the extent to which the activities in an occupation can be described completely in a set of written instructions.” We use an almost identical definition for process codifiability and define it as “the extent to which the activities that constitute a business process can be described completely in a set of written instructions.” Mani et al. (2012) observe that the transfer of critical process-level information results in an improved understanding of the process execution rules (process codifiability) by the provider, which in turn could lead to better contract outcomes. This leads us to our first hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> The higher the codifiability of the process, the greater the output quality.

## 2.2. The Dual Governance Mechanism

The dual governance mechanism that we study works through the client and the provider jointly exerting managerial control on the functioning of offshore employees (agents) of the provider. In the field of OT, the two well-studied approaches to the problem of coordination in bilateral alliances are modularization (discussed above) and ongoing communication. Ongoing communication involves facilitating electronic and other forms of communication between remotely located entities such as vendors and clients (Langlois 2002, Puranam et al. 2009, Srikanth 2007, Ulrich and Eppinger 1999). In contrast, the phenomenon of a client’s managers exercising direct managerial control involves much more than just ongoing communication. Several researchers (Kaiser and Hawk 2004; Knowledge@Wharton 2002, 2005; Seshasai et al. 2010) have observed that exercising direct managerial control goes far beyond ongoing communication. It involves the client’s managers directing the offshore agents to execute specific tasks in specific ways, monitoring their actions in real-time, and exercising managerial oversight over the offshore agents that execute business processes.

If modularization and ongoing communication are the key to achieving greater alliance success, then an obvious question that arises is: Why do clients even resort to establishing mechanisms of direct managerial control when they could simply rely on a combination of communication and contractual incentive clauses to induce the desired level of quality? The answer to this question lies in the source of paradigmatic divergence of theory between neo-institutional economics (NE) and the information processing view (IPV) of the firm. The NE literature has looked at the problem of agency through the prism of moral hazard and the threat of opportunistic behavior by the provider. In this view, the client would use outcome controls (incentive provisions) in the contract to induce optimal effort from the provider (even though a first best solution will not be possible, as per Baker et al. 2002). This theoretical perspective does not, however, explain the client’s efforts at exerting realtime managerial control over the behavior of the offshore agents of the provider. We offer two reasons for this observation: (i) all 139 processes in our study have been contracted on the basis of a single, unambiguously defined, measured, and recorded attribute of quality—the error rate and (ii) the actual error rate for each process is fully verified by the client and vendor—in fact, it is recorded in each period in the vendor’s payment log and the client’s and constitutes the basis of payment to the provider by the client. Furthermore, all of the contracts in our data set are fixed price (fee) contracts, which as Mani et al. (2012) observe transfer the risk of underperformance completely to the provider. What this means is that the client should be able to write a contract with penalty provisions for suboptimal output quality to guard against possible opportunistic behavior of the provider, especially since the output quality 4error rate5 is verified and recorded. Therefore, there should be no need to monitor and manage work in process. Why then do clients resort to the dual governance mechanism? To answer this question we turn to the IPV perspective of the firm.

Theorists in the IPV tradition (Galbraith 1973, Tushman and Nadler 1978) hold that the greater the uncertainty of the task, the greater the amount of information that has to be processed by organizations in order to achieve efficient outcomes. When the process codifiability is low, there is a greater task related uncertainty, thus requiring a greater amount of information that needs to be processed by the provider’s agents in executing processes. However, merely relying on transmission of information does not ensure that the information is both fully understood and correctly implemented in process execution. Eisenhardt (1985) observes that a high level of task uncertainty favors deploying behavior control and socialization mechanisms over outcome controls (contractual clauses). When faced with task uncertainty, client firms exert behavior controls to both facilitate the processing of information and to enforce to the extent possible that the implications of the processed information have been correctly implemented in the execution of processes rather than to guard against moral hazard and opportunistic behavior. This leads us to our next set of hypotheses about the dual governance mechanism and its impact on output quality in offshore BPO.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2A</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> The quality of output is increasing in the extent of the client’s efforts to manage offshore agents.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2B</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> The quality of output is increasing in the extent of the provider’s efforts to manage offshore agents.

## 2.3. Process Codifiability and Dual Governance

If process codifiability and the dual governance mechanism are both associated with higher output quality, how does process codifiability impact on the effectiveness of the dual governance structure? Researchers in OT have recently sought to investigate how alliances are shaped by outcome controls and process controls. Tiwana (2008) argues that the simultaneous use of both technological modularity and process controls could not only be redundant but also result in performance-decreasing consequences in interfirm alliances. Srikanth and Puranam (2011) observe that once a common ground between the client and provider is created to share tacit knowledge and organizational protocols, the interfirm alliance would seek to minimize ongoing communication. These observations lead us to ask the question does process codifiability determine whether the efforts at behavior control by the client and the provider have performance-enhancing or performance-decreasing consequences? The implications of Eisenhardt’s study (1985) on task programmability and uncertainty (codifiability) would also suggest that where task uncertainty is low, simultaneous direct managerial control by the client and provider could well result in suboptimal efficiency outcomes. Thus we extend the idea of performancelowering impact of redundant interaction (Eisenhardt 1985, Srikanth and Puranam 2011, Tiwana 2008) to the dual governance mechanism and posit that process codifiability has a moderating impact on the dual governance mechanism and determines when dual governance improves or lowers output quality. This observation leads us to our next set of hypotheses:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3A</sup> <sup>(H3A).</sup> For processes of low codifiability the client’s and provider’s efforts at managing the offshore agents are complementary.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3B</sup> <sup>(H3B).</sup> For processes of high codifiability the client’s and provider’s efforts at managing the offshore agents are substitutes.

Given that process codifiability has a moderating impact on the dual governance mechanism, does it also determine the magnitude of independent impact<sup>2</sup> that the efforts of the client and behavior have on output quality? More generally, when would the client’s efforts at behavior control have a greater independent impact than the provider’s? Researchers in the field of OM—notably Kellogg and Chase (1995)— argue that high levels of codifiability of information allow for the contact between the buyer (client) and supplier (provider) to be less tightly coupled, whereas low levels of codifiability require more closely coupled contact between the two. When process codifiability is high, process context and task execution rules can be effectively transferred to the provider’s managers and the client’s managers are not significantly more effective than the provider’s in driving output quality. However, where process codifiability is low, the clients’ managers with their deeper understanding of their business context and task execution rules are at a significant advantage when compared to the provider’s. We combine these views with the IPV perspective (outlined in §2.2) and posit that the client’s efforts at behavior control are likely to have a higher independent impact on output quality than the provider’s for processes of low codifiability, whereas the client’s managers have no such advantage for processes of high codifiability. This observation leads to our next hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> For processes of low codifiability the client’s efforts at behavior control will have a greater independent impact on output quality; the difference in the independent impact of their efforts will be insignificant for high codifiability processes.

## 2.4. Governance and Interorganizational Information Systems

Researchers in organization design observe that coordinating work across the boundaries of the firm can be made more efficient by enhancing the observability of work in progress, especially of actions and outcomes (Crampton 2001). How do clients achieve enhanced visibility of work at an offshore site? They use interorganizational information systems that can track the working of an agent in real-time. In the example discussed in §1.2, the client’s managers use an interorganizational information system to sample the work of the offshore agents in real-time (tasks 2A–2C in Table 1(A)). Mani et al. (2012, p. 619) argued the need for process specific interorganizational information systems in bilateral governance when they observed that “the dynamic relationship between the business process and the business environment” meant that significant levels of information “need to be communicated, processed and translated into decisions in certain BPO relationships.” How does the exchange of information help align actions of the client and provider? Mani et al. (2010) posit that the information exchange between the provider and client enables joint action, thus making bilateral governance more effective. Researchers suggest that the use of interorganizational information systems to exchange information between the client and provider helps to align and coordinate the actions of the two parties, thus enabling better governance within bilateral BPO alliances. This leads us to our next hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> The use of process specific, interorganizational information systems results in a higher quality of output under the dual governance mechanism.

2.5. Role of Outcome Controls in Offshore BPO We now turn our attention to the role of contractual incentives (outcome controls) in determining the level of output quality. These contractual measures take the form of penalties (levied on the provider) for levels of (output) quality below the level specified in the service level agreement (SLA) and rewards for exceeding the SLA-specified level of quality. Bajari and Tadelis (2001) contrast incentives with transaction costs in procuring construction projects from contractors and observe that when output quality is not verifiable and when there is considerable ex post adaptation (change of specifications after the work begins), cost plus contracts are preferred to fixed price contracts by the client. In contrast, Mani et al. (2012) point out that fixed price contracts combined with incentive clauses transfer all of the risk to the provider, especially because the quality of output is verifiable in BPO and there is no ex post adaptation involved. Extant research also points to the importance of contracting arrangements as a principal determinant of the survival of service providers (Susarla and Barua 2011). We combine these perspectives with the fully verified and recorded measure of output quality in our study and posit that incentives tied to output quality levels will induce higher output quality from offshore BPO providers. This observation leads us to hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6A</sup> <sup>(H6A).</sup> The quality of output is increasing in the size of incentives specified in the contract.

Offshore execution of business processes in a sense represents a “nested” agency problem where incentives need to be aligned first between the client and the provider and then between the provider and its agents (employees). We term this nested agency problem as a two-echelon principal agent problem. By sharing some of the incentives that it receives from the client with its agents, the provider can ostensibly induce a higher level of effort from its employees<sup>3</sup> (Luft 1994, Hannan et al. 2005). This leads us to hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6B</sup> <sup>(H6B).</sup> The quality of output is increasing in the proportion of agents in the offshore process execution team that have quality-linked incentives.

In addition to the above we use several controls in our model of effects. Chen and Bharadwaj (2009) observe that prior interaction between the client and provider leads to greater familiarity for both parties with each others’ decision-making styles, business processes, and corporate cultures. This in turn would result in a clearer understanding of mutual needs and better specification of monitoring and review procedures at the level of the contract. Based on these observations we include the extent of prior exposure between the client and provider as a control variable. We also include other standard control variables, including those for the size of the client; prior experience of the provider with BPO; and categorical dummy variables such as dummies for client and provider, the country where the process is executed, and the business function performed by the process. In §3 we discuss aspects of our empirical research design and data selection.

## 3. Empirical Research Design

We conducted field research to collect detailed information from clients and providers of offshore BPO services about features of the contract such as incentives, the extent of managerial control exerted over offshore agents, and the output quality in each period. We use two kinds of data sets: a cross-sectional data set consisting of 11 provider and five client firms that entered into 139 bilateral offshore BPO contracts and a time series data set drawn from a balanced panel of 21 processes from four providers and five clients yielding 36 time series observations per process over a period of 18 months (756 panel data observations in all). The four providers and five clients from which we obtained panel data were a proper subset of the 11 provider firms and five clients from which crosssectional data were obtained. The 139 processes in the cross-sectional data set are different from the 21 processes in the panel data set with no overlapping processes between the two data sets. Given the sensitivities<sup>4</sup> surrounding outsourcing, it took us several meetings and about a year of active contact with the client and provider firms (as well as with a thirdparty firm<sup>5</sup> that provided consulting and research services to these firms) to gain access to the data. Processes in our database were offshored to four countries: India, Mauritius, Singapore, and Thailand.

## 3.1. Data Selection

Initial contact with the clients, providers, and a thirdparty research and consulting firm was initiated by three researchers.<sup>6</sup> We sent communications to 107 of the Fortune 500 firms that were known to have outsourced business processes to offshore providers and 41 offshore BPO providers. The research design required that both parties of a bilateral contracting pair—a client and the provider with whom it had contracted—had to agree to share data at the process level. Only nine clients and six providers were willing to share data with us. Of these we could only find four such bilateral contracting client-provider pairs. From these four bilateral contracting pairs— consisting of two clients and three providers<sup>7</sup>—we were able to secure access to 29 contracts for which we had complete cross-sectional data. As this was insufficient for rigorous analysis we subsequently contacted the third-party consulting firm whose overseas offices were run by the alumnus of a university with which one of the researchers was associated. This firm contacted its clients and providers and made available to us bilateral contracting pairs of three additional clients and eight providers. We were able to get process level data for 110 contracts via this firm. This gave us a total of 139 contracts for which we had cross-sectional data. For these 139 processes, the incentive payments (both penalties and rewards) were tied to a single measure of quality—the error rate. There was a single cross-sectional measure of quality (number of errors made during the year divided by the process volume for the year) that was recorded for the whole year at the process level. This measure was signed off on by the client and the provider and formed the basis of the incentive payments to the provider.

Panel Data. From the larger group of client and provider firms that gave us data, we were able to get panel data from a subset of firms—consisting of four providers and five clients—about 21 contracts, where detailed time series data about process output quality, volume of work done, and other attributes were recorded in each period and formed the basis of the payment made to the vendor. This left us with a balanced panel of 21 processes for which we had time series data consisting of 36 data points per process.

Data for the survey was collected from three sources: contract documents, via a survey instrument, and from the vendor payment log. In the next section we discuss how the variables were operationalized.

## 3.2. Operationalization of Variables

We begin the discussion of operationalization with time invariant variables extracted from the crosssectional data set.

Process Output Quality (Q). This is the dependent variable in our estimation model. The actual error rate of each process is recorded in the vendor payment log and forms the basis of payment by the client to the provider. The actual error rate was captured from the vendor payment logs. Process quality is defined as 1−error rate. The quality of the ith process is given by $Q _ { i } = 1 - e _ { i } \colon 0 \leq Q _ { i } \leq 1$ , where $e _ { i }$ is the error rate of the ith process.

Incentive (INC). For each contract in the data set, we extracted the penalties (rewards) built into the contract for output quality below (above) the level specified in the contract. The incentive variable is operationalized as a ratio of the quantum of penalty (reward) levied on the provider to the price paid to the provider. If the penalty for unit error rate in excess of the SLA specified quality level is a fraction of the price $P ^ { * }$ given by $f _ { p }$ and the reward for unit error rate below the SLA-specified level of quality is given by $f _ { r } ,$ then the incentive range is given by $P ^ { * } \cdot f _ { r } -$ $( \dot { - } \dot { P ^ { * } } \cdot f _ { p } ) = P ^ { * } ( f _ { r } + f _ { p } )$ and the quantum of incentive of the provider per unit price paid for the ith process is given by $I N \hat { C } _ { i } = P _ { i } ^ { * } ( \hat { f _ { r i } } + \hat { f _ { p i } } ) / P _ { i } ^ { * } = f _ { r i } + f _ { p i }$ . For the ith process, the incentive variable is given by $I N C _ { i }$

Process Codifiability (COD). We define process codifiability (per Mithas and Whitaker 2007) as the extent to which the activities that constitute a business process can be described completely in a set of written instructions measured on a 1–7 Likert scale at the level of each process. The codifiability of the ith process is given by $C O D _ { i }$

Agent Incentive (AIV). This variable is a ratio that captures the proportion of a team of agents working on a process that has bonuses tied to quality of output. For the ith process, with $m _ { i }$ agents and of whom $n _ { i } \leq m _ { i }$ agents receive quality-linked bonuses, $A I V _ { i } =$ $n _ { i } / m _ { i } \le 1$

Process Specific Information System (PSIS). This is a binary variable that captures if a process specific interorganizational information system was used to track and monitor the process execution in real time $( \Upsilon { \mathrm { e s } } = 1 , \ : \mathrm { N o } = 0 )$ . For the ith process, $P S I S _ { i } = 1$ or 0.

We now define the key time variant variables, extracted from the panel data set.

Process Output Quality (Q). This is the dependent variable. For the 21 processes for which we had panel data available, we captured the quality of output for each process in each period. As mentioned earlier the error rate logged in the vendor payment log in each period formed the basis of payment to the vendor. Process quality in the tth period is defined as 1 − error rate (for that period). The quality of the ith process in the tth period is given by $Q _ { i t } \dot { = } 1 - e _ { i t } \colon 0 \le \mathrm { \bar { ~ } } Q _ { i t } \le 1 ,$ where $e _ { i t }$ is the error rate of the ith process in the tth period.

Provider Managerial Effort (PB). This variable captures the extent of direct managerial control exerted by the provider’s managers on the offshore agents at the level of each process in each period. Each process has one or more managers that work for the provider and who exert managerial control over the agents that execute that process. We estimate the time spent by each manager in directly managing the offshore agents as a fraction of the total time spent by each manager and weight it by the number of agents that report to the manager. The definition of the variable is extended to cover a process with N operational managers. For the ith process, in the tth period, that has $\bar { N _ { i t } }$ managers managing the process, where the fraction of time the jth manager spends on the behavior control of agents is given by $w _ { j t } ,$ the number of agents that report to this manager is $n _ { j t } ,$ then $\begin{array} { r } { P B _ { i t } = \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { \mathbb { N } _ { i t } } w _ { j t } n _ { j t } / ( \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { \hat { N } _ { i t } } n _ { j t } ) } \end{array}$ , where $P B _ { i t }$ is the provider’s managerial effort for the ith process in the tth time period.

Client Managerial Effort (CB). This variable captures the extent of direct managerial control exerted by the client’s managers on the offshore agents (employees) of the provider at the level of each process in each period. For the ith process, in the tth period, where $M _ { i t }$ managers manage the process and where the fraction of time the jth manager spends on exerting direct managerial control over the agents offshore is given by $c _ { j t } ,$ if the number of agents managed by this manager is $m _ { j t } ,$ , then $C B _ { i t } =$ $\bar { \textstyle \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { M _ { i t } } } c _ { j t } m _ { j t } / ( \bar { \textstyle \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { M _ { i t } } } \bar { m _ { j t } } )$ , where $C B _ { i t }$ is the client’s behavior control effort of the ith process in the tth time period. The values of $N _ { i t } , n _ { j t } , M _ { i t } , m _ { j t } , w _ { j t } ,$ , and $c _ { j t }$ were captured in each time period via our survey.

Process Volume (Vol). The process volume completed and delivered in each period is recorded in the vendor payment log and forms the basis for payment by the client to the vendor for work done in each period. The normalized value of the process volume variable for the ith process in the tth period is given by $V o l _ { i t }$ . Since processes in our sample vary widely, the units of measurement of process volume also vary widely depending on the nature of the process. To control for this variance we used the standard normal Z-score for the process volume in each period.<sup>8</sup> For the ith process in the tth period, if the raw volume of work executed is given by $W _ { i t } ,$ then the normalized variable $V o l _ { i t }$ is given by $\dot { V } o l _ { i t } = ( W _ { i t } - \mu _ { i } ) / \sigma _ { i } ,$ where $\mu _ { i }$ and $\sigma _ { i }$ are the mean and standard deviation, respectively, of the ith process calculated over all (36) time periods.

Control Variables and Categorical Dummy Variables. We discuss the operationalization of control variables and categorical dummies below. We begin with the control variables.

Prior Association Between Client and Provider (PACP). Length of prior association between the client and provider (measured in months) when the contract for each business process was signed, given by $P A C P _ { i }$ for the ith process.

Process Owner (PO). A binary variable that captures whether there is a single manager within the client firm that is responsible for documenting the quality of the output received from the provider $\hat { ( Y = 1 , } N = 0 )$ , given by $P O _ { i }$ for the ith process.

Average Agent Experience (AEX). Average experience (in months) of the team of offshore agents working on each process. For the ith process, if there are $m _ { i }$ agents and the jth agent has $k _ { j }$ months of experience, then $\begin{array} { r } { A E X _ { i } = ( \bar { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { m _ { i } } k _ { j } ) / m _ { i } } } \end{array}$

Target Quality Level (SLAQ). This is computed at the process-level from the maximum acceptable error rate specified in the contract (SLA). For the ith process $S L A \dot { Q _ { i } } = 1 - \bar { e } _ { i }$ where e¯ is the maximum permissible error rate specified in the SLA for each process.

Number of Agents (NAG). Number of agents executing the process, measured at the level of the process. For the ith process it is given by $N A G _ { i }$

Client’s Prior Exposure to Offshore BPO (CPEO). Length of client’s prior exposure to offshore BPO (measured in months) when the contract for each business process was signed. For the ith process it is given by CPEO .

Client Size (CSIZ). The size of the client’s market capitalization measured in USD billions at the time of the contract. For the ith process it is given by CSIZ .

Provider Exposure to BPO (PEXP). Length of the provider’s prior exposure to offshore BPO (measured in months) when the contract for each business process was signed. For the ith process it is given by $P E X P _ { i }$

Categorical Dummies. The offshored business processes belonged to one of the four business functions (identified by Mani et al. 2012) given by finance, marketing, human resources, and operations. A categorical dummy variable was used for each business function. In addition, these categorical dummy variables were also used to code the data for the region to which the process was offshored (four dummies) and the client firm that offshored the process (five dummies). Table 2 provides descriptive statistics and correlations of the variables. In §4 we discuss the findings of the model.

## 4.2. Time Invariant Factors—Results from Cross-Sectional Data

In Table 3, we report two models based on Equation (1): a GLM model and an ordinary least squares (OLS) model.

Process Codifiability. The results from the two models are very similar and provide strong support in favor of Hypothesis H1. The findings of both the OLS model $( \dot { \beta } = 0 . 0 3 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and the GLM model $( \beta = 0 . 3 9 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ are statistically highly significant and show that process codifiability is associated with higher output quality. An increase in process codifiability lowers the task uncertainty and thereby increases the quality of output. We will return to the moderating role played by process codifiability on the dual governance mechanism when we analyze the model based on time series data.

Process Specific Interorganizational Information Systems (PSIS). It is seen from columns 2 and 3 of Table 3 that there is strong support for H5; the use of PSIS is positively associated with quality of output. Again both models, OLS $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 \bar { 7 } , p < \mathrm { \bar { 0 } } . 0 1 )$ and GLM $( \beta = 0 . 4 3 9 , \ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , show that greater use of PSIS is associated with higher output quality. Prior research points to the “observation effect,” wherein monitoring information workers using optical and/or electronic monitoring mechanisms (Aron et al. 2011, Zuboff 1989) at their site of work results in information workers being less likely to overlook process level controls. Our results show that PSIS can become very effective tools of governance in offshore BPO; they enable the client’s managers to observe work in progress and create an electronic observation effect.

Outcome Controls: Incentives (INC). We hypothesize about two sets of incentives. The first is reflected in the principal agent problem between the client and the provider (H6A) and the second between the provider and the provider’s agents (H6B). Columns 2 and 3 (Table 3) offer strong support for Hypothesis H6A (OLS  = 00076, p < 0001; GLM $\beta = 0 . 4 2 0 ,$ $p < 0 . 1 )$ thus offering further evidence that incentives have a positive impact on output quality. Srikanth and Puranam (2011) observe that offshore BPO falls under a category of outsourcing known as service provisioning, which involves the repetitive delivery of work in each period (where the metrics tracking output quality are the same in each period). The 139 processes in our data sets were contracted based on a single, unambiguously recorded measure of output quality. Under these conditions, incentives have a significant impact on the quality of output.

A second aspect of the agency problem has to do with possible incentive incompatibility between the provider and its employees—i.e., the two-echelon principal-agent problem. Although the provider may receive rewards for achieving higher quality of output from clients, it may not share these with the offshore agents that execute the process. It has been suggested in prior research (Luft 1994, Hannan et al. 2005) that rewards for quality of output could be an effective way of aligning the incentives between workers and employers. We find that this is indeed the case. It can be seen from Columns 2 and 3 (Table 3) that there is strong support for Hypothesis H6B in both estimation models (OLS  = 00053, p < 001; GLM $\beta = 0 . 7 3 2 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , which suggests that the greater the proportion of agents that have rewards tied to output quality, the greater the resulting quality of output. Finally, both the OLS and GLM models show that (the control variable) prior association between the client and provider is associated with higher output quality. This is consistent with Chen and Bharadwaj (2009), who observe that prior association leads to a greater understanding and better coordination between the client and provider. ## 4.3. Time Variant Factors—Model and Results from Panel Data

We now turn our attention to the time variant factors—the extent to which the provider and the client’s managers’ efforts are directed at managing the offshore agents in each period. We use a model of effects that isolates the time variant factors (where all variables were de-meaned to remove process-level tendencies).

$$
\begin{array}{c} Q _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} C B _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} P B _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} V o l _ {i t} \\ + \beta_ {4} C B _ {i t} \times P B _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

We test this model using a balanced panel data set of 21 processes over 36 time periods. We present the results of the data analysis in Table 4, where we present four models in all: fixed effects (FE), random effects (RE), first difference (FD), and a model based on the quasi-likelihood estimation (QLE) method. Note that process codifiability does not change from period to period and does not enter the estimation model.

It can be seen from columns 1 through 4 of Table 4 that all four models produce very similar and consistent estimates of effect sizes. The Hausman test $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 9 ,$ , Prob $> \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 7 9 1 )$ suggests that the difference between coefficient estimates of the FE and RE models is insignificant; we therefore use the estimates from the RE model. The estimates of the RE model in column 2 provide very strong support for Hypotheses 2A and 2B (client’s managerial effort $\beta = 0 . 8 2 5 , p <$ 0001; provider’s managerial effort $\beta = 0 . 2 3 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ For both clients and providers, greater efforts at controlling the behavior of agents are associated with a higher quality of output.<sup>10</sup> The model estimate for the interaction term—CB × PB—would suggest that the efforts of the client and provider, although individually beneficial, are substitutes when taken together (the parameter for the interaction term is negative). Such a conclusion would ignore the role played by process codifiability on the dual governance mechanism. To understand how the managerial efforts of the two firms actually interact we need to separate the processes by their levels of codifiability and analyze the nature of their interaction.<sup>11</sup>

## 4.4. Moderating Effect of Process Codifiability

In Table 5 we separate the panel data of 21 processes based on the levels of process codifiability into three categories: low codifiability processes with process codifiability levels ranging from 1 to 3, high codifiability processes with process codifiability ranging from 5 to 7, and processes of moderate codifiability (codifiability level of 4). This partitioning of processes also corresponds to the midpoint of the Likert scale used to measure process codifiability.

It can be seen from Table 5 that there are nine low codifiability and nine high codifiability processes. To model the moderating impact of process codifiability on output quality, we run the same estimation model above in Equation (2) on the two panels—low and high codifiability processes, respectively—separately and contrast the estimates of the coefficients including those of the interaction terms. Table 6 shows the results of the estimation models on the two panels.

It can be seen from Table 6 that once again there is very strong evidence in support of H2A and H2B. For low codifiability processes it is evident from columns 1 through 4 that all four models produce very similar and consistent estimates of effect sizes. The Hausman test $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 2$ , Prob $> \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 9 0 )$ favors the RE model (column 2). As before it can be seen that for low codifiability processes higher levels of effort made by both the client $( \beta = 1 . 6 4 6 , ~ p <$ 00015 and provider $( \beta = 0 . 1 6 9 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ in managing offshore agents are associated with higher output quality levels. What is interesting here is the behavior of the interaction term (as hypothesized in H3A and H3B). We now turn our attention to the findings for high codifiability processes shown in columns 5 through 8 in Table 6. As before it is evident from columns 5 through 8 that all four models produce very similar and consistent estimates of effect sizes. The Hausman test $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 3$ , Prob > $\chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 8 6 9 )$ favors the RE model. The findings of this split panel analysis provide strong evidence in favor of Hypotheses 3A and 3B, which posit the moderating effect of process codifiability on the dual governance mechanism. As we observed in §2, when processes are characterized by low codifiability, there is significant task uncertainty (IPV), and the required information for executing processes cannot be fully transferred through written specifications via a contract. Banerjee and Duflo (2000) show that when it is difficult to transfer information and cover contingencies fully through contracts, norm-governed relationships are needed. The dual governance mechanism enables the enforcement of norms through managerial control and sets up what Bapna et al. (2010) have called relational governance within the bilateral BPO alliance. As we observed earlier in §2.3, the implications of Eisenhardt’s (1985) study would also suggest that for highly codifiable processes, simultaneous managerial control exerted by the client and provider would result in redundancies and inefficient duplication of work. For these highly codifiable processes, the client and the provider are both enforcing the same protocol of task specifications that has been codified in extensive detail and frequently replicate each other’s work and create additional redundant work for agents.

Endogeneity. We summarize below the results of the tests of endogeneity. We identified the regressors that were possibly endogenous and extracted four instrumental variables (IV) that we used to instrument the two potentially endogenous regressors. We present below a brief discussion of the IVs (for expositional brevity)—the complete discussion, including statistical correlations, appears in the online appendix (available as supplemental material at http://dx.doi .org/10.1287/isre.2014.0550).

Choice of Instruments for Incentive (INC). Researchers have shown that in offshore BPO, contractual SLAs require that vendors randomly sample both the finished output and work in progress for errors at prespecified random sampling rates (Aron et al. 2008, Knowledge@Wharton 2005, Seshasai et al. 2010). For the 139 processes for which we have crosssectional data, the SLA specified sampling rates for finished output and work in process were extracted from the contract. We also obtained the actual average sampling rates from the QA documents. Based on these we constructed two IVs which measure the extent to which the vendor exceeded the SLA specified sampling rate in audit of the final output (termed as final audit) as well as the extent to which the vendor exceeded the SLA specified random sample rates for work in progress. The variable Excess Sampling Rate is operationalized as follows. Excess Sampling Rate of final output,<sup>12</sup> given by ESR\_F, is defined as

$$
E S R \_ F _ {i} = Q A F _ {i} - S L A Q F _ {i},
$$

where $Q A F _ { i }$ is the actual fraction of finished output that was randomly sampled for QA and ${ \dot { S } } L A Q F _ { i }$ is the SLA specified proportion of finished output to be sampled for the ith process.

Choice of Instruments for Process Codifiability (COD). For each process there are a number of tasks that constitute the process. For instance, in the example of BPO discussed in Table 1(A), it can be seen that there are eight tasks that constitute business process. For the 139 processes in our cross-sectional data set, we extracted the number of tasks associated with each process. We define this variable as Process Task Index (PTI). $P T I _ { i } = k _ { i } ;$ ; where $k _ { i }$ is the number of tasks that constitute the ith process. Note that the actual set of tasks associated with a process defines the process (Mithas and Whitaker 2007).

We also extracted a second IV for process codifiability (COD). Recent research by Fettke et al. (2010) and Scheer and Brabänder (2010) shows that process specification and codification techniques first identified by Malone et al. (1999) have been combined into the domain of business process management (BPM) and are deployed as five principal BPM tools in specifying the nature of business processes (Fettke et al. 2010, Scheer et al. 2010). We identified five BPM tools used for process description and codification. These are (i) data flow diagrams (DF), (ii) work flow diagrams (also known as process flow diagrams), (iii) data dictionaries, (iv) task rules, and (v) output metrics. For each process in our cross-sectional database, we extracted from the process specification the number of BPM tools used. Each one of the five BPM tools above used to define a business process structure was coded as 1 if it existed and 0 if it did not. These scores were combined into a single variable Business Process Definition Index (BPDI). The BPDI score could range from 0 if none of the above tools were used to 5 if all of the above tools were used.

$$
B P D I _ {i} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {5} t _ {i j}, \quad t _ {j} \in \{0, 1 \}.
$$

The actual scores ranged from 1 to 5. Our expectation is that the higher the score, the greater the extent of process description and therefore, the greater the process codifiability (Malone et al. 1999, Scheer et al. 2010). We performed the full suite of tests of endogeneity, including the tests for three kinds of hazards of endogeneity; these are (i) endogeneity in the model, (ii) use of weak/partial instruments, and (iii) endogeneity in instruments (Cameron and Trivedi 2005, Greene 2011, Stock et al. 2002).

The Two Stage Least Squares Estimation Model. In stage 1, we regress the two endogenous variables against the instruments given by $Z \ ( 1 3 9 \times 4$ matrix) and all other regressors given by X (139 × 22 matrix) including all of the control variables. The DV in the first stage equations are the two potentially endogenous variables $X _ { 1 }$ and $X _ { 2 }$

First Stage.

$$
\begin{array}{l} X _ {1} = X \beta_ {1} + Z \alpha_ {1} + \varepsilon_ {1}, \\ X _ {2} = X \beta_ {2} + Z \alpha_ {2} + \varepsilon_ {2}. \end{array}
$$

This results in the fitted values of the two endogenous regressors, given by $\hat { X } _ { 1 }$ and ${ \hat { X } } _ { 2 } ,$ respectively, that we use in the second stage regression. In stage 2 we regress the dependent variable $Q _ { i }$ (quality of output) against the two fitted endogenous regressors and all other regressors (including all control dummies).

Second Stage. The second stage regression is given by

$$
Q _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} \hat {X} _ {1 i} + \gamma_ {2} \hat {X} _ {2 i} + \beta_ {3} X _ {3 i} + \beta_ {4} X _ {4 i} + \dots + \epsilon_ {i}.
$$

The Hausman specification test is used to verify if the model without IVs and the model with IVs (2SLS) are both consistent (free of endogeneity).<sup>13</sup> We accept the null hypothesis as per the Hausman model specification test (Hausman m-statistic = 28074 with $p = 0 . 2 5$ (for $\chi ^ { 2 } ( 2 4 ) )$ . This implies that the model without the

IVs is the better estimator (note that when both models are consistent the OLS model without IVs is the more efficient estimator as it has a lower asymptotic variance).

We also tested for the strength of instruments by performing the Shea’s partial R-squared test. We find strong support for the strength of IVs—Shea’s minimum eigenvalue statistic, 2004101, is greater than the 2SLS relative bias critical value (11.04 and $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ The Shea statistic is also greater than the threshold value of the 2SLS nominal 5% Wald test. This shows that the IVs that we use are strong instruments. Finally, we tested for the exogeneity of the IVs by running the Sargan test. The results again showed strong evidence that the IVs were not endogenous. The computed Sargan test statistic = 3059755 with a $p = 0 . 4 4 3 9$ (for $\chi ^ { 2 } ( 2 ) )$ ) allows us to reject the alternative hypothesis (HA) that the model is not overidentified and some of the instruments are endogenous.

We also tested to make sure that the two potentially endogenous regressors mediated in the relationship between the IV and the dependent variable. We used the three canonical tests for mediation—the Sobel test, the Aroian test, and the Goodman test (Baron and Kenny 1986, Sobel 1982). All three tests affirmed that the two regressors mediated in the relationship between the IV and the dependent variable (at $p <$ 0005 for INC and $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ for COD). In addition to these we also performed a hygiene check. We ran the two estimation models—the 2SLS with IVs and the model without IVs (the model that we use in this study). The results are very similar—all of the hypothesized effects are supported by both models (whereas the model without IVs has the lower asymptotic variance and is therefore the more efficient estimator). Therefore, we can conclude that the estimation models that we use are robust and without endogeneity. In §5 we discuss the insights from this study as well as its limitations.

## 5. Conclusions and Limitations

Our research unearths the role played by process codifiability in determining output quality. Prior research in OT has viewed the problem of coordination between the client and provider through the prism of modularity. Process codifiability is different from modularity; where modularity is about specifying interfaces between the client and provider (Tiwana 2008), process codifiability is about specifying exactly what tasks constitute a process and how each task is to be executed (Mithas and Whitaker 2007). We demonstrate the direct impact of process codifiability on output quality, where higher process codifiability is associated with a higher output quality.

We make two novel contributions to the literature on BPO and offshore bilateral alliances; these have to do with the concept of the dual governance mechanism and the moderating impact of process codifiability on the dual governance mechanism. We show using panel data that process codifiability has a significant moderating impact on the managerial efforts of the client and the provider. Process codifiability determines when the two are complementary and when they are substitutes. The efforts at behavior control of offshore agents by the client and provider are complementary only for low codifiability processes; for high codifiability processes, they are substitutes and have a performance-decreasing impact on the BPO alliance. Implications of research in OT (Puranam et al. 2009, Srikanth and Puranam 2011) suggest that there are possible detrimental effects of prolonged ongoing communication once a sufficiently robust common ground has been established. We show from our model of effects based on panel data that it is not merely ongoing communication that has a performance-lowering impact. What causes the performance-decreasing interaction between the two efforts under the dual governance mechanism is the extent to which process information can be codified; for highly codifiable processes, the client’s managers’ efforts interact to have a performancelowering impact. This brings us to an important theoretical contribution of this study—the dual governance mechanism.

This is the first study that separates the efforts made by the client and the offshore provider in each period and analyzes the independent impact of each, as well as their interaction effects, on output quality. The theoretical premise of the dual governance mechanism is an interesting departure from extant research in the fields of OT and strategy (Baldwin and Clark 2000, Langlois 2002, Puranam et al. 2009, Srikanth 2007, Ulrich and Eppinger 1999) where researchers have argued that the objective of the modularity strategy is to actually reduce dependencies between the vendor’s agents and the client. In contrast, we show that the dual governance mechanism— with its direct and ongoing dependencies between the agents of the provider and the client’s managers— has performance-increasing effects for low codifiability processes in bilateral BPO alliances.

Another area in which we extend the body of theories in IS research is the use of interorganizational information systems by clients to monitor work in progress at an offshore BPO provider’s site. Mani et al. (2012) posited that significant levels of information exchange between the provider and client would be needed for effective functioning of the bilateral BPO contract. We are able to provide strong evidence in support of this claim by showing that the use of interorganizational information systems at the process level to exchange information between the provider and the client was positively associated with higher levels of quality (H5). Our findings suggest that the use of PSIS extends the scope of bilateral governance in offshore BPO.

The study also has some insights for practitioners. We show that the independent impact of the client’s managers’ effort is greater for processes of low codifiability (H4). This would suggest to the providers’ managers that they should allow the client’s managers greater leeway in managing the offshore agents for low codifiability processes. For clients, too, the implications of the two-echelon agency problem are relevant. Clients often attempt to write elaborate penalty clauses for different kinds of errors committed by the client’s employees—which are very difficult to enforce because of their complexity, as Susarla et al. (2010) point out. Instead, it would be preferable for clients to insist that providers share some of the rewards that they receive for higher quality with their employees through quality-linked bonuses.

## 5.1. Limitations and Extensions

This research has some limitations. The only way to gather time series data about actual quality of output at the process level is to conduct a field study. This limits our reach in terms of the firms that we can empanel in the study because very few firms were willing to record and share detailed data in each period with us. A second problem has to do with the extreme political sensitivity that surrounds offshore outsourcing. As a consequence, most firms that we contacted were reluctant to entertain any requests for data, let alone share detailed data sets spanning a period of several months. This imposes constraints on the extent to which these results can be generalized.<sup>14</sup> A third limitation has to do with the inability to compare the relative magnitude of the impact of behavior controls and outcome controls. Behavior controls (managerial efforts) change from period to period, whereas the incentives built into the contract (outcome controls) do not. For this reason the two variables appear in two different estimation models. Thus there is no theoretically sound way in which the impact of a time invariant variable and that of a time variant variable can be compared. This study will not be able to answer the question of which of the two sets of controls—incentives or managerial effort—is more effective in inducing higher output quality.

We believe that several interesting and significant extensions can be made to this research. Based on our results, it would also be interesting to study the impact of two-echelon incentives in contracts where the client formally specifies what fraction of quality bonuses paid to the provider must be shared by the provider with its agents. A second area of study would be to contrast the functioning of BPO alliances with a firm’s proprietary operations in the same country (often called “captive centers”). It would be interesting to investigate the functioning of captive centers and contrast them with offshore BPO alliances.
