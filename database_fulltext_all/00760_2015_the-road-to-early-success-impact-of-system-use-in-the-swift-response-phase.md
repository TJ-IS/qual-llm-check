# The Road to Early Success: Impact of System Use in the Swift Response Phase

Yu Tong Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon, Hong Kong, yutong@cityu.edu.hk

Sharon Swee-Lin Tan, Hock-Hai Teo Department of Information Systems, National University of Singapore, Singapore 117417, {distans@nus.edu.sg, disteohh@nus.edu.sg}

W<sup>hen</sup> <sup>an</sup> <sup>enterprise</sup> <sup>system</sup> <sup>is</sup> <sup>introduced,</sup> <sup>system</sup> <sup>users</sup> <sup>often</sup> <sup>experience</sup> <sup>a</sup> <sup>performance</sup> <sup>dip</sup> <sup>as</sup> <sup>they</sup> <sup>struggle</sup> with the unfamiliar system. Appropriately managing this phase, which we term as the swift response phase (SRP), is vital given its prominent impact on the eventual success of the system. Yet, there is a glaring lack of studies that examine the SRP. Drawing on sensemaking theory and early postadoptive literature, this study seeks to propose a theory-driven model to understand how different support structures facilitate different forms of use-related activities to induce a positive performance in the SRP. The model was tested through a two-stage survey involving 329 nurses. The results demonstrated the discriminating alignment between information system (IS) use-related activity and support structures in enhancing system users’ work performance in the SRP. Specifically, suitability of impersonal support moderated the effects of standardized system use and individual adaption on performance, whereas availability of personal support only moderated the effect of nonstandardized system use on performance. For moderating role of personal support, IS specialists support had a lower influence than peer-champion support and peer-user support. This study contributes to the extant literature by (1) conceptualizing the turbulent SRP, (2) applying sensemaking theory to the initial postadoptive stage, (3) adding to the theoretical debate on the value of system use, and (4) unveiling the distinct roles of support structures under different types of use activities. Practical suggestions are provided for organizational management and policy makers to deal with the complexities in the SRP.

Keywords: early system success; sensemaking in organization; swift response phase; shakedown phase; system use; support structure

## 1. Introduction

Effective management in the early postadoptive stage is crucial to ensure a successful enterprise system implementation. Approximately 70% to 85% of enterprise resource planning systems fail because the anticipated benefits are not realized fast enough, if at all (Ganly 2012). The lack of user appreciation at an early stage creates a vicious cycle and derails even the most well-envisioned implementation of information systems (ISs) (Lapointe and Rivard 2005). Given that the worldwide spending on enterprise systems is projected to reach USD \$320 billion in 2014, which demonstrates the strongest growth rate (6.8%) among all categories of the information technology (IT) spending of firms (Gartner 2014), the importance of the early postadoptive stage, which is also known as the shakedown phase, cannot be underestimated.

The shakedown phase refers to the period from which the system becomes functional and accessible to users until normal or routine use is reached (Markus and Tanis 2000). This phase coincides with the J -shaped postadoption curve in which many firms experience a “performance dip” immediately after the implementation of an enterprise system, which is followed by a period of improvement before normal use is reached (Applegate et al. 2009, p. 313). Unlike previous studies on the shakedown phase (Morris and Venkatesh 2010, Sykes et al. 2014), this study emphasizes the importance of dichotomizing the shakedown phase into the swift response phase (SRP) and the steady adjustment phase (SAP) to enable the proper management of SRP. The SRP is a short period at the beginning of the shakedown phase when users experience a performance dip, recover from the dip, and begin to see a positive performance. Qualitative studies observe the dip occurring immediately (Goh et al. 2011), shortly (Boudreau and Robey 2005), and in the first few weeks after the system rollout (Lapointe and Rivard 2005, Strong et al. 2014) (see Table 1, panel A for a summary of the literature on performance dip). This post-go-live dip is also widely acknowledged in the popular press. Top management is advised to be aware of this interim dip after rolling out the enterprise system (Weightman 2010), which typically lasts for one to two months for an enterprise resource planning system (CFO Publishing 2013).<sup>1</sup>

In the SRP, many system users tend to undergo a steep learning curve and struggle with even the basic system functions as they attempt to make sense of the system (Boudreau and Seligman 2005). This sensemaking process tends to be accompanied by a torrent of complaints, unnecessary work delays, and additional work burdens that cause users to doubt whether they can restore preimplementation performance or achieve a high performance (Goh et al. 2011, Scott et al. 2005). The initially receptive users may also be disenchanted with the system (Scott et al. 2005). If the sensemaking process of users in the SRP is not managed properly, user resistance may lead to eventual system abandonment (Lapointe and Rivard 2005). By contrast, if users overcome and restore the initial dip in performance and become confident in achieving a favorable performance in the SRP, they will move on to the next phase, SAP, which refers to the period when users have acquired basic system knowledge and are able to steadily interact with the system until normal use is reached (Accenture 2006, Goh et al. 2011). During the SAP, when users perceive the new system to be valuable, their initial resistance will be lowered (Kim and Kankanhalli 2009) and they may commit themselves to changes that result in normal or routine use (Saga and Zmud 1994). Therefore, ensuring performance in the SRP is important for the successful progression to SAP and the eventual success of the shakedown phase. Given its importance and its differences from SAP, SRP, as a largely unexplored topic, is worthy of a separate investigation.

This study focuses on the SRP from the sensemaking perspective. When users are confronted with a new system, they make sense of such a system through hands-on interaction/use (Weick 2001). As shown earlier, this sensemaking process by users in the SRP tends to result in negative experiences, such as a decline in performance because of the unfamiliarity and difficulties encountered during the initial system interaction. Therefore, a key management challenge is how to induce a positive sensemaking outcome in the SRP so that users can successfully enter the SAP. Given that the outcome of sensemaking is socially constructed (Weick 1995), organizational environment in the form of support may serve as a reference in the sensemaking process of users to help interpret the effect of a system (e.g., performance). In the SRP, “hypercare” in the form of multiple types of support is often provided to reduce the learning curve of users, minimize the performance dip, and shorten the performance restoration period (Accenture 2012, CFO Publishing 2013). With strong support, some system users, despite facing the challenges, may derive positive feelings toward the system in the SRP (Strong et al. 2014).

Traditional support structures, such as training, documentation support, and specialist support (Kim and Kankanhalli 2009, Sykes et al. 2014), as well as informal peer support (Sykes 2015) provide necessary information that enables employees to work effectively in a new enterprise system environment. Prior literature on the shakedown phase found that peer support could be more effective than traditional support structures (Sykes 2015, Sykes and Venkatesh 2015). However, these studies did not delineate between the SRP and the SAP in the shakedown phase. As users in the SRP and the SAP may have different levels of user experience and system familiarity, their needs for system support may also differ. The role of these support structures in the SRP remains unclear.<sup>2</sup> When multiple support cues are available in the environment, sensemakers tend to select minimal cues in corresponding situations for constructing interpretations (Weick 1995). Given that support structures provide different types of information and contexts in which the information is provided (Sykes 2015), we expect that users who engage in different types of system use behavior in the SRP require different support structures.

This study aims to answer the following question: In the SRP, how do organizational support structures facilitate different types of system use behavior to induce a favorable performance? Performance is defined as the extent to which an enterprise system can affect the job performance of a user. Job performance has long been recognized as a measure of a successful IS implementation (DeLone and McLean 2003) because the actual value of an enterprise system hinges on whether users can perform system-enabled tasks effectively (Sykes 2015). We develop a model that examines the moderating effects of support structures on the relationship between IS use-related activity and performance. In particular, we posit that when system users make sense of a new enterprise system in the SRP, support structures can play different roles in helping users alleviate the turbulent experiences in their early system interactions and quickly recover from the performance dip. This study contributes to the IS postadoptive literature by conceptualizing the SRP and by theorizing the relationships among ISrelated use behaviors, support structures, and user performance. This enriched and nuanced understanding can help firms deal with the unique challenges in the SRP.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: Individual Sensemaking in Organizations

A new enterprise system is an equivoque to the employees (Weick 2001). An equivoque is “something that admits of several possible or plausible interpretations and therefore can be esoteric, subject to misunderstandings, uncertain, complex and recondite” (Weick 2001, p. 148). Sensemaking theory holds that when confronted with an equivoque in an organization, employees may be overwhelmed by the equivocality, and their identity in the organization (e.g., self-efficacy) may also be challenged (Weick 1995). Therefore, they attempt to rationalize and reconstruct their identity (e.g., maintaining a positive self-conception) by intentionally engaging in a sensemaking process (Weick 1995).

The sensemaking process is best understood as “construction via actions” and “bracketing cues” (Weick 1995, p. 8). First, the most distinguishing property of sensemaking is that actions must precede the interpretation of the equivocal situation (Weick 1995). In other words, the sensemaking process is retrospective and occurs only after the actions transpire (Weick 1995). This view is based on the assumption that simply understanding an activity (e.g., through training) is insufficient to trigger a committed interpretation of the equivoque. When an individual physically engages in an action (e.g., system-induced actions), the individual should scan the environment, identify meaningful information, and construct the meanings of the equivoque-interrupted work (Jasperson et al. 2005). Therefore, the outcomes of sensemaking are partly constructed by the actions of employees, which can be reactive because of an equivoque-induced change in the work routine (e.g., standardized system use and nonstandardized system use based on the job requirement) or a proactive enactment on an equivoque-interrupted work (e.g., adaptation actions initiated by users).

Second, sensemaking does not occur in isolation only through retrospective actions but is rooted in social construction (Weick 1995). When employees engage in actions to interpret and construct the meanings of an equivoque, they tend to be overwhelmed and confused by the equivocality. Therefore, these employees look for information cues from the environment to prioritize and clarify the meanings of such equivoque (Weick 1995). They read and select relevant and favorable pieces of information from the work environment (e.g., support structure). The selected information serves as a reference to direct their sensemaking process to where plausible meanings of the equivoque-interrupted work (e.g., system-related performance) are constructed and become the output of the sensemaking process (Weick 1995).

Sensemaking theory has been applied to examine different types of equivoques such as wildland fire (Whiteman and Cooper 2011) and postmerger integration (Monin et al. 2013). This theory is also applied in the IS domain. Weick (2001) argues that a new system is an equivoque to the employees of organizations. Jasperson et al. (2005) develop a conceptual framework that depicts individual sensemaking as an evaluative process in the IS postadoptive stage. Recent empirical research applies the sensemaking lens to understand the IS implementation. Jensen and Aanestad (2007) observe through a case study that healthcare professionals actively enact the original way of using the system and form different conceptions of the system, work practices, and system implementation through sensemaking. Hsieh et al. (2011) apply sensemaking theory to examine the stable postadoptive stage by showing that user-assessed technology/service quality can affect system use and performance through feedback mechanisms (see panel C of Table 1 for a review of the literature on the sensemaking of an enterprise system).

Prior literature lends credence to the theoretical appropriateness of sensemaking theory as an overarching lens for understanding the IS postadoptive phenomenon. In this study, we depict sensemaking in the early postadoptive phase or SRP. The implementation of a new enterprise system is an equivoque to organizational users. On one hand, users can make limited sense of this multifeature enterprise system a priori. On the other hand, the current work routine of users tends to be altered by the introduction of the enterprise system (Goh et al. 2011). Consequently, in the SRP, users who are new to the enterprise system tend to struggle even with the simplest system functions (Boudreau and Seligman 2005). Therefore, their experiences from the work-related actions in this phase (e.g., system-induced actions) tend to be recondite with the steep learning curve and uncertainties, which result in negative interpretations (e.g., performance dip). Such experiences accentuate the need to draw on appropriate support structures to facilitate their sensemaking in the SRP. Therefore, the discriminating alignment of users between support structures and system-induced actions, and how such alignment affects their interpretation of system-related performance, should be understood. Although system-induced actions can be classified in various ways, this study focuses on IS userelated activity, which is defined as “the set of behaviors individuals undertake concerning a specific task-technology-individual context” (Barki et al. 2007, p. 174). IS use-related activity encompasses three types of actions, namely, technology interaction behavior (i.e., interacting with the IS to accomplish tasks), task-technology adaptation behavior (i.e., changing certain IS functions or how they are used in the organization), and individual adaptation behavior (i.e., individual modification to adapt to the IS). We posit that the technology interaction behavior can be further categorized into standardized and nonstandardized system use according to the system features. Adapted from Saga and Zmud (1994), standardized system use in this study refers to the IS utilization of users for accomplishing tasks with limited variations in the set of steps that they need to follow. Conversely, nonstandardized system use refers to the IS utilization of users for accomplishing tasks with great variations in the set of steps that they need to follow. In other words, the sequence of steps is not standardized but depends on the specificities of a situation. Such categorization enables us to differentiate the difficulties that system users tend to encounter in the SRP. Categorizing standardized versus nonstandardized system uses is also consistent with the view that technology can be characterized by its features (Griffith 1999).

## 3. Research Model and Hypotheses

Figure 2 depicts our research model. By controlling the factors that influence system outcomes (i.e., work experience, education, age, system complexity, and system commitment), we expect that the extent to which system-induced actions can enhance a user’s interpretation toward performance in the SRP may vary depending on the support structures.

## 3.1. Baseline Model: Support Structures (Personal and Impersonal)

An organizational user who is exposed to a new system in the workplace can face difficulties and uncertainties in his or her interactions with the system (Weick 2001). A favorable support structure under such circumstances serves as a medium for users to interpret the system and facilitate their learning process. Different support structures exhibit various characteristics (Sykes 2015). Personal and impersonal support structures differ in terms of resource customization and distribution. Personal support is a customized and shared resource that can provide specialized help according to the need of users but may not be available at all times (i.e., right information but not necessarily at the right time). For instance, IS specialists are shared in the organization and can only attend to a limited number of requests at a time. Peer users and champions are resources that are shared in the department. Hence, help from personal support is not always available when needed. Conversely, impersonal support, such as documentation, is a standard and individual resource that is readily available to every user (i.e., right time but not necessarily the right information). Given the differences between these two support structures, we assess the dimension that is critical to the support structure to be helpful to users in the SRP. For personal support, we examine the availability of supporting personnel in assisting users who encounter difficulties during system use (i.e., availability of personal support). For impersonal support, we examine the congruence between common written instructions and daily work demands and processes (i.e., suitability of impersonal support).

Users experience a steep learning curve and struggle even with the basic functions in the SRP (Boudreau and Seligman 2005). Therefore, we expect that both personal support and impersonal support have important roles in facilitating initial system sensemaking. When personal support is available to users, they can seek one-on-one help and clarification for the problems they encounter in the SRP (Ash et al. 2003). Therefore, access to personal support enables users to have a smooth initial system experience (e.g., overcoming the learning curve and realizing the system benefits). When a user perceives that the impersonal support is highly suitable, user-related workflows also become detailed and relevant to his or her related tasks. This suitability reduces workrelated uncertainties in the SRP and serves as a catalyst for enhancing user performance (Naveh et al. 2005). Therefore, the baseline relationships for our research model can be stated as follows:

<sup>Baseline</sup> <sup>Relationships.</sup> In the SRP, the availability of personal support and the suitability of impersonal support are positively related to the system-related performance of users.

When engaging in a sensemaking process (e.g., through system-induced actions), employees proactively select relevant and favorable references to help them interpret the equivoque (Weick 1995). In the SRP, given that system users are pressured to cope with large amounts of changes, we posit that instead of exerting immense effort by carefully trying all available support structures, users tend to select a support structure that can help them quickly solve a problem encountered in a system-induced action. This argument is in accordance with that of cognitive psychology studies, which hold that human beings are generally cognitive misers and tend to select methods that use a few cognitive resources instead of those with the best accuracy (Payne et al. 1993). In §§3.2 and 3.3, we examine the nature of various system-induced actions in the SRP and the selection of appropriate support structures.

## 3.2. Reactive Behaviors (Standardized and Nonstandardized System Use)

In the SRP, users must quickly use the system to perform their daily tasks (i.e., standardized and nonstandardized system use). However, users can be easily overwhelmed by the significant workflow changes in this phase (Boudreau and Robey 2005), which largely hinder them from realizing projected improvements in their work performance (Lapointe and Rivard 2005). Given that the performance dip is often observed in this phase (Applegate et al. 2009), we posit that the two forms of reactive actions based on the system-induced job requirement—standardized use and nonstandardized use of the system—are unlikely to directly enhance the performance of users. Consequently, a support structure is needed to facilitate the sensemaking process of users through standardized system use and nonstandardized system use. Given the different natures of these two types of system use, we expect that users demonstrate various preferences when selecting impersonal and personal support structures to fulfill their initial system work.

When users engage in a high extent of standardized system use in the SRP, they use system tasks repetitively with standard procedures. Such repeated use of the system by users can facilitate their formation of an impression on encountered problems (Denrell 2005). A clear understanding of problems leads to an insignificant demand for facilitation from others (i.e., users can search for the solutions on their own by following the written document). Compared with personal support, impersonal support can provide timely suggestions and reduce the hassles of personal contact, such as calling the hotline service, describing the problem (Sykes 2015), or interrupting the work of others (Perlow 1999). Therefore, users tend to select impersonal support when they require help while engaging in standardized system use. A suitable impersonal support (i.e., detailed and appropriate) enables users to refer to the document and identify solutions for standardized system use in a timely manner. In doing so, users construct a positive meaning for their work with the system in the SRP. Conversely, low suitability of impersonal support results in an inappropriate sensemaking reference for users on standardized system use. In this case, the problems they identify are not likely to be resolved and may even be intensified, thus undermining their performance.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> In the SRP, the suitability of impersonal support positively moderates the relationship between standardized system use and performance of users.

When engaging in nonstandardized system use, the actual manner by which an individual user performs a system-related task depends on the circumstances. For instance, users can go through different paths to edit a performed task within the system. Therefore, when engaging in a high extent of nonstandardized use in the SRP, users tend to become confused and frustrated with the system-related work. When a situation becomes unmanageable, system users tend to seek additional support through personal contact (Weick 2001). When personal support for the new system is available, users can receive customized services (Sykes 2015) that help them identify and solve problems through conversation. In this manner, the sensemaking process of users through nonstandardized use tends to result in positive outcomes (e.g., performance). By contrast, when personal support is unavailable, the confusion from nonstandardized use is unlikely to be alleviated and undermines the performance of users.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> In the SRP, the availability of personal support positively moderates the relationship between nonstandardized system use and performance of users.

## 3.3. Enactment Behaviors (Task-Technology Adaptation and Individual Adaptation)

People can take action to create (part of) the information cues for sensemaking (Weick 1995). Therefore, the adaptation behaviors of users create enacted cues that can alter their interpretation of the systeminduced work performance. Task-technology adaptation behavior refers to “all behavior directed at changing or modifying an IT and how it will be deployed and used in an organization” (Barki et al. 2007, p. 176). Adaptation behavior can exist in the following forms according to the degree of learning effort: exploitation or utilizing past experience/knowledge to refine and extend existing technologies, and exploration or experimenting on innovations (Gupta et al. 2006). The system users in the SRP who are unfamiliar with the system are unlikely to experiment with systemrelated innovations. Therefore, these users engage in refining and extending the system or related tasks to serve their work preference (Jasperson et al. 2005). When users spend considerable effort to recommend changes to the system or related work tasks, they tend to customize the system/task to the status they deem fit (Barki et al. 2007). Therefore, when users engage in task-technology adaptation behavior, they tend to create positive sensemaking cues, and their systemrelated performance is improved.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3A</sup> <sup>(H3A).</sup> In the SRP, the task-technology adaptation of users is positively related to their performance.

The effect of task-technology adaptation is independent from that of impersonal support. Impersonal support, such as documentation, only comprises standard workflows that have been planned/designed for use with the new system and do not contain information on a potential future system or work enhancements. Therefore, this form of support becomes inapplicable when changes are made to the system or tasks. By contrast, personal support, such as IS specialists, peer users, and peer champions, can provide valuable advice to users that can facilitate their task-technology adaptation. Given that a multitude of change requests occur in the SRP system/task (Applegate et al. 2009), the efforts in adapting the system/task have a relatively high possibility of not being implemented in time or being overridden by other subsequent changes (e.g., system updates). Under such circumstances, communicating with personal support can help users effectively convey their suggestions, which can then be prioritized.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3B</sup> <sup>(H3B).</sup> In the SRP, the availability of personal support positively moderates the relationship between task-technology adaptation and performance of users.

Individual adaptation behavior refers to the “modifications that individuals make to themselves in order to adapt to the IT” (Barki et al. 2007, p. 176). User adaptation can be achieved through two types of learning activities: (1) proactively communicating with others to obtain relevant information and (2) independently exploring the system to increase self-proficiency (Barki et al. 2007). Users enact an equivocal situation by engaging in individual adaptation in the SRP and acquiring system-related information. In this way, users tend to understand the system comprehensively and achieve a positive sensemaking outcome, that is, improved performance (Boudreau and Robey 2005).

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4A</sup> <sup>(H4A).</sup> In the SRP, the individual adaptation of users is positively related to their performance.

Although the preceding arguments suggest a positive relationship between individual adaptation and performance, this relationship tends to be contingent on both the suitability of impersonal support and the availability of personal support. When users engage in individual adaptation in the SRP, both impersonal and personal support tend to serve as references for them to understand a system-induced work.

In the setting in which users spend minimal effort to learn the system in the SRP (i.e., individual adaptation is low), having suitable impersonal support (e.g., detailed and relevant use-related workflow) can help them reduce work-related uncertainties (Naveh et al. 2005) and acquire knowledge needed for the new system. As a result, the system-related performance of these users can be enhanced. Inversely, when users engage in a high level of individual adaptation in the SRP, they exert a tremendous effort to learn the new system, such as making frequent calls to reach IS specialists or spending a significant amount of time to digest documents (Barki et al. 2007). Under such circumstances, the benefits of a suitable impersonal support structure that influences the sensemaking of users become less salient.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4B</sup> <sup>(H4B).</sup> In the SRP, the suitability of impersonal support negatively moderates the relationship between the individual adaptation of users and their performance.

Similar to impersonal support, the availability of personal support also negatively moderates the relationship between individual adaptation and performance. When users communicate with others less proactively (i.e., have a low extent of individual adaptation) (Barki et al. 2007), the high availability of personal support can help them easily obtain one-on-one customized help to identify and solve the problems that they encounter in the SRP (Ash et al. 2003, Sykes 2015). Therefore, their performance in the SRP can be improved. By contrast, when users actively approach others (i.e., have a high extent of individual adaptation), the effectiveness of personal support structure tends to decrease. For instance, when users devote effort in making frequent calls to solve system-related problems (i.e., high individual adaptation), they may still obtain personal support even if the availability of such support is low. Under such circumstances, the moderating role of personal support becomes less salient.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4C</sup> <sup>(H4C).</sup> In the SRP, the availability of personal support negatively moderates the relationship between the individual adaptation and performance of users.

## 3.4. Types of Personal Support Behavior

Recent studies on support structure highlight the advantages of peer support over support from IS specialists because peers possess better work domain knowledge than IS specialists, who instead excel in system knowledge (Sykes 2015, Strong and Volkoff 2010). For instance, in a hospital, the system-related questions raised by nurses in the SRP (e.g., why certain medications are not allowed by the system)

may involve specific healthcare knowledge, and different answers may be obtained from various medical units (Ash et al. 2003). Hence, IS specialists are less likely to provide constructive support than champion users and peers. Therefore, when personal support is expected to moderate the effects of nonstandardized use, task-technology adaptation, and individual adaptation on performance, IS specialist support tends to exert a lower influence than peer-user support and peer-champion support.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> IS specialist support has a lower moderating role than peer-champion support and peeruser support in the effects of nonstandardized system use, task-technology adaptation, and individual adaptation on performance.

## 4. Research Methodology

The focal enterprise system is a closed-loop medication management system (referred to as the medication system hereafter) used in the in-patient setting to coordinate and facilitate medication-related tasks such as physician prescription, pharmacist verification, and nurse medication administration (Patterson et al. 2002). By electronically integrating medication administration records and administration processes, the medication system strengthens the adherence of nurses to the five rights of medication administration (i.e., right patient, right time, right drug, right dosage, and right route). Nurses from a public hospital in Southeast Asia (referred to as the focal hospital hereafter)<sup>4</sup> were selected as subjects in this study for three reasons. First, nurses constitute the majority of the medication system users. Unlike physicians, nurses do not possess a high degree of autonomy with regard to medication system use within the hospital. Therefore, their interaction with the medication system is a more representative reflection of enterprise system use in firms than that of physicians. This sample selection enables us to generalize our findings to other enterprise system contexts in the SRP. Second, the enterprise system implemented in a firm needs to be incorporated into the work process. In the focal hospital, nurses were required to use the medication system to perform their medication administrationrelated tasks. Third, the system should be recently implemented to assess the sensemaking process of users in the SRP. During our first contact, the focal hospital was about to implement the medication system in all of its in-patient units in response to the government initiative of consolidating and sharing medical records electronically throughout the country.

All nurses underwent training before using the system. The hospital operated in dual mode (system + paper) for one week before completely switching to a pure system setting.

Qualitative and quantitative data were collected in this study. First, we collected qualitative data in the focal hospital during the pilot phase of the medication system implementation to obtain a favorable contextual understanding and to aid the hypothesis development. Second, we conducted a two-stage survey shortly after the medication system implementation as the main method to test the research model. Performance indicators were collected one month after the first survey. The qualitative and quantitative data collection processes are described in §§4.1 and 4.2

## 4.1. Qualitative Data Collection

The techniques to collect qualitative data included group meetings, on-site observations, dialogues with IS professionals, acquisition of related documents, and interviews. We participated in group meetings at the beginning of the implementation of the medication system project (i.e., eight months before the final rollout). We attended 18 sessions and recorded detailed minutes of the meetings. Ten on-site observations were conducted in the pilot phase during the implementation of the medication system in two medical wards of the focal hospital. For each observation, we followed one nurse and recorded her detailed actions and schedule. Some examples of the recorded actions included “pushing computer on the wheel and medication trolley inside the cubicle of patients,” “marking the medication as done,” “leaving the medication at the bedside table without serving the patient,” and “teaching another nurse how to edit the information in the medication system.” The average duration for one observation was three hours and the longest observation lasted six hours. Questions and doubts during the observations were clarified only when the observed nurses took short breaks. We conducted five formal interviews with nurses and key personnel in the project (e.g., the project director) to further understand their experience in and opinion on the pilot phase and system implementation. We collected relevant documents, including detailed workflows, before and after (projected) system implementation.

We obtained an in-depth understanding of the study context through these meetings, observations, interviews, and documentation activities. All four types of IS use-related activities (i.e., standardized system use, nonstandardized system use, task-technology adaptation, and individual adaptation) were observed in the pilot phase. Table 2 provides examples of these activities in the context of the medication system. In addition, we were assured of the placement of multiple types of support structures at the focal hospital. For instance, impersonal support was provided in the form of unified standard workflows and was distributed in hard copy to every nursing station and in soft copy across all computers installed with the medication system. Such information provides legitimacy and grounds our understanding on the research model and the development of our survey items.

## 4.2. Quantitative Data Collection

We used a two-stage survey that involves nurses at the focal hospital to validate the hypotheses presented in our research model. The survey method provides a basis to generalize the research findings, enables the results to be replicated, and delivers statistical power. Consistent with prior research, independent and dependent variables were collected at two different times to establish certain causal relationships (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2012). The unit of analysis in our study was the individual nurse at the hospital. We attempted to explain and predict the outcome of the initial sensemaking of nurses in the SRP with regard to the IS use-related activity and support structures. Selecting a single hospital enables us to control for extraneous effects at the organizational level and to effectively detect effects at the micro level. The first data collection was conducted approximately one month after the medication system was implemented throughout the hospital. Data were not collected immediately after the implementation so that users could accumulate experiences for initial sensemaking. The second data collection was conducted one month after the first stage.

We adapted previously validated questions when possible. This strategy facilitated the validity of measures and enabled the results to be compared across studies. When previous suitable measures were unavailable, we developed new questions by reviewing the relevant literature and the context of this study. The validated formative items for tasktechnology and individual adaptations were adapted from the scale of Barki et al. (2007). Although the data captured by Barki et al. (2007) are in the stable stage, they acknowledge the applicability of these measures in the SRP. Prior literature also acknowledges that system users who are unfamiliar with the system adapt the system or related tasks to better serve their work preference in the initial postadoptive stage (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2010, Jasperson et al. 2005).

Technology interaction behavior was measured using different types of system use (Barki et al. 2007). Instead of using tasks for general business systems, such as problem solving, decision justification, and customer service as adopted by Barki et al. (2007), we selected the tasks for standardized/ nonstandardized system use based on the study context. We identified four tasks for standardized use (i.e., mark medication administration task, view medication orders performed by doctors, track status of existing orders, and check new STAT order) and three tasks for nonstandardized use (i.e., suspend medication/nonmedication orders, use intervention notes, and edit performed task) based on the observations on medication administration processes and interviews with nurses during the pilot phase of system implementation.

The suitability of impersonal support was adapted from the measure of the safety rule and regulations suitability proposed by Naveh et al. (2005). The three sources of personal support were identified on the basis of the qualitative data collected in this study. The eight criteria for performance were adapted from prior literature (Davis 1989) and the situation in the current context (e.g., patient safety). Davis (1989) defines perceived usefulness as the extent to which the system assists users to improve their job performance. This well-established measurement captures the different dimensions of work improvement, such as to accomplish work more quickly, to increase productivity, and to enhance effectiveness. Given the comprehensive coverage of this work performance measure, we decided to adapt the measure to capture the outcome of the initial sensemaking of users (i.e., interpretation of the performance in the SRP). The operationalization and sources of all constructs in this study can be found in Online Appendix A (available as supplemental material at http://dx.doi.org/ 10.1287/isre.2015.0578).

Several measures were obtained to validate the survey instrument. First, we consulted several senior researchers to identify and rectify potential problems in the framing and phrasing of questions. Second, we conducted one session of unlabeled and labeled sorting by recruiting eight postgraduate IS students for each session. Minor modifications were made on certain items to address the concerns raised by the “judges.” Third, we sought to ensure face validity by asking the deputy director of nursing and the director of medical informatics to comment on the survey questionnaire and highlight the questions they considered confusing or difficult to answer. Several modifications in the phrasing and framing of the questions were made according to these suggestions.

Our survey package consisted of a cover letter that states the objective of the study and the survey questionnaire. The medication system description was included in the survey questionnaire to improve the validity of responses. Several examples were also provided to help the respondents recall system features when they answered the questionnaire (e.g., editing a performed task such as changing tasks from complete to incomplete and adding comments to the task). Except for those who participated in the pilot phase, all in-patient nurses who used the system to perform daily tasks were invited to participate in the survey. For the first round, the nurses were invited to complete the survey in the hospital auditorium during their break time. Participation was voluntary, and a lunch coupon that could be used in the hospital canteen was provided to the participants for each completed survey as a token of appreciation. The same procedure was used for the second round of the survey. We also adopted a focused survey method for the nurses who did not return for the second round. The survey package was placed inside an envelope and distributed to the nurses who completed the first survey but did not participate in the second one. The nurse’s employee ID was placed on top of each envelope because such placement was less sensitive than indicating the name of the nurse. After completing the survey questionnaire, the nurses were directed to seal and return the envelopes that contained the completed questionnaire. The nurses were assured that all responses would remain confidential. Only the research team from the authors’ institution would have access to the raw data. Moreover, only aggregated statistics would be published and provided as feedback to hospital management.

A total of 359 nurses participated in the first round of the survey, and 332 nurses, including three nurses who did not participate in the first round, participated in the second round. The entries of the 329 nurses who completed both rounds of the survey were recorded. Logistic regression was performed to assess the bias caused by the exclusion of 30 respondents in the second survey because the response was dichotomous and highly skewed (Sax et al. 2003). The dependent variable, RESPONSE\_MISSING, was equal to one if a survey respondent did not return to the second survey and zero otherwise. The independent variables were all of the key research variables (i.e., control variables, independent variables, and moderating variables in the research model). The results of the logistic regression showed that none of the key research variables had a significant effect. Therefore, the second sample was not significantly different from the first sample. Table 3 summarizes the demographic information of the respondents. Among the 329 nurses, 97.6% of the respondents (321) were female. A relatively even distribution was observed in the age categories. The top three age categories in terms of the number of nurses were 20–24 (73 nurses, 22.2%), 25–29 (69 nurses, 21%), and 50 and above (57 nurses, 17.3%).

## 5. Data Analysis

Power analysis was conducted to estimate the required sample size (Franz et al. 2007). Assuming a small effect size $( f ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 5 )$ , 16 predictors (including all independent variables, interaction terms, and control variables) and 0.95 alpha power at 5% significance level, the required sample size for the hierarchical multiple regression analysis (fixed model, R<sup>2</sup> increases) was 262. Thus, our actual data point (329) was deemed adequate for data analysis.

## 5.1. Measurement Model Evaluation

The measurement model of this study was assessed using convergent and discriminant validities for all reflective constructs (i.e., performance, suitability of impersonal support, and system commitment). Convergent validity, which reflects the unidimensionality of constructs, was assessed using item reliability tests, Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability of constructs, and average variance extracted. Item reliability, Cronbach’s alpha, and composite reliability values should be larger than 0.707 to meet the satisfactory convergent validity. Similarly, the average variance extracted should be larger than 0.5 (Fornell and Larcker 1981). Table B.1 in Online Appendix B shows the test results for these three constructs. All scores exceeded the acceptable level, indicating significant convergent validity. Discriminant validity, which reflects the degree of difference of each construct from indicators in other constructs, can be assessed using factor analysis and construct correlation. Factor loadings should be larger than 0.7 to meet the satisfactory discriminant validity. All indicators exceeded the acceptable level and were loaded correctly on their intended construct compared to the other constructs (Table B.1 in Online Appendix B). The second method tested whether the square root of the average variance extracted for a construct was larger than its correlations with the other constructs. The reflective constructs satisfied this criterion. Generally, the results indicated strong evidence of convergent and discriminant validities. Multicollinearity tests were conducted, and the variance inflation factors for all constructs were less than the threshold value of 3.3 (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw 2006). The test of condition index also showed that no construct suffered from multicollinearity problems.

Four formative constructs (i.e., standardized system use, nonstandardized system use, task-technology adaptation, and individual adaptation) were validated using the guidelines of Cenfetelli and Bassellier (2009) and Petter et al. (2007). Table B.2 in Online Appendix B shows the test results for variance inflation factors, weights, and loadings. Weights provide the relative contribution of indicators to assigned constructs, and loadings show the absolute importance of indicators (Cenfetelli and Bassellier 2009). A multicollinearity test was conducted on the formative construct indicators. Table B.2 in Online Appendix B shows that all statistics of variance inflation factors for formative measures were smaller than 3.3. Thus, multicollinearity was not identified in our formative measurement.

We noticed a cooccurrence of negative and positive indicator weights for formative constructs except for individual adaptation. Although this phenomenon may threaten the interpretation of individual formative indicators, the structural effects within the model were less likely to be affected (Cenfetelli and Bassellier 2009). We explored the possible suppressor effect by assessing the bivariate correlation between the suppressor and the indicators, and between the indicators and the constructs (i.e., loadings). We further evaluated the measures and checked for any conceptual overlap among the selected indicators. The removal of an indicator is performed only when conceptual overlap exists (Cenfetelli and Bassellier 2009). Among the indicators with negative weights, Task Tech E (“modification to the system 0 0 0”) was determined to have a conceptual overlap with the first three indicators of these constructs (“improvements to functionalities/interface/hardware”) and a relatively high correlation with other indicators (i.e., the correlation was 0.185, 0.630, 0.601, and 0.752 with the first four indicators). Furthermore, this indicator showed a marginal absolute contribution to the construct (i.e., the loading was 0.157). Thus, the last item on task-technology adaptation was deleted. The other indicators with negative weights did not conceptually overlap with other indicators and were important dimensions to define the focal constructs. For instance, support from IS specialists is commonly recognized as a form of personal support provided by organizations (Sykes 2015). The items for standardized system use and nonstandardized system use also captured the different aspects of medication system-related tasks. Thus, we decided to retain these negative items for the completeness of the construct.

## 5.2. Structural Model Tests

Given that our independent variables are formative, the conventional pair-wise multiplication of indicators, which is used to test moderating effects with reflective constructs, is unsuitable for our study. Instead, we adopted the two-stage approach proposed by Henseler and Fassott (2010) for hypothesis testing. Latent variable scores were obtained using the partial least squares path model in the first stage (see Table 4 for the descriptive statistics and intercorrelations of the latent variable scores). The saved latent variable scores were centered and multiplied to build the interaction terms in the second stage. Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was used in our final hypotheses testing. All statistical tests were assessed at the two-tailed 5% significance level.

Adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ was used to assess the explanatory power of the model. Control variables were entered in step 1, and all centered independent variables were entered in step 2. The interaction terms were entered in step 3. ## 5.3. Endogeneity Test

We collected data for our independent and dependent variables in two waves to establish the direction of causal links between our independent and dependent variables, and to mitigate potential endogeneity issues. The Hausman test was conducted by correlating the error term of the full model with all of the predictors. No significant correlation was detected, suggesting that endogeneity is not a serious concern.

To further investigate the possible feedback loop between performance and IS use-related activity, we conducted the two-stage least squares test to assess the extent of reverse causality of performance on task-technology adaptation given that its main effect on performance was significant. We selected “collectivism” as the instrumental variable because it satisfied the criteria of a suitable instrument that was highly correlated with the endogenous variable $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ task-technology adaptation) but less correlated with the dependent variable $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ performance). Previous studies show that an individual with a high extent of collectivism tends to be more cooperative (Wagner 1995) and less driven and entrepreneurial (Morris et al. 1994). Therefore, such a person may tend to accept the status quo for the sake of the common good and be less likely to proactively engage in task-technology adaptation in the SRP (i.e., challenge the status quo and cater to one’s own needs). Moreover, collectivism, which emphasizes acceptance of the current situation in the work environment (Morris et al. 1994), is unlikely to be related to performance in the SRP. The statistical result confirms that collectivism is an appropriate instrumental variable (eigenvalue F = 8071; p = 00003). The results of the first stage test empirically show that collectivism significantly affected task-technology adaptation (path coefficient = −00250; t = −2095), and the results of the second stage show that task-technology adaptation remained significant (path coefficient = 00691; z = 2032) and that the significance of other results remained consistent.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

Drawing on sensemaking theory, this study attempts to examine how organizational support structures can facilitate different types of IS use-related activities to enhance performance in the SRP. The results show that the interpretation of system-related performance in the SRP is based on the interplay between users’ interaction with the system and support structures provided in the work environment. The suitability of impersonal support and the availability of personal support have differential effects on the effect of system use on performance.

The findings of prior studies in the shakedown phase (Sykes and Venkatesh 2015) and the stable phase of postadoption (e.g., Barki et al. 2007, Hsieh et al. 2011) show that IS use can directly affect the performance of users. However, the present study finds that neither standardized system use nor nonstandardized system use (i.e., conventional IS use behavior) has a significant direct effect on performance in the SRP. This finding is consistent with previous anecdotal observations that the performance dip is likely to occur right after the rollout of an enterprise system (Applegate et al. 2009). Different from users in the SAP or in the stable postadoptive phase in which these users have become comfortable and familiar with the system, users in the SRP have limited knowledge and familiarity with the system. Therefore, their usage of the system is unlikely to have a direct effect on performance.

Consistent with our H2 and H3, as well as Figures 3(a) and 3(b), users engaging in standardized system use and nonstandardized system use in the SRP rely on different support structures as the sensemaking reference. Users tend to have a clear understanding of the focal problem when they frequently engage in standardized system use. Thus, compared with less suitable impersonal support, suitable impersonal support can help these users better solve the problem, thus resulting in better performance (solid line in Figure 3(a)). By contrast, users tend to refer to the inappropriate reference when the suitability of impersonal support is low. Under such circumstances, those engaging in more standardized system use could undermine the performance (dotted line in Figure 3(a)). When users engage in nonstandardized system use at a higher extent, they become more confused with the challenges. Under such circumstances, available personal support could provide customized support, thus helping address the challenges and improve the initial performance (solid line in Figure 3(b)). However, when personal support is not available, the confusion as a result of higher nonstandardized use could undermine the performance of users (dotted line in Figure 3(b)).

Contrary to H3B, the moderating effect of personal support on the relationship between task-technology adaptation and performance is insignificant. A possible explanation could be that task-technology adaptation tends to occur in the form of feedback or workarounds (Goh et al. 2011). Although limited experience is accumulated in the SRP, users typically do not have a problem in framing their feedback on how the system or process should be modified to improve the performance of users. Workarounds tend to be the result of the resistance of users to changes in the new system, and such resistance is unlikely to receive personal support. Another plausible reason is that organizations are more open to feedback and suggestions from users in the SRP because they are still attempting to determine initial problems and issues with the system or work processes. Although personal support is a possible feedback channel, other feedback channels such as through managers (e.g., user or unit managers) are likely to be provided, decreasing the influence of availability of peer support for task-technology adaptation.

Consistent with our H4B, the increasing extent of individual adaptation enhances performance when a user perceives a low extent of impersonal support (dotted line in Figure 3(c)). However, Figure 3(c) demonstrates that this relationship can become negative when the suitability of impersonal support is high (solid line in Figure 3(c)). A plausible explanation could be that the suitable impersonal support (e.g., detailed and relevant use-related workflow) can help them reduce work-related challenges and thus enhance work performance when users spend minimal effort to learn the system in the SRP (i.e., individual adaptation is low). However, users do not only rely on the impersonal support but also use multiple sources to learn the system when they engage in a high level of individual adaptation in the SRP. Given that users are new to the system in SRP, they may make the suboptimal choice when they select the learning sources (e.g., instead of referring to the impersonal support, users may choose to explore the system by themselves). Therefore, the performance may be lower when individual adaptation is low than when individual adaptation is high.

This study shows that support structures in SRP have a lesser effect on adaptation behavior than on conventional IS-use behavior. Specifically, support structures do not moderate the other proposed effects of adaptation behavior on performance, except for the suitability of impersonal support on individual adaptation. However, standardized system use is moderated by the suitability of impersonal support. Nonstandardized system use is moderated by the availability of personal support. The difference in the moderating effects of support structure may be attributed to the fact that in the SRP, support structures are provided to aid users to improve learning about the system to fulfill their job requirements rather than helping them modify the system/workflow.

## 6.1. Theoretical Contributions

This study contributes to the IS postadoptive literature by being the first to conceptualize and empirically examine the SRP. Qualitative evidence and reports of practitioners have unveiled the performance dip that occurs in the first few weeks after the system rollout (Goh et al. 2011, Lapointe and Rivard 2005) and the consequences if the dip is inappropriately managed (Boudreau and Robey 2005, Lapointe and Rivard 2005). However, studies that scrutinize this important early stage of the shakedown phase are scarce. This study further distinguishes two distinct phases (SRP and SAP) in the shakedown period and specifically focuses on the SRP. Our study highlights the unique challenges of users in the SRP and develops a research model to explore the effective means to enhance the performance of users in this phase. Therefore, this study contributes to a more refined and nuanced understanding of the shakedown phase (e.g., Morris and Venkatesh 2010, Sykes et al. 2014) and encourages more focus on the SRP.

Second, by complementing prior research that examined sensemaking in the stable postadoptive stage (Hsieh et al. 2011), this paper explains how sensemaking theory can serve as an overarching lens to understand the early postadoptive phase in the SRP. Specifically, we illustrate how key components of sensemaking theory are instrumental to conceptualize the antecedents, moderators, and dependent variables in our research model. We find that users understand the SRP through retrospective actions such as reactive behavior and enactment behavior. Collecting performance data after IS use-related activity as part of our research design is also consistent with the perspective that the sensemaking outcome is developed based on retrospective actions. Furthermore, our study highlights the importance of support structures to aid the sensemaking process of users in the SRP because sensemaking in the SRP tends to be recondite with a steep learning curve and uncertainties. Another study on sensemaking in the stable postadoptive stage found the role of feedback to be salient (Hsieh et al. 2011). Therefore, the need to refine certain sensemaking constructs and relationships as the boundary condition changes, such as the salience of certain sensemaking components over others in different phases of IS postadoption, is vital.

Third, this study contributes to the literature on IS use. IS use is recognized as a decisive construct of system success in the literature (Barki et al. 2007, DeLone and McLean 2003). Despite considerable attention, prior literature that examined the effect of IS use on employee performance presented mixed findings (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006). The findings of this study contribute to this theoretical debate (DeLone and McLean 2003) by identifying the role of IS use to stimulate the initial gains in the SRP. Specifically, consistent with the context-specific theorizing approach recently advocated in IS research (Hong et al. 2014), this study identifies four types of IS use-related activities and theorizes their effects on performance in the context of the SRP. The insignificant direct effect of conventional IS use constructs (i.e., standardized use and nonstandardized use) on performance in our study demonstrates that IS use in the SRP can exhibit a contextual distinguishing effect compared to prior research in the shakedown (Sykes and Venkatesh 2015) and the stable phases of postadoption (e.g., Barki et al. 2007, Hsieh et al. 2011).

Fourth, this study adds to a more nuanced understanding of support structures and IS use by depicting how support structures play differentiated roles to influence the effects of IS use-related activity on performance. Prior research on the role of support structure in the shakedown phase found that peer support results in more improved employee outcomes than traditional support structures (Sykes et al. 2014, Sykes 2015). Our findings suggest that impersonal support as a form of traditional support structure in SRP can also play an important role in standardized system use and individual adaptation. The interactions between IS use-related activity and support structures also provide supporting evidence for the important yet complex role of IS use to stimulate the initial gains in the SRP. Such findings reconfirm the need to conceptualize IS use as a multidimensional construct (Benbasat and Barki 2007).

## 6.2. Limitations and Future Research

Notwithstanding our contributions, several limitations of our study should be noted. First, our findings are inevitably influenced by the focal context of the context-specific theorizing approach. For instance, formative measures for standardized system use and nonstandardized system use were developed based on the medication system. Different dimensions of personal and impersonal support structures were selected on the basis of the difference between these two support structures in the SRP. Therefore, we encourage future research to explore these constructs and measures using different types of enterprise systems, industries, and postadoptive phases. Moreover, the salient sensemaking components and relationships could change as boundary conditions change (e.g., SRP versus stable postadoptive phase). For instance, feedback can be important in the latter postadoptive stages (Hsieh et al. 2011) but not in the SRP. Support structures could be more important in the SRP. Future research could test this conjecture and apply the sensemaking lens to different stages of IS postadoption.

Second, this study used sensemaking theory as the theoretical lens to understand the SRP and select the core constructs (e.g., perceptual measure of performance was selected to assess how users make sense of the system-induced work performance). Considering the limited research and importance of the SRP, we encourage further studies to explore other potential theories and select different measurements in this phase (e.g., archival data for rating from managers as a measure of actual performance).

Third, despite our attempt to mitigate endogeneity issues through a two-wave data collection approach, reverse causality between IS use-related activity and performance may not be fully eliminated. We conducted the Hausman test and showed statistically that reverse causality might not be a serious concern in our study. We also showed statistically and conceptually why performance would not have a reverse causal effect on task-technology adaptation. We recognize that the feedback loop from performance to other

IS use-related activity (e.g., IS use) has been acknowledged in the literature on IS success (DeLone and McLean 2003). However, IS use in our study (i.e., standardized use and nonstandardized use) is a reactive action induced by the medication system in the work setting. The extent of these two behaviors is mainly based on the work requirement and is less likely to be influenced by performance. Therefore, the feedback loop from performance is less likely to be a serious concern. Nonetheless, future research could employ other methods, such as a field experiment, to further verify the causality proposed in our model.

## 6.3. Implications on Practice

This study has important implications on practice. First, support structures play a significant role in influencing performance achieved through IS use in the SRP. Management should be aware that mandating the use of a system alone does not guarantee success (with the exception of task-technology adaptation behavior). Instead, more attention should be given to the provision of personal or impersonal system support and ensure the accessibility of personal support and suitability of impersonal support for the workflow of users. Given the negative moderating effect of impersonal support on the impact of individual adaptation on performance, caution is necessary to avoid excessive individual adaptation in the SRP or provide more guidance beyond the impersonal support for this group of users.

Second, peer-champion support and peer-user support have the more significant influence than other forms of personal support. Therefore, management should encourage a collaborative environment to facilitate peer-user support. More emphasis should also be given to training nursing champions and involving them throughout the initial stages of the IS implementation. Unlike IS specialists, the major responsibility of champions tends to be professional work (e.g., patient care) rather than facilitating system acceptance. Management can create a better environment for champions to perform their role, such as decreasing their workload or motivating them through salary bonuses.

Third, given that personal support is helpful for nonstandardized tasks and impersonal support can facilitate the sensemaking for standardized tasks in the SRP, more effort is required to analyze systemrelated tasks to separate standardized and nonstandardized tasks. Moreover, supporting personnel should be trained to help new users solve problems related to less standardized tasks.

Fourth, from a policy standpoint, this study explores whether associating an incentive scheme with system use is sufficient to ensure favorable performance in the SRP. The significance of IS use as an integral part of system success is endorsed by healthcare policy makers. The meaningful use of healthcare IS has been touted to improve the quality of healthcare services in the United States. Therefore, the use of healthcare IS has been established by the U.S. government as the primary criterion for hospitals to receive government incentives (Pazinski 2009). Given that use has a minimal direct effect on performance, the results of our study can prompt policy makers to consider extending the scope of the incentive scheme in the SRP to include system structures, such as recognizing hospitals with detailed and suitable use-related procedures.

This study presents a nuanced theoretical understanding on how a user within an organization can make sense of a new enterprise system in the SRP. We revealed the discriminating alignment between IS use-related activity and support structures to improve performance using sensemaking theory. Organizational management and policy makers can create specific strategies based on our findings to manage the performance dip in the SRP.
