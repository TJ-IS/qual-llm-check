## A B S T R A C T

Grounded in search theory, and set in the context of the U.S. lodging industry, our study focused on consumer power and two of its facets: the relationship between buyers and sellers, and the role of traditional intermediaries. We sought to contribute to the search literature and its explanation of the role of IT progress on consumer power by investigating the relationship between prices and consumer search activity, the ability of consumers to bypass traditional intermediaries, and the conditions under which suppliers can respond to the apparent increase in consumer power. We used a dataset of 31,644 observations representing daily availability and reservation transactions for 79 lodging brands collected over a nine-month period. Our results provided both support for the general tenets of search theory and some interesting extensions, indicating that there was a negative, non-linear relationship between aggregate consumer search activity and product price. We found that consumers, although less efficient, search more than traditional intermediaries and, as a consequence, find lower prices. Finally, we found support for the proposition that product differentiability moderates the relationship between aggregate search activity and product price.

## 1. Introduction

The Internet and the Web, have changed the way that business is conducted. Though e-commerce facilitates comparison shopping and tends to reduce price, the major benefit is to business firms, which now have access to more powerful IT than ever before. Businesses can differentiate their offers and impede competition by making it more difficult for buyers to assess tradeoffs between product price and value. Information is used to augment or customize products and services enabling CRM and Business Intelligence (BI) strategies.

Consumer power has been characterized in two ways. First, between customers and suppliers, power results from buyers ability to improve their negotiating position and reduce prices. Second, consumer power allows buyers the ability to specify their demand and identify goods that best fit their requirements without having to rely on intermediaries whose interests may only be partially congruent with theirs [11].

Literature to date has analyzed price dispersion [5], price differentials between online and offline stores [1], and the role of switching costs in electronic markets [3]. Following this tradition, our work focused on the effects of IT advances on the balance of power between businesses and their customers, with a goal of furthering theoretical understanding and enabling us to propose guidelines for managers. Using archival data in the context of the lodging industry, we directly tested whether decreasing search costs, and the ensuing increased search activity, have had a tangible impact on prices. We also attempted to determine whether managers in different product segments were responding differently to technological progress.

Much of the prior research in this area has been in the form of mathematical model development and experiments. However, as Brynjolfsson and Smith said: ‘‘even the best theorizing [in this area] will need to be based on empirical observations’’. We are aware of no research that has measured and directly linked the amount of aggregate consumer search activity on the Internet and on traditional channels using actual consumer data and we hoped to fill this gap.

## 2. Theoretical framework

Search theory assumes that both buyers and sellers in a market face uncertainty about price and that to reduce that uncertainty they engage in a costly seeking and vetting of available options. Individuals who engage in this search behavior constantly weigh their preferences and reservation price against the incremental cost of continuing the process. Prices in the market are not independent as intermediaries are generally aware of competitors’ prices.

## 2.1. Decreasing marginal returns

Search theory assumes that a rational buyer will continue to search until the expected benefits of further search is less than the incremental cost of evaluating another option—that is, when their marginal return to search is zero or negative. While the shape of the relationship between the aggregate amount of search and price decline has not been empirically tested, there is evidence that as buyers gather information, the expected benefit of seeking new information decreases (they experience diminishing returns). Price dispersion creates the buyer expectation that further search will allow them to discover a sought good at lower price. Yet, customers can often quickly assess the range of prices available for products of interest. While a number of factors are clearly at play, early searches are likely to yield the most significant gains, because as buyers search for more options the probability of their finding better deals decreases. Thus, we should witness a non-linear negative relationship between aggregate search effort and product prices.

We considered two models representing a negative non-linear relationship. First:

$$
\frac {d P}{d S} = b + a S\tag{1}
$$

where P is product price, S is aggregate search, and a and b are constants such that $a > 0 , b < 0$ and jbj - jaj. This model implies that the rate of change in price decreases linearly as the amount of aggregated search increases. So for each additional unit of increase in aggregate search, the rate of decline in prices changes by a constant amount a. Solving this differential equation yields a quadratic curve:

$$
P = c + b S + a S ^ {2}\tag{2}
$$

where c is a constant representing product price when there is no search. The caveat to this solution is that price declines in nonlinear fashion until aggregate search reaches the minimum of the quadratic, at $S = - \left( b / 2 a \right)$ , and as aggregate search goes beyond this level, prices would actually increase.

The second model hypothesizes, instead of a constant rate of decline in price, a declining rate of decline as aggregate search increases:

$$
\frac {d P}{d S} = a e ^ {b S}\tag{3}
$$

where a and b are constants with $a < 0$ and $b < 0 .$ . Solving this differential equation yields:

$$
P = c + d e ^ {b s}\tag{4}
$$

where $d = a b > 0$ and c is the constant of integration. This is an exponentially declining curve which asymptotes at c, the minimum price that sellers charge for their product. This model appears to have the advantage of never having price increase as aggregate search increases. Comparison of the performance of the two models was made by using the information theoretic $\mathsf { A I C } _ { \mathrm { c } }$ criterion.

The effect of decreasing marginal returns when searching for lower price has not been empirically tested, though the consumer behavior literature has suggested that consumers may benefit little from investing much time searching [10]. Diminishing return to search also helps explain the counterintuitive notion that depth of search behavior is best described by a declining asymptotic function, even in the face of low search costs [6].

Proposition 1. The negative relationship between aggregate search activity (i.e., the total amount of search for a given product) and product price is non-linear.

## 2.2. Search, price, and the role of IT

In commodity markets, where price is the only variable differentiating the products, search theory posits an inverse relationship between search costs and both the amount of buyers’ search time and sellers’ prices. When faced with high search costs, buyers accept higher prices because they to incur significant expense in seeking and evaluating incremental offers. As search costs diminish, buyers face lower barriers to increased searching and thus can evaluate more suppliers.

IT reduces search costs, leading to an increase in both the aggregate amount of search and individual search efficiency [13]. It follows that in electronic markets for homogeneous products, consumers are more likely to identify and avoid higher prices for the same products, increasing competition and ultimately leading to lower prices [4].

In diversified markets, where goods are not homogeneous, price details are not the only type of information needed for consumer decision making. Buyers must also evaluate the characteristics and quality of available goods in order to identify those that best fit their needs [7]. In diversified markets a decrease in search cost does not necessarily result in a decrease in price; the ultimate effect of technology-induced, lower search costs on price depends on the relative effect of increase in price and product search cost [8]. As search costs fall, both buyers and sellers benefit due to the emergence of new markets. However, if search costs decrease significantly, buyers can exhaustively survey the marketplace and find products that best fit their needs and thereby increase competition and cause sellers to reduce prices.

However firms also have access to IT and can respond by making their products harder to compare, though a more viable long-term strategy may be to leverage the power of IT to reduce the costs in order to better tailor products to individual needs or to price discriminate using individual- or segment-level information. It is difficult to predict the outcome of this technological $\ " \mathrm { b a t t l e } "$ between buyers and sellers, and little empirical research exists on the topic. Yet, economic theory suggests that the products characterized by a higher degree of differentiability are more amenable to IT-driven differentiation and price discrimination, allowing the firms to reduce price pressure and hold or even increase prices. It follows that in more differentiated markets we should witness a reduction in consumer power induced by the declining search costs introduced by new technologies. Although this proposition has yet to be tested empirically, early modeling by Kuksov [15] showed that when product design is treated as an endogenous variable within the control of the firm, industry profits may increase, not decline, in the face of lower search cost and increasing consumer search activity.

Proposition 2. Higher levels of aggregate consumer search are associated with significantly lower product prices.

Proposition 3. The negative relationship between higher levels of aggregate consumer search and product prices becomes less pronounced as the differentiability of goods increases.

In summary, the literature has proven very useful in interpret ing the impact of IT on the balance of power in buyer–seller relationships. However, significant gaps remain with respect to the shape of the relationship between prices and search activity, the role of intermediaries, and the potential for firms to respond to the apparent increase in consumer power.

## 3. Research setting and measures

The context for our investigation was the lodging industry, which provided some important advantages for our study: reservations are digital products, thus the process of purchasing is not procedurally different when performed through online versus traditional channels. Specifically, the process of making a hotel reservation is that an availability request is issued, in which the traveler seeks to identify suitable hotels with suitable vacant room type and amenities (e.g., exercise room and pool) for the expected dates of occupancy, The outcome is a list of properties with their room rates; from this list the traveler makes a selection and books a specific room at a given rate.

## 3.1. Aggregate search activity

The standardized measure of aggregate search activity we used was the Look-to-Book (LtB) ratio. They are obtained by dividing availability requests by the actual reservations received in a unit of time. These may be computed at various levels, such as the chain, brand, or individual property, for any period of time, such as daily, weekly, monthly, and for different channels of distribution or specific reservation source. For example, a LtB ratio of 300 for Brand A on January 1st 2003 indicates that a total of 300 availability requests per booked reservation were issued for Brand A hotels on that day. The higher the LtB ratio the more a given product has been scrutinized prior to a purchase. Thus LtB ratios represent a direct measure of the amount of aggregate search for a product; it does not measure an individual consumer search effort but measures the search activity for a given product by all customers in a given time interval.

Use of actual shopping data mitigates some of the limitations of research using consumer self-reporting of search behavior, or inferences based on the growth of general Internet use. This is particularly important in travel services, where different web sites may have dramatically different degrees of shopping. Using actual LtBratiosexperiencedby eachsupplier(i.e., thehotel brand)provides a precise measure of the quantity of aggregate search activity that occurred for each purchase. Also, because LtB ratios are measures of actual consumer search, they mitigate some of the limitations to generalizability associated with experimental approaches.

## 3.2. Price

A brand is a segment-specific lodging product that offers a standardized array of services and amenities across its affiliated properties. Large hotel operators (e.g., Marriott, Hilton) manage a number of brands in their portfolios in an effort to segment their offerings and cater to a wide array of customers. Room-nights, the lodging product, are sold using daily rates. On any given day, a brands’ Average Daily Rate (ADR) can be computed as the mean of all of the room rates reserved by travelers booking with the chain on that day. ADR is particularly well suited to our study because it represents an average of what the consumers paid for the product without confounding effects such as shipping costs, taxes, or other fees.

## 3.3. Distribution channels

Traditionally, hotel inventory has been sold either directly, to walk-in guests or those phoning the hotel, or through an intermediary, such as a travel agent, typically using a Global Distribution System (GDS). The GDS originated as an inventory management and distribution system for airlines, but morphed into an electronic distribution channel for other travel products when hotel chains, car rental companies, cruise lines, and other suppliers built interfaces connecting their reservation systems to it. When we conducted our study, there were four major GDS (Amadeus, SABRE, Galilelo, and Worldspan) with different geographic coverage and market reach. GDSs are typically older systems that rely on character-based user interfaces and require knowledge of application specific codes. Thus they cannot offer many ofthe toolsthatInternet intermediariesenjoy (such as graphical interfaces andmatrix displays), butthey offer a fast and efficient search and booking tool for trained travel agents as well as providing useful administrative tools.

With the advent of the Internet, prospective travelers could performonline availability requests and book their own reservations directly through the suppliers’ web site, an online travel agency, or a collection of other intermediaries such as wholesalers and portals.

Although many travel agents have access to the Web and could purchase from supplier sites and online intermediaries, the GDS channel offers a number of advantages (e.g., the ability to combine different travel products for one passenger, automatic commission accounting, consolidated commission tracking and processing and payment, and volume accounting) that do not exist when a travel agent uses the consumer channel.

## 3.4. Product segmentation

The lodging product is highly segmented, with the low end (economy class) characterized by fairly standardized, near-commodity products, and the upper echelon (luxury) characterized by unique, tailored properties and services. As the number of amenities and the quality of the services increase, so does the room rate, the brand’s rating, and the firm’s ability to differentiate its offerings.

## 4. Hypotheses development

As aggregate search activity increases, lower prices for a product are likely to be discovered in the market. However, because of diminishing returns, the longer the search process continues the less likely a buyer is to find a lower price. We should therefore witness a negative relationship between LtB ratio and ADR that is a non-linear declining function.

Hotel operations are characterized by high fixed costs and a perishable product. As a consequence, lodging firms have become very adept in the use of revenue management techniques, and seek to optimally price their product by demand analysis and forecasting. This ability to revenue manage is particularly important around high-demand travel dates (e.g., New Year’s Eve). For these, operators know that demand for the limited supply of room-nights available will guarantee high occupancy rates. Consistent with search theory, it is around high-demand dates that customers engage in extensive search activity. As rates stay high, customers seek to find rooms and deals providing significant savings.

Thus, around high-demand dates, widespread shopping and high ADR are likely to coexist. This idiosyncrasy of the lodging market, however, suggests that a strictly decreasing monotonic function may not be the most appropriate representation of this trend. This suggests the use of a linear model with a quadratic component that declines at a declining rate over the first part of the data range but allows for a rise in its later portion. If this is not true, then the exponentially declining model should provide a superior fit to the data. Thus we hypothesized:

Hypothesis 1. There is a non-linear, negative relationship between LtB ratio and ADR that exhibits a significant quadratic term.

Agency theory holds that for information asymmetry, uncertainty, and risk, there is a potential mis-alignment between the interests of the agent (travel agents) and those of the principal (the traveler). Though acting on behalf of travelers, travel agents are paid a commission from the supplier proportional to the total price of the trip booked. Prior to the advent of the Internet, travel agents represented the channel of distribution with the lowest search cost for consumers; they could investigate multiple products at the same time, using the GDS and offer several options to the consumer. Consumers dissatisfied with the options provided to them had to find a different travel agent and repeat the search. Work on the incentive system of commissioned agents has shown that their income-maximizing strategy is sometimes at odds with their principals’ best interests. Travel agents incur costs when searching for accommodations and, since their commission is a percentage of the booked room rate, they reduce their revenue when they identify lower prices. Thus, the profit maximizing strategy of commissioned agents consists of offering enough options so that consumers can make a decision, and then close the sale rapidly.

Conversely, when searching online, consumers can visit multiple intermediaries’ (e.g., Orbitz) and suppliers’ web sites (e.g., Hilton.com) at a fraction of the search cost they face when visiting brick-and-mortar travel agencies. Moreover, the value proposition of online intermediaries is their ability to pool substantial product information in an ‘‘easy-to-consume’’ format, thus contributing to a further increase in the aggregate amount of search (see Fig. 1 for an example).

When prospective travelers search and book independently they retain the savings they are able to generate through increased search. As a result, we can expect that the aggregate amount of search activity produced through the Internet will be significantly higher than the amount produced through a traditional travel agent. We hypothesized:

Hypothesis 2. LtB ratios are significantly higher for the Internet channel than the traditional channel of distribution (GDS).

Hypothesis 3. ADR are significantly higher for the traditional channels of distribution (GDS) than the Internet channel.

Although consumers have access to more information than ever before, firms can respond by collecting information about customers in an effort to better differentiate their products. This is a common occurrence in the lodging industry where brands strive to create special packages, track customers, and customize experiences through the implementation of strategic initiatives [9] such as CRM or BI. But these approaches may hold different potential for different sellers, depending on the nature of their product. In the lodging industry, brands are clearly segmented along a continuum of services and amenities from the economy segment (a near-commodity) to the luxury segment (with unique properties). For example, budget hotels provide lodging essentials—a clean room with a bed, television, and bathroom—but few amenities, while luxury hotels have unique premium locations, ample and diverse amenities, and superior service.

We therefore posit that the lodging segment moderates the relationship between LtB ratio and ADR. Specifically, the negative relationship between search and price is weaker for highly differentiated products than for more homogeneous ones.

Hypothesis 4. The degree of differentiability of the product moderates the relationship between LtB ratio and ADR, with the relationship becoming weaker as differentiability increases.

## 5. Methodology

Our study used archival data consisting of a dataset of daily Lookto-Book (LtB) ratios and Average Daily Rates (ADRs), by distribution channel, for 79 hotel brands in the United States. The time frame of this dataset was January 1st 2003 to September 22nd 2003. We single-sourced our dataset from a live database that captures both reservations made on the Internet and those made through travel agencies, ensuring that records from different channels were directly comparable—allowing us to capture actual aggregate consumer behavior. For each brand in the dataset, and by channel, our data provider computed daily LtB ratios by dividing the number of availability requests for all the properties in the brand on that day by thenumberofbookedreservations.Foreachbrand,thedailyADRwas computed by averaging the daily rate associated with each reservation.

We used multivariate analysis, using the General Linear Model, to test our hypotheses: a linear contrast for hypotheses two and three, and multiple regression with linear and quadratic terms to test hypotheses one and four. In all cases we controlled for differences between brands, months, and brand  month interactions before computing the contrasts and regressions. We decided to control for these effects since brands, months, and brand– month combinations were not equally represented across channels or within segments of the industry. This also reduced residual variation, thereby improving the power of our tests. Controlling for the brand  time interaction reduced the effect of brand-specific systematic events occurring at a specific time (e.g., consumers shop more heavily for luxury brands during a specific period of time, or different brands run promotions at different times).

## 6. Results

The first step in our analysis was a purification stage. Both LtB ratios and ADR must, by definition, be positive and within a reasonable range of values; outliers were removed as follows: a data point was discarded if,

(a) its ADR was more than four interquartile ranges above the third quartile $( A D R > 4 1 6 ;$ resulting in the removal of 101 data points, or 0.31% of the original dataset), or

(b) its LtB ratio was more than four interquartile ranges above its third quartile (above 2260; resulting in the removal of 665 data points, or 2.06% of the original dataset). ## 6.2. Hypothesis 2

We hypothesized that the Internet channel would yield a significantly higher degree of customer shopping behavior, as captured in the LtB ratios. This hypothesis was supported by a linear contrast comparing the two channels $( p < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ after controlling for time, brand, and brand  time interactions. Specifically, we found that the Internet channel fosters a degree of shopping that is several hundred units higher than the traditional GDS channel (adjusted means: GDS: 116.60; Internet: 622.34).

## 6.3. Hypothesis 3

We hypothesized that the Internet channel would yield a significantly lower price, as captured by ADR; this was supported by a linear contrast comparing the two channels $( p < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ after controlling for time, brand, and brand  time interactions. Specifically, we found that lodging brands saw a significantly lower ADR for reservations sold on the Internet channel than through the traditional GDS channel (adjusted means: GDS: \$133.57; Internet: \$119.54).

To evaluate segment differences, we computed the average slope of the curve for each segment by integrating its derivative across the range of LtB ratios; however, we constrained the LtB ratio ranges to those exhibiting negative derivative. This was done to control for the effect of abnormal shopping dates. Thus, MinLtB represented the lower limit in the LtB ratio range for each channel, while MaxLtB represented the value where the relationship founds its global minimum for each channel. This calculation produced an overall index (I<sub>S</sub>) for each segment (S), based on the segment’s relationship between LtB ratio and ADR. This index allowed us to make a cross-segment comparison of the effects of search activity on price by segment. The average value of each slope was obtained using the formula:

$$
I _ {S} = \frac {1}{\text { MaxLtB } - \text { MinLtB }} \int_ {\text { MinLtB }} ^ {\text { MaxLtB }} (2 a R + b) d R\tag{5}
$$

where R represents the LtB ratio, and a and b are the coefficients of the quadratic and linear terms, respectively.

The I index represents the average rate of change in ADR per unit of LtB ratio across the range of LtB ratio values (i.e., the marginal savings for each incremental availability request, averaged across the indicated range of LtB ratios). The average slopes by segment (see Table 4) suggested that overall consumer search behavior had a different effect on room rates in each segment. More specifically, the results showed general consistency with our proposition that room rates for more differentiable products were less responsive to increased shopping. Mid-scale (Q3) and limited service products (Q2) showed the highest degree of responsiveness to shopping behavior, while the economy segment (Q1) displayed, unexpectedly, the lowest average slope.

## 7. Discussion

Grounded in the tenets of search theory, we focused on the effects of IT advances on the balance of power between firms and their customers in order to explain the impact of IT on consumer power by investigating the shape of the relationship between prices and aggregate consumer search activity, the ability of consumers to bypass traditional intermediaries, and the conditions under which suppliers could respond to the apparent increase in consumer power.

## 7.1. Decreasing marginal returns to search

While the exact form of the relationship between price and aggregate consumer search activity had not previously been evaluated, search theory suggested that consumers face decreasing marginal returns when searching. We therefore hypothesized the existence of a negative, non-linear, relationship between Look-to-Book (LtB) ratio and Average Daily Rates (ADRs), to allow for the idiosyncrasies of the lodging industry, where high-demand dates engender the coexistence of high search and high prices. Our data confirmed both this negative and non-linear relationship.

From a theoretical standpoint, this result is important. First, it empirically confirms prior research results by using a dataset of actual aggregate consumer transactions to identify the relationship between aggregate search activity and price. Second, it provided empirical support for the non-linearity of the relationship, providing a test of the hypothesis that consumers experience diminishing returns in their search activity.

It is interesting to note that, while the general quadratic relationship between LtB ratio and ADR held for both the traditional electronic distribution channel (e.g., travel agents) and the Internet channel, only in the consumer channel (i.e., the Internet) did we witness a change in sign in the derivative (i.e., a price minimum after which an increase in aggregate search activity resulted in higher prices); however though this result was statistically significant, the magnitude of the quadratic term was relatively small. In the traditional channel the relationship is monotonic. This difference is consistent with expectations grounded in agency theory. Travel agents have access to better information than the average consumer and realize the futility of searching extensively on high-demand dates. Also, their commission-based incentive structure is such that agents earn more by limiting the time they spend searching for the lowest price.

## 7.2. The role of traditional intermediaries

Empowered by the ability to directly access a distribution channel with low search costs, consumers engage in significantly higher levels of aggregate search activity and prices online are likely to be significantly lower than those available through the traditional channel.

Our data supports both of these expectations and confirms that the advent of the Internet has granted consumers increased power with respect to the traditional channel intermediaries. However, our results suggest that it may be impossible for sellers to avoid some loss of market power and also that traditional intermediaries will be increasingly threatened by electronic channels that allow consumers low cost ways to find and purchase goods.

Our results have immediate implications for practice: those intermediaries who have historically based their value proposition on their preferential access to information must rethink their strategies. Thus travel agents must, develop an expertise and a focus on complex products (such as packages and cruises) that enable the firm to offer consulting services. Increasing the level of service and personalization offered and using customer relationship management strategies provide another avenue for future viability.

Our data showed that, in the aggregate, online consumers are less efficient than professionals. Specifically, we found that search efforts by travel professionals yielded, on average, twice the return of those of consumers in terms of the detection of lower prices. This result is intuitively appealing, as most online travelers are relatively unsophisticated and have limited knowledge, skills, and training in online search. The lodging product was shown to be well suited to the characteristics of the Internet because it required significant information search on the part of customers for a geographically dispersed product. When buyers are ready to purchase, they place a reservation for a hotel stay to be enjoyed in the future. Thus, the Internet provides customers with an experience that has few, if any, drawbacks compared to the traditional acquisition process.

## 7.3. Possible reactions to increasing consumer power

Empowered by ease of use and readily accessible advanced IT, consumers are now able to evaluate and purchase lodging products. The relationship between aggregate consumer search activity and prices is negative, and consumers search more and find lower prices than those identified by travel agents. However, we empirically verified that those firms that commercialize more differentiable products have been less impacted by declining consumer search costs.

There were systematic differences between industry segments in the average slope of the relationship between aggregate consumer search activity and prices. Our expectation that the more homogeneous segment would exhibit a high degree of sensitivity to shopping while the upper segments, characterized by more differentiability, would be less sensitive to consumer search behavior was generally supported.

The most puzzling result was produced by the economy segment (Q1 $I _ { S } = - 0 . 0 0 2 3 )$ , which exhibits the lowest degree of sensitivity to shopping. This counterintuitive result may be due to the limited degree of pricing freedom of operators in this segment: because of the homogeneous nature of a simple product, operators in the economy segment are unable to change prices beyond a minimal range, irrespective of the amount of shopping they observe.

We tested this by computing an index of within-segment price dispersion measuring the standard deviation of rates (ADR) charged by each brand and then averaged these brand scores within their respective segments (Mean Std. A comparison of within-segment price dispersion indices supports the notion that prices in the economy segment of the lodging industry fall in a very tight band. The mean standard deviation of ADR in this segment (\$9.17) is about half of that in the next segment (limited service: \$17.16). Thus firms can counter growing consumer power when they have room to differentiate their product in the marketplace. Recent work in the modeling tradition shows how in differentiated markets, despite increasing price pressure, aggregate search activity enabled by technology also had a market-expansion effect that may lead to higher prices, broader product differentiation and improved profits [2]. As our empirical results indicate, service industries are an example where this dynamic can occur, but in almost any industry IS affords firms the ability to differentiate their offers through personalization and customization.

There is evidence that bundling is emerging as a viable strategy to counter increasing consumer power. The travel industry offers an early example: dynamic packaging, a bundle of travel products (e.g., air transport, lodging, car rental, entertainment) that are assembled and priced together in real time based on customer preferences, availability and inventory of the component products, seasonality, etc. Dynamic packaging is thought to hold promise in decreasing the effectiveness of buyer shopping behavior, even for near-commodity products (e.g., economy lodging).

While most large lodging operators have global or multinational operations, consumers and online consumer behavior exhibit cultural differences, which can be significant. For example, recent research uncovered systematic differences between customers from collectivist cultures (i.e., Hong Kong) and from individualist cultures (i.e., Australia) with respect to trust of online portals and peer group online recommendations [12]. Others point to uncertainty avoidance and long-term orientation as moderators of the intention to engage in e-commerce transactions [14].

## 8. Limitations

We believe that the use of an archival methodology and a dataset of actual transactions represent the strength of our study, but the measures we used do have some drawbacks. LtB ratio represents an aggregate measure of search activity, but they suffer from some inflation, since the manner in which a search is parameterized determines the number of products that are investigated. Often, in order to save time during the search process, buyers do not spend time to parameterize their search, relying instead on the display to identify the product of interest quickly. However, such requests are used to compute LtB ratios for the brands on that day, which creates more noise (i.e., inflated LtB ratios) which will tend to dilute the relationship between aggregate search activity and prices—a limitation that makes for a more restrictive test of our hypotheses.

AnotherlimitationisduetothehighlyaggregatednatureofADRin our dataset. We were able to obtain average prices for the properties in the brands, but could not identify individual rates charged by the properties within the brands. However, we believe that the high degree of aggregation of our measure is a strength, because aggregating results across all properties within a brand helps to reduce the effect of individual-level initiatives or location-specific effects. Nonetheless, this reduction of local effect comes at a cost. The measure becomes more diluted and measurement errors increase.

## 9. Conclusions

Our principal objective was to contribute to search theory and to our understanding of the impact of emerging electronic markets. We sought to offer an empirical test of theory predictions using an archival methodology and a dataset of actual transactions set in the context of the U.S. lodging industry. We also sought to extend our analysis across a range of products characterized by different degrees of differentiability in order to understand the strategic implications of the growing availability of powerful computing devices to both firms and consumers.
