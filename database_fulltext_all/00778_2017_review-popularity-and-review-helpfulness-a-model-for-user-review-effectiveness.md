# Review popularity and review helpfulness: A model for user review effectiveness

Jianan Wu

E. J. Ourso College of Business, Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, LA 70803, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 8 July 2016 Received in revised form 14 March 2017 Accepted 17 March 2017 Available online xxxx

Keywords: Online user reviews Review popularity Review helpfulness Review effectiveness

## a b s t r a c t

The wide adoption and perceived helpfulness of online user reviews on consumers' decision making have energized academic research on the assessment of review effectiveness. Although the literature probed the impacts of user reviews on various elements of review effectiveness independently, little research has done to examine them jointly. Inspired by communication theories, we conceptualize a framework for user review effectiveness in which we focus on the joint assessment of its first two elements: Review Popularity and Review Helpfulness. We develop our hypotheses regarding the effects of the user review determinants on both Review Popularity and Review Helpfulness, and further develop an operational model to empirically test our hypotheses using data collected from Amazon. Our study suggests that disentangling Review Popularity and Review Helpfulness in assessing review effectiveness is not only conceptually sounding, but also managerially beneficial. We find that Review Popularity is as important as Review Helpfulness in review effectiveness evaluations. Review determinants may play opposite roles on Review Popularity and Review Helpfulness (e.g., valence), and can drive review effectiveness via Review Popularity or Review Helpfulness or both, These findings offer new insights for various decision makers to harvest user review effectiveness in online markets.

## 1. Introduction

As a powerful marketing communication mix for online markets with important impact on product sales (e.g., [1,8,9,16]) and consumers' willingness-to-pay (e.g., [29,30]), online user reviews have attracted much attention in the literature. A key managerial decision for involved decision makers (e.g., review hosting firms like Amazon) is managing user reviews to elevate user review effectiveness. The literature recognizes that review effectiveness is a complex construct which ultimate product sales or consumers' willingness-to-pay is an incomplete criterion for its evaluation and thus begins to study review helpfulness recently (e.g., [20,27,31]). Conceptually, this progression on understanding review effectiveness is parallel to the inquiry on the effectiveness of traditional marketing communication mix (e.g., advertising) since review helpfulness is an intermediate measure of review effectiveness preceding the ultimate measures of review effectiveness like product sales and consumers' willingness-to-pay.

The empirical studies on review helpfulness were largely motivated and made possible by the settings of review hosting firms which often provide consumers' judgment publically on whether a review is helpful. Take Amazon for example, for each user review, consumers not only read its rating and text content, but also see how many votes it attracts from their peer users and how many votes, among the votes attracted, are helpful. The lack of an appropriate conceptualization of review helpfulness has perhaps facilitated different operationalizations of review helpfulness, mainly for a matter of convenience. For example, review helpfulness is often operationalized as the ratio of the number of helpful votes to the number of votes received (e.g., [8,14,20,31]). This operationalization of review helpfulness is conveniently constructed, but has a shortcoming: a review with high ratio of helpful votes does not always warrant its helpfulness to consumers. For example, the helpfulness of a review with only few votes which happen to be all helpful votes is not endorsed by consumers even if the review has the highest ratio of helpful votes at 100%. The literature recognizes the importance of the number of votes received in review helpfulness assessment, but is unclear on what role it plays in the assessment. Researchers have attempted to address the problem with different approaches. The first approach posits that the number of votes should play a weighting role in review helpfulness. Namely, review helpfulness should be measured by a product of the ratio of helpful votes and the number of votes, which leads to the operationalization of review helpfulness as the count of helpful votes (e.g., [3,5]). This idea is interesting, but incurs a similar caveat: a review receiving one hundred helpful votes is not perceived to be helpful to consumers if the review attracts one thousand votes in total, because only 10% are helpful votes. The second approach posits that the number of votes should play a sample selection role in review helpfulness (e.g., [14]). Namely, the ratio of helpful votes is sampled by those voted reviews, and thus should be corrected with sample selection in the estimation. However, limiting the role of number of votes as a

The third approach posits that the number of votes should play no role in review helpfulness by assuming that the number of votes and the ratio of helpful votes are independent (e.g., [24]). Although this approach recognizes the difference between review readership and review helpfulness, it ignores the dependence between the two: a helpful vote of a review first has to be a vote of the review, but the reverse may not be true.

The different operationalizations of review helpfulness further guide different empirical specifications for studying how review determinants affect review helpfulness, resulting in mixed findings. We summarize in Table 1 the main characteristics of the studies on review helpfulness in the literature. Take the effect of review valence on review helpfulness for example, [20] argues that the conformity hypothesis drives the nonlinearity of the effect which is also moderated by product types: the effect is U-shaped for search products (e.g., [8,20]), but inverted U-shaped for experience products (e.g., [14,20]). These studies significantly improve our understanding of the effect of review valence on review helpfulness, but questions remain. For example, it is unclear how the conformity hypothesis and the moderation of product types may apply for those studies where the effect of review valence on review helpfulness is either linearly increasing when review helpfulness is operationalized as the ratio of helpful votes (e.g., [22]) or linearly decreasing when review helpfulness is operationalized as the count of helpful votes (e.g., [5]).

For decision makers (e.g., review hosting firms, sellers, and buyers) to better understand and/or manage review helpfulness, it is important to have a conceptual framework for review effectiveness in which review helpfulness can be defined and the impacts of review determinants on it can be examined. With the inspiration of the marketing communication effectiveness models (e.g., [15]), we posit that review effectiveness is multi-dimensional and has review helpfulness as an important element. We organize the rest of the paper as follows. We first develop our conceptual framework of user review effectiveness and our hypotheses on how review determinants affect various elements of review effectiveness. Next, we develop an operational model to empirically test our hypotheses and compare our model to the existing models in the literature using two data sets collected at Amazon.com. We further highlight how our model may be used to support various decision makers for harvesting review effectiveness. We finally discuss the limitations and future research of our study.

## 2. Conceptual framework and hypothesis development

## 2.1. Conceptual framework

The effectiveness of communication mix is well studied in the marketing literature. There are two perspectives to understand the complexity and richness of communication effectiveness. From communication outcome perspective, the hierarchy-of-effects model on advertising by [15] is widely adopted. This model argues that in the long term, the effectiveness of marketing communications is a conversion process of three effects: cognitive effect, affective effect, and conative effect. For example, an effective advertising makes a high proportion of the targeted consumers aware, a high proportion of the aware consumers like, and a high proportion of the liking consumers purchase. Recently, the hierarchy-of-effects model has inspired the multi-stage model development and empirical examination of the email effectiveness in viral marketing where email effectiveness is conceptualized as a multi-dimensional construct (e.g., [6]).

From consumer information processing perspective, the accessibility-diagnosticity framework on word-of-mouth (WOM) is also commonly recognized. This framework argues that consumers activate different types of information processing when a piece of information is received, thus offers the consumer behavioral underpinnings for the hierarchy-of-effects model (e.g., [10]). Because online user reviews are essentially electronic WOM, this framework has recently been adopted by researchers to study review helpfulness which is conceived as a corresponding communication outcome of information diagnosticity (e.g., [20]). Similarly, the multi-process conceptualization for review effectiveness has also begun to emerge in the literature. Researchers suggest that participation of reviews and polarity of reviews may be driven by two different voting behaviors of consumers and should be studied separately (e.g., [24]).

Inspired by these two perspectives on the effectiveness of communication mix, we sketch a conceptual framework for review effectiveness in Fig. 1. The first element of review effectiveness is Review Popularity. This element represents the propensity of a review in attracting consumer attention (e.g., [24]), and is similar to the awareness component in [15] and the information accessibility component in [10]. For example, a review may attract many or few reads or votes by the consumers. The next element of review effectiveness is Review Helpfulness. This

Our conceptualization of review effectiveness offers a framework in which we can overview the various studies on review helpfulness and understand their relationships. Fig. 1 shows that most of the current studies focus on investigating the relationship either between review determinants and one single element of review effectiveness (e.g., [8, 20,29]) or between two different elements of review effectiveness (e.g., [9]). Furthermore, our conceptualization offers guidelines on how different elements of review effectiveness may be assessed and connected operationally. For example, the theories specify the dependences between Review Helpfulness and Review Popularity: the former is conditional on the latter because the two effects are hierarchical by the hierarchy-of- effects model, and information diagnosticity requires information accessibility by the information processing theory. Fig. 1 shows that our study is the first to analyze two different elements of review effectiveness jointly. ## 2.2. Hypotheses on review effectiveness

We define RP of a review as its intensity of having information accessible to consumers and thus motivating consumers to read and vote. We measure RP by the number of total votes the review attracts. We posit that superior RP propensity is driven by effective review determinants.

We specify the RP propensity $\lambda > 0$ as a function of review determinants:

$$
\ln \lambda = \sum \alpha \text {   Review   Determinants. }\tag{1}
$$

We define RH of a review is its intensity of making information diagnostic to consumers and thus shaping up consumers' evaluation and attitude for its given RP. We measure RH by the ratio of helpful votes the review attracts. By the same token, we specify the RH propensity θN0, conditional on RP, as a function of review determinants:

$$
\ln \theta | \mathrm{RP} = \sum \beta \text { Review   Determinants } + \beta_ {0} \mathrm{RP}.\tag{2}
$$

We consider review determinants in three categories: 1) review characteristics (Valence, Depth, and Life), 2) reviewer characteristics (Credibility), and 3) review hosting website characteristics (Framing). We discuss the theoretical prediction of the effects of these determinants on RP and RH separately.

## 2.2.1. Valence

Review valence is the overall sentiment of a review. It is often operationalized as review ratings in the literature (e.g., [5,29]). Review valence is the most important and widely researched review determinant in studying review effectiveness because it not only provides an overall quantitative assessment of a review, but also offers high accessibility to consumers' information search. Although the most widely studied review determinant, the focus of valence is primarily on its effectiveness on RH only. Recently, [13] found that products with lower average ratings attract more user reviews, which may suggest a positive effect of valence negativity bias on RP at the product level.

In the literature of marketing and social psychology, it is long acknowledged that the negativity bias of a communication message is an attention grabber (e.g., time spent looking at it) and motivation enhancer. Negatively valenced information also receives more weight on impressions than positively valenced information, and negative framing is more effective than positive framing when the processing is focused on message content (e.g., [18]). Negativity bias stands out because most outcomes are perceived as positive (the figure-ground

On the contrary, the studies on how Valence affects RH are ample (see Table 1). Two streams of research exist in the literature. The first stream is unconditional and has RH operationalized as the number of helpful votes. The second stream, also the main approach in Table 1, is conditional and has RH operationalized as the ratio of helpful votes. Since our definition of RH is similar to the second stream, we focus on this stream of research for hypothesis development. We will briefly revisit the relationship of the results between the two streams later in the Discussion section.

There are two main arguments on the valence on RH. The first argument focuses on the positivity bias of valence and shows that the main effect of valence on RH is positive or linearly increasing (e.g., [22]). A meta-analysis by [23] found that the main effect of valence on RH is weak positive or non-significant. The second argument considers both positivity bias and negativity bias by focusing on the extremity of review ratings. Motivated by the conformity hypothesis, it posits the relationship between valence and RH as either V-shaped where the symmetric point is pre-specified by the average rating of the reviews (e.g., [3,8,9]) or U-shaped where the symmetric point is inferred by a quadratic specification (e.g., [20,31]). However, studies in this stream do not seem to show consistent empirical findings. Some studies show that unequivocal reviews have stronger impact on RH (e.g., [8,9,31]) and, suggesting a U/V-shaped extreme bias effect while others show that equivocal reviews have stronger impact on RH (e.g., [14]), suggesting an inverted U/V-shaped social conformity effect (e.g., [7]). Furthermore, the mixed empirical findings are interpreted by a moderate effect by product type (e.g., [20]): review valence is U/V-shaped for search products where product quality assessment is possible without experiencing the product (e.g., an electronic hardware), but inverted U/V-shaped for experience products where product quality assessment is hard without experiencing the product (e.g., a video game).

However, a further analysis shows that the two different conceptualization of valence effect on RH may be reconciled as a positive (may not be constant) or monotonously increasing (may not linearly) effect of valence on RH. A check of the empirical results of [20] for the pooled sample (i.e., their Table 4) shows that the quadratic impact of rating is of Ushape with the symmetric point at rating = 0 Star because the coefficient of rating is statistically insignificant. Thus, the quadratic impact over the domain of ratings (i.e., 1 Star to 5 Star) is represented by the right half of the quadratic curve, suggesting a monotonously increasing effect for review ratings on RH, consistent to the positive effect reported by [22]. Even for the separated samples in [20], the conclusion seems to be the same. For search product sample (i.e., their Table 5), the impact of review rating on RH has U-shape with the symmetric point at rating = 0 Star, replicating the monotonicity we see in the pooled sample. For experience products (i.e., their Table 6), the impact of valence on RH has inverted U-shape with the symmetric point at rating = 4.44 Star, showing that the impact is represented by the left half or the monotonously increasing half of the quadratic curve almost over the entire domain of ratings.

Previous studies also concern how product type may moderate the effect of valence on RH. Pan and Zhang [22] shows that the effect of rating on RH is stronger for experience products than for utilitarian products. We expect product type may moderate the effects of Valence on RP and RH in the similar way.

Ha1. A review with higher valence has a lower impact on RP propensity, and the impact is stronger for experience products than for search products.

Ha2. For given RP, a review with higher valence has a higher impact on RH propensity, and the impact is stronger for experience products than for search products.

The contrast effects of negativity bias are commonly identified in the extant literature. For example, the negativity bias has contrast effects not only on product awareness and on product evaluation in advertising (e.g., [19]), but also on product awareness and product sales in product reviews (e.g., [2]). A behavioral underpinning beneath the contrast effects of negativity bias may be that consumers may activate different judgment protocols when facing different decision tasks. For example, in impression-formation research (e.g., [26]), negativity bias is positive when morality judgment is activated, but is negative when ability judgment is primed.

## 2.2.2. Depth

The depth of a review is its comprehensiveness. Although recent researches are more focused on the sentiments of review text contents (e.g., [3,13,32]), the length of a review is often thought as a good operationalization for review depth and is shown to have a positive impact on RP (e.g., [24]). There are two perspectives to understand why longer reviews may generate higher awareness for readers and are more likely to motivate them to respond. From perception perspective, the size effect in advertising shows that longer reviews take more space on review page, so they are catchier in consumers' browsing. For example, [28] shows that a larger size of a print advertisement drives more website traffic. From information perspective, [20] argues that longer reviews may motivate more people to vote because their additional contents have more potential to generate a reaction from the reader. Therefore, the depth of a review makes the review information more accessible for consumers and thus is expected to positively affect the RP.

The depth of a review is also expected to positively affect RH because it enhances the perceived diagnosticity of the information provided by the review. Longer reviews have higher extensiveness of review contents (e.g., [20]), and are perceived to be written by knowledgeable users, to carry more information, and thus to be more helpful to the consumers (e.g., [3]).

Hb1. A deeper review has a higher impact on RP propensity.

Hb2. For given RP, a deeper review has a higher impact on RH propensity.

## 2.2.3. Life

The life of a review refers to its age (e.g., [22]). Studies on advertising, word of mouth, and product reviews in the literature show that there is a positive association between the life of a marketing communication mix and the propensity of impressions it generates possibly because early messages accumulate more exposures and enjoys a positive pioneering effect. For example, [28] observes that in an advertising campaign, ads placed earlier in print media drive more website traffic than ads placed later do on average. More specifically on user reviews, early reviews of a product attract more attention from consumers because less product information is available (e.g., [2]). Indeed, [24] shows that longevity of a review has positive effect on RP. On the other hand, the pioneering effect of review life may be mitigated because of the recency sorting tools offered by many review hosting firms to aid consumers' searching of reviews. For example, Amazon's Newest First sorts all the reviews of a product in order from the newest to the oldest, which offers the consumers an opportunity to find the most recent reviews.

## 2.2.4. Credibility

The credibility of a reviewer refers to the perception or ability of the reviewer who provides credible information. For example, Amazon displays whether a review is written by someone who either actually purchased and experienced the product already or have expertise in the product category. Research studies in marketing communication literature show that information source credibility plays an important role in affecting consumer information processing because credible information builds consumer trust. For example, in studying the effectiveness of viral email marketing, [6] argues that trust between the email senders and recipients should exert a great influence on the awareness stage of the email communication because the information sent by credible senders is perceived to be less risky and to show higher economic value in using the information. Therefore, we expect that reviewer credibility has a positive impact on RP.

Bruyn and Lilien [6] argues that perceptual affinity between senders and receivers also positively affect the interest stage of email communications. For example, in the context of user reviews at Amazon, a reviewer who is listed as having made a “Verified Purchase” of a product may help build better perceptual affinity between the reviewer and the voter. The perceptual affinity generated by credible reviewers help enhance the information diagnosticity and trust of their reviews and thus the RH. Indeed, [8] shows that disclosed identities of reviewers have a positive effect on RH.

Hd1. A review posted by a more credible reviewer has a higher impact on RP propensity.

Hd2. For given RP, a review posted by a more credible reviewer has a higher impact on RH propensity.

## 2.2.5. Framing

Studies in the advertising literature show that the form variables of advertising like placement of an ad in the print media have impacts on various advertising effectiveness such as recall, readership, informativeness, and attractiveness (e.g., [21]). Consistently, studies in online marketing literature show that an attractive placement of an ad in a print medium (e.g., the right-most column of a print) has a positive impact on website traffic (e.g., [28]) and an attractive placement of a banner ad in Internet (e.g., the top locations of a webpage) attracts more click-throughs than other locations in the page do (e.g., [12,28]). Because of this well-established effect in marketing communications, many review hosting firms provide sorting tools for consumers to search reviews. For instance, Most Helpful First (MHF hereafter) determines the default position of a review at Amazon's review homepage. MHF sorts all reviews of a product from the most helpful to the least helpful at Amazon's discretion. Besides MHF, Amazon also features the Top Positive Review and Top Critical Review at the top of the review homepage.

The marketing communication literature suggests that the brand image is often positively associated with the communication appeals (e.g., endorsers) and communication vehicles (e.g., media). The reviews located at on prime positions of MHF or top positions of review home page at Amazon are perceived to have Amazon endorsement. As such, a review framed at a prime location not only has a higher chance to be read, but also is perceived to have a higher association with RH.

He1. A review framed at a superior page location has a higher impact on RP propensity.

He2. For given RP, a review framed at a superior page location has a higher impact on RH propensity.

## 3. An empirical study

## 3.1. Data and descriptive statistics

We collected data of user reviews on two products at Amazon for our study: Fire TV streaming box by Amazon (FTV hereafter) and The Last of Us for PlayStation 3 (LOU hereafter). There are several important considerations for product selections. First, FTV is an Internet streaming hardware with many key attributes searchable (e.g., processor, memory, remote controller, Expandable storage, USB port etc.) while LOU is a popular video game in recent years with major attributes experiential. Shortly, FTV represents a typical search product while LOU represents a typical experiential product (e.g., [20]). Second, FTV is priced at \$99 while LOU is priced at \$59.99 when they were released, suggesting that peers' opinions matter in consumers' reasonably high-involvement purchase decisions. Third, both products are popular. The popularity of the products allows for sufficient data accumulation within relatively shorter time period.

We collected FTV data in September 4, 2014 and LOU data in October 19, 2015. Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics of both data sets. FTV and LOU were released on April 3 of 2014 and June 14 of 2013 respectively. Although the time of data accumulation on FTV (154 days) was much shorter than that on LOU (857 days), the number of reviews of FTV (9484 reviews) is much higher than that of LOU (3272 reviews), showing that search products are much more popular than experience products in attracting user reviews, a fact reported by previous studies in the literature (e.g., [22]). Because of the short time for FTV data accumulation, we rechecked its customer reviews five months later on February 5, 2015. Although the total number of reviews is more than doubled to 21,685, the proportions of each Star level being 7%, 5%, 10%, 17%, and 58% for 1 Star to 5 Star respectively are similar to those reported in Table 2 for FTV. This indicates the arrivals of user reviews of FTV have reached the stable process when our data was collected.

Table 2 shows some interesting observations on the impact of Valence on RP and RH. We notice that lower Star reviews attract higher number of votes for both FTV and LOU, suggesting that the negativity bias of Valence seems to boost the RP regardless of the product type. However, we observe that higher Star reviews harvest higher ratio of helpful votes for both FTV and LOU, suggesting that the positivity bias of Valence seems to work on RH regardless of the product type. Furthermore, the proportions of reviews with no votes are 37% for FTV and 38% for LOU. The high proportion of no vote for search product FTV and even higher proportion of no vote for experience product LOU are consistent to previous reports (e.g., [22]).

To motivate our operational model, we plotted the histograms of the Number of Votes and the Number of Helpful Votes in Fig. 2. Both distributions for number of votes and number of helpful votes seem to follow a Poisson distribution for both FTV and LOU. Based on this observation, we formally develop next an operational model for understanding RP and RH along the conceptual framework elaborated in the previous section.

$$

An important characteristic of RP in the empirical studies is that it has a high proportion of reviews with no votes (RNV hereafter). For example, the proportions of RNV are 15% and 31% for search products and experience products respectively in [22]. Table 2 shows that the proportions are even higher at 37% and 38% for FTV and LOU respectively. Although the literature recognizes the uniqueness of RNV and its impact on sample selection bias in estimating RH, it conceptually does not differentiate RNV from the other reviews because it treats RNV as generated by the same voting behavior as other reviews (e.g., [14]).

We argue that the different RNVs may be governed by different voting behavior underpinnings. Using the widely adopted media reach concept in communication analysis, we partition RNV into two different categories: 1) those reviews, possibly buried in the middle of the large pile of reviews and primed by neither MHF sorting nor recency sorting at Amazon, are never reached by consumers (RNR hereafter), and 2) those reviews. although reached by consumers. do not motivate the consumers enough to vote because of its poor quality (RNM hereafter). In other words, $\mathrm { R N V } = \mathrm { R N R } + \mathrm { R N M } .$ . Although both types of reviews are not voted, the different behavioral underpinnings beneath them have an important implication on examining review effectiveness: RNM is an intrinsic part of RP and thus its propensity is affected by the same set of RP determinants, but RNR is not.

To take into consideration the two different behavioral underpinnings, we use a zero-inflated Poisson model to study RP in which a

Bernoulli process $\mathsf { B } ( p )$ accounts for $P = 0$ due to RNR and a Poisson process (3) accounts for the rest of the voting behavior for RP. Accordingly, the probability density of P is given by:

$$
f (n; \lambda) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} P r (P = 0) = p + (1 - p) e ^ {- \lambda}, & i f n = 0 \\ P r (P = n) = (1 - p) e ^ {- \lambda} \frac {\lambda^ {n}}{n !}, & i f n > 0 \end{array} \right.\tag{3'}
$$

which explicitly shows that the probability a review attracts no votes $\operatorname* { P r } ( P = 0 )$ is affected by both RNR propensity p and RP propensity λ. Following the literature, we specify the logit of p as a linear function of RNR determinants as follows:

$$
\ln \frac {p}{1 - p} = \gamma_ {0} + \gamma \text {   RNR   Determinants.   }\tag{1'}
$$

where RNR determinants may include product characteristics and search design of reviews at hosting websites etc. (e.g., product type).

$$
\begin{array}{l} L L (p, \lambda , \theta | k, n) = \left[ \sum_ {n = 0} \ln \left\{\frac {p}{1 - p} + e ^ {- \lambda} \right\} + \sum_ {n > 0} \{n \ln \lambda - \lambda \} + \sum \ln (1 - p) \right] \\ \quad + [ \sum \{k \ln \theta - n \ln (1 + \theta) - \ln k! - \ln (n - k)! \} ]. \end{array}\tag{7}

ZIPB has three advantages over the current single-stage models in the literature (Table 1). First, our model is different from the singlestage models in Table 1. For example, [24] considers two independent models for RP and RH by ignoring the conditional structure between RP and RH. Our joint model (6) shows that the RP and RH are not independent. Second, our empirical specification analytically shows the disentanglement of RP propensity λ and RH propensity θ. The single-stage models listed in Table 1 essentially either 1) estimate RH by ignoring RP and measure the helpfulness with the ratio of helpful votes (e.g., [20]) or 2) estimate the marginal distribution of helpful votes H by appending the RP into RH and measure the helpfulness with the number of helpful votes (e.g., [3]). In other words, they either ignore RP propensity λ or estimate the mixed propensity $\scriptstyle \lambda ( { \frac { \theta } { 1 + \theta } } )$ of RP and RH, instead of RH propen-<sup>þ</sup>sity θ per se. Third, our specification explicitly disentangles the no voting behavior (RNV) due to no awareness (RNR) and due to no motivation (RNM), not only avoiding the possible over-estimation of the effectiveness of the review characteristics as in the single-stage models, but also offering an opportunity for managers to infer the no voting behavior beyond the mere observations. Finally, it can be shown that the marginal distributions of both P and H are Poisson. Our operational model thus has a good face validity to represent the distribution characteristics for both FTV and LOU as exhibited in Fig. 2.

## 3.3. Variable specifications and analysis results

The variables are defined in Table 3. We specify Valence, Depth, Life, Credibility, and Framing as the determinants of RP (Eq. (1)) and RH (Eq. (2)). Because it is inconclusive whether the effect of Valence on RP and RH is linear or quadratic, we consider two specifications for Valence. Valence specification 1) does not superimpose any constraint about the relationship between Valence and RP or RP and thus gives us the freest examination of the effects of Valence on RP and RH propensities respectively. Valence specification 2) superimposes a linear relationship for testing our hypotheses Ha1 and Ha2. Framing is specified by assuming that users are unlikely to read more than three pages of reviews for a given product because of the information overloading after they sort MHF. ## 3.3.1. Reviews with no votes (RNV)

The overall goodness-of-fit AIC shows that ZIPB2 is significantly better than ZIPB1 for both FTV and LOU, suggesting that there are indeed two different processes of voting behavior in our data sets. A significant portion of the reviews will not reach the consumers (RNR), a result expected because of consumers' search costs (e.g., [12]).

We plotted the estimated proportions of RNR, RNM, and RNV from ZIPB2 in Fig. 3, in both absolute measure (within sample) and relative measure (within RNV). The left panel of Fig. 2. shows the comparable observed proportions of RNV between FTV and LOU, matching the comparable estimated proportions of RNV (the RNV bars in the left panel of Fig. 3). However, the underpinning voting processes are quite different between the two products. The AIC in Table 4 shows that the improvement of ZIPB2 over ZIPB1 is much higher for FTV than for LOU, suggesting that it is much more likely to observe RNR in FTV than in LOU. ## 3.3.3. Depth

Consistent with the literature (e.g., Table 1), we find that Depth is one of the review determinants which has the most consistent impact on RP and RH. Depth has a significant and positive impact on both RP and RH for FTV and LOU separated or pooled, strongly supporting hypotheses Hb1 and Hb2. This result shows that a longer review brings up both RP and RH. A longer review has dual effects on review effectiveness: larger in size attracts more consumers to browse and vote, and higher perceived diagnosticity more likely makes consumers perceive the review helpful too.

## 3.3.4. Life

We find mixed supports for our hypotheses regarding the impact of Life on RP and.

RH. The impact of Life on RP is positive for FTV and thus strongly supports Hc1, but is negative for LOU and thus does not support Hc1. On the other hand, the impact of Life on RH is negative for FTV and does not support Hc2, but is insignificant for LOU and thus supports Hc2. The mixed findings on Life may be due to the consumer behavior of using the Newest First sorting tool at Amazon. In general, because the perceived quality for search products is more standardized and objective than that for experience products, consumers may use Newest First much more frequently for experience products. This behavior mitigates the pioneering effects of reviews for experience products and may suggest a moderation effect of Life on RP and RH by product type. Further investigation is needed to better understand the roles of review life in generating RP and RH.

## 3.3.5. Credibility

We find mixed supports for our hypotheses on Credibility as well. On FTV, Credibility has a negative impact on RP and does not support Hd1, but has a positive impact on RH and supports Hd2 as hypothesized. On LOU, Credibility has negative impact on RP again and does not support Hd1, but is insignificant on RH and is not against Hd2. Perhaps better operationalizations of this variable are needed to understand this effect further (e.g., disclosure of reviewer identities as in [8]).

## 3.3.6. Framing

We find Framing has consistent effects on both RP and RH as expected. It has significant and negative impacts on both RP and RH for FTV and LOU separated or pooled. Because Framing is defined as the ranking of the position, i.e., lower value of Framing for higher ranking and better location, the significant and negative impacts strongly support hypotheses He1 and He2. Not surprisingly, reviews with higher ranking in MHF or being featured on top of the review paper at Amazon (thus lower value in Framing) not only motivate more readings and votes, but also more likely motivate helpful votes because they are endorsed by Amazon. This finding suggests that hosting websites of user reviews could significantly alter the effectiveness of a review by changing how it is framed at the review hosting firms.

The negative coefficients of the control variable Popularity on RH for both FTV and LOU show a diminishing effect of all review determinants as RP increases: everything else being equal, more popular reviews are less likely to be helpful on average, a finding consistent with [20].

## 3.4. Model comparison

## 3.5. Implications on decision support

An important managerial decision for user review hosting firms is: how should the firms organize online user reviews to raise review effectiveness? Amazon's ideas to the decision problem are two: the MHF and the featured positive/critical reviews. Amazon does not reveal its methodologies of generating the MHF and selecting the featured reviews, but the top-ten reviews of MHF in both FTV and LOU in Table 7 show that Amazon indeed recognizes the importance of both RP and RH, but weighs RH more than RP, in its decisions in review effectiveness management.

Our review effectiveness sheds light on the decision problem in two important aspects. First, firms may have different objectives on review effectiveness: higher RP? higher RH? or Combined Effectiveness CE of both RP and RH? These objectives can be assessed by $E [ P ] , E [ H | P ] ,$ or E[H] respectively. Second, review hosting firms may not directly affect most of the review determinants (e.g., Length, Timing, Life, and Credibility), but can select the reviewers with right determinants to deliver their effectiveness via Framing (e.g., pinned on top of the review page or enlisted on top of a review helpfulness ranking list like MHF by Amazon).

Take the overall assessment of review effectiveness CE using E[H] in our operational model for example, we have, by law of iterated expectations, $\begin{array} { r } { E [ H ] = E _ { P } [ E [ H | P ] ] = E _ { P } [ \mu P ] = ( 1 - p ) \lambda \mu = ( 1 - p ) \lambda ( \frac { \theta } { 1 + \theta } ) } \end{array}$ where <sup>þ</sup>p,λ,θ are given by Eqs. (1), (2), and (1′). We report in Table 8 the marginal effects of review determinants on CE calculated by the estimated coefficients and at the means of the review determinants in ZIPB3. We find that the total marginal effects across all determinants on RH is much larger than that on RP, suggesting that E[H] as a measure of CE has a good face validity since it coincides with what Amazon does for MHF in which RH correlates MHF much more than RP does in Table 7. Therefore, E[H] can be used to assess the potential overall effectiveness of a review with its review determinant values when the review is posted. This assessment is essential for CE management because if the predicted high power reviews do not appear on prime spots and on time, they may be ignored permanently and thus produce no effectiveness on either RP or RH since consumers do not read many reviews in general.

In addition, CE can be used by managers to understand how each review determinant contributes to the overall effectiveness of the review. The decomposition of CE into the effectiveness on RH and RP derived from our model lend insightful explanations for the overall effectiveness the current single-stage models do not offer. Table 8 shows that the effects of review determinants on CE is largely consistently across the two data sets FTV and LOU, suggesting that our overall effectiveness assessment is reasonably robust. On the other hand, the overall effect path of a review shows a complex pattern. For example, we find that Depth has positive impact on CE (i.e., a longer review is more effective) because Depth has positive impact on both RH and RP. We also find the Framing has negative impact on CE (i.e., a higher MHF ranked or featured review

The implications discussed above for review hosting firms are also valid for buyers and sellers because an effective management of user reviews by review hosting firms would reduce search cost for the buyers and increase review effectiveness for sellers at the same time. Specifically, the consistent positive effects of Depth in both RP and RH shows that in-depth reviews are highly effective for both buyers and sellers. Although sellers may have no control on how buyers write their reviews, they could influence the overall effectiveness by inviting more in-depth reviews (e.g., expert reviews, video reviews etc.).

## 4. Discussion

To address the effective decision support problem for online user review hosting firms, we propose a conceptual framework for understanding user review effectiveness in the spirit of hierarchy-of-effects model in marketing communications. With an operational model and two data sets collected from Amazon.com, we show that our framework not only is empirically supported, but also offers deeper insights than the competing models and exhibits better potentials on assisting various decision makers in making effective use of online user reviews.

Our framework also sheds light on reconciling the empirical findings in the literature. Table 1 shows that the effect of Valence on RH is positive if RH is operationalized as ratio (e.g., [22]), but is negative if RH is operationalized as count (e.g., [5]). In our framework, the expectation of RH is E[H] if RH is measured by count H, but is E[H|P] if by ratio, and $\begin{array} { r } { E [ H ] = ( 1 - p ) \lambda ^ { * } ( \frac { \theta } { 1 + \theta } ) = E [ P ] ^ { * } E [ H | P ] } \end{array}$ . We calculate both expectations <sup>þ</sup>of RH for each Star level of Valence with other covariates fixed at their respective means for both FTV and LOU data sets, and plot them in Fig. 6 (we use a log-scale for the expectations in the charts). The exhibit shows that even though the E[H|P] increases (i.e., the positive effect of Valence on RH by ratio as in [22]), E[H] actually decreases (i.e., the negative effect of Valence on RH by count as in [5]) because E[H] is dominated by the negative effect of Valence on RP.

Our research raises questions that need to be addressed. First, our review effectiveness model is proposed for better conceptualizing review helpfulness with empirical data availability. We invite more research on developing a stronger theory for studying review effectiveness. Methodologically, we need to consider the dynamic interactions between review hosting firm decisions and consumer voting behavior. For example, the Framing of a review by Amazon may not only affect, but may also be affected by, the RP and RH propensities of the review. Managerially, the empirical specifications of RP and RH can be extended to cover more determinants (e.g., review contents) in order to understand review effectiveness comprehensively (e.g., [4,24,31,32]). In addition, more and larger data sets should be used to establish the generalizability of our results more solidly.
