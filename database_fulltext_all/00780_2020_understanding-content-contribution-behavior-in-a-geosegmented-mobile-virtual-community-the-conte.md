# Understanding Content Contribution Behavior in a Geosegmented Mobile Virtual Community: The Context of Waze

Chenhui (Julian) Guo,<sup>a</sup> Tae Hun Kim,<sup>b</sup> Anjana Susarla,<sup>a</sup> Vallabh Sambamurthy<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. We examine content creation in a geosegmented, crowdsourced social mobile virtual community app, Waze. We conceptualize a virtual and spatial factor, virtual crowdedness (defined as the density of Waze users in a particular geospatial location), and we examine its role in encouraging user contribution. We posit that the relationship be tween virtual crowdedness and user contribution is driven by the tension between audience effects and bystander/content saturation effects. We analyze a panel data set of user contributions on Waze from New York City to test our hypotheses. First, our findings indicate that although virtual crowdedness has a positive influence on total number of contributions, the magnitude of the influence decreases as virtual crowdedness increases. Second, the concave-down increasing relationship is more pronounced for rush hours with high physical crowdedness than for non-rush hours with low physical crowdedness. A variety of robustness checks and alternative analyses based on matching estimators and spatial econometric models further support the main conclusions while mitigating concerns about endogeneity and spatial autocorrelation. Our findings provide several key practical implications for platform designers in that they should allow for users to visualize density of usage as well as improve the design of social features for encouraging user contribution to the mobile virtual community.

## 1. Introduction

A user-generated content (henceforth referred to as UGC) platform refers to a system that enables users to contribute, consume, and evaluate digital content. A related model is the phenomenon of peer production (Benkler 2006), where decentralized communities of contributors collaboratively contribute content in a commons-based approach. A major issue in both types of platforms is that content contribution is made by a relatively small proportion of users (von Krogh et al. 2003, Kane 2011). However, such platforms cannot survive without active users willing to invest their time and effort in generating compelling content that is valuable to other users. Building such platforms has been posited to be a chicken-and-egg problem: although high-value contributors are difficult to attract, it is challenging to sustain participation in the community without such contributors (Athey and Ellison 2014). Typically, UGC platforms tried to incentivize participation through monetary rewards (Sun et al. 2017) or gamification tools such as virtual badges, status hierarchies, and symbolic award (Anderson et al. 2013, Goes et al. 2016, Gallus 2017) whereas peer production platforms relied on prosocial incentives. The issue of continuous participation of engaged users is even more compelling in the era of mobile commerce, where there is a higher rate of user attrition.

We investigate a popular crowdsourced mobile navigation app, Waze, in which users provide, refine, and verify traffic-related updates and information in real time. Waze exhibits features of both UGC and peer production platforms. Regarding the peer production aspect, Waze provides prosocial incentives for participants to provide initial alert postings (which we label “primary contributions”) about traffic events and conditions, such as traffic congestion, accidents, stalled vehicles, and hidden police officers.<sup>1</sup> Waze also promotes subsequent contributions (which we label “secondary contributions”) wherein participants could refine initial contributions through feedback and confirmative contributions such as posting comments and thumbs-up on alerts. Because initial contributions by users are often validated through secondary contributions, the high-quality informational content on Waze creates a cycle of participation whereby users who post alerts have an incentive to keep doing so. Such hyperlocal information provided by Waze users is not available through traditional navigation apps. Waze allows its users to dynamically update their path based on local and real-time information crowdsourced from an active community of contributors. By enabling distributed collaboration to provide a public good (trafficrelated information) through a geolocation-based crowdsourced mobile navigation app, Waze has become a self-organized community (e.g., Lakhani and Panetta 2007) providing highly relevant and local spatiotemporal knowledge through a peer production model.

Given the spatiotemporal nature of the mobile virtual community, we define a novel concept, virtual crowdedness, which is defined as the density of users in the locally neighboring areas shown on the app, as distinct from physical crowdedness, which refers to the local density of traffic. Specifically, we analyze the role of virtual crowdedness<sup>2</sup> in encouraging three types of user contribution: initial contribution via alert postings (primary contribution) and feedback contribution via comments and confirmative contribution via thumbs-up (secondary contributions). Drawing on existing literature, we address the following research questions: (1) Does total contribution increase along with virtual crowdedness? (2) How does the magnitude of the relationship change as virtual crowdedness increases? (3) How does the relationship of virtual crowdedness and total contribution interact with physical crowdedness? (4) Do secondary contributions increase as virtual crowdedness increases?

We measure the virtual crowdedness and the three types of contributions for users in New York City using Waze’s application programming interface (API) and then investigate the relationship between virtual crowdedness and the different types of user contributions at a geographic area level. We employ multiple empirical models controlling for spatial and temporal confounding factors. To strengthen the empirical results, we adopt matching methods that implement a quasiexperimental research design. Several robustness checks are also conducted with multiple alternative estimators and spatial panel data models. The findings highlight the role of virtual crowdedness in encouraging Waze users to engage in primary and secondary contributions. First, although we find a positive relationship between virtual crowdedness and total contribution, the magnitude of this relationship decreases as virtual crowdedness increases. This result suggests a concave-down increasing relationship between virtual crowdedness and the total number of contributions Such a relationship could be explained by the competing effects of the audience size that drives participants’ prosocial motivation to engage in contributing behavior and by the bystander and content saturation effects that crowd out contribution. Second, the relationship is more pronounced in periods of peak physical crowdedness such as during rush hours. Third, Waze users are likely to engage more in secondary contributions by commenting on and verifying the initial alerts when they perceive a higher level of virtual crowdedness. That is, the contributors’ decision is contingent on the level of effort required to contribute and the urgency level of content demand. Waze users’ prosocial motivation of secondary contributions, compared with an initial contribution, could be relatively less influenced by the bystander and content saturation effects, as virtual crowdedness increases.

Our findings may help mobile app designers and developers to understand how to encourage users to contribute to such settings and how user contribution needs to be independent of the bystander and content saturation effects that can disincentivize user contribution. For app designers, our findings suggest design features wherein a user’s ability to visualize the density of usage enhances a user’s prosocial motivation to contribute to mobile virtual communities. Understanding the role of virtual crowdedness in encouraging user contribution around urban areas promises new opportunities for the big data and Internet of Things (IoT) initiatives. Our research is also in the tradition of citizen data science and provides practical guidelines for the design of smart cities and intelligent transportation systems. By enabling individuals to reveal knowledge about local commuting conditions, apps such as Waze are providing private solutions to public problems such as traffic congestion. Apps such as Waze provide vast swathes of citizen transportation data that transit authorities may not collect or do not share with the public in the first place. Our work also serves to point that public-private partnerships and data sharing between Waze and local transit authorities will help in individuals creatively coming up with crowdsourced solutions to the problems faced by cities and urban transport.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We first describe the research context, Waze, and its user community features in Section 2. Then, we introduce the theoretical background and hypotheses in Section 3. Section 4 introduces the data and main variables, followed by Section 5 presenting the empirical approach. We report the empirical results and additional analyses in Section 6. Finally, Section 7 summarizes the findings and discusses theoretical and practical implications.

## 2. Research Context 2.1. Waze

Waze is one of the most popular mobile apps that serve as a global positioning system (GPS) navigation and mapping tool for real-time, crowdsourced traffic information<sup>3</sup> (see the Waze screenshot in Figure 1). Waze users pinpoint locations on the map to create initial alerts on accidents, traffic jams, traffic speed, police traps, and road hazards, as well as to react to and verify the initial alerts through secondary contributions, such as adding comments, and confirmative contributions through the thumbs-up feature. “Alert” is a social feature that allows Waze users to originally announce their traffic experiences, such as changes and emergencies in the traffic conditions, to other users. “Comment” and “Thumbs-up” features enable Waze users to generate additional content via comments and a thumbs-up icon on the posted alerts. The value of Waze is in utilizing a real-time collective crowd of citizens to capture hyperlocal rapidly varying traffic information rather than depending on traffic information generated from algorithms or a few expert observers (Howe 2008). Such crowdsourced user contribution provides fast and intuitive traffic content, which is more helpful for drivers in a socialized and localized manner than the top-down information from GPS apps.

## 2.2. Physical Crowdedness and Virtual Crowdedness

When driving with the Waze app, users observe other vehicle drivers outside of their automobiles physically as well as other Waze users on smartphone screens virtually. Such virtually observable peers form the perceivedvirtual crowdedness of Waze users (the density of other users who a focal user observes on the navigation map), which refers to the number of Waze users per square kilometer. In the Waze app, users perceive virtual crowdedness by viewing the avatars of other users. Once a particular user chooses to visualize his or her avatar on the app,<sup>4</sup> the avatar will show up (or blink, depending on the quality of the wireless signal) on the real-time map. In Figure 2, we demonstrate the situations with a low versus a high number of avatars shown on the Waze app running on a smartphone. The more avatars a focal user could observe, the higher virtual crowdedness he or she will perceive. Such a concept of a virtual crowd (a.k.a. a group of agents in a virtual setting) has been discussed in prior literature on virtual worlds (Benford et al. 1997, Animesh et al. 2011), although it is novel in the context of mobile commerce. It is important to note that virtual crowdedness is a virtual concept, distinct from physical crowdedness (Stokols 1972), which is defined as the degree of population density per unit physical

## 3. Theory and Hypotheses 3.1. Theoretical Background

The issue of user contributions in UGC platforms has been explored by a stream of information systems research (e.g., Ma and Agarwal 2007). Members of a UGC platform desire to help others by engaging in prosocial behavior with their content as a public good. Because such content is completely free to other members, but there is a cost for producing it, inevitably, for rational members there is a natural tendency toward “free-riding” behavior (Andreoni 1988). Theories of public goods would suggest, given the public goods nature of UGC, there could be an undersupply of such content because of insufficient incentives. Because members of UGC platforms contribute voluntarily, the key issue for the platforms’ survival is to have a sustainable level of user participation.

Previous studies on user participation in online communities highlight the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic motivations (Hars and Ou 2001, Lerner and Tirole 2002, Roberts et al. 2006). As an intrinsic motivation driven by basic human needs, pure altruism is believed to be a critical factor for personal motivation toward voluntary work in online communities (Hars and Ou 2001). By contrast, extrinsic motivations are often closely associated with material rewards or benefits associated with participation, such as financial motivation (Horton and Chilton 2010, Sun et al. 2017), use value motivation (Roberts et al. 2006), and reputation or image-based motivation, wherein individuals are motivated by others’ perception of their social status, skills, or wellbeing (Ariely et al. 2009). Prior literature posits that members in online communities are motivated by gamified incentives (Anderson et al. 2013, Goes et al. 2016, Gallus 2017), future job market opportunities (Hars and Ou 2001, Lerner and Tirole 2002, Hann et al. 2013), and professional reputation or status (Roberts et al. 2006).

It is still substantially challenging for UGC platforms to motivate user participation through intrinsic means because financial incentives could potentially crowd out or reduce intrinsic motivation (Deci et al. 1999, Gneezy et al. 2011) and image-based motivation (Ariely et al. 2009). Increasingly, therefore, platforms are deploying programs to reinforce members’ reputational or image-based motivation. We focus on how such reputational or image-based prosocial motivations are reinforced through intensive social interactions among members of such communities. Previous studies suggest that members of online communities are more motivated when the group size is larger (Zhang and Zhu 2011), when there are a larger number of followers (Shriver et al. 2013, Toubia and Stephen 2013, Goes et al. 2014, Qiu and Kumar 2017), or when socially comparable peers participate more (Chen et al. 2010, Huang et al. 2019).

Our study is also related to the growing literature on mobile commerce, especially studies focusing on the location features of mobile apps. For instance, Ghose et al. (2013) point out that the drawbacks of mobile devices, such as smaller screen sizes and less convenient input method, could be offset by benefits associated with temporal and geographical features of mobile devices, because users benefit from using a mobile phone to search for geographically close matches (Ghose et al. 2013), and users tend to consume rather than generate content when they are traveling (Ghose and Han 2011). Recent research also uncovers valuable insights into the impact of locationspecific and context-specific factors on consumers choices. For example, Luo et al. (2014) find that consumers receiving coupons from mobile devices behave differently, depending on their spatial and temporal proximities to the product or service. It is important to note that the spatial-temporal nature of the mobile context is related to both online and offline versions of “crowdedness.” Andrews et al. (2016) suggest that physical crowdedness (i.e., the degree of population density or number of people per unit area) has a positive effect on consumer responses to mobile advertising.

Increasingly, popular mobile applications for citizen science and crowdsourced reporting allow users to share running/cycling activities (e.g., the Strava app), make localized recommendations (e.g., the Nextdoor app), report criminals and find missing people (e.g., the Citizen app), and so forth, in a real-time and localized manner.<sup>5</sup> It is of great importance to investigate participation in such geolocation-enabled crowdsourcing apps and the relationship between plat form design features and outcomes, such as user satisfaction, quality of reporting, social benefits, etc. However, very limited prior work has considered the important context of geolocation-enabled crowdsourcing apps. Two recent studies on OpenStreetMap, a crowdsourced digital mapping platform that relies on members’ voluntary work, investigate how competitive entry of Google Maps (Nagaraj and Piezunka 2018) or externally sourced content seeds (Nagaraj 2019) could influence the existing members’ contribution or health of the whole community. In a similar vein, we focus on the key concept of virtual crowdedness, which is an intracommunity contextual factor derived from the spatiotemporal dynamic participation patterns in mobile communities, and examine its impact on user contribution. In addition, we consider the more complicated situation in which physical crowdedness is intertwined with virtual crowdedness in its impact on user contribution. This study is therefore closely tied to the practical implications of design features of such geosegmented crowdsourcing platforms, the outcome of which would lead to significantly dif ferent levels of user content generation.

## 3.2. Hypotheses Development

Waze provides a location-specific, mobile, and social platform in which users share their daily driving experience with other users, which is identified as prosocial behavior. Specifically, on the basis of the levels of user participation, we identify three types of prosocial behavior, including the (1) initial contribution to original traffic content, as well as (2) feedback contribution and (3) confirmative contribution to additional traffic content. The latter two types are labeled as secondary contributions. The remaining Waze users consume the real-time traffic information nearby but do not contribute; such behavior is defined as free riding. In the context of geolocation-enabled crowdsourcing of traffic content, the prosocial behavior and free-riding behavior together determine the dynamics of user contribution to such online UGC platforms.

Users of the Waze mobile virtual community benefit from free contribution crowdsourced by active users. Because users are contributing local and highly relevant spatial knowledge, this could be described as public goods provision. The classic public goods model suggests that an individual who decides to offer a share of public goods from his or her income gets utility from consumption of the remaining private good (which could be the active contributor’s local information not shared on Waze), as well as an entire amount of the public good (which is knowledge of local traffic conditions shared with other users), which is the sum of public goods offered by the group of individuals. Models of pure altruism would suggest an undersupply of public goods, because, in equilibrium, rational individuals tend to choose to offer nothing (the commons problem). Andreoni (1989, 1990) proposed an impure altruism model in which the individual gets an additional utility from the act of “warm-glow” giving. Such an impure altruism model suggests that individuals will not free ride off the contributions of others. Prior literature has also suggested that reputation or image plays a vital role in prosocial behavior (Ariely et al. 2009). Similarly, Harbaugh (1998) introduces a prestige motive for charitable behavior, in which donors are motivated by public recognition that depends on the amount of the donation. Such a reputation or image-based behavioral explanation would suggest that the increased visibility of a positive social image will motivate prosocial behavior (Ariely et al. 2009).

In the Waze context, it is reasonable to assume that a contributor gets a reputational reward each time he or she contributes. However, to gauge whether virtual crowdedness affects the reputational reward, and thereby the propensity to contribute positively or negatively, is far from straightforward. Primarily, the question is, do the social effects triggered by the displayed avatars of other users on the phone screen (i.e., virtual crowdedness) generate a positive or negative impact on user motivation? Prior studies suggest that virtual communities are more likely to create prosocial content when they are geographically closer to each other and share common interests (Blanchard and Horan 1998), when they have a larger potential audience (Zhang and Zhu 2011), and when they have more (similar) followers in social networks (Shriver et al. 2013, Toubia and Stephen 2013, Goes et al. 2014, Qiu and Kumar 2017, Peng et al. 2018). From this perspective, an individual perceives a greater reputational reward from a larger virtual audience. Meanwhile, pure altruism and warm-glow motivation may be also positively associated with audience size. Therefore, we conceptualize the positive influence of the group size of potential users on contribution behavior as the audience effect.

However, prosocial motivation could also be negatively impacted by the number of contributors; when the reputational reward needs to be shared among multiple contributors, each contributor gets a smaller piece of reputational reward, diluting his or her motivation to contribute. For instance, Duncan (2004) developed an “impact philanthropy” model of public goods provision based on which the contributions of other donors can reduce the focal donor’s charitable fulfillment. Prior literature on public goods has posited there is free-riding behavior because the marginal utility of giving would decline as the group size increases (Isaac and Walker 1988, Andreoni 2007). The theory of the bystander effect from the social psychology literature also explains such free-riding behavior in online communities (e.g., Fischer et al. 2011). The bystander effect refers to the phenomenon that an individual’s likelihood of helping decreases when bystanders are present in a critical situation, mainly caused by the psychological process of diffusion of responsibility—the psychological cost associated with nonintervention is shared among bystanders (Latané and Nida 1981). Such helping behavior refers to not only offline emergencies such as saving a person’s life and calling the police but also nonemergency incidents such as answering a door and leaving a tip in a restaurant. Remarkably, recent studies report that the bystander effect also occurs in the virtual world of the internet. The exemplars of the online bystander effect are found in the studies on electronic helping behavior such as answering email requests or sharing virtual knowledge (Barron and Yechiam 2002, Blair et al. 2005).

In our context, virtual crowdedness signals not only the number of potential beneficiaries on the demand side but also the number of potential contributors on the supply side. The bystander effect suggests that when others are present and available to contribute, individuals tend to feel that the psychological cost of not contributing could be shared with other users. Therefore, they could be demotivated to contribute content when they perceive that there are many other users. For particular potential contributors, the higher the virtual crowdedness (i.e., number of bystanders) they observe, the lower the benefit of the contribution they could expect, and their likelihood of contributing is lower. Contributors may be less likely to contribute content when they observe someone else has already contributed similar or the same content, which we term the content saturation effect. Although both bystander and content saturation effects empirically work in a similar manner to reduce contribution, conceptually, the difference be tween them is substantial. The bystander effect here is caused by a potential contributor’s diminished sense of public responsibility (i.e., propensity to contribute), when they expect others will contribute; content saturation effect is driven by a contributor observing that others have already contributed the information available to the contributor (i.e., actual contribution behavior). Therefore, we conceptualize the negative impact of the group size of potential contributors on the provision of crowdsourced content as the bystander and content saturation effect.<sup>6</sup> Table 1 describes these competing effects and serves as a road map for hypotheses development.

3.2.2. The Role of Physical Crowdedness. We rely on the same reputational reward framework to elaborate on the moderating role of physical crowdedness. Primarily, a higher physical crowdedness makes potential contributors believe that their contributions are more valuable to the other users, because others could change their driving routes to save more commuting time. Therefore, potential contributors perceive stronger pure altruism and warm-glow motivations with physical crowdedness, which makes their reputational rewards from contribution stronger,<sup>8</sup> thus enhancing the audience effect. However, stronger reputational rewards also imply the “diffusion of responsibility,” or content saturation, which could reduce the marginal benefit of contribution more when there appear to be more potential contributors. Consequently, as virtual crowdedness increases, potential contributors tend to perceive salient bystander and content saturation effects, until ultimately, content contribution reaches the highest point, where potential contributors cannot obtain a positive reputational reward for additional contribution (i.e., marginal return approaches zero). In such a geosegmented mobile virtual community where the informational content could easily get congested and saturated, physical crowdedness should strengthen the bystander and content saturation effects more compared with the audience effect. Altogether, high physical crowdedness would cause potential contributors to perceive a higher marginal return of contributing content when virtual crowdedness is low, but their marginal return reduces faster as virtual crowdedness increases.

Therefore, given a higher physical crowdedness (compared with a lower physical crowdedness), we conjecture that the total contribution level increases at a higher (lower) rate, but the increasing rate reduces faster (slower) as virtual crowdedness increases.

The reputational reward framework suggests a relatively higher but faster diminishing perceived marginal return of contribution when physical crowdedness is higher. An additional explanation, based on driver psychology, could be that when the road is physically crowded, the average perceived speed of the traffic is lower and the perceived stress of the driver is higher. As a result, Waze users have more time to pay attention to their smartphone screens and are more likely to use the Waze app to vent about being caught in traffic, implying that the impact of virtual crowdedness should be stronger because it is more cognitively salient to the potential contributors. We therefore hypothesize that physical crowdedness intensifies the influence of virtual crowdedness.

Hypothesis 2. Physical crowdedness positively moderates the effect of virtual crowdedness on contribution.

3.2.3. Initial vs. Secondary Contributions. Besides the impact of virtual crowdedness on the overall amount of contributions, it is also interesting to examine its effect on the proportions of different types of contributions. In the Waze context, feedback contribution via comments provides additional information by amplifying and patching up the initial traffic content. Such feedback contribution is valuable information to Waze users, because it enhances the reliability of initial contribution by providing additional information about the traffic events, such as traffic jams and car accidents. Given the various motives for the three types of contributions, users’ contributions likely differ by the levels of virtual crowdedness. When an individual perceives that someone else might post an initial contribution (or when they observe another person making an initial contribution), the bystander (and content saturation) effect implies that there is little motivation to make another initial contribution, whereas additional feedback contributions are stil valuable in that having more comments on alerts heightens awareness about the urgency of traffic events. Meanwhile, the focal users’ motivation for feedback contributions is less likely to be reduced by the bvstander and content saturation effects. because potential contributors will still feel responsible to amplify or patch up the initial traffic content, even though other contributors are available. As virtua crowdedness increases, potential contributors tend to perceive a faster diminishing marginal return for making initial contributions compared with feedback contributions. When Waze users perceive a lower level of virtual crowdedness, an initial contribution is more beneficial, whereas feedback contribution becomes increasingly attractive as virtual crowdedness increases. In other words, as virtual crowdedness increases, potential contributors would tend to make more feedback contribution rather than initial con tribution. We therefore propose a positive relationship between virtual crowdedness and the proportion of feedback contribution.

Hypothesis 3A. As virtual crowdedness increases, potentia contributors perform relatively more feedback contribution.

Waze users could also verify the posted alerts by tapping the thumb-up icons, generating additional content, which signals the collective confirmability of the initial contribution. More thumb-up acknowledgments on an initial alert indicate that the traffic event is serious for other users and influential to their driving. Waze users are engaged in making confirmative contributions when they perceive responsibility for informing the other users of the initial contribution. Similarly, potential contributors believe confirmative contribution to be valuable to users, even though virtual crowdedness makes initial contributions less beneficial, because of a faster diminishing marginal return of initial contributions. Potential contributors could then be willing to engage in confirmative contributions even when they observe a high level of virtual crowdedness. Hence, we posit a positive relationship between virtual crowdedness and the proportion of confirmative contribution.

Hypothesis 3B. As virtual crowdedness increases, potentia contributors perform relatively more confirmative contribution

## 4. Data

## 4.1. Data Collection

This paper analyzes a panel data set for New York City (NYC), which is currently one of the most populated cities in the United States. We collect a large-scale data set for 98 days in 2016 from multiple sources of urban data, including the Waze API, U.S. Census Bureau, New York Police Department (NYPD), DemographicsNow, and Weather Underground. The 14 weeks of data consist of 7 weeks from January 11 to February 28 (in the winter) and the other 7 weeks from July 11 to August 28 (in the summer). A driver-level panel data set is not available because, unfortunately, the data sets do not provide a unique identifier for each driver. Instead, a zone-level panel data set was collected because of the real-time dynamics of moving users and their activities in each zone. By exploiting the data collection capability, we captured Waze data every hour to take a snapshot per hour for the 14 weeks. Thus, the data set has in total 2,352 time periods (24 hours × 98 days). During the observation time windows, we obtained 5,005,924 pieces of user contributions (2,112,547 alert postings as well as 827,125 comments and 2,066,252 thumbs-up acknowledgments on the posted alerts).

Given the large number of zones in NYC, the raw point data were aggregated to the level of neighborhood tabulation area (NTA) by using their coordinates<sup>9</sup> on the map across the five boroughs: Bronx (38 NTAs), Brooklyn (51 NTAs), Manhattan (29 NTAs), Queens (58 NTAs), and Staten Island (19 NTAs). We excluded five NTAs where Waze users’ contributing activities are not available (e.g., Central Park in Manhattan). Hence, the final data set includes 190 NTAs over the five NYC boroughs. As a result, this paper analyzes a set of balanced panel data with 446,880 observations (190 NTAs × 2,352 hours). In Figure 3, we visualize the density of total contribution and Waze users, showing a clear positive correlation.

## 4.2. Variables and Summary Statistics

4.2.1. User Contribution. We examine three types of user contributions—namely, traffic alerts, comments, and thumbs-up acknowledgments. In particular, we measure total contribution of Waze community users as the summation of all three types of contributions in NTA i at time t (NumContribution). Furthermore, we construct the proportion of comment contributions (PropComment) and proportion of thumbs-up contributions (PropThumbUp) measured in each NTA at each time period.

4.2.2. Virtual and Physical Crowdedness. The independent variable of interest is VirtualCrowdedness— that is, the number of Waze users per unit area in NTA i at time t. This variable indicates the density of nearby users as potential contributors who a focal user can observe on his or her smartphone screen virtually when using Waze.<sup>10</sup> An important moderator of virtual crowdedness is physical crowdedness. In practice it is difficult to measure the real-time physical crowdedness of each NTA. Instead, on the basis of the stable daily traffic pattern, we utilize time intervals for each day to classify time periods into rush hours or non-rush hours and utilize that dichotomy as a proxy for physical crowdedness.<sup>11</sup> We explicitly define rush hours as the peak traffic time in the morning (7:00–9:59 am) and in the afternoon (4:00–6:59 pm) on the weekdays, based on which we further construct a binary variable, RushHour.

4.2.3. Location Condition. Waze users are exposed not only to virtual crowdedness but also to various offline factors such as location-specific conditions. We obtain yearly-based location condition variables, including traffic density (TrafficDen), settled population density (SettledPopDen), and floating population density (FloatingPopDen), which are used as time-invariant control variables. Location characteristics not only indicate local driving conditions but also reflect the profiles of potential users and citizens that the focal users can observe outside of their automobile when driving, which affects their contribution.

## 5. Empirical Approach

Our empirical analysis focuses on several key outcome variables that describe the volume and types of content generation. Specifically, the total number of contributions of the users in a certain NTA is measured to capture the volume of content generation at an aggregate level. Previous literature on public goods provision mainly concentrates on average contribution levels, because it is important to understand how the equilibrium level of contribution is formed from an economic perspective. For our research context, it is more important to understand how the UGC platforms gather enough content created by a crowd of users. Furthermore, to test whether virtual crowdedness influences Waze users’ decisions on what types of content to create, we employ proportions of secondary contributions as the outcome variables.

## 5.3. Regression Models for Feedback and Con<sup>fi</sup>rmative Contribution

To verify the impact of virtual crowdedness on feedback and confirmative contributions (Hypotheses 3A and 3B), we further define the proportion of feedback contribution (PropComment) and proportion of confirmative contribution (PropThumbUp) as dependent variables (i.e., proportions of comments made or thumbs-up given among all the contributions in NTA i at time t):

$$
y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {1} V C _ {i t} + \gamma_ {1} Z _ {1, i t} + \gamma_ {2} Z _ {2, i} + \gamma_ {3} D a t e + \gamma_ {4} H o u r + \varepsilon_ {i t}.\tag{3}
$$

We apply the pooled OLS, fixed effects, and random effects models using the same control variables and time dummies. However, as mentioned before, it is possible that there are zero-valued total contribution counts for a given NTA i at time t. In this case, the measures of the proportions are invalid and may cause a sample selection issue. Thus, if the sample selection is associated with the characteristics of the NTA at that time, the estimated effect would be biased. We ignore the observations with invalid proportional values, whereas in a robustness check, we employ the Heckman selection model (Heckman 1979) to deal with selection bias. Briefly, in the main stage of the Heckman model, we include the identical set of cova riates, whereas in the selection stage, we include additional variables to satisfy the exclusion restriction for the Heckman selection model. We discuss the details about the Heckman selection model in Online Appendix B.

To test Hypothesis 2, we utilize the dichotomy rush hour and non-rush hour as the proxy for physical crowdedness. ## 6.4. Alternative Analysis Based on Matching Method

We conducted panel data regression analyses with a rich set of control variables to examine the relationship between virtual crowdedness and total contribution. The conclusions are valid only if virtual crowdedness is exogenous because the included control variables capture all the alternative explanations, including the dynamic traffic status that influences a user’s decision on contribution. If the exogenous assumption does not hold, the relationship should be regarded as an association rather than causality. In this regard, we apply the matching estimators to mitigate endogeneity as a result of alternative explanations on the impact of virtual crowdedness on total contribution. In contrast with most studies that employ matching (where the treatment variable is dichotomous), we have a continuously valued treatment variable— virtual crowdedness. We assign observations into multiple treatment groups according to the heterogeneous levels of virtual crowdedness, and then we test their effects on total user contribution. Specifically, we discretize the continuous treatment variable $\left( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } V C _ { i t } \right)$ into five groups by creating five equally wide intervals, from 0 to 50, along with its distribution after excluding zeros. Data observations are classified into five groups ranging from group 1 $( 0 < V C _ { i t } \leq 1 0 )$ , representing very low virtual crowdedness, to group $\mathrm { ~ \bar { ~ } { ~ } ~ } ( 4 0 < V \bar { C } _ { i t } \leq \bar { 5 } 0 )$ , representing very high virtual crowdedness.

Next, we employ matching estimators between every pair of adjacent groups using total contribution as the outcome variable. Specifically, we define the observations in the higher group as the treatment group and observations in the lower group as the control group (e.g., group 2 as the treatment versus group 1 as the control). Because the size of the lower group is much larger than the size of the higher group, we apply a one-to-one matching algorithm without replacement to find a most similar “counterfactual” match for each observation in the higher group, to obtain the average treatment effect on the treated (ATET). We apply coarsened exact matching (CEM), which approximates a fully blocked experimental setting, as suggested by King and Nielsen (2019).<sup>14</sup> CEM overcomes one of the biggest drawbacks of propensity score matching (PSM) in that it requires a predetermined functional form for the first-stage model. In brief, we first discretize the major continuously valued variables into four quartiles and find exact match candidates based on discretized continuous variables and categorical variables. Then, among the exact match candidates, we choose the observation in the control group having the smallest Mahalanobis distance to the focal observation in the treatment group. To verify the effectiveness of the matching process, we follow previous literature (Haviland et al. 2007, Xu et al. 2017) to perform a balance check by comparing the standardized bias before and after matching. The balance check results in the Online Appendix D demonstrate that coarsened exact matching leads to much more balanced treatment and control groups.

As shown in Table 5, the estimated effect of virtual crowdedness is significant with the largest magnitude between groups 2 and 1 $( \mathrm { A T E T } = 6 . 1 \bar { 8 } 2 , p \mathrm { - v a l u e } <$ 0.001). Moreover, the effect is still significant but much smaller for the comparison between groups 3 and $2 ( \mathrm { A T E T } = 3 . 1 9 3 , \mathrm { } p \mathrm { - v a l u e } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and between groups 4 and $3 \mathrm { \ : ( A T E T = 4 . 0 1 6 , \ : p – v a l u e < 0 . 0 0 1 ) }$ . We observe an insignificant effect between groups 5 and 4 (ATET = <sup>−</sup>0.298, p-value = 0.850). For robustness checks, we apply alternative matching estimators— namely, PSM and nearest-neighbor matching—and the results are shown in Online Appendix $\mathrm { E } ^ { 1 5 }$ The results demonstrate a clear pattern in the relationship between virtual crowdedness and overall user con tribution: the impact of virtual crowdedness on total user contribution fades away as virtual crowdedness increases; in highly crowded areas, a unit increase in virtual crowdedness only leads to a few additional contributions. The result supports the concave-down increasing trend of total contribution with respect to virtual crowdedness, after mitigating the endogeneity issue by matching.

Furthermore, we split the entire data into non-rush hour and rush hour subsamples and conduct the matching estimations for each subsample. As shown in Table 5, both subsamples show a reducing effect of virtual crowdedness, and the positive effect of virtual crowdedness is higher in rush hours compared with non-rush hours. However, we could not thoroughly verify the moderating effect of physical crowdedness (Hypothesis 2) using a subsample matching method, because we do not observe a stronger concavity $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the effect declines faster) for the rush hour subsample. One plausible explanation is that we split the data into non-rush hour and rush hour, whereas the two subsamples are dissimilar in terms of confounding factors that influence total contribution.<sup>16</sup>

## 6.5. Alternative Analysis Based on Panel Data Spatial Models

One of the issues of NTA-aggregated data is that NTAs are artificially determined geographical areas that are not separated by physical boundaries in the real world. For example, drivers on the different sides of the same street might belong to two different NTAs. Because NTAs are geographically connected, Waze users’ behavior in neighboring NTAs may correlate to each other as a result of the interactions between adjacent areas. This is the so-called spatial autocorrelation in econometrics literature (Elhorst 2014). Without considering spatial autocorrelation, the estimated result may lead to substantial biases. Therefore, we apply spatial econometric models to check the robustness of our main conclusions under spatial autocorrelation. Specifically, to deal with the spatial nature of our NTA-level data, we apply a panel data spatial autoregressive model (panel SAR) for total contribution,<sup>17</sup> specified as follows:

$$
y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \rho \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} w _ {i j} y _ {j t} + \boldsymbol {\beta} \mathbf {X} _ {i t} + \gamma_ {1} D a t e + \gamma_ {2} H o u r + \varepsilon_ {i t}.\tag{4}
$$

In this equation, $\mathbf { X _ { i t } } = [ V C _ { \mathrm { i t } } , V C _ { \mathrm { i t } } ^ { 2 } , V C _ { \mathrm { i t } } \times R u s h H o u r _ { t } ,$ $V C _ { \mathrm { i t } } ^ { 2 } \times R u s \hat { h } H o u r _ { t } , Z _ { \mathrm { 1 , i t } } , Z _ { 2 , \mathrm { i } } ]$ is the same vector of covariates that we have used in the quadratic regression model in the previous subsection. To capture the spatial autocorrelation, we construct the $n { \mathrm { - } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { b } } \mathbf { y } { \mathrm { - } } n ^ { 1 8 }$ weighting matrix W, which describes the geographical connections between any two NTAs. Specifically, the nby-n spatial weighting matrix W is expressed as

$$
W = \left( \begin{array}{c c c c} \omega_ {1 1} & \omega_ {1 2} & \ldots & \omega_ {1 n} \\ \omega_ {2 1} & \omega_ {2 2} & \ldots & \omega_ {2 n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \omega_ {n 1} & \omega_ {n 2} & \ldots & \omega_ {n n} \end{array} \right),
$$

where $\omega _ { i j } = 1$ if zone i and zone $j$ share at least a boundary or a point, $\omega _ { i j } = 0$ if they do not share any boundary or point, and $\begin{array} { r } { \omega _ { i j } = 0 \mathrm { i f } i = j . } \end{array}$ . In other words, element ω of the spatial weight matrix W is equal to 1 if NTA i and NTA j are neighboring or equal to 0 if NTA i and NTA j are not neighboring to each other. The diagonal elements $\omega _ { i i }$ are set to 0, because, in principle, we do not allow spatial effects from themselves. We take row normalization such that the sum of elements in each row equals 1. Using the row-normalized spatial weighting matrix W, the spatial autoregressive term $\begin{array} { r } { ( \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { N } w _ { i j } y _ { j t } ) } \end{array}$ represents the vector of average total contribution levels of the neighboring NTAs. Therefore, the parameter $\rho$ for the spatial autoregressive term captures the average spatial effect (a form of autocorrelation) of the neighboring areas on the focal area. We utilize the same set of NTA-specified fixed effects and time fixed effects in the panel SAR model In addition, user behavior may demonstrate some time delay, which indicates the existence of temporal autocorrelation along the time dimension. To deal with temporal dynamics in addition to spatial dynamics, we further apply the dynamic panel data spatial autoregressive model (panel DSAR):

$$
\begin{array}{c} y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \rho_ {1} y _ {i, t - 1} + \rho_ {2} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} w _ {i j} y _ {j t} + \rho_ {3} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} w _ {i j} y _ {j, t - 1} + \beta X _ {i t} \\ + \gamma_ {1} D a t e + \gamma_ {2} H o u r + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

In the dynamic SAR model, the additional timelagged dependent variables $\left( y _ { i , t - 1 } \right)$ and time-lagged spatial autoregressive term $\begin{array} { r } { ( \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { N } \dot { w } _ { i j } y _ { j , t - 1 } ) } \end{array}$ together handle the temporal dynamics that may influence total contribution in the focal NTA. The former captures the temporal dynamic effect from the previous period’s dependent variable of the focal area, similar to the autoregressive term of the dynamic panel data models, whereas the latter captures the average spatial dynamic effect from the previous period’s dependent variable of the neighboring areas. Again, we estimate the panel DSAR model with the same set of covariates as before.

We present the panel SAR estimation result in columns (1) and (2) of Table $6 ,$ which shows that the main results still hold after controlling for spatial autocorrelation. As shown in column (1), the coefficient of the linear term of virtual crowdedness is significantly positive (coefficient = 0.626, p-value < 0.001), whereas the coefficient of the squared term is significantly negative (coefficient = <sup>−</sup>0.006, p-value < 0.001). The spatial autoregressive term, $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { N } w _ { i j } y _ { j t } , } \end{array}$ has a significantly positive effect (coefficient = 0.353, p-value < 0.001), which has directly verified the existence of positive spatial autocorrelation. Column (2) has verified our theoretical prediction on the interaction between virtual crowdedness and physical crowdedness, with the same coefficient signs. One of the advantages of the spatial autoregressive model is that one could derive direct, indirect (spillover), and total marginal effects of exogenous variables on the dependent variable. Specifically, if a particular explanatory variable in a certain area changes, not only would this affect the dependent variable in the focal area but also cause the dependent variables in all the neighboring areas to change. The former is called direct marginal effect, and the latter is called indirect or spillover marginal effect; the summation of them is called total marginal effect. We calculate marginal effects of the main explanatory variables and show the results in Online Appendix F. We find that, quantitatively, the direct effect of virtual crowdedness is about 2/3 of the total effect of virtual crowdedness, whereas the spillover effect is about 1/3 of the total effect. The total effect is quite close to the marginal effect estimated by the nonspatial fixed effects model. It showcases that it is meaningful to capture spatial autocorrelation in such research context, as the virtual crowdedness in the focal area will have an influence (in the same direction) on total contribution in the neighboring areas. Similarly, for the moderating model, we find that the direct effects for the moderating effect of physical crowdedness are smaller than that estimated from the (nonspatial) fixed effects model, whereas the indirect effect is substantial.

## 6.6. Additional Robustness Checks

To strengthen our conclusions, we conduct two additional robustness checks. First, our main result is based on an hourly pseudo-panel data from which we demonstrate the relationships between different types of user contributions and virtual (physical) crowdedness, given the fast-changing nature of local real-time traffic condition. As the one-hour discrete time window is defined artificially, one could argue that the main empirical results might be sensitive to the length of the time window. To show the robustness of the time window choice, we conduct a sensitivity analysis by perturbing the time window; specifically, we redefine the observation unit as an NTA at a three-hour time interval,<sup>20</sup> and we rerun the models for testing all the hypotheses. Consequently, we obtain qualitatively consistent results for the predicted relationships (see Online Appendix C).

Second, in the main analysis, we employed a quadratic form of virtual crowdedness to verify its relationship with user total contribution. Such an artificial parametric approach might be quite restrictive to reflect the true relationship behind it. For robustness checks, we instead measure virtual crowdedness without any parametric functional form by creating a series of dummy variables for virtual crowdedness values in intervals from 0 to 50 and test Hypotheses 1 and 2. Again, the result is highly consistent, as the estimated effects of virtual crowdedness dummies increase in an increasing concave trend, and such concavity for rush hour observations is stronger (see Online Appendix C). To visualize the result, we depict the estimated effects of virtual crowdedness (with confidence intervals) as well as the graphical comparison between rush hour and non-rush hour cases in Figure 5.

## 7. Discussions and Conclusion

It is essential to understand what encourages or discourages mobile app users to engage in mobile virtual communities by contributing to UGC as a public good even without employing any monetary rewards. Our main findings suggest that virtual crowdedness plays a pivotal role in encouraging users on a mobile navigation app to contribute to their mobile virtual community. The effects of such virtual and spatial factors on social engagement vary by its degree and the types of user contribution.

## 7.1. Implications for Research

Our findings fill a gap in the related literature on social mobile technology, as prior research has almost exclusively focused on mobile commerce or platform models. Most recent studies have mainly focused on mobile app design and advertising characteristics or on aspects of consumer behavior in the mobile commerce app context (e.g., Ghose and Han 2014 and Han et al. 2016). Moreover, consumers’ use of mobile apps and responsiveness to mobile promotions has been exhaustively explained by contextual factors (e.g., Ghose and Han 2011 and Luo et al. 2014). Andrews et al. (2016) find that physical crowdedness has a positive effect on consumer responses to hypercontextual mobile advertising. Our findings extend the current understanding of mobile app usage to the case of a crowdsourced public good. That is, socially crowdsourced traffic information depends not only on physical factors alone but also on virtual factors.

Our main findings lead to several theoretical insights. First, as hypothesized, virtual crowdedness has a positive and concave-down relationship with total user contribution. The concave-down increasing relationship suggests that the reciprocity among Waze users varies by the degree of virtual crowdedness. The positive relationship depicts that prosocial behavior in terms of total user contribution increases as virtual crowdedness becomes denser. However, the additional user density slows down the increasing trend of total contribution beyond a certain threshold of user density. As more people use the platform, the collective users tendency to be free riders increases; therefore, user contribution is decreasing in the growth of user density. The findings are theoretically explained by the tension between the audience effect and bystander/content saturation effect, which together influence the decisions of potential contributors. In particular, potential contributors, as rational decision makers, will be involved in a cost-benefit trade-off to decide whether to contribute content. Virtual crowdedness becomes a double-edged sword for total contribution, probably because it not only signals the potential demand of content but also signals other available contributors. Given the diminishing return of virtual crowdedness on total contribution, our result implies that the bystander/content saturation effect is relatively more salient than the audience effect, implying that virtual crowdedness reduces prosocial behavior per user.

Second, the relationship between virtual crowdedness and total contribution is more concave-down for rush hours with higher physical crowdedness, compared with non-rush hours with lower physical crowdedness. This finding suggests that for rush hours with higher physical crowdedness, the increasing but concave influence of virtual crowdedness on total contribution is amplified. That is, for rush hours, virtual crowdedness more effectively intensifies total contribution at the beginning, but it is also more harmful to total contribution at the end. The moderating role of physical crowdedness is probably because real-time crowdsourced local traffic information is more valuable to users with a higher physical crowdedness. Therefore, physical crowdedness, which is associated with severe traffic congestion and frequen automobile accidents during rush hours, could intensify the influence of virtual crowdedness on the total con tribution of Waze users.

Finally, we find that virtual crowdedness has an impact on contribution type, as it could sheer users to focus on initial information sharing via alert postings, which is regarded as initial contributions, or to additional information sharing via comments and thumbsup acknowledgments, which is defined as secondary contributions. We conjecture that secondary contributions are probably less sensitive to the bystander/ content saturation effect so that it should become more attractive to contributors when virtual crowdedness increases. Our empirical results have verified that the proportions of both feedback contribution (i.e., comments) and confirmative contribution (i.e., thumbs up) are positively associated with virtual crowdedness.

## 7.2. Implications for Practice

These findings provide mobile app designers and developers with practical insights on how to encourage app users to engage in user contribution to UGC further, as follows. First, the highly concave relationship between virtual crowdedness and total user contribution may be explained by the dominance of the bystander/content saturation effect over the audience effect in our research context. It implies that displaying users’ virtual activities on the Waze map can both encourage or discourage prosocial behavior of a group of users (as a whole community), depending on the perceived degree of user density. Based on our results, the Waze app needs to display a proper density of users on the navigation map, which sometimes could differ from the actual density of the crowd. For instance, when the virtual community is highly crowded with active members, it is reasonable to shrink the group size of the displayed members.

Second, the relationship between total contribution and virtual crowdedness tends to plateau after a certain threshold, which causes a bottleneck in content generation.

This reduced increasing rate of total contribution is more severe in rush hours with high physical crowdedness. One of the key challenges for a crowdsourced content platform is to encourage mobile app users to continue sharing information and breaking the content bottlenecks. Because contributors are motivated by positive social image resulting from prosocial behavior, the platform could leverage social interactions to incentivize voluntary contribution. For instance, platform designers may reinforce the positive social image of major or top contributors who are of vital importance to the survival of such crowdsourced content platforms by spotlighting their contribution. In virtual communities, gamification tools such as badges and status hierarchy tend to be effective in encouraging major contributors. In addition, platform designers may amplify the motivational power of audience effect by echoing the positive feedback and gratitude from the other users. In our context, contributors who posted alerts could be further informed by the app about the cumulative number of feedback comments and thumbs-up acknowledgments they received.

Third, we find that users make a secondary, rather than an initial, contribution when they have a moderate (i.e., not too high) motivation for prosocial behavior. That is, secondary contribution is less impacted by the bystander/content saturation effect compared with initial contributions. The results provide evidence of our theoretical foundation that users are involved in a cost-benefit trade-off when deciding not only whether to contribute but also what to contribute. Although a secondary contribution is an important complement to an initial contribution, platform designers could also take actions to nudge users toward initial contributions. For example, to reduce the production cost of initial contribution, platform designers may introduce speech recognition features so that users could post initial alerts without tapping on the smartphone screens. Similarly, to increase the reputational reward associated with an initial contribution, platform designers could build reputation systems that assign a higher amount of nonmonetary rewards (e.g., reputation score) for initial contribution than for secondary contribution. With such design features, crowdsourcing platforms could maintain optimal proportions for different types of contributions.

## 7.3. Limitation and Future Direction

This study also suffers from some limitations. First, because of data limitations, we are not able to keep track of each driver’s behavior over time. Therefore, we cannot study the probability of content generation for a particular user conditional on personal characteristics as well as physical and virtual contextual characteristics. Instead, we position our major analysis using a pseudo-panel data model that tracks cohorts of drivers in each geosegmented area over time. Utilizing multiple empirical methods, we then test the hypotheses using data at an aggregate area level. The NTA-specific fixed effects are included to control for the area-specific unobserved heterogeneity that does not change over time. To handle the spillovers caused by the spatial autocorrelations between behaviors of users in the neighboring areas, we employ spatial econometric models.

Second, the analyses are exposed to the potential endogeneity of virtual crowdedness. To deal with the concern, we draw on previous literature on public goods provision to make reliable theoretical suppor for the hypotheses. We then test the hypotheses and tease out confounding factors with panel data models that include a rich set of control variables regarding location condition, traffic condition, and weather condition, as well as NTA and time fixed effects. Furthermore, the findings are supported consistently not only by multiple estimations such as count data model and Heckman selection model but also by robustness checks based on various matching estimators and spatial econometric models. We believe that such theoretical and empirical efforts are enough to resolve the endogeneity concerns on the findings.

Finally, we acknowledge that rush hour may not be a perfect measure for physical crowdedness, because it potentially correlates with many other observed or unobserved factors. However, we rely on this proxy for the following three reasons. First, congestion pricing policies throughout the world routinely define certain hours of the day on weekdays as a “peak hours” to charge a higher fee from motor vehicles operating in the predetermined urban areas, which provides an economic rationale to the employment of “time of day” as a simple proxy of physical crowdedness. Second, unobserved mental status and personal characteristics of the users may directly impact their perception of physical crowdedness. Because Waze usage corresponds to driving behaviors, there is no reliable way to measure road rage or stress experienced while on the road, which requires telematics data on driving behavior. Because the perceived complexity of road conditions correlates with physical crowdedness, we use a rich set of road condition features to control for that. Third, we believe that such a correlation still exists, even if we find other measures for physical crowdedness, including some proxy measures such as average traffic speed or severity of traffic jams, as well as some direct measures of traffic crowdedness. We acknowledge that no matter what control variables we use, there remain omitted variables that correlate to rush hour, given that our identification strategy does not rely on an entirely experimental setting. Future studies could combine detailed local data and data of driver behavior from telematics instead of using rush hour as a proxy for physical crowdedness.

Future research should explain the complex dynamics of peer production and content generation in the mobile app context. Because of the growth of smartphone devices, it is essential to study voluntary contribution behavior in geographically segmented virtual communities. Besides, future research could examine the economic and social value of crowdsourced content in navigation apps or software. Using our research context as an example, researchers could investigate the effectiveness of crowdsourced traffic information in relieving traffic congestion or solving traffic problems. In a more generic context, researchers might address the social and economic value of socially crowdsourced collaboration among a variety of mobile app users.

## 7.4. Conclusion

Smartphone users are citizens who actively use a variety of mobile apps around urban areas and interact with each other in mobile virtual communities. We explore the impact of design features and the displayed user density, which we term virtual crowdedness, on user behavior in the online mobile virtual communities. This paper describes how users engage in a mobile virtual community regarding the relationship between virtual crowdedness and total user contribution in the specific context of Waze navigation app. We adopt multiple econometric methods to analyze large-scale, locational, and real-time citizen data. Our findings advance the current understanding of mobile app usage by addressing the impact of virtual crowdedness as well as understanding platforms that combine crowdsourcing with peer production.
