# Project preparedness and the emergence of implementation problems in ERP projects

David Sammon \*, Frederic Adam

Business Information Systems, University College Cork, Ireland

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 1 June 2007 Received in revised form 26 June 2009 Accepted 5 September 2009 Available online 19 September 2009

Keywords: ERP CSFs Project preparedness Implementation problems Collective case study

## A B S T R A C T

The problems that organizations face in implementing an enterprise-wide ERP project are linked to their level of understanding of what is involved in such an endeavor and how it influences their initial preparations. We sought to demonstrate empirically the causal relationship between the organization’s preparedness and the emergence of implementation problems. We examined four case studies to extract insight into the criticality of certain factors and the type of problems created when no moderating measures were taken by project managers. Consequently, we developed a predictor-outcome mode mapping a lack of preparedness with implementation problems.

## 1. Introduction

ERP packages are one of the important software purchases of medium and small organizations, though they have been available for more than 10 years and have become the underpinning of IS for many organizations. However, ERP implementation problems are not understood by many managers [10] and their expected benefits are seldom realized. The magnitude and complexity of an enterprise-wide project poses considerable risks; therefore, strategies for controlling risk factors are paramount in achieving some degree of success. Some work has been conducted to identify the key players and activities that contribute to ERP success [17] and it seems that managers who make an initial evaluation of a project, determining its rules, players, goals, constraints, and project manager’s responsibility and authority are more likely to be successful. Thus, an organization’s state of readiness is extremely important. However, little is understood about what it means for an organization to be mindful [19] in its approach to investing in an ERP package.

We conduct an investigation into the managers’ level of understanding of ERP project implementation and the preparations that should be made to increase the likelihood of its success.

To achieve this objective, we begin with a review of the ERP literature on principles of preparedness and its four typical ‘macro implementation problem areas. We then use four in-depth case studies to determine empirically the causal relationship between preparedness and outcomes.

## 2. Principles of preparedness and implementation problem areas for ERP project implementations

While organizations experience problems throughout all phases of the ERP project life-cycle, many of those experienced in later stages originated in the early stages. As in all IT implementations, inadequate user training and lack of user skills have been major reasons for failure. A number of authors have commented on the importance of preparedness for embarking on an enterprise-wide IT project. However, none attempted to examine the impact of these factors on the emergence of specific problems and on the outcomes of the project. We identified the notion of preparedness and classified its components in a framework (see Table 1) in order to formulate an initial set of principles of preparedness to be used in our analysis of case data.

We also attempted to structure the problems encountered by organizations into four macro-level groupings termed implementation problem areas (see Table 2).

These represent a synthesized view of ERP implementation problems. As can be seen from the descriptions, they are also a first step in providing a foundation for a causal link between actions of managers, and events that occur throughout the ERP life-cycle.

## 3. The research approach

A collective case study strategy, with intrinsic studies of several cases, was adopted. By studying a number of cases, we investigated the causal relationships between principles of preparedness and the emergence of implementation problems in ERP projects. To be included in our study, an organization had to have undertaken at least one ERP project, and, at the time of first contact, be in the post-implementation phase for all or part of the project, depending on the implementation approach followed. Originally we found 20 possible organizations and held discussions with 11 on their participation in our research; of these, 4 agreed to contribute to our study. These were: SerCom Solutions, Banta Global Turnkey (BGT) (acquired by R.R. Donnelley in January 2007), the Irish Health Service (now the Health Service Executive – HSE), and An Post. An Overview of these 4 organizations is presented in Table 3.

Multiple cases offer investigators a deeper understanding of processes and outcomes and provide a good picture of locally grounded causality. In our study, the four cases were complementary due to the variety of approaches adopted by the different ERP teams and the broad array of events which they allowed us to study. From the outset, trying to follow these cases led to a better overall understandingof the varietyoforganizational approaches pursued in implementing ERP packages, and the preparations made. We initially decided to take a holistic view of ERP project outcomes and not only focus on their implementation. This was particularly important for three of our four cases, where major follow-on investments were required, contributing to the excessive duration of the projects.

## 3.2. Data gathering

Our primary sources of data were Interviews and Analysis of Documents providing details of the project and its outcomes; we used the documents to corroborate, and clarify the data collected through interviews. A total of 84 h of interviews was conducted on site over a 2-year period. Interviews varied in length from 1 to 3 h and followed a guide (see Appendix A). All personnel interviewed were key decision-makers and the most knowledgeable persons in making the decisions at the start of the ERP project; we met with most several times. All interviews were audio-taped and transcribed. Following the first round, transcripts were sent to the informants for review. During the second/third round of focused interviews, ambiguities and discrepancies were clarified and information from the first round was confirmed. ## 3.3. Data analysis

Lack of analytic validity and a need for transparency and shareability of data and conclusions are issues of importance in qualitative research. Data analysis involves: data reduction, data display, and drawing conclusions; with constant iteration between the analytical stages. Data reduction involves selecting, focusing, simplifying, abstracting, and transforming the data in the field notes or transcripts into summaries, coding, testing themes, etc. This sorts, discards, and organizes the data so that conclusions can be drawn and verified. As part of the data reduction process and in an effort to build a chain of evidence for each case, a series of explanatory effect matrices were used. These matrices also served as a means of data display. Furthermore, as we progressed through each case analysis, a series of mental maps in the form of cause– effect loops were constructed.

A danger exists when investigators reach ‘premature’ or ‘false conclusions’ as a result of information processing bias. However, there are several analysis techniques that can help reduce such errors. In our study, a separate within-case analysis was undertaken for each case. This helped in coping with the excessive volume of data. The process allowed the unique patterns of each case to emerge before attempting to generalize patterns across cases. We then followed this up with cross-case comparison (see Fig. 1).

## 3.3.1. Within-case analysis

The within-case analysis required detailed case study narratives for each site. They provided a common frame of reference allowing better understanding and structuring of the data that informants recalled of their ERP project. Furthermore, these accounts could be triangulated with documentation to ensure that each case was evaluated correctly. Each narrative contained: the organizational background; business rationale and organizational requirement; software selection process; project implementation phase; and the post-implementation environment. Therefore, the case narratives examined the ‘what’ aspect of the approach taken by each organization in their ERP project; insight into the approach taken, the problems experienced, and the system in-use.

## 3.3.2. Cross-case analysis

There are various strategies available in searching for crosscase patterns. We used our narratives to compare the cases systematically, illustrating the similarities and differences between them. The principles of preparedness were used to categorize the preparedness that each organization experienced with its project, and the implementation problem areas were used to categorize its problems. Therefore, the analysis resulted in an explanatory effects matrix, which mapped the principles of preparedness against the implementation problem areas for each case. This resulted in a matrix of case groupings, where the causal link between a lack of preparedness and the emergence of implementation problems dictated the position in the matrix. A group of three cases (BGT, the Health Service, and An Post) was identified as having similar experience for most of the principles of preparedness. The remaining case, SerCom, was then compared to this grouping to highlight contrasting experience in aspects of preparedness and implementation problems. Contrary to our initial expectations and impressions at the start of the cross-case analysis, similarities in the implementation experience between BGT and the Health Service were very close.We used our explanatory effects matrix to show the output of the iterative cross-case analysis process. It confirmed our assumption that a strong causal relationship existed between lack of preparedness and the emergence of implementation problems, when the principles of preparedness are plotted against the four implementation problem areas. An empirical basis for the claim that ‘Y is explained or caused by X’ was used. Ultimately, such causality introduces the question of timing; which requires assumptions about connections that may exist between events. Therefore, once an understanding of what has happened is known, and the question of why can be answered; then the question of what should be done to avoid such problems can be considered.

## 4.1. Preparedness relating to ‘Organizational Goals’ and the impact on ‘The Fit

In SerCom, business change and business restructuring coupled with an inability of the existing systems to support such change were predominant drivers in implementing the ERP project. SerCom was well prepared and had prioritized the critical functional areas that were to be implemented. In contrast, in BGT, Y2K compliance in the US operation was the sole driver of its ERP project and it was never BGTs intention to implement the ERP system in Europe. At the time of project initiation there were no real business objectives defined, other than addressing Y2K compliance. Obviously, this revealed a lack of an enterprise-wide view.

In An Post, the existing reporting systems’ inability to meet business and management information requirements, due to changing customer needs and the requirement to adapt to an increasingly liberalized and competitive environment, were the main drivers for the project. However, An Post failed to evaluate the key processes that affected their goals.

In the Health Service, the vision and primary goal of the project was never clearly articulated, understood, and agreed upon. Where the primary goal of the Personnel, Payroll and Related Systems (PPARS) project may have been to transform Human Resource Management in the Health Service, it was simply perceived as the computerization of employee records and replacement of the existing agency-specific HR/Payroll systems. Furthermore, from the outset, involvement in the project was not compulsory across the Health Service and those agencies involved were given total autonomy on how they approached it. Therefore, no real strategic business need existed and the Health Service failed to identify and prioritize the crucial business processes that needed to be provided.

## 4.1.1. Effect of causal linkage on project outcomes

The existence of a strategic business need and the ability to prioritize the critical business processes is key to ensuring that an ERP project delivers business value and the required functionality. However, the identification of business needs is not enough and managers must use analytical skills to translate their business objectives into actionable and prioritized plans for the ERP project.

## 4.2. Preparedness relating to ‘BPR’ and the impact on ‘The Fit’ and ‘The Plan’

Both BGT and the Health Service implemented the ERP package on a site-by-site basis, over relying on ‘as-is’ site specific business processes, instead of developing standardized and best practice processes. Thus, they never prioritized requirements or introduced reliable and realistic process improvements across the entire organization. SerCom, in contrast, embraced BPR from the start. BGT, realizing (though too late) that they were not receiving any benefits from their ERP, began to introduce BPR through a number of separate process improvement initiatives a number of years after the start of their ERP project. Neither BGT nor the Health Service worked as a single organizational entity and thus lacked an enterprise-wide infrastructure.

The An Post case shows that organizational decision-makers may be aware of the need to analyze business processes and reengineer them, but if ‘as-is’ business processes are assumed to be optimal and simply automated, this awareness will not save the implementation. This fall-back reliance on ‘as-is’ processes could be traced back to a lack of business personnel representing some business units on the project team. An Post experienced delays and cost escalations, while not achieving their desired outcomes.

## 4.2.1. Effect of causal linkage on project outcomes

Developing an in-depth understanding of existing processes and of any changes or improvements that will be made to them is critical in giving organizational actors a reliable vision of the impact of the changes at an enterprise-wide level.

## 4.3. Preparedness relating to ‘Implementation Methodology’ and the impact on ‘The Fit’ and ‘The Plan

In BGT, no formalized approach was adopted in managing the project. As a result, a series of modifications, add-ons, and additional project initiatives, had to be undertaken, to help define a BGT process infrastructure, while addressing some of the critical issues impacting the support and use of the ERP system. They have since adopted a formal approach to undertaking and managing future projects. In fact, they now focus on the importance of documentation; project objective; project scope; project personnel; and project time-frame, as a result of the lack of preparedness at the start of their ERP project.

An Post’s initial decision to implement the Sales Order Processing and Account Receivable modules prior to implementing the General Ledger (GL) module (or indeed even before implementing the new Chart of Accounts) showed poor rationale and highlighted their lack of preparedness. The implementations were carried out in a non-integrated environment, causing additional delays in retrofitting the package when the G/L module was introduced. In addition, phase 1 of the implementation process suffered from inadequate documentation. This, with no record of sign-off by the end-users, contributed to several disagreements; end-users complained that their reports would not run; however, the Business Support Team could not assist, as it did not know how the reports were to function. Finally, throughout phase 1 of the project the JD Edwards OneWorld package was upgraded, adding to the delays in the project schedule. It was therefore clear that An Post’s weak implementation approach contributed to the delays in implementing all remaining modules of the project, within the allocated time and budget.

In the Health Service, preparations for the governance of the project were poor. A 2002 report commissioned by the Irish Government recommended that the issue of governance be addressed if the project was to continue. However, at the beginning of phase 2, no change was made to the approach, inherited from phase 1. The problems suffered by the project were compounded by changes in scope between phases; the implications of these were not understood. Crucially, the needs that were being addressed in phase 2 were not adequately considered or planned; this lead to considerable cost and time overruns and the PPARS project was abandoned.

In contrast to the other three cases, SerCom achieved their goals: the scope, was defined quite narrowly, the timeline for completion of the project was aggressive and not perceived by those involved in the project as being set too far into the future. These elements were instrumental in the success of the ERP implementation.

## 4.3.1. Effect of causal linkage on project outcomes

A lack of a coherent implementation methodology; a failure to emphasize clear and consistent project scope focusing on critical aspects of the business; no appropriate documentation of requirements to be addressed; and no clearly defined lines of authority, responsibility, or accountability leads to substantial problems during implementation.

## 4.4. Preparedness relating to ‘Best Business Personnel’ and the impact on ‘The Actors’, ‘The Change’ and, ‘The Plan

All four organizations experienced some problems across all the implementation problem areas because the principle of ‘Best Business Personnel’ was not addressed at the outset. The IT

Director, whom so many claimed was the key to the ERP system functioning so smoothly in SerCom, believed that possessing internal skill sets was crucial in building flexibility in the system, and being able to continue to manage that flexibility post system delivery. Therefore, while the structure of the project team was important, its diversity (who was representing each functional area), was also important. Within SerCom, certain functional areas fared better than others, depending on the caliber of staff they picked.

In An Post the project team, consisted of predominately external consultants and business resources with low levels of knowledge of business operations (where some of these resources were contract-based or even staffed by newly graduated employees). An example of the types of problems resulting from a failure to understand the importance of allocating the best business personnel occurred in the LetterPost area of An Post, whose team was led by a Sales Accountant; who lacked autonomy. When issues required attention, clearance had to be obtained at a higher level, which resulted in delays in the implementation phase, with constant meetings needed to resolve low level issues.

A similar problem was identified in BGT where attracting and retaining resources of a high caliber proved extremely difficult. The critical element of success from BGTs perspective was to have an agreement between business and IT resources of the skill sets they possessed and then forge a tight working relationship between them. However, they seem to have tried to hire and train, instead of retaining resources with an exceptionally high appreciation of business and IT, and the BGT business.

## 4.4.1. Effect of causal linkage on project outcomes

A reluctance to provide the best internal business resources is rooted in a lack of top management commitment and support for the project. This leads to an immature understanding of existing and future business processes, while expectations of what the ERP package can deliver are not met. However, even when organizations are mindful of this principle of preparedness, implementation problems emerge in areas that are less well served by their representatives. Indeed, personnel that cannot be spared by the business are generally the ones that are most required on the project.

## 4.5. Preparedness relating to ‘Approach to Training’ and the impact on ‘The Fit’, ‘The Actors’, and ‘The Change’

The evidence from our four cases suggests that training did not receive the attention it required and the impact of the failure can clearly be seen, particularly in BGT and An Post.

In An Post the ‘super-users’ did not always deliver quality training to the end-users in the business units. A similar experience was observed in SerCom, where the quality of training delivered to the functional areas by some super-users was inadequate. This led to time delays in the system functionality knowledge being transferred to end-users. The root cause of these problems was that less experienced internal business users became ‘super-users’ for their functional areas, who then trained the end-users in their area.

## 4.5.1. Effect of causal linkage on project outcomes

The importance of embracing the ‘super-user’ concept, using the best internal business resources, has a major impact on the outcomes of a project and an organization’s ability to use and support the system after its delivery.

## 5. Conclusions

Overall, SerCom displayed excellent awareness and preparedness for their enterprise-wide ERP project initiative compared to the other organizations. As a result, it experienced a successful project outcome and can be characterized as having been mindful in their approach to their ERP project.

![](/api/attachments/28WCAM9Y/fulltext/images/40c00fa6c6b2e1581af6b43706c5a2e08ee4c4cb8d6e3ca17b24b690e98a4a2e.jpg)  
Fig. 2. A causal model of the preparedness-outcome relationship. The font size and style of the four impacts on outcomes represents the extent to which they are affected by low levels of preparedness.

An Post demonstrated a high level of awareness for the project initially, but, their preparation was inadequate at the planning stage and they then faced a number of setbacks throughout the project life-cycle. It can be characterized as a deviant case, in that managers displayed a high awareness of what was involved, but failed to translate it into proper preparation for the project.

Both BGT and the Health Service shared a number of common characteristics in terms of their experiences with their ERP projects. Both found themselves undertaking a large IT project, without the required level of business support. This lack of preparedness in the area of standardization and deployment of an enterprise-wide business process infrastructure lead to an extended project time-frame, with an associated escalation in costs, and a poor fit between the software and the business.

Finally, BGT, the Health Service and An Post demonstrated a lack of appreciation of an enterprise-wide view of the organization and they paid very little attention to standardizing processes that increased the efficiency of the business.

We believe that a mindful preparation for an ERP project should lead to fewer implementation problems. In particular, the attention of all relevant organizational actors must be focused on the need for proper preparation and on the early identification and correction of potential problems. Our understanding of the causality between preparedness, implementation problems and project outcomes are presented in Fig. 2.

Our core finding is that the level of fit between the ERP application and the organization is the most heavily dependent outcome. ‘The Fit’ is impacted by the five key principles of preparedness. Our second core finding is that the ‘Best Business Personnel’ principle of preparedness should be the primary focus of management at the start of an ERP project, as it can have negative impacts on all outcomes.

Our study justified these observations and decomposed the underlying causality between the way organizations prepare for enterprise-wide ERP projects and the benefits they stand to achieve, based on the actual outcome of the implementation.

## Appendix A. First round interview guide

1. A certain problem existed for which a solution was required. How did the organization identify the problem and the subsequent solution?

2. What was the business vision/logic which provided a strong rationale for the ERP project?

3. What technological (systems), organizational (process) and financial inefficiencies were identified and how was it perceived that they constrained current and future business needs?

4. What type of systems and applications existed on the legacy systems? In what way were these applications deficient? What business requirements did these applications fulfil?

5. What amount of organizational change was envisaged?

6. What approach did the organization follow in justifying the investment required by the acquisition of ERP?

7. What were the expected benefits from the introduction of the ERP?

8. In light of the fact that a decision was made to implement an ERP (problem identification), how was the package evaluation and selection process conducted?

9. In what way did any of the decisions made at this ‘problem identification’ stage affect the project’s implementation?

10. What were the expected benefits of the project?

11. How did the project team manage the expectations of the different stakeholders in the organization?

12. What type of tangible and intangible improvements were realized following the implementation?

13. What factors were critical to the successful implementation of the ERP project?

14. Why and how were these factors critical to the ERP project implementation?

15. In having implemented an ERP system, what would you do differently?
