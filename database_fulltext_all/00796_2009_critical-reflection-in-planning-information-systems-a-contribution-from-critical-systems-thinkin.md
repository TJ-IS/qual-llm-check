# Critical reflection in planning information systems: a contribution from critical systems thinking

José-Rodrigo Córdoba

Centre for Systems Studies, Business School, University of Hull, Cottingham Road, Hull, HU6 7RX, UK, email: j.r.cordoba@hull.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper presents a methodological framework to support the process of information systems (IS) planning in organizations. It draws on the ideas of critical systems thinking (CST), a research perspective that encourages the analysis of stakeholders’ understandings prior to the selection and implementation of planning methods. The framework emphasizes continuous identification of concerns from stakeholders, and facilitates critical reflection in the exploration of possibilities for improvement. Some of these possibilities might require the support of IS and communication technologies. To define the framework, two systems theories are used: boundary critique and autopoiesis. The first one enables critical reflection on values and assumptions about potential situations or marginalization. The second one fosters continuous dialogue, listening and mutual collaboration between participants. With these theories, the framework enables people to reflect on issues of inclusion, exclusion and marginalization, and to participate in the design of plans for improvement. Ultimately, the definition of this framework suggests the importance of critical reflection about ethics to improve the practice of IS planning.

Keywords: information systems planning, critical systems thinking, boundary critique, autopoiesis, improvement, ethics

## INTRODUCTION

Organizations are currently using information technologies (IT) as a necessity for survival. The times when IT and information systems (IS) associated were considered rare, a luxury or even advantage are past. With increasing collaboration and new forms of networking (Castells, 1996), the use of IS can open new opportunities for communication and interaction. For IS planning, new technologies and ways of working bring new challenges for organizations. Their rapid incorporation to business processes makes it difficult to reflect on the thinking that underpins the definition of plans to design and implement IS in organizations (Galliers, 2004).

There is an emerging number of approaches to support the definition and design of IS in organizations (Fitzgerald et al., 2002; Avison & Fitzgerald, 2003), and it is often assumed by these that the goals of IS planning are the by-product or result of commitment to organizationa goals and membership (Earl, 1993; Earl, 1999). Implicitly, it is assumed that individuals can easily make these goals as their own (Willmott, 1993).

The inclusion of people and their perspectives has been facilitated with participative and systemically oriented processes of IS planning (Checkland, 1990; Galliers, 1995; Mingers, 1995c; Stowell, 1995a; Checkland & Holwell, 1998; Champion & Stowell, 2001), and IS plans are designed to address different issues regarding the implementation of IS. However, the process of IS planning could generate situations of marginalization of people when certain groups of people privilege their interests at the expense of other groups (Ulrich, 1983; Ivanov, 1991; Jackson, 1992; Midgley, 2000; Howcroft & Wilson, 2003; Córdoba & Midgley, 2006). Therefore, the process and goals of IS planning should be subjected to critique by those groups of individuals who are to be involved and affected by IS plans and designs.

This paper presents a framework to IS planning that fosters inclusion and critical reflection. Inclusion means the inclusion of different stakeholder groups and their concerns about the process of planning. Critical reflection involves promoting awareness on potential implications of situations of marginalization of people and issues throughout the process. The framework is inspired on critical systems thinking (CST), a research perspective that encourages the analysis of stakeholder understandings prior to the selection and implementation of planning methods. Using the systems theories of boundary critique and autopoiesis within CST, inclusion is developed in relation to people’s concerns on their ways of life and potential consequences when individuals or their concerns become marginalized from decision-making in IS planning.

The paper begins by situating current developments in the realm of IS planning, highlighting the importance of reflecting about the inclusion of people and their concerns or goals in this process. The ideas of CST are introduced, and the theories of boundary critique and autopoiesis are explained in detail. Based on these theories, a methodological framework to support IS planning is defined. Reflections from using this framework in practice lead to consider the issue of ethics of the process. The paper concludes by assessing the usefulness of the framework for IS planning, and by highlighting the importance of critical reflection on ethics to complement the use of CST.

Before we start, it is important to declare two important assumptions that influence the definition of this framework. First, information systems are considered social artefacts that people can shape according to their interests and their particular context of action, interests and perspectives (Zuboff, 1988; Checkland, 1990; Hirschheim et al., 1991; Checkland & Holwell, 1998). This notion aims at embracing the process of IS planning as a social process, where different concerns and interests of people can be included to inform the definition of IS plans to facilitate emancipation and improvement.

Second, the framework emphasizes the importance on ethics as an issue related to the marginalization of people and their concerns in IS planning. Ethics in this paper is defined according to systems thinking (Churchman, 1979; Midgley, 2000), where it is conceived of as reflecting on and acting upon potential consequences of actions for oneself and others so their conditions can be improved. Towards the end of the paper, this view is updated to involve dealing with tensions between different ethical perspectives, with each of them having thei own view of what doing ‘good’ is or should be.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS (IS) PLANNING

Traditionally, IS planning has been conceived of as a process of defining the goals of using IS, as well as identifying the most appropriate areas and applications for implementation of technology to support such systems (Lederer & Sethi, 1988; Earl, 1993; Lederer & Salmela, 1996; Galvis, 1997; Robson, 1997; Galliers et al., 1999; Piccoli & Ives, 2005). Through the process of IS planning, organizations define their use of information and communications technologies to support organizational strategies (Earl, 1998; Peppard & Ward, 2004). To date, the field of IS planning in organizations remains an area in continuous development (Galliers, 2004), in which a variety of views and perspectives can be identified.

Ward & Griffiths (2002) and Galliers (2004) distinguish several ‘eras’ in the development of IS planning and design approaches. Although not developed sequentially, in each era there are different purposes and hence different approaches to this process. The first two eras focus on computer data processing (DP) and management of information. Their value resides in informing the design of systems to make current activities more efficient. As Nolan (1979) argues, the possibility of using computer systems in organizations generates concerns about solving operational problems (DP). This is followed by a spread of computer use and the control the use and access to information (management of information). A third ‘era’ in IS planning arises because of the need of organizations to focus on their customers and to generate competitive advantages with IS (Lederer & Sethi, 1988; Galliers, 2004). In this era, emphasis on external relations and competitiveness can lead organizations to rethink their activities and even redefine the scope of their business. This can be achieved by incorporating IT for e-business, enterprise resource planning and knowledge management (Galliers, 1999).

In the ‘fourth’ era of IS planning, the importance of aligning the definition and use of IS with business strategies and organizational structures has been reviewed and contested (Galliers, 1993; Sabherwal et al., 2001). IS planning is also being conceived of as a continuous and emergent (Mintzberg & Waters, 1985) process of learning (Reponen, 1998), and therefore characterized by continuous tinkering, improvisation and innovation (Ciborra, 1994) in the use of IS. Thus, IS planning can be better understood as a continuous making sense of how IS and technologies can help businesses in different aspects (Earl, 1993; Galliers, 2004; Peppard & Ward, 2004). Making sense include the definition of IS strategies (why), their content (what), their associated IT (how), their management of change (when), and their implementation (who and with whom) (Galliers, 1999). The result is the development of organizational capabilities (Galliers, 1995; Ward & Griffiths, 2002; Peppard & Ward, 2004) that are reflected in the underlying organizational structures, culture, skills and competencies of people, and which enable the continuous exploitation of information and technologies to generate sustainable (and not partially competitive advantage) (Earl, 1993; Peppard & Ward, 2004).

Through the above eras, in particular in the latter one, a common assumption to support IS planning is that participation of stakeholders contributes to success (Mumford, 1983; Wood-Harper et al., 1985; Checkland & Scholes, 1990; Earl, 1993; Galliers, 1995; Stowell, 1995b; Remenyi & Sherwood-Smith, 1999). With the participation and involvement of people, a continuous process of learning can take place, and, therefore, organizations can develop their IS capabilities. Participation enables continuous communication between stakeholders and facilitates the adequate choice of appropriate planning strategies (Galliers, 1991; 2004) to develop sustainable advantages. However, how involvement of stakeholders takes (or should take) place and unfolds through IS planning is not explicitly subjected to critical reflection. There is a strong emphasis on getting people to commit themselves ‘to embrace the right behaviours and values to work with information’ (Peppard & Ward, 2004, p. 171) – in other words, getting people to contribute to organizational goals via IS planning. There is little, though, on reflecting about how people’s inclusion in IS planning can be improved considering their own values, concerns and ideas.

Therefore, in IS planning, there is a need to provide alternative frameworks to enable those involved and affected by it to critically reflect on and develop the inclusion of their concerns through the process. As it will be seen later in the paper, critical thinking in IS planning should also include reflection on potential situations of marginalization where different groups of stakeholders and their own values, concerns or goals about the process might not be taken into account in decision-making.

## CRITICAL SYSTEMS THINKING (CST)

Participation of people and the inclusion of their views in situations of social design are issues that have been explored in detail in the literature of CST (Flood & Jackson, 1991; Mingers, 1992, 1997; 2005; Flood & Romm, 1996; Midgley, 1996; 2000; Gregory, 1996; Jackson, 2000; 2003a). CST can be understood as a continuous dialogue between systems-thinking practitioners who are concerned with improvement in social design. As a research perspective, CST embraces the following commitments (Midgley, 1996):

• Critical awareness – examining and re-examining taken-for-granted assumptions, along with the conditions that give rise to them.

• Emancipation – ensuring that research is focused on improvement, defined temporarily and locally, taking issues of power (which may affect the definition) into account.

Methodological pluralism – using a variety of research methods in a theoretically coheren manner – becoming aware of their strengths and weaknesses to address a corresponding variety of issues.

With the above commitments, CST explicitly shares with critical theory a concern for critica awareness and emancipation (Alvesson & Willmott, 1992; 1996; Brooke, 2002). Jackson (1992) uses the above commitments of CST to warn IS researchers about the use of one unique theoretical rationality or methodology in IS development as a ‘one size fits all’. He suggests that it is essential to become aware of the different strengths and weaknesses of theories, methodologies, techniques and methods. This will enable researchers to design a ‘whole system of inquiry’ (Midgley, 2000) to address a variety of questions about a situation, so that different improvements can be discussed and designed.

There are two main strands of developments that can be distinguished currently in CST given their emphasis on the nature of what constitutes ‘true’ pluralism (Ulrich, 2001; Jackson, 2003b) and improvement (Flood & Romm, 1996; Midgley, 2000). A detailed discussion of these two strands can be found elsewhere (Midgley, 2000; 2003; Ulrich, 2001; Jackson, 2003b). What follows is a summary of one of the strands in which this paper is based, that of using systems boundary critique.

## BOUNDARY CRITIQUE

The term boundary critique was first coined by Ulrich (1996) in relation to the work of Churchman on systems boundary judgements (Churchman, 1968, 1970; 1971). Churchman provides an alternative understanding to the notion of a system boundary to traditional concepts used in management science and operations research. For him, a boundary is an intellectual and inter-subjective construct that defines the knowledge and people to be considered relevant for analysis in a social design (Churchman, 1968; 1970). This means that the boundaries of a system are not given by the structure of reality (Midgley, 1996), but defined between people. A social design can be seen as a system that is defined with an idea of what improvement means for the planner or the decision maker of the system (Churchman, 1968). This notion might change if the views of other stakeholders affected and benefited by the actions of the system are included. In other words, what appears to be an improvement for a group of people (decision makers) might not be so if the boundaries of analysis (people to be considered relevant) are ‘pushed out’ (Churchman, 1970). Shifting these boundaries could also lead to the reconsideration of existing understandings of who constitutes a genuine decision maker (Churchman, 1970).

Churchman encourages planners to challenge taken-for-granted assumptions about a social design as a way of genuinely pursuing improvement. This involves putting these assumptions to a process of dialectical debate where different views are ‘swept in’. Only if assumptions stand after debate can planning proceed and secure improvements for present and future generations. This is a way of being critical about systems boundaries adopted for social design.

Churchman’s ideas have inspired a number of further developments in systems thinking (Ackoff, 1981; Checkland, 1981; Ulrich, 1983; Midgley, 2000). For instance, Ulrich (1983) deepens into the nature of critique of boundaries and develops a methodology called Critical Systems Heuristics (CSH) to inquire into situations of social design. Ulrich argues that the process of setting boundaries is intimately linked to human values. To these differences, Ulrich claims that critical thinking needs to be exerted by participants about the boundaries and their values influencing plans. Critique should aim at avoiding ‘hardening’ of some taken-for-granted assumptions that influence decision-making, as well as enabling collective reflection and debate on any implication that decisions will have for different stakeholders. For Ulrich (1983), only when this debate has taken place and people consent on decisions and implications can planning proceed.

Following the work of Churchman and Ulrich, Midgley (1992) is interested in what happens when two groups of people have different judgements and therefore set up different ethica boundary judgements about the same situation. This can be seen in Figure 1.

In the figure, there are two boundaries, and each of them has a set of values (or ethics) associated with them. Midgley (1992) calls the narrower boundary the primary boundary and the wider one the secondary boundary. Between the two boundaries is a marginal area that contains issue or people that are of concern to those operating with the wider boundary, but are excluded from the concerns of those using the narrow (primary) boundary. The margina elements become the focus of conflict. This conflict can sometimes be productive, or when it is judged to be unproductive, it can be resolved, but in many social situations, this kind of resolution does not happen. According to Midgley (1992), situations of conflict are stabilized by the imposition of a sacred or profane status to the marginal elements. When the margina elements are made sacred, the secondary boundary is taken as the main source of decisionmaking and action. Conversely, when a status of profane is attributed to the marginal elements, the primary boundary is reinforced and whatever is in the margins is ignored or devalued. The terms ‘sacred’ and ‘profane’ are not meant in a purely religious sense, but indicate the valued or devalued status of marginalized elements. There is rarely a consensus about whether marginalized issues or people are sacred or profane, but by institutionalizing value judgements in social rituals, the conflict can be stabilized with one set of values dominating.

The above description can be used to guide inquiry about boundary judgements in situations by encouraging reflection on the processes of inclusion, exclusion and marginalization. It can help to bring different perspectives from stakeholders and to understand different assumptions and values. This can also enable participants to identify elements that they have not been recognized when adopting particular boundaries for analysis. With boundary critique, the focus of planning can shift from organizational concerns to others that individuals find valuable and can be considered in the definition of plans. This does not mean that organizational concerns (i.e. efficiency) are abandoned. Instead, reflection on boundaries could support the identification of elements lying between the margins whose treatment might contribute to improve the successful implementation of plans. These elements are often called ‘organizational issues’ or ‘political aspects’ in IS implementation (Doherty & King, 2001; McAulay et al., 2001; Howcroft & Wilson, 2003; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 2003). Their importance is recognized, but little has been said about how to identify and deal with them. Several examples of marginalization (relating to unemployment, customer relations, ecological sustainability and labour disputes) can be found in Midgley (1992; 1994), Midgley et al. (1998) and Yolles (2001).

Thus, the above ideas on boundary critique can enable IS planning practitioners to explore issues of participation and marginalization through the planning process, taking into account wider concerns of individuals, groups and organizations than those usually seen (e.g. marginalized) as impacting on it. A variety of viewpoints, concerns and issues from different stakeholders can be surfaced and ‘swept in’ the analysis (Churchman, 1970), with the possibility of identifying and reflecting on various values, concerns and their supporting assumptions (Ulrich, 1983). In particular, situations of marginalization can be identified to inquire about potential exclusions of people and issues from analysis and decision-making. With different boundaries and purposes for improving a situation being identified, inquiry can be developed by defining a ‘whole’ system of interrelated research questions, each of them to be addressed with a particular method or parts of it (Midgley, 1990). This is called ‘creative design’ of methods, and facilitates the partitioning of methodologies into methods to flexibly address the complexities and emerging issues of concern (Midgley, 1990; 2000).

The use of boundary critique does not mean an endless process. If we recognize that what we consider as relevant knowledge in a situation is dependent on boundary judgements (Midgley, 2000), then inevitably we will be valuing certain elements (issues and people), as well as marginalizing others. The use of boundary critique would serve to support identification of the impacts that any decision could have on different groups of stakeholders. Decisions will have to be made, and therefore a boundary will have to be adopted with its potential implica tions for decision-making and action.

Boundary critique has been employed to inform interventions in the design and implementation of IS (Clarke & Lehaney, 2000a,b; Warren, 2000; Clarke, 2001; Ulrich, 2001; Córdoba & Midgley, 2003). In these situations, its use has led those involved to identify a variety of purposes about a process of IS planning and design, and select appropriate methods for intervention. However, there has been little emphasis on the identification and management o situations of marginalization. This could be partly due to the assumption that in the interventions where boundary critique was used, apparently, there was no unsolvable conflict between stakeholders regarding their goals for IS planning processes (Warren & Adman, 1999; Clarke & Lehaney, 2000b). This paper extends the use of boundary critique into IS planning by surfacing different issues (including marginalization) to inform decision-making. This will enable people to co-design their own IS plans according to what they think is important for their lives as a ‘whole’. In order to support the surfacing of different issues of concern, the main tenets of the theory of autopoiesis are now introduced.

## AUTOPOIESIS

While boundary critique is essentially a social theory, autopoiesis provides a biological explanation of how human beings interact as living systems. A full description of it is beyond the scope of this paper, but more detailed accounts can be found elsewhere (Maturana & Varela, 1987; Mingers, 1995b; Luhmann, 1996). The word ‘autopoiesis’ can roughly be defined as ‘self-producing' (Mingers. 1995b: Luhmann. 1996). An autopojetic or self-producing system is one that seeks to maintain itself and, when it interacts with its environment, it takes actions that are determined by its current structure. Because the organization of the system and its current structure determine what the system is capable to respond to, its external environment can only ‘trigger’ changes, it cannot cause them (Maturana & Varela, 1987; Maturana, 1988).

What this tells us about human beings as autopoietic systems is that we seek to maintain ourselves as living beings through our interactions with our environment. However, our knowledge and understanding is limited, but it can change over time: We are capable of learning with others. We can acknowledge limitations in our views and engage in conversations with others about how do we want to coordinate to coexist together (Maturana, 1998). Over time, a particular use of language to coordinate coordinations of actions may become more and more elaborated, allowing people to exist in very subtle, well-coordinated relationships – and these can be distinguished in language conversations. Therefore, whole rational domains of interaction, relevant to human activity systems, are created in language. People participate in and move between different domains guided by their dispositions for action – essentially, their emotions. Maturana (1988) argues that love has historically been the most important emotion for the development of social relationships, and without love human beings could never have become social animals. Love is an emotion concerned with empathy and a willingness to coexist with and respect each other, as it enables new conversations and coordinations of actions to develop in language.

The theory of autopoiesis has been applied to the understanding of social systems (Luhmann, 1996), public administration (Brans & Rossbach, 1997) and family therapy (Bilson,

1997). In addition, the ideas of autopoiesis have also contributed to a redefinition of the meaning of information in the design of IS as being dependent on the ‘coupling’ of individuals with their external environment (Winograd & Flores, 1987; Mingers, 1995a). Although autopoiesis can be seen as descriptive about the biological nature of relationships between individuals, it also brings an ethical imperative: that of enabling people to recognize the limitations of their views in a situation, to acknowledge that we ‘see what we can see, and what we do not see does not exist’ (Maturana & Varela, 1987, p. 242). Our cognitive systems are limited but not fully determined by our biology. Accepting this should lead people to recognize our limitations and collaborate with each other in language conversations, taking into account the possibility of respecting each other’s concerns.

For the practice of IS planning, this imperative implies that for any improvement of a situation, we can take into account broader concerns that stakeholder groups have in different scenarios of their lives (business, community, family, etc.), as well as the impacts that any decision in IS planning could have in any of such scenarios (Córdoba, 2002). Based on the possibility of sharing a variety of concerns in language conversations, appropriate spaces fo communication can be structured to facilitate cooperation and collaboration. The use of autopoiesis can complement boundary critique by facilitating conversations to identify the boundaries associated with a variety of concerns. The attitude of openness and listening to the concerns of others that autopoiesis brings can inform the process of inquiry by inviting IS planning participants to recognize and value a diversity of views. With autopoiesis, any systems boundary being considered as relevant for IS planning could be brought to discussior and challenged constructively by other observers of the same situation (involved and/or affected), in relation to people and issues that they see as relevant in relation to wider concerns (e.g. marginalized elements). This would enable individuals to be critical about assumptions that are normally taken for granted (including the researchers themselves), identify possible issues of common concern (and therefore opportunities for dialogue), and define ways forward to address these concerns.

Although the use of the theory of autopoiesis can encourage people to accept (in principle) any concern from others as legitimate (they come from people’s own history of conversations in different rational domains), it needs to be said that not every concern should be accepted as ‘desirable’ (Maturana, 1988, p. 31). In this regard, the use of boundary critique can help people to structure debate about the implications of any action for improvement. As it would be difficult to produce plans which are ‘beneficial to all’ with reduced resources (Ulrich, 1983; 1993), the use of boundary critique could help people to identify and address some marginalized elements (concerns and people), and to assess the implications of a particular boundary to guide analysis and decision-making in the IS planning process.

## A METHODOLOGICAL FRAMEWORK FOR IS PLANNING: DISTINCTION, DIALOGUE FOR IMPROVEMENT AND BOUNDARY CRITIQUE

Drawing on the ideas of both boundary critique and autopoiesis, the following is a framework to support IS planning and advance the use of systems thinking in IS practice. There are tw main phases or ‘orientations’ for inquiry. First, there is an orientation of ‘distinction’ in which participants identify a variety of issues of concern about their way of life as a ‘whole’. It is important that individuals bring issues from various domains of action where they live (family, business, community, etc.), and that they are able to surface these through conversations with others within an atmosphere of mutual respect and collaboration. In the distinction orientation, issues are given equal importance to those related to the use of IS or those related to the goals of IS planning (i.e. efficiency and competitive advantage). This gives people the possibility o sharing some concerns with others, acknowledging differences in values and opinions (no necessarily achieving consensus), and establishing an environment of mutual trust and collaboration.

With the above identification, a ‘dialogue for improvement’ orientation can proceed. Participants select some concerns as themes for dialogue. They share their views about the themes selected and explore potential opportunities for improvement. The exploration of themes and possibilities can be supported by the use of any systems-based methodology or method like Soft Systems Methodology (SSM), (Checkland, 1981), Interactive Planning (Ackoff, 1981), Critical Systems Heuristics (CSH) (Ulrich, 1983) or Strategic Assumptions Surface Testing (Mason & Mitroff, 1981). For instance, a SSH root definition (Checkland, 1981) and conceptua model can be developed as a ‘system’ to address a selected set of themes and explore alternative ways of taking action about them. Another example is the use of the questions of CSH (Ulrich, 1983) to help participants reflect on the adoption of boundaries to guide the implementation of any desired improvement on a theme as a system to be improved. Additionally, in the dialogue for improvement orientation, some methods of IS planning can also be used to address aspects related to the provision of information to support action. This is the case for example of methods and techniques from the domains of SSM including Multiview (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990; Avison et al., 1998), client-led design (Stowell, 1995a), objectoriented analysis, information engineering or systems development that can be used to support the definition or design of information requirements.

The above two orientations are complemented by continuous boundary critique. As understandings of a situation evolve in IS planning and implementation (Irani, 2002), new concerns from people might be identified that would require reflecting on the purposes of IS planning and the use of different theories, methodologies or methods. For instance, those facilitating the process might point out elements that participants (or themselves) privilege (i.e. methods or issues of concern) and bring other elements that they think are relevant for the process – in other words, any marginal element. This could then lead to switching between orientations to discuss further about ‘what and who’ is to be benefited (and marginalized) from any action, the values that will drive IS plans, and the implications of adopting any boundary. The process can be seen in Figure 2.

As seen in the figure, the process is iterative between orientations. The distinction of some concerns might (or might not) lead to establishing dialogues for improvement to explore and define possibilities for action in IS plans. At any time of the process, questions of boundary critique and marginalization can be used to reflect on judgements and values about issues, methods and people that are (not) being considered as relevant. The use of these questions aims to encourage participants to reflect on potential situations of marginalization generated by the planning process, as well as to explore the consequences of adopting particular bound aries for analysis and decision-making.

## PUTTING THE FRAMEWORK TO WORK

Having presented the framework, the following is a narrative of how it was used in practice at an IS planning exercise at Javeriana University, Bogotá campus, in Colombia in 1999. A full detail of this project is out of the scope of this paper and can be detailed elsewhere (Córdoba, 2002). The project began when research administrators at Javeriana became interested in having support from Hull University in UK in order to conduct an exploration of the different understandings that people at the institution were having about the role(s) of IT and systems. An initial proposal detailing the framework was sent and approved by the senior research management of Javeriana. The exploration was going to take approximately 6 months to complete, and a research team was set up onsite, including two academics from both Javeriana and Hull Universities.

For the distinction orientation, the plan for the project included initially 10 interviews with staff members, students, project administrators and other stakeholders, as well as four group workshops. The aim was to identify a variety of concerns about the use of IS and relate these with existing plans for improving this use. For the orientation of ‘dialogue for improvement’, it was planned to undertake four exercises of design (as explained later) with people who had taken part in the ‘distinction’ orientation. In the project proposal, it was also stated that additional interviews and group exercises could take place in either orientation as a way of broadening the boundaries of participation in the project (Midgley & Milne, 1995).

## Distinction orientation

During the ‘distinction’ orientation, the aim was to identify a variety of concerns from stakeholders about what they valued as important to improve education in the Colombian society. The research team wanted to identify any commonalities and differences among participants, as well as any possibilities for improvement to be supported by the use of IS. Prior to interviews and workshops of this orientation, the research team prepared a collage of photos about Colombia that was presented to interviewees. In terms of the theory of autopoiesis, these can be regarded as representing different rational or conversational domains that people might be engaged with (Maturana, 1988). The team wanted to generate an atmosphere of mutual respect and engagement with the situation of the institution and the country at that time. Each participant was asked to select two or three pictures that reflected their own concerns. After they selected the pictures, the team asked the following questions:

1 What is your concern about? What are the values associated with it?

2 Why do you emphasize this concern or issue more than others? Are there any marginalized issues we need to consider?

3 If action has already been taken along these lines, why has it not been successful? Are there any possibilities for action now?

4 Who else should be included in this process of inquiry?

5 What other issues of concerns should be taken into account? From whose point of view?

The first question intended to elicit a variety of issues of concern and the values associated. The second aimed at identifying potential issues of concern that were relevant at a particular time, and distinguish potential issues that were being marginalized or excluded from analysis. The third looked at potential possibilities or obstacles for improvement that people saw for the achievement of desired improvements. The fourth and fifth questions encouraged people to include other individuals or issues of concern in the planning process. As a result of the answers to the fourth question, another 10 stakeholders were included in the distinction orientation to be interviewed or invited to take part in a workshop. These included members o the University’s student union, Jesuit priests, other academics and a group of members of a parish related to Javeriana who wanted to be involved. With the students and members of the parish, a workshop was conducted with each group.

In the group sessions, the above questions were shared in groups of three, so people could listen to the concerns of others and identify other stakeholders or issues to be considered in the planning efforts. During the course of interviews and workshops, the team adopted a form of inquiry based on the existence of a ‘critical friend’ for the interviewer and interviewee (Weil, 1998; 2004). The critical friend was either a second member of the research team or the clerical support staff. His/her role was to take notes and to facilitate a critical evaluation of each interview. The critical friend would attempt to identify moments when the interviewer(s) might have imposed his/her (their) own agenda(s), and raise any concerns she/he felt had been marginalizing in the discussion. As stated by the theory of autopoiesis (Maturana, 1988), our way of reading situations is biologically conditioned, and we need to engage in conversations with others in order to identify elements that might be marginalized by their or our own views.

As a result of these activities, a variety of concerns, ideas, values and expectations about how to improve the current situation in the Colombian society as a whole (not only about work-related issues) was collected. The team produced an ‘enriched’ picture that reflected these elements. This is a modified version of a ‘rich’ picture (Checkland, 1981), as it contains elements from the perceived reality and also possibilities for taking action and improving it. A diagram of the picture is presented in the appendix of this paper (enriched picture). The picture was continuously modified as new interviews and group sessions took place, and often participants identified issues of concern for the use of IS at Javeriana and in Colombia in general. Some of their concerns included:

1 The need to establish a culture of respect for each other, collaboration and solidarity.

2 Adopting internationally recognized education standards/practices.

3 Providing access of low-income groups to education.

4 Using information technology (ICT) to support distance education and learning.

During the interviews and workshops, the research team (we) explicitly stated that we were also part of the planning process, and therefore we had particular boundaries that we could be privileging. For instance, at the beginning of each interview/workshop, we declared our views about the importance of considering IS as social constructions as mentioned at the beginning of this paper. We also shared our own concerns and expectations about the use of IS at Javeriana. We declared that Javeriana needed to address the needs of marginalized groups (i.e. distance education students and students with scarce resources) in the definition of IS plans. In this way, we provided a way of avoiding imposing our own views and to engage in constructive dialogue with participants as stated by the theory of autopoiesis (Maturana, 1988).

As a result of this explicit declaration of our concerns, values and boundaries, some participants showed willingness to talk more freely about their own concerns, and we felt we promoted the inclusion of a variety of issues about the use of IS. We also modified our initial methodology, given the fact that some participants showed discomfort with the use of photos to identify concerns. We left the use of pictures as optional for interviews/workshops. This shows that the use of boundary critique enabled us to identify some elements that were being privileged and marginalized, allowing us to reflect on alternative courses of action and their corresponding boundaries. However, this reflection was embedded into tensions. During the course of activities we also noticed that some participants continuously required us to become ‘planning experts’ and lead the implementation of changes. This aspect will be explored in more detail later in the paper.

## Dialogue for improvement orientation

Having produced the enriched picture that contained different concerns about education and the use of IS at Javeriana, we asked participants if they were willing to address some of these concerns in more detail, and explore possibilities for improvement to be included in future IS plans. The majority of them (32 out of 40 after we rolled out the initial boundaries of participation) responded affirmatively. With this response, we then proceeded to move to the ‘dialogue for improvement’ orientation. The aim was to support participants in the design of human activity systems whose actions contributed to address such concerns, and see if any action for improvement would require the design and implementation of IS.

To meet this aim, the research team organized a series of five group ‘design’ workshops. In these, we presented the enriched picture and asked participants to select a set of three or four issues of concern around which we could develop possibilities for improvement. To help in this selection, the research team grouped issues into ‘themes’ to facilitate dialogue. After participants selected some issues or themes, the team proposed defining possibilities for improvement of the current situation in two ways:

1 By developing root definitions and conceptual models of activity systems for issues. These are methods derived from SSM (Checkland, 1981). Conceptual models are systems models that describe the activities that are described in a root definition and are needed to suppor purposeful action in a situation.

2 By designing an ‘ideal’ system that would enable action to address the concerns being identified. This method is taken from Ackoff (1981)’s Interactive Planning Methodology. An ideal system portrays a future scenario where a situation is designed with particular features. The characterization of features enables participants to identify different areas in which action needs to be taken to bring the current situation towards the ideal one. For example, one of the themes identified was that of ‘improving the delivery of education with the use of IS and technologies at the computer science engineering department’.

In both of the above ways, the team also encouraged reflection on boundaries by using Ulrich (1983)’s CSH questions to identify different boundaries that participants considered relevant to guide the definition of any possibility for action, and structure debate about different alternatives. For instance, the above-named system to improve the delivery of education was then subjected to reflection on boundaries, and Table 1 is a summary of the answers obtained from participants.

As seen in Table 1, in this particular example, it is interesting to note the different purposes that IS could support (i.e. preparing students and providing discipline-based knowledge, as well as broad knowledge), and how these lead participants to make explicit their own values. There is also a variety of clients to be served and different stakeholders participating in the implementation of the system (i.e. business organizations. lecturers. students and citizens). The use of systems methods within the framework proposed provided people with opportunities to explore different alternatives for action. It was possible to identify different stakeholders views, associated boundaries, and suggest courses of action to address concerns from different groups of people.

In addition to discussions on boundaries, during the workshops, the research team chal lenged the views of participants by bringing to discussion elements (issues and people) that they perceived were being marginalized. 1 Improving the coordination between projects (including those on information systems and communication technologies and the evaluation of the social impacts that they were to have.

2 Developing an information system to support the delivery of lectures and research projects (discussed above).

## DISCUSSION

It is reasonable to claim that the framework developed to support IS planning enabled the identification of a variety of concerns from different stakeholders of the process. The framework facilitated mutual interaction between people. It helped them to engage in dialogue and to reflect on how their own concerns could be addressed. In the realm of IS planning, it has become important to address a variety of ‘soft’ issues whose treatment could ensure more effective implementation of IS and generate opportunities for organizational change (Galliers, 1993; 2004; Remenyi & Sherwood-Smith, 1999; Doherty & King, 2001; Estevez & Pastor, 2003). At Javeriana, the framework helped participants to identify issues that were considered important to improve the use of IS in the institution (i.e. fostering a culture of solidarity). It also contributed to broadening the scope of IS use (i.e. using IS and technologies to facilitate access to and flexibility in education).

Moreover, the identification of different issues and suggestions for action also generated a context in which IS and technologies were to be used. This context can be characterized in terms of elements that are interrelated. It can be said that in the case of Javeriana, the implementation of IS and technologies reguired developing a culture of respect for each other. broadening opportunities for access to education, and making IS more flexible to requirements for both students and staff. The framework contributed to generate a ‘holistic’ system of initiatives for action whose values, relationships and elements were being defined by partici pants according to what they thought was needed at the institution as a whole. At a more general level, these findings could be used to improve the practice of IS planning and evaluation by informing the definition of plans with broader organizational issues and practices that need to be taken into account to ensure good practice (Galliers, 1991; 2004; Avison et al., 1998; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 1999).

However, the implementation of suggestions for action as a holistic plan proved difficult for various reasons. The suggestions for improvement were presented to the senior research management at Javeriana. They were welcomed and praised for their value and relevance. However, managers found it difficult to commit themselves to their implementation. They asked the research team (the experts) to lead the process of change, to which we declined. In this respect, we made explicit our own values and their corresponding boundaries: we argued that it was the participants (including managers) who should be in charge of taking action about their own concerns, and in this respect, we were privileging our own boundary that marginalized ourselves from implementation. Managers declined to take responsibility for the imple mentation of changes. For them, following the existing plans (with a 5-year horizon) was the right thing to do, and resources had already been allocated to them (including people’s time). The situation can be depicted in Figure 3.

The figure shows two boundaries surrounding the definition of IS: one (primary boundary) that sees them as supporting ‘institutional’ purposes, some of which included using IS (for instance intelligent and online digital libraries) to support Javeriana’s international accreditation. The secondary boundary reflects the outcomes of the planning process generated by participants and the research team. In the latter boundary, other elements were taken into account (i.e. participation of stakeholders, flexible education, culture of solidarity and wider and flexible access to knowledge via IS and technologies). Between these two boundaries, there were tensions that were being stabilized by referring to the existence of institutiona plans. To reinforce the primary boundary (and therefore to devalue the elements lying within the margins), one of the managers strongly argued when being presented with the suggestions for improvement, ‘. . . our [institutional] plans took a great deal of time to elaborate . . . they should not be challenged . . . we should be implementing rather than questioning them’.

Although the existence of conflict is acknowledged by the theory of boundary critique (Midgley, 1992; 2000) little is said about how to deal with it. Midgley acknowledges that the use of boundary critique does not free people from the effects of power. It simply gives people more scope to ‘judge who they are, what situation they are in, and how they should act’ (Midgley, 1997, p. 283). In our framework, the use of the boundary critique enabled us to distinguish different boundaries, but did not provide much guidance on how to deal with tensions emerging when there is disagreement about which boundary needs to be privileged. Neither the use of the systems theory of autopoiesis nor its ethical encouragement for mutual respect helped us to deal with the prevalence of institutional plans that were being defended (mostly by senior managers) as the only valid' ones to be implemented

In our view, the tensions can be characterized as the result of some institutional practices that were being maintained on ‘ethical’ grounds at the expense of new initiatives. Despite being considered as the ‘valid’ and ‘right’ things to do, such practices were not subjected to discussion or review; their underlying values did not seem to be fully known or shared by those involved in the planning process. From this appreciation of the tensions, it can be said that institutional plans were seen as the ‘right [and perhaps the only] thing [possible] to do’ (brackets added). This partly explains why the research team was being delegated as ‘experts’ to lead the implementation of alternative suggestions for improvement (as a way of easing out potential tensions), and why some project participants were ‘too busy’ to undertake other commitments than those currently being assigned by their managers (as a way of not engaging into tensions). It appeared as if the ethics of individuals (including researchers themselves), or what they considered ethically relevant needed to consider – and was influenced by – what institutionally was already being accepted as (in)valid.

The above situation supports Wilson (1997)’s claim that critical research in IS needs to consider situations where debate (i.e. disinterested dialogue) can become closed to matters than can be regarded by some as of common concern. To Wilson, enabling people to gain knowledge through and with open debate (disinterested dialogue) does not necessarily mean that they will generate rationally arguable (and relevant) claims or that they can leave out the historicity of a situation, or that the distribution of power will be more symmetrica among parties after debate. Wilson argues that there needs to be critical awareness about the limitations and value implications of what is being defined as an improvement, as wel as how it is being defined, with a view of considering potential constraints and coercions in the situation at hand. This also includes considering the limitations of using critical research approaches.

Wilson’s argument is useful to help us recognize that in the institutional context of Javeriana there were inevitably tensions between forms of ethics (including ours as ‘participative' and ‘emancipatory’), none of which could be regarded as the ‘true’ one to pursue; each of these forms had their own rationale that considered them valid. After the research team withdrew its participation from the project (based on our own ethical values as explained above), it seemed that an appropriate topic to address was that of power and how it was influencing the ethics of IS planning processes (Córdoba, 2002). In CST, the notion of power has been explored, and the ideas of Michel Foucault (1977; 1982; 1984a, c) have been put forward to inform social interventions (Flood, 1990; Valero-Silva, 1996; Vega-Romero, 1999). Foucault talks about the constitution of individuals as subjects of power. Power is a pervasive phenomenon – it operates across institutions; its operation can target both populations (macro level) and individuals (micro level) at the same time (Foucault, 1977). According to Foucault, power is not an objective force, it only exists individuals influence and are influenced by the conduct of others (Foucault, 1984a). Power becomes a strategic situation, a kind of matrix in which actions influence other actions (Foucault, 1977; 1984a). This also includes the deployment of different ways in which individuals see themselves as being and acting ethically, in other words different forms of ethics (Foucault, 1977; 1984b).

To the influence of power, however, Foucault encourages individuals to ‘no longer being, doing or thinking what we are, do, or think’ (Foucault, 1984c, p. 46), and act within what is possible to do via power, as individuals cannot ever be exterior to power relations (Foucault, 1977). We can recognize the limits of what constitutes ethical action, with a view of reflecting on how we can (re)develop new forms of ethics by using existing power rela tions. At Javeriana, we needed to reflect on and understand how institutional practices of planning were being accepted via power, and relate them to possibilities of developing alternative and ethical ways of proceeding. A possibility could have been to rephrase ou suggestions for improvement within what institutional plans stated as a way of getting them implemented.

In this regard, there is a possible way out (or maybe a way in!) for the problem of emancipation in IS research as highlighted by Wilson (1997). This way ‘in’ could be to include ethical reflection as a way of defining improvements that are appropriate for oneself and others, and that might be in tension with or that could be implemented via existing ethical forms (i.e. existing ways of doing the right thing). Ethical reflection would mean exerting critical thinking about forms of being, doing and acting, which are deployed via power relations in order to use them to pursue improvements more adequately. In other words, ethical reflection means to be able to use power relations to our own advantage as we see fit (Taket, 1994; Brocklesby & Cummings, 1996).

Since our practical use of the framework at Javeriana in 1999, it has been proposed to integrate Foucault’s ideas on ethics with boundary critique (Córdoba, 2002) and other systems thinking methodologies (Córdoba, 2006). It is argued that Foucault’s ideas on power and ethics can be used in IS planning to promote self-reflection about how we (subjects) have become – via power relations – the type of ethical subjects we are now, and what we can do about it. A better understanding of individuals’ own ethics can lead us to recognize, for instance, other issues and people (including ourselves) that we could be marginalizing when we raise concerns or define improvements in the planning process (some of which could be included to ensure implementation of plans). This type of reflection can then inform further enquiry into what type of ethical individuals we want to be, as well as what how we can pursue more adequately – via power relations – our own ethical concerns.

## CONCLUSION

This paper has presented a framework to support practitioners in dealing with the complexities of IS planning. The framework is inspired in critical systems thinking and draws on the ideas of the systems theories of boundary critique and autopoiesis. The framework advances the use of these theories in the realm of IS by combining them. It encourages the identification of a variety of concerns from different stakeholders, and facilitates reflection on the exploration of possibilities for action that might include support from ICTs. Moreover, the framework aims at extending reflection beyond traditional forms of involvement in IS planning. It promotes the definition of plans according to the participants’ own concerns and values, some of which could be marginalized from analysis and decision-making.

In practice, the use of the framework at Javeriana University in Colombia enabled the definition of a variety of concerns about and suggestions for the improvement of existing IS plans, and made possible the generation of a ‘whole’ set of suggestions to address different bu related aspects that influence the definition and use of IS. However, the exploration of situations of marginalization would require a deeper analysis of how concerns and values can enter into potential tension with existing initiatives, and how the latter embed forms of power that deploy what is ethical to do and to be in a planning process.

These findings suggest the importance of deepening into the ethics of IS planning and how reflection about it can be enhanced to inform further action. To address this issue, it has been proposed to complement the framework with the use of Foucault’s ideas on ethics and power. These ideas can allow us as IS practitioners to reflect on what type of individuals we have become and we want to be with regard to existing forms of power available to us. The development of complementary frameworks that explore issues of power and ethics in IS planning constitutes a very relevant area for further research.

## Biography

José-Rodrigo Córdoba is a Lecturer in Managemen Systems at the Business School. University of Hull in the UK. Prior to getting his MA and PhD in Management, he worked as an IT project manager in his native country (Colombia). His interest in systems thinking originated while he was studving for a degree in Computer Science and Systems Engineering at Los Andes University in Bogotá, Since then, he has published several papers and book chapters on critical systems thinking, information systems, ethics and power.
