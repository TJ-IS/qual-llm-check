## Abstract

This paper explores the nature of, and possibilities arising from, dynamic collaboration, where large numbers of people can collaborate on an evolving set of initiatives, without prior knowledge of each other. It references early examples of dynamic collaboration including Topcoder, Innocentive, Zopa, and Wikipedia. It then speculates about the future of dynamic collaboration.

## Introduction

he media, IT vendors and others often write very dramatic articles about how IT has transformed businesses, industries, and even countries. But if we imagine a historian in 2100 looking back at the use of IT in business and society, I believe they would label the second half of the 20th century as the pre-history of IT.

This period has been about the ‘automation of the factory’ for businesses. They may well characterize this era as an extension of the use of handheld calculators. From a societal perspective, the historian would see developments to date as using the online channel to do things the same way as offline channels; depending on their written style, they may ridicule this as artificially imposing the constraints of the physical world on the online world.

They would see the decade or so (that we are in now), as a period of fumbling around trying to move on from automating the supply chain to informating management processes, and experimenting with exploiting the characteristics of connected enterprises and societies. They would cite failed knowledge management and business intelligence initiatives, and dot com failures, as examples of painful learning processes.

They would, more positively, see the 10 years following as businesses and societies starting to realize the power of information and communication technologies to create new business models, new forms of management, and possibly even new forms of government. The key to all of these is new forms of collaboration.

No one would argue that human collaboration is a powerful means of achieving more than individuals can. That is why societies and companies exist. But physical limitations and organizational structures vastly limit the potential, in terms of who one can collaborate with, when the collaboration takes place, and how the collaboration is conducted.

When you start to remove the constraints of who, when and how, new forms of collaboration become possible, often unlocking huge amounts of value. We have seen some early examples of this:

\- Asynchronous and remote collaboration. An inspiring early example of this comes from Rocketdyne. A maker of parts of rocket engines, Rocketdyne was motivated in the mid-1990s by the possibility of creating space travel that was affordable to the commercial sector. A conventional engine project used 250 to 1000 engineers. Looking for radical innovation, they created a project with six people in six companies, who used early versions of internet tools which were skunkworks from Lockheed, never met in person, used 10% of their time. They first innovated the turbopump, a critical component of the engine. Through radical simplification, they reduced the number of parts from 250 to 4, and the cost down to 1% of the previous version.

\- Embedded collaboration. Here collaboration is part of a business process, as opposed to being a separate process. An example: a factory worker is filling in a form on an ERP system. He doesn’t know the part number he needs to enter. With one click, he gets a list of colleagues around the world who know the answer, and are currently online and interruptible. With a second click, he can open either a voice or text chat with them, whether they at a PC or on a mobile.

\- Dynamic collaboration. Conventionally, collaboration happens between a small, fixed number of participants,

whose roles are clear, to achieve a predefined goal. In dynamic collaboration, many are involved, participants come and go, and goals evolve over time. There are a number of such communities, perhaps the clearest example of which is Topcoder, a bounty-based software development business model with over 100,000 participants, described in detail below. We even see IT powering physical dynamic collaboration with the phenomenon of ‘flash mobs,’ relatively large groups of face-to-face meetings, and having established trust. Many of these preconceptions are based on the notion of static collaboration, since in the past it has been impossible for very large numbers of parties previously unknown to come together to collaborate.

## The potential of dynamic collaboration

Conventional (static) collaboration is a fixed arrangement between a relatively small number of known parties, with a clear, predefined goal. A typical example is a project team, committee, or working group in a company.

Dynamic collaboration is a set of evolving opportunities among a potentially large, shifting group of parties, who can freely join and leave and can be unknown to each other outside the collaboration.

One of the best examples of a dynamic collaboration is Topcoder. Founded in 2001, as a set of sponsored online competitions for developers, TopCoder has evolved to channel its 95,000-strong developer community to deliver enterprise software solutions through its unique competition-based model. Ask Topcoder to develop a piece of software for you, and they will agree the price, create a specification, break it into components, then run each component as an online competition. The winner gets the prize, the second-place gets half, and everyone else gets feedback on their contribution. The judges are 100 toprated community members.

Other such bounty-based models exist, such as Equitrader in financial trading, and Innocentive in pharmaceuticals. Set up in 2001, Innocentive describes itself as an open innovation marketplace, and allows seeker organizations to pose scientific challenges, and solvers to offer solutions for a reward. For example, at the time of writing, there was a challenge posed on Innocentive on 3 April 2009, entitled ‘Highly Charged Hydrogel without Ion Condensation,’ with a reward of US\$50,000, and a deadline of 24 August 2009. As part of the Innocentive model, seekers and solvers can remain anonymous.

As a new business model, inevitably it is a little immature, but nonetheless it is clearly a genuinely new way to harness the potential of large numbers of people, including highly skilled people who are motivated by the task, who it may be hard to find conventionally. There is also clearly an interesting risk-shift going on, where much of the development risk is taken on by the virtual community.

More generally, any kind of collaboration makes people more productive and more motivated, but there are four specific reasons I believe dynamic collaboration is exciting:

\- The knowledge and skills you have access to. At the same time that many enterprises have increasing concerns about the quality and appropriateness of their internal skill sets, and the availability of new skills in the marketplace, where the right business model can be found, dynamic collaboration potentially connects you to the best skills available, for the time you need them, at a price created through the broadest possible competitive mechanism.

\- New ways of collaborating. Electronic collaboration between large numbers of people allows more sophisticated tools. This includes the ability to jointly work on a model or document at the same time, the ability to mix asynchronous (e.g. email) and synchronous communications (e.g. instant messaging) in one initiative, and new mechanisms for expressing and aggregating knowledge and opinions, such as prediction markets. Hewlett-Packard and others have used prediction markets to successfully outperform conventional forecasting techniques.

\- Genuinely new business models. Dynamic collaboration is not just about being more efficient or effective, it’s about new business models. The bounty-based model is just one early example. Another is the open source model for software (and other creative content) development, Linux being a well-known example in the software space, and Wikipedia, the online encyclopaedia being another example. A third model is large many-to-many marketplaces, an interesting example is Zopa, which allows people to lend money to each other, without involvement of a bank.

\- Genuinely new organization and management models. In Gary Hamel’s recent book ‘The Futureof Management’ (Hamel and Breen, 2007), he identifies three forms of innovation: product, processes, and management. He rightly bemoans the lack of innovation in management compared to the other two. There is the potential to at least inform, if not replace, some aspects of governance and management with dynamic collaboration.

## All collaboration is hard, dynamic collaboration is harder

There are many challenges in getting collaborative projects off the ground, and then making them successful. Firstly, it can be hard justifying investment in a collaboration platform and associated tools unless there is a specific, pressing need for collaboration. At the time of writing, the economic downturn makes it even harder to justify anything without a very clear, relatively short-term ROI. Real option techniques could be used to justify a collaboration platform, but in my experience senior managers have little appetite for these techniques because of the complexity and intangibility.

Secondly, collaboration is intrinsically hard. Almost by definition, it cuts across individual, departmental and enterprise boundaries. In a societal context it cuts across demographic, language, and cultural boundaries. More generally, collaborations are rarely confined to an entity that has a single, clear power structure. In fact, collaborations with the greatest value are typically the ones which transcend the most boundaries, as those give a greater diversity of input, in terms of skills and style.

Dynamic collaborations are even harder, because of their scale. More importantly, they are difficult because they involve shifting groups of unknown parties, shifting goals, roles, and motivations, and also because they can be culturally threatening. Even if the aggregated opinion of 10,000 strangers consistently forecast demand for your products better than your marketing department, would you be comfortable using them instead?

For small team collaborations, getting the right people is key. Amazingly, in the extremely successful Rocketdyne project mentioned earlier, the first year of the 2-year project was spent finding the people. The project manager, Robert Carmen described this as finding ‘the six best people in the world’ to do the job. Who the best people are depends on the context and task, but in general three types of attribute are important: skills and capabilities, openness/creativity, and co-operative style.

\- Governance includes the ability for members to participate in self-governing teams, and for more senior members (based on performance) to act as moderators and as judges.

\- The technical model is also scalable, such that many threads of activity and teams can efficiently and reliably co-operate.

\- Because of the thin-client interface, no special software is needed, only a standard browser.

\- The models of these businesses also ensures that work is presented in ‘bite-sized chunks’ so that participating does not involve, for example, multi-year commitments.

The challenge is then to aggregate them in a way that distils the insight, and avoids a resource bottleneck. Mentioned earlier, one example is prediction markets, which uses a stock market like mechanism for participants to ‘buy’ or ‘sell’ ideas, with high ‘priced’ ideas winning out. In the context of bounty-based models, such as Topcoder, aggregation tools include a distributed judging mechanism, to allow a distributed set of judges to aggregate their opinion.

\- Reputation tools. Allowing complete strangers to participate in a collaboration with no vetting seems like a risky proposition, but two things help. First, ensuring you are involving them in the right type of collaboration; for example, you wouldn’t plug a complete stranger into operating a mission critical process or system, but you would take ideas from them, and vet them internally. Second, providing a feedback mechanism to punish unhelpful behaviors and weak contributions and reward helpful behaviors and strong contributions. A great example of this is eBay ratings. Just as many of us have trouble thinking of online payment mechanisms such as Paypal with similar import as physical currencies, most of us currently think of conventional credentials like university degrees as much more important than, say, Topcoder ratings. But the truth is that online reputations are hard to come by, and allocated by a community rather than a single, potentially influence-able, body. Some programmers, for example, are now putting their Topcoder ratings on their CVs. In a sense, reputation tools, combined with some consequences (e.g. your participation is limited or prohibited if your reputation is below a certain level) can become an automated governance mechanism.

## The future of collaboration

There are two dimensions in which I see dynamic collaboration growing to generate more value.

Collaboration Everywhere. As the internet reaches everywhere, the opportunities for collaboration multiply. This is partly driven by technology innovations and partly by availability, affordability and other adoption factors globally. Two particularly important technical trends are improvements in mobile internet access (such as better device input and output capabilities, and lower latency, higher speed connections), and the ‘real world web.’ The real world web refers to the increasing connectivity of physical objects to the Internet. Some concrete examples include radio frequency id chips making physical objects trackable, increasing levels of in-car electronics and wireless connectivity technology, and connectivity and intelligence in white goods.

Collaboration Redefined. Inevitably individuals, companies and government agencies have been limited in imagination in how to exploit the new collaboration capabilities outlined in this paper. The key driver here will be imagination, essentially millions of companies and billions of people finding innovative answers to the question ‘If you could connect any number of people and companies in any way at any time, what value could you create?’ We are seeing some early examples, for Wikipedia, the social lending site Zopa, and Topcoder (all mentioned above). Inspiration may come from some unusual sources, such as internet-connected gaming, the world of viruses and botnets, and although it may be unpalatable to say so, even guerrilla warfare and terrorism.

Terrorists, for example, typically have to operate as much more loosely coupled organizations, having to be very responsive in fast-changing environments with powerful threats to their missions. It is impossible, and usually undesirable, for them to prescribe working practices, or document roles and hierarchies. Instead can only rely on vision and mission as their co-ordinating mechanism.

I don’t believe it is an exaggeration to say that companies’ and governments’ ability to use and envisage new collaboration opportunities may be sufficiently important to determine winners and losers in some competitive arenas. It may be unclear as to which arenas these are, but it seems clear that most, if not every, enterprise would benefit from experimenting with new forms of collaboration.

A determinant of success may well be involvement of what Marc Prensky (2001) calls digital immigrants – people for whom IT is a part of their basic literacy, and who are less limited in their thinking by purely physical models. I see a recognition of this in some companies who endeavor to learn by observing how their newer employees work and collaborate. For example, some organizations have begun to employ reverse mentoring, with junior staff mentoring senior staff around new capabilities.

In short, my belief is that the new collaboration is a vast source of potential, not waiting for technological developments, but for our imaginations to catch up.
