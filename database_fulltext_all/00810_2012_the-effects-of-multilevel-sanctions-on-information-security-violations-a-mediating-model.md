# The effects of multilevel sanctions on information security violations: A mediating model

Ken H. Guo <sup>a,</sup>\*, Yufei Yuan <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business, Western New England University, 1215 Wilbraham Road, Springfield, MA 01119, USA <sup>b</sup> DeGroote School of Business, McMaster University, 1280 Main St W, Hamilton, ON, L8P 4M4 Canada

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 11 February 2011 Received in revised form 1 June 2012 Accepted 4 July 2012 Available online 10 September 2012

Keywords: Information security policy violation Organizational sanctions Workgroup sanctions Personal self-sanctions Multilevel sanctions

## A B S T R A C T

We proposed and empirically tested a mediating model for examining the effects of multilevel sanctions on preventing information security violations in the workplace. The results of the experiment suggested that personal self-sanctions and workgroup sanctions have significant deterrent effects on employee security violations, but that the effect of organizational sanctions becomes insignificant when the other two types of sanctions are taken into account. Theoretically, the study pointed out the importance of personal self-sanctions and informal workgroup sanctions. Practically, our results suggested that an ‘‘influencing’’ strategy may be more effective than an ‘‘enforcing’’ one in information security management.

## 1. Introduction

Security policy is one of the key elements in organizational IS security-management. The importance of a well-established security policy is recognized by scholars and practitioners. By imposing organizational sanctions, organizations can help ensure that proper measures are in place to protect there is and reduce undesirable uses that may cause problems. The idea of implementing strict policies and imposing organizational sanctions is based on deterrence theory, which suggests that people are rational and pursue their self-interest by minimizing costs or pain and maximizing benefits or happiness. Wrongdoers should be punished so that punishment serves as an example to deter others.

There have, however, been mixed and inconclusive findings about the deterrent effect of formal sanctions. Bulgurcu et al. [1] found that sanctions were significant antecedent to user intentions to comply with security but Siponen and Vance [10] suggested that the influence of formal sanctions was not significant when neutralization mechanisms were taken into account. Other factors, such as moral commitment [3] may come into play to influence employee behavior. To understand the effect of organizational sanctions, some have suggested that additional contingency variables, methodological and theoretical issues should be investigated [2].

In an organizational setting, sanctions are not limited to formal sanctions (in security policies) but three other levels of sanctions have been cited: codes of ethics, informal control (at the group level via social norms), and personal (self-control). Tyler and Blader [15] suggested that command-and-control methods and selfregulatory approaches (employees’ desire to adhere to rules) have different effects on rule-compliant behavior in organizations. Some studies ([e.g. 5]) have examined the effects of social norms (informal control) on employee compliance behavior, but our understanding of how different levels of sanctions affect employee security-related behavior is incomplete.

We therefore attempted to determine the effect of different levels of sanctions in an integrative manner by asking: Is the effect of organizational sanctions on an employee’s violation of security policies influenced by all levels of sanctions?

## 2. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

Employee security-related undesirable behaviors can be categorized in many ways [13]. Here we focus on policy violations that: (1) knowingly break rules (employees violate security policies that they know exist); (2) are voluntary (actions are not forced by other parties, e.g. supervisors); (3) are intentional (employees make conscious decisions to engage in the action); and (4) non-malicious (employees are not trying to cause damage).

## 2.1. Organizational sanctions

A sanctioning approach is either a formal control or it involves command-and-control action. One type of formal control is rules and procedures, which try to ensure employees’ compliance by imposing sanctions. Similarly, the command-and-control approach assumes that rule-following behavior is a function of the costs and benefits that employees associate with their noncompliance with the rules. When taking this approach, Tyler and Blader suggest that organizations need to take an active role: ‘‘enforcing rules’’ by providing incentives (to encourage desirable behavior) and also imposing sanctions (to discourage undesirable behavior).

The application of the deterrence theory in information security management is a good example of organizational sanctioning approach. The effect of organizational sanctions has been examined in a number of studies [e.g. 4–6,8]. Using punishment to prevent user violations of policies can be characterized as an ‘‘enforcing’’ approach, relying on the coercive power of management to discipline wrongdoers.

## 2.2. Workgroup sanctions

These are imposed by members of a group on their co-workers. In contrast to organizational sanctions, workgroup sanctions are generally informal, and group members do not have any coercive power granted by the organization. Sanctions are established via socialization activities that are imposed by people in a group. In an organizational setting, employees interact with other workgroup members (supervisors or peers) on a daily basis.

In a social sanctioning system, individuals’ behavior is shaped by the approval or disapproval of their colleagues. The sanctioning is often informal and intangible and thus, may not to be verbal or direct [9]. According to the theory of reasoned action, perceived social norms influence behavioral intentions.

Some studies have examined the effect of social norms on end users’ security-related behavior [4,5]; the results normally supported the effect of social norms.

## 2.3. Personal self-sanctions

Employees generally respond to external norms in one of two ways: complying or non-complying. There is a third way: in this, employees may ‘‘feel a personal responsibility and desire to bring their behavior into line with corporate rules and policies’’. Organizations that encourage rule-following behavior by focusing on employees’ intrinsic motivation are taking a ‘‘self-regulatory approach’’. In this, employees are assumed to adhere to rules and policies because of their ‘‘intrinsic desire’’ to do so. In internal sanctioning systems, the norm is internal to individuals who are carrying out an action, and sanctions are applied internally by the individuals. The advantage of internal sanctions is that individuals engage in responsible behaviors when there is no one watching and thus costly enforcement of external sanctions is not necessary.

Only a handful of previous studies have studied the impact of internal factors. Personal normative beliefs are the moral obligation to perform an act. In a recent study, Bulgurcu et al. found that perceived intrinsic cost is positively related to employee intention to comply with security policies (mediated by cost of noncompliance and employee attitude toward compliance). And Son [11] found that intrinsic motives (perceived legitimacy and perceived value congruence) can explain employees’ compliance with organizational security policies.

The following hypotheses (as shown in Fig. 1) were therefore posited:

H1. Employees’ personal self-sanctions will negatively influence their intentions to violate security policies.

H2. Organizational sanctions perceived by employees will negatively influence their intentions to violate security policies.

H3. Workgroup sanctions perceived by employees will negatively influence their intentions to violate security policies.

Although each of the three levels of sanctions have been hypothesized as having independent effects on employee security violations, they may, however, be related to one another: the internally imposed sanctions may be influenced by organizational and workgroup sanctions. Social control and formal control can only be ultimately implemented through the personal agency of self-controlling individuals. Through the internalization of a social norm, individuals come to have an internal sanctioning system that provides punishments when they carry out an action proscribed by the norm or fails to carry out an action prescribed by the norm. Furthermore, self-regulatory systems not only provide a basis for purposeful action but also mediate the effects of most external influences. Based on these arguments, we further propose:

H4. Organizational sanctions perceived by employees are positively associated with their personal self-sanctions.

H5. Workgroup sanctions perceived by employees are positively associated with their personal self-sanctions.

Organizational sanctions may also influence workgroup sanctions – because workgroups are situated in the organization. Norms within a workgroup may be shaped by organizational policies and control measures. Liao et al. [7] found that perceived sanction severity and certainty had a significant influence on user subjective norms to Internet misuse. Accordingly, we proposed:

H6. Organizational sanctions perceived by employees are positively associated with perceived workgroup sanctions.

## 3. Research method

We conducted a survey of organizational computer users in Canada to test our proposed model. Instead of asking survey participants general questions about information security policy violations, we used four scenarios to which they were asked to respond. This method has two advantages: it provides survey participants with contextual information so that they can make realistic responses within the context and it helps alleviate nonresponse issues caused by the sensitivity of information security matters. The four scenarios were developed from three sources: (1) academic journals and trade publications; (2) interviews with IS practitioners (including IS professionals at a large North American consumer electronics retailer); and (3) interviews with academic experts. The scenarios reflected four security issues related to user authentication and access control (writing down passwords), hardware (using portable USB keys to carry sensitive business data), software (downloading and installing free software from the Internet), and network (using insecure public wireless connections). Measurement items for the constructs were either adopted from the IS literature or developed by the authors. Following the recommendations in the literature [14], we conducted a series of validation procedures, including sorting, pretest, and pilot testing. The final survey questionnaire, including scenarios and measurement items, is shown in Appendices A and B.

The survey was conducted anonymously in two ways: paperbased and web-based. In both, participants were asked to respond to one of the four scenarios (randomly distributed). In the paperbased survey, participants were recruited in business areas in Toronto. Potential participants were selected and given a survey package along with a postage-paid return envelope and a \$10 coffee card if they used a computer at their workplace on a regular basis and were willing to participate. Among the 250 survey packages distributed, 167 (67%) were returned. In the web-based survey, invitations were sent to 2543 email addresses across Anglophone Canada. The email addresses were collected from publicly available websites, including that of a job placement agency and several government agencies (such as transportation administration). To avoid privacy violations and to reduce the concern of email spam, we did not track whether an individual did or did not respond. For the same reason, we did not send reminder emails. At the end, 168 of the targeted email receivers submitted response to the survey. This relatively low response rate (6.5%) was probably due to incorrect addresses or junk mail filters, etc. Also, many people may have simply deleted the survey email without reading it or declined due to company policy. Indeed low response rates are not unusual in randomly targeted email surveys [see 12]. After incomplete responses being removed, there were 306 usable data points in the combined sample (see the characteristics in Table 2).

## 4. Results

We used the partial least square (PLS) method to check the psychometric properties of the measurement items and to test the proposed multilevel sanction model. The software package was WarpPLS 3.0. which can handle both linear and non-linear relationships between constructs.

Before testing the model, we checked for common-method bias. Although prior research indicated that the effect was not substantial, checking for such method bias is strongly recommended [14]. We conducted a Harman’s single-factor test and the result suggested that there was no substantial bias in our study.

## 4.2. Structural model

The proposed multilevel sanction model was evaluated by the structural model of the PLS analysis. One of the key indices is $R ^ { 2 }$ values of dependent variables, which represent the predictiveness of a theoretical model. The standardized path coefficients indicate the strength of the relationships between independent and dependent variables. In our study, a bootstrapping re-sampling procedure (with 100 samples) was carried out to estimate the significance of paths in the structural model. In the PLS analysis, we also controlled for the effects of the following factors on employees’ intentions to violate information security policies (the dependent variable): age, gender, employees’ job position (staff, middle manager, or senior manager), survey method (paperbased or web-based), scenario, and organizational size.

As shown in Fig. 2, the proposed model explained approximately 35% variance for employee intentions to violate information security policies, 42% variance for employee personal selfsanctions, and 22% variance for workgroup sanctions. Based on the minimum 10% criterion, which requires that the $R ^ { 2 }$ value of a dependent variable should be at least 10% in order to make any meaningful interpretation, the proposed model demonstrated explanatory power.

The majority of path coefficients were significant, thus providing support for H1 (personal self-sanctions on intention, $\beta = - 0 . 1 5 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , H3 (workgroup sanctions on intentions, $\beta = - 0 . 4 1 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , H4 (organizational sanctions on personal self-sanctions, $\beta = 0 . 2 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , H5 (workgroup sanctions on personal self-sanctions, $\beta = 0 . 4 7 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and H6 (organizational sanctions on workgroup sanctions, $\beta = 0 . 4 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The only exception was organizational sanctions, which had a nonsignificant effect on employee intentions to violate security policies (H2). Among those control variables, only employees job positions (staff, middle manager, or senior manager) had a significant effect $( \beta = 0 . 1 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ on the intentions to violate security policies, while other control variables (age, gender, survey method, scenario, and organizational size) did not.

These results suggested that the effect of organizational sanctions was fully mediated by personal self-sanctions and workgroup sanctions. ## 5. Discussion and conclusion

## 5.1. Key findings

Our prime question was:

Is the effect of organizational sanctions on employee violation of security policies influenced by the other two types of sanctions? The answer was ‘‘yes.’

Our results also suggested that both personal self-sanctions and workgroup sanctions had significant negative impact on employee intentions to violate security policies. The effect of organizational sanctions, however were non-significant when the other two types of sanctions were included.

Personal self-sanctions play an important role in preventing security violations in the workplace since individuals will be less likely engage in insecure behaviors if they feel they are accountable for their actions.

Workgroup sanctions have significant negative influence on security violations. More specifically, disapproval of workgroup members will discourage employees from engaging in behavior that violates organizational security policies. The results also confirm the significant association between workgroup sanctions and personal self-sanctions. This is consistent with social cognitive theory, which suggests that people construct their own behavioral standards partially based on social influence.

Organizational sanctions do not have a significant direct effect on preventing employee security violations. Prior studies have shown that the effect of organizational sanctions diminishes when other factors are taken into account. For example, Siponen and Vance found that the effect of organizational sanctions was reduced when employees took neutralization techniques to justify their behaviors. One possible reason for the diminished effect of organizational sanctions was that punishment can only provide short-term benefits. Indeed, it can foster negative behaviors and attitudes, including resentment and sabotage.

Although we did not find evidence for supporting the direct effect of organizational sanctions, the results suggested that formal sanctioning measures did have an indirect effect on preventing employee security violations through workgroup sanctions and personal self-sanctions.

Employee position. The results suggested that the more senior an employee’s position, the more likely he or she will violate security policies. This suggests that managers may be more ‘‘businessorientated’’ (versus IT oriented) and may downplay the importance of complying with security policies. This finding appears to confirm D’Arcy and Herath’s conjecture that the possible effect of employee position should be considered as a contingency variable in IS security studies.

## 5.2. Theoretical contributions

Our study makes several important contributions to the literature. First, drawing upon social psychology and ethics theories, we suggested that personal self-sanction should be considered in the context of IS security management. Prior research has mainly focused on organizational sanctions. The introduction of personal self-sanctions allows us to understand how employees regulate their behavior in using organizational IS.

Second, our study contributed by investigating and delineating the indirect effect of organizational sanctions through personal self- and workgroup-sanctions. Although organizational sanctions may help prevent undesirable employee behavior, this approach may not be as effective as personal self-and workgroup-sanctions, which may influence employee behavioral intentions.

Third, by investigating and delineating the indirect effect of organizational sanctions, our study identified and integrated two contingency constructs (personal self- and workgroup-sanctions) that should be considered in studying of formal deterrence in the IS security context. As D’Arcy and Herath argued, inclusion of relevant contingent variables helps isolate the deterrent effect of formal organizational sanctions.

Lastly, our results suggested an alternative approach to conducting deterrence-based IS security research: focus on informal workgroup- and personal self-sanctions instead of finding contingency variables that may covary with organizational sanctions.

## 5.3. Practical implications

Given the limited direct effect of organizational sanctions, an enforcing strategy may not be the best choice; based on the results of our study, an influencing strategy may be better. It can be implemented by educating employees so that they hold themselves accountable for their actions; but employees are normally evaluated on their business performance rather than how they deal with security issues. Thus information security education should focus on the link between employees’ actions and business risks, rather than information security that is taken out of business context. Alternatively, managers can influence employees’ actions through their workgroups. One tactic for establishing a workgroup sanctions is to train some role models who understand security issues and can help advocate policy compliant behavior.

## 5.4. Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the cross-sectional survey research method implemented in this study does not establish the causal relationships between variables. The causality can only be inferred from underlying theories. Second, the four scenarios used in this study may not represent all possible employee violations of information security policies. Third, the study focused on employee behavioral intentions. Lastly, the current study was conducted in Anglophone Canada. Cultural factors should be considered when interpreting the results.

## 5.5. Conclusion

Based on the deterrence theory, organizational sanctions have been seen as a key measure for managing IS security. However deterrence theory alone does not provide a complete understanding of employee behavior. Drawing on relevant social psychology and ethics theories, we proposed and empirically tested a mediating model of sanctions. Our results suggested that personal self-sanctions and workgroup sanctions have significant negative impact on employee intentions to violate security policies. The effect of organizational sanctions becomes non-significant when the other two levels of sanctions are considered.

## Appendix A. Security scenarios

Survey participants were given one of the following scenarios.

A.1. Scenario 1 – password write-down

Alex is a senior manager at your organization, which recently installed a computer system for customer record management. The information technology (IT) department gave users their own usernames and passwords. Different users have different levels of access to the system (e.g. what they can see and what they can do). For security and privacy reasons, the IT department implemented a policy stating that users are accountable for the information they access. Users are required to keep their passwords to themselves and not let other people know or use them. Users who fail to follow the policy may be subjected to disciplinary actions ranging from warning to termination of employment. Finding it difficult to remember the password, Alex wrote down her username and password on a sticker and attached it to the computer she usually uses.

A.2. Scenario 2 – unauthorized portable devices for storing and carrying organizational data

Chris is a business manager at your organization. Periodically Chris makes presentations to your organization’s business partners or works from home. As a result, Chris often uses personal USB drives to copy data back and forth. Your organization’s IT policy, however, prohibits users from attaching unauthorized devices to the corporate network and computers. The IT department argues that the use of unauthorized devices can cause security problems, e.g. loss and disclosure of confidential corporate data and spreading of computer viruses. Employees who fail tofollow the policy may be subjected to disciplinary actions ranging from warning to termination of employment.

## A.3. Scenario 3 – installation and use of unauthorized software

Jordan is a business analyst at your organization. Jordan uses computers on a daily basis to do financial analysis and prepare management reports. Jordan recently was given a new computer. However, the new computer is missing a piece of software that Jordan needs for preparing reports. Believing that purchasing the software may take some time, Jordan managed to download and install an open source but similar software (free of charge) from the Internet. Installation of unauthorized software, however, is not permitted according to your organization’s policy. The IT department insists that unapproved open-source software may damage security and expose the corporate network to external attacks. Users who fail to follow the policy may be subjected to disciplinary actions ranging from warning to termination of employment.

A.4. Scenario 4 – using insecure public wireless network for business purposes

Kelly is an accounting manager at your organization. Kelly uses a corporate laptop while traveling to other sites or working from home. Kelly often brings the laptop to do some work when having a coffee at coffee shops. One thing that Kelly likes is that many coffees shops nowadays offer free wireless Internet access. The IT policy of your organization, however, does not allow its employees to use public free wireless connections for business purposes due to security reasons. Most free wireless connections and communications are not encrypted and may be intercepted by hackers. Users who violate the policy may be subjected to disciplinary actions ranging from warning to termination of employment. Although aware of the security policy, Kelly continues to use free public wireless access when working out of the office.

## A.5. Instructions

Following each scenario, participants were given instructions similar to the following statement (revised for each scenario):

‘‘Based on the information described in the above scenario, please indicate the extent (on a 1-to-7 scale) to which you agree with the statements if you were Kelly. 1 = Strongly Disagree; 7 = Strongly Agree. The expressions of ‘‘the action’’ and ‘‘the behavior’’ refer to Kelly’s action of using an unsecure public wireless network for business purposes as described in the scenario.’’

## Appendix B. Measurement items

## B.1. Organizational sanctions

The following items were adapted from [3]:

[Ogsc1]: The likelihood my organization would punish me for engaging in the action is (very low . . . very high).

[Ogsc2]: I will be reprimanded eventually if my organization is aware of my action.

[Ogsc3]: If the management decides to punish me, the punishment would be (not severe at all . . . very severe).

## B.2. Workgroup sanctions

A workgroup is operationally defined as the functional unit (e.g. department) in which all personnel report directly to the same supervisor (or manager) and interact to complete unit tasks. Four items were created to measure workgroup sanctions perceived by users:

[Wgsc1]: My coworkers will believe it is wrong to engage in this action.

[Wgsc2]: My supervisor will disapprove this action.

[Wgsc3]: My supervisor will not object to this action. [R]

[Wgsc4]: My coworkers will think that I should do this action. [R]

## B.3. Intention to violate security policy

Two items were created to measure user intention to engage in the behavior described in each scenario:

[Intn1]: I would do [the behavior] if I were the person. [Intn2]: I would do [the behavior] if I were in a similar situation.

## B.4. Personal self-sanctions

Three items were developed to measure personal self-sanctions about their actions (as described in the scenarios):

[Sfsc1]: I should be held accountable for violating this security policy.

[Sfsc2]: I should not be blamed if my action causes any damages to computer security. [R]

[Sfsc3]: I should be held accountable if my action causes any negative consequences.

Note: [R]: Reverse item.
