# Requirements determination for common systems: turning a global vision into a local reality

Laurie J. Kirsch <sup>\*</sup>, Mark H. Haney <sup>1</sup>

Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

Available online 17 October 2005

## Abstract

A ‘common’ information system is one that is intended to satisfy the needs of multiple user groups within a firm for a particular functionality (e.g. one order entry system to be used by all divisions of a global firm). Such systems are often comprised of both core (or common) software modules, as well a local modules to support regional requirements. One of the most significant challenges for any infor mation systems (IS) project is determining information requirements. The requirements determination process is especially dificult in this context in which common, global requirements must be forged from disparate, and potentially conflicting, local needs and priorities. This paper reports on case studies of two projects undertaken to globally deploy common systems, and it develops a mode of the requirements determination process. The proposed model posits stakeholders engage in knowledge acquisition and negotiation processes in order to determine global requirements as well as to foster consensus and buy-in, on the part of the local regional stakeholders, to the global system. The findings also suggest that knowledge acquisition is a relatively structured, rational approach to learning about local needs and requirements, where stakeholders share an awareness of the corporate vision for global standards and agree on the need for a new system. Negotiation, in contrast, is less structured and more political, with diferent parties lobbying for their own interests as they endeavo to identify common requirements for the new global system. Finally, the results suggest that the requirements determination process is influenced by an articulated corporate vision for a global system, needs and priorities of the local regions, and the organizational roles and skills of key stakehold ers. Implications of the results are drawn and suggestions for future research are ofered - 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Common systems; Global systems development; Information requirements determination; Case study

## 1. Introduction

In part as a response to globalization trends in business and in part as a desire to leverage economies of scale, firms are increasingly building large common information systems (IS) and implementing them around the globe (Hunter and Beck, 2000; Guez, 1996; Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991). Such systems span geographic boundaries to enable and facilitate global business processes. These systems include applications that support a single worldwide business process as well as integrated solutions such as SAP R/3 that enable cross-functional, dispersed business processes (Collins and Kirsch, 1999, p. 13). A common system, as described here, is comprised of core software modules designed around common global requirements to accommodate the needs of multiple business units within a firm.<sup>2</sup> These core modules can be augmented with local modules to support regional or local diferences in laws, regulations, or work rules.

Much of the challenge surrounding the development of any large information system stems from the dificulties inherent in the early definitional phase of a project (Alvarez, 2002; Davidson, 2002; Browne and Rogich, 2001; Vessey and Conger, 1994). During this phase, which is usually referred to as ‘requirements determination’, the vision for a solution takes shape, project goals and objectives are defined, and specific requirements are identified (Byrd et al., 1992). Successfully navigating this phase requires stakeholders to share and integrate their knowledge about business processes and information technologies (Ocker et al., 1998; Walz et al., 1993).

Prior research has identified many problems associated with the requirements determination phase of a development efort. These include communication dificulties (Urquhart, 2001; Byrd et al., 1992), process uncertainty (Davis, 1982), and interviewing skills of IS designers and analysts (Marakas and Elam, 1998; Agarwal and Tanniru, 1990). Curtis et al. (1988) have argued persuasively that one of the most dificult and persistent problems is the lack of appropriate domain knowledge within the design team. They conclude that there is a ‘thin spread of application knowledge’ in large IS projects, and point out the difficulty of building a common understanding of the application domain area and the devel opment process across design team members. Determining common global requirements requires the project stakeholders to share knowledge about local business processes and to develop an agreement about redesigned, common business processes (Collins and Kirsch, 1999; Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991). However, because stakeholders from various locations often have diferent needs and priorities, sharing and integrating local knowledge in order to forge consensus about a common business process may be challenging (Davidson, 2002; Kuljis et al., 1998; Walz et al., 1993).

Project teams deploying common systems are, therefore, faced with a thorny task: understanding local business processes and information needs, and developing common global requirements that the local business units will agree on. Many firms, though, have little experience designing, building, and implementing such systems, and find there are few available templates for guidance (Collins and Kirsch, 1999; Kuljis et al., 1998). Moreover, because the deployment of common systems often afects business units around the world, diferences in language, culture, and time zone can have considerable impact on the systems development process (e.g. Carmel, 1999; Guez, 1996; Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991; Robey and Rodriquez-Diaz, 1989). The purpose of this paper is to examine how common global requirements are forged from local domain knowledge. In particular, this paper reports on two case studies of projects to develop core modules of large common systems, which were designed for global deployment.

## 2. Background literature

Requirements determination is ‘a set of semi-structured and related activities performed to facilitate the gaining of an understanding of a business problem’ (Marakas and Elam, 1998, p. 38). It involves analysts working with users to develop an understanding of a business problem, to develop specific objectives and requirements for an information system, and to evaluate various alternatives to addressing the problem (Byrd et al., 1992).

To determine requirements, design teams need to learn about the problem domain (Robillard, 1999; Walz et al., 1993; Salaway, 1987). Appropriate domain knowledge includes knowledge of the business activity or process as well as technical expertise required to build and implement a system (Curtis et al., 1988). When determining globa requirements for common systems, domain knowledge related to business activities spans the boundaries within the firm because the system is designed to meet the needs and objec tives of multiple business locations (Kuljis et al., 1998; Ambrosio, 1996).

There are a wide range of techniques that might be utilized to learn about the appropri ate domains and determine requirements, including structured or unstructured interviewing, prototyping, brainstorming, observing users, and cognitive mapping (Browne and Rogich, 2001; Hutchings and Knox, 1995; Byrd et al., 1992; Agarwal and Tanniru, 1990; Salaway, 1987). Structured interviewing, in which an analyst asks the users a set of directed questions, remains a highly popular and widely used technique (Browne and Rogich, 2001; Agarwal and Tanniru, 1990). Several researchers have conducted experiments to study structured interviewing techniques, which have been found to be more efective than unstructured techniques in terms of performance and knowledge extraction (Agarwa and Tanniru, 1990). Evidence suggests that content, semantic, and process structuring of the interviewing activity is valuable (Marakas and Elam, 1998). Moreover, prompting techniques—a set of directed, context-independent questions based on an analysis of the requirements elicitation task—have also been shown to be efective in eliciting requirements (Browne and Rogich, 2001). In their experimental study, Moody et al. (1998) describe the cognitive interview, in which the analyst uses five principles of memory retrieval to guide the user in recall. The results of the study suggest that the cognitive interview is more efective and eficient than the ‘standard information requirements interview’ in which no cues are used to aid recall. While Moody et al. did not characterize the cognitive interview as ‘structured’ and the standard interview as ‘unstructured’, it is clear that the former approach applies considerable more structure to the interviewing process than the latter.

Complementing the experimental studies are field studies of requirements determination. These field studies have often characterized requirements determination as a dialectic process in which analysts and users engage in discussion and debate over needs and require ments (e.g. Urquhart, 2001; Walz et al., 1993; Curtis et al., 1988). Curtis et al. (1988) found that significant efort was needed to facilitate a common understanding of both the application domain and how the system should be built to support that domain. Similarly, in their intensive study of the design process, Walz et al. (1993) noted that knowledge exchange occurred through discussions, and they observed substantial constructive conflict, in which team members learned about each others’ requirements and skills through dialectic, educational communications. Other researchers have reported on similar dialectic processes. Beyer and Holtzblatt (1995), for example, advocate an ‘apprenticeship model’, where the analyst and client engage in intense communication, discovery, and learning as they strive to design a system. A number of researchers emphasize the importance of learning during design eforts, and describe the necessity of intense interactions between users and analysts in order to successfully deploy information systems (e.g. Alvarez, 2002; Wastell, 1999; Lyytinen and Robey, 1999; Salaway, 1987). Indeed, participatory design, which first took root in Scandinavia, emphasizes the central role that users play in designing and developing IS and work practices (Kyng, 1991; Clement and Van den Besselaar, 1993).

Though it is possible that project teams rely on multiple methods for determining requirements (Marakas and Elam, 1998), there is still considerable reliance on the structured interview (Agarwal and Tanniru, 1990). There are few studies specifically examining how global requirements for common systems are determined, although there is evidence that when multiple customers or users are involved in the project, learning about the domain is extremely dificult (Kuljis et al., 1998; Walz et al., 1993). Instead of negotiating a common solution, the interests of local sites can be favored over global corporate interests (Jain et al., 1998; Robey and Markus, 1984). Moreover, integrating knowledge from diferent areas into a holistic design is neither straightforward nor painless. In their field study, Curtis et al. (1988) found that difering customer needs resulted in conflict and change, and the project teams found it dificult to negotiate and enforce agreements.

These studies suggest that, in the context of deploying global systems, a process that allows for intense discussion, debate, and negotiation may be more useful than structured interviews because there are competing coalitions who must identify and agree to a set of common requirements. Indeed, in a direct comparison of the interview and the dialectic approach in the context of ill-structured problems, Boland (1978) found that the dialectic approach, characterized by information sharing, mutual suggestions, and reciprocated critiques, resulted in higher quality designs. However, understanding the role of these intense interactions in facilitating requirements determination has been underemphasized in both the research and practitioner literature (Kirsch and Beath, 1996), while much more attention has been focused on the role of the interview in gathering domain knowledge (Pitts and Browne, 2004). Moreover, few studies consider how multiple approaches, such as interviewing and negotiating, might be utilized in concert—how, for example, they might be synergistic (Byrd et al., 1992). In addition, there is little empirical evidence related specifically to how common, global requirements are determined (Kuljis et al., 1998). Accordingly, this paper addresses the following specific research question: how do project teams forge common global requirements from local domain knowledge?

## 3. Research approach

A qualitative research approach was utilized, which is appropriate when studying ‘how questions (Yin, 1994). This approach facilitates an in-depth examination of the phenomenon in its organizational setting (Benbasat and Goldstein, 1987), and is consistent with this paper’s theory-building goal (Pare´ and Elam, 1997; Eisenhardt, 1989; Benbasat and Goldstein, 1987).

The overall design and conduct of this positivist case study were guided by Eisenhardt’s (1989) approach to building theory from case study data. Suggestions from Yin (1994),

Miles and Huberman (1994), and Strauss and Corbin (1990) were also incorporated into this study, as the earlier work of these authors similarly influenced the development of Eisenhardt’s approach.

## 3.1. Site selection and description

Two case studies, chosen from a larger set of cases, were selected for this particular study.<sup>3</sup> The Credit system was designed to provide a worldwide, common approach to letters of credit processing for a large global bank. The system was custom developed by an internal IS group. Prior to the Credit project, there were numerous systems installed around the world, designed for a wide range of software and hardware platforms, supporting a variety of letters-of-credit business processes. Many of these systems were 20 years old, and increasingly dificult to maintain. Because of the variety of implemented solutions, there were no economies of scale to be leveraged in terms of support, maintenance, and upgrades. Over time, the central information technology (IT) organization realized that a common system would result in common hardware and software across the bank. This IT group also recognized the opportunity for reengineering the business process in conjunction with building a common global information system. For a 2-year period prior to the oficial start of the project, division executives, regional business representatives, and centralized and local technology representatives met on numerous occasions to debate the merits of a common business process and system. Eventually an understanding was reached about the need to have a common global solution, and the decision was made to develop the Credit system in Hong Kong, but to place project management responsibility in the United States.

The Hong Kong developers built a version of the Credit system, which was installed in several Asian locations. However, the other regions felt that the solution was not a global one, in that the functionality accommodated the Asian regions but was not particularly well suited for other locations. Moreover, the geographic split of development and project management proved unworkable. Therefore, the decision was made to move development back to the United States, where the IT group developed another version of the core modules that was thought to be truly global in scope. Local IT groups in Asia and the United Kingdom were responsible for interface development. When the system was installed at a particular site, an implementation team was formed, which consisted of local and global representa tives, and which was coordinated by a global implementation manager located in the United States. At the time of this case study, this version was implemented in several Asian, North American, and European sites, and preparations were being made to implement it in the remaining sites. In total, the application would be deployed in 31 sites around the globe.

The Metrics project was intended to support the hardware repair business of a global company that develops and markets hardware and software solutions. The project involved the deployment of a data warehouse package as well as the in-house custom development of specific warehousing applications related to hardware metrics. Because of the autonomous business units in this firm, there were few instances of common hardware metrics across units. Some time prior to the Metrics project, an efort was undertaken to standardize a set of key metrics. The approach of this prior project was to let country managers select the metrics they felt were relevant for their countries from a pre-specified set. Though a common set of metrics was implemented, it was in name only: there were no standard measures because each country had the ability to code around ‘standard’ definitions. This earlier system, considered a failure, did not foster global management since there was little consensus regarding the definition or calculation of any particular metric. Consequently, managers spent considerable time discussing diferences in data meanings rather than addressing real global business issues.

The Metrics project was broken into phases. Phase I included the implementation of the hardware and the data warehouse infrastructure, as well as the first set of standardized metrics definitions and applications. The goal of Phase II was to standardize another set of metrics and to build software solutions around those metrics. The project structure was ‘loose’. There was a core group of individuals located in the United States responsible for defining the solution, coding warehousing applications for very specific business problems, and implementing the solution around the globe. The US team, though collocated at headquarters, was an international team experienced in working with people from diferent cultures and countries. There was also a development team in the United Kingdom responsible for the highly technical coding. Implementation teams, consisting of both global and local representatives, were formed for each of the three worldwide regions. Eventually, the Metrics solution would be implemented in 68 countries worldwide. At the time of the case study, Phase I implementations were completed, and the firm was working on Phase II deployments.

These two case studies were chosen for theoretical sampling reasons (Eisenhardt, 1989) in that the requirements determination phenomenon is obvious and observable, facilitating this theory-building efort. Moreover, the cases are similar in terms of project size (large) and implementation progress (systems rolled out to some, but not all, sites); there was also significant focus on the front-end of both projects with international input and participation in both cases.

The sites difer in terms of organizational dimensions and context, which is desirable in a theory-building efort in order to explore diferences in the phenomenon of interest. In particular, there is variation in location, industry, and culture. The bank, which is headquartered in a large eastern US city and employs about 70,000 people worldwide, is structured hierarchically and functionally. Historically, each business unit within the bank had its own IT department, which was responsible for building and implementing information systems tailored to the business unit’s needs. However, the software and hardware infrastructure was centrally managed, although there were few existing infrastructure standards. At the time of the case study, the firm was merging with another global financial institution, whose structure and culture difered dramatically. For instance, the IT function was completely decentralized, and individual country managers exerted considerably more power and discretion than the managers in the other firm. During the data collection period, the employees were struggling to adapt to the new, merged organization.

In contrast to the bank, the technology firm, with global headquarters in the western US, is more team-focused and entrepreneurial in culture. Though the firm employs over 114,000 people worldwide, it has retained its entrepreneurial and innovative roots, and it relies on local autonomy, expertise, and management. Instead of a strict commandand-control hierarchical structure, the firm both encourages and fosters teamwork and open communication. There is a centralized IT group, which provides infrastructure services and support throughout the company. The firm also has an information management (IM) department, whose goal is to proactively work with the business users to help them develop technology-informed strategy and tactics to meet their needs. The Metrics project was the responsibility of the IM department.

In addition, six representatives from the IM unit were interviewed. Since the IM unit is neither an ‘IS’ unit nor a ‘business’ unit, but a liaison unit between IS and the functional units, all six of these individuals bridge the gap between technology and business needs. Over the years, they had all gained valuable experience working closely with both the IT and business communities. In their current roles, they partnered with the business community, and focused on deploying technology-based solutions that would facilitate the firm’s need to share information across divisional boundaries.

All individuals interviewed were located in the US. These on-site interviews were taperecorded, with both the interviewer and a scribe present at each session. The interviewer conducted the question-and-answer session, while the scribe took notes, clarified responses, and asked follow-on questions as needed. The tapes of the sessions were later transcribed by a third party, yielding 238 pages of transcripts. Subsequently, the first author listened to the tape recordings in order to correct any errors in the transcribed documents; this author also checked the interviews against the scribe’s notes, correcting details or adding information where appropriate.

The on-site interviews were augmented with telephone interviews and with project documentation. At Credit, three telephone interviews were conducted, two with the senior IS manager prior to on-site data collection and one with a senior business manager afterwards. Further, the researchers were given a project summary, which included a description of the business problem, project mission and objectives, technical architecture of the global solution, and customer access to the solution. Prior to the on-site visit at Metrics, one telephone interview of the IS manager was conducted. The researchers also were given a copy of the project charter, which contained information about the project goals, objectives, scope, deliverables, milestones, schedule, risk assessment, and approvals. In addition, the researchers had a copy of the IM departmental charter, as well as data from the project web site. From these sources, 85 pages of additional documentation were collected, which, when combined with the interview transcripts, yielded a total of 323 pages of raw data.

Steps were taken during data collection to establish construct validity and reliability. In particular, the use of a case study protocol and a case study database help to ensure reliability (Yin, 1994). For this study, the case study protocol documents the specific interview questions as well as the procedures to be followed in collecting data. A case study database was built that contains raw notes from the interviews, transcriptions, notes from the scribe, project documentation, and general information about the firm. The case study database also contains detailed case study write-ups created after data collection was completed.

Establishing construct validity involves the use of multiple sources of evidence, the establishment of a chain of evidence, and having key informants review the draft case study reports (Yin, 1994). In this study, all three approaches were utilized. In particular, multiple respondents provided evidence about the phenomenon of interest; the project documentation also provided evidence. A chain of evidence is established by linking the data, the analysis, and the results. This is accomplished by logical argumentation through out the paper, and by citing relevant material from the case study, as is done in later sections of this paper. Finally, case summaries were prepared after data collection was completed, which key informants read and reviewed. After reading the summary, the Credit key informant requested a few minor changes to better disguise the firm. These changes were made and later approved by the informant. A senior manager at Metrics reviewed and approved the case summary.

## 3.3. Data coding and analysis

Data analysis began with a close reading of all interview transcripts and a review of additional case data. This first step allows for immersion in the data and a holistic view of each case. Each transcript was then read at least once more, with many read multiple times. Any passage referring to the process of information requirements determination was highlighted.

In the next step of the analysis, a matrix was used to organize the data (Miles and Huberman, 1994). This matrix was populated with passages referring to the way in which information requirements were determined. The source of the passage and a pointer back to the original document were added to the matrix. In addition, data from one source was substantiated or supported by another person’s comments or project document before being inserted into the matrix. In this way, data source triangulation, in which data are collected from multiple sources to corroborate the same fact (Yin, 1994, p. 92), is achieved. The full and completed matrix serves as the basis for answering the research question; excerpts of this matrix are shown in Table 2.

The next step involved coding the data. Each passage was read and assigned a concep tual label that signified how global requirements were forged. The purpose of this technique, called ‘open coding’ (Strauss and Corbin, 1990), is to identify the constructs underlying the quoted passage. Next, similar concepts were grouped into broader catego ries that encompassed the underlying concepts (see Table 2). This process is known as ‘axial coding’ (Strauss and Corbin, 1990). Specifically, comments that were labeled ‘learning about the business or technical domain’ and ‘identifying potential requirements’ were grouped into a category called ‘knowledge acquisition.’ Comments labeled as ‘forging agreement to a global system’, ‘building commitment to a global system’, ‘compromising on requirements’, and ‘fostering efective communication’ were grouped into a category called ‘negotiation’. The concepts and categories identified during open and axial coding become the basis for developing a theory of common, global requirements determination. The process of integrating and relating the concepts and categories into a theory is called ‘selective coding’ (Strauss and Corbin, 1990).

## 4. Results

In both cases, events prior to the oficial start of the project were central to learning about business unit priorities, corporate goals, and system requirements. At the bank, growing awareness at headquarters of the need for a standard solution led to the Credit project. This awareness first awakened within the IS community, mostly due to the high cost of maintaining multiple software solutions on various hardware platforms. Some business users—specifically, corporate operations managers—also came to appreciate the high maintenance and enhancement costs of the systems. In addition, these business users wanted a solution that would facilitate innovation in the letters-of-credit business process, and they wanted all customers of the bank to have a common interface no matter where in the world they interacted with the bank.

The Metrics project was aligned with a key corporate business initiative to become more global. An important focus of this initiative was the gradual difusion of standards to facilitate a worldwide perspective and management approach. Headquarters was taking on responsibilities for major strategic processes, and managers there needed a global perspective on these processes.

From these interests and initiatives, common goals emerged within the corporate headquarters of both firms. Managers embraced a global vision—the worldwide deployment of a common system to support or enable a common, global business process. While prior events helped to forge agreement on an overall goal, they did not contribute to a detailed understanding of the system requirements and design; nor did the managers appreciate the dificulty of making their global vision a local reality. It was an arduous process to ham mer out the detailed global requirements, and it required two key processes: knowledge acquisition and negotiation.

Knowledge Acquisition. From Table 2, it is clear that project participants spent some efort learning about local business and technical domains. Within the broader user com munity at both firms, the business process was enacted in a variety of ways, and there was a range of existing technical solutions to support those business processes. Therefore, uncovering domain knowledge—about business processes, existing systems, and possible solutions—took time. For the Credit project, key business stakeholders traveled with the Manager of Analysis & Q/A to diferent sites, where they examined existing systems to determine if one of them was suitable for serving as the global system. The Manager of Analysis & Q/A remarked:

I was part of that, going around the world, looking at the diferent systems out there and seeing if this was the system we wanted to take and go forward with that.

After visiting a number of locations, the group of managers found no existing system that was adequate and, therefore, they decided to custom design and build a new system. They again met to identify potential requirements. The Senior IS Manager explained:

. . .regional managers came together. . .to talk about, what do I need the system to do? How do we make this better? How do I streamline my profits? How do we have this one system?

The Metrics project followed a similar course. The general approach for surfacing the dif ferent views of each metric was to bring together representatives of the business units in order to learn about their business processes. Rather than including managers from each business location, ‘key’ representatives were identified; these were representatives from the eight countries with the largest volume of business. The Business Project Sponsor explained this approach as follows:

You know, we don’t need to go to every corner of the world. I’m sorry, but some people are more important than other people to some degree. So we captured the 7 or 8 countries that made 90% of the activity, 85% of the activity.

These representatives, along with the User Manager who facilitated the requirements determination process, were sent to London for a 3-day meeting in which the goal was to discuss and reach consensus on metrics definitions and measurements. The idea was to gather domain knowledge from the local regions about metrics and their usage. As the Business Project Sponsor commented:

We started from the root to say, ‘OK, when you enter the time that you used to solve the problem, how do you do it, what do you take into account?’ Really go down to the very basics.

Each representative came to the meeting with a list of key measures from his region or country and the business rationale for each measure. The representatives, led by the User Manager, then catalogued and prioritized these measures. The User Manager facilitated this process by posing the following questions:

• Why do you define and measure a hardware repair metric as you do?

• What’s the history behind your approach?

• What are the relative benefits to your approach?

Thus, during that meeting, specific metrics and system requirements were identified and documented: ‘And so in the space of that 3-day meeting, we did the cataloging, we did the prioritization’ (User Manager, Metrics).

In both firms, then, the requirements determination process included a stage of knowledge acquisition, in which the participants sought to identify, document, and understand existing business processes and technologies. Project participants went about this task in a rather dispassionate way, eliciting domain knowledge and cataloging potential requirements. However, as they tried to meld the local domain knowledge into specific global system requirements, the focus changed from acquiring knowledge to negotiating consensus, and the process became much more impassioned.

Negotiation. As can be seen from Table 2, in addition to knowledge acquisition, determining requirements involved considerable negotiation, a ‘give-and-take’ among participants to arrive at a set of common global requirements. Individual knowledge about local domains would be compared and integrated with others’ knowledge to derive requirements that would meet the needs of all. This negotiation process encompassed several activities, starting with a process of forging agreement to a global solution. Though a global vision for the system was articulated by corporate IT (Credit) or central management (Metrics), local regions were not necessarily ready to abandon local solutions, despite agreeing in principle with the need for standardization. As the Senior IS Manager at Credit remarked: ‘Getting people to buy-in to the concept of global, not an easy task.’ The User Manager at Metrics found that participants were not always interested in agreeing to one common solution:

Ultimately, all countries came to the meeting believing that we had to have worldwide standardization, but also believing that theirs was probably going to be it [the standard].

Not only was it dificult to foster agreement among the participants for a common, global solution. Building and maintaining individual commitment to the global solution was a constant battle:

I think it’s perseverance and I think it’s working with people to make them understand that there’s something in it for them. And that’s fundamental. Otherwise, no one would do anything. When you’re talking to someone and say, ‘We all want to look at the same business the same way, but you can do some other things, which I expect you to do, to manage your daily business.’ There’s a little bit of intrusion because no one wants to show someone else how they’re doing, but it’s done in a benign way, not to kind of hit them over the head, but understand the business and see where we can help in terms of coming up with diferent processes or better tools and things like that. But a lot of it is perseverance. You just work and communicate and negotiate (Business Project Sponsor, Metrics).

## Similarly, the Global Training Manager at Credit observed:

When you manage a global project and you’ve got diferent regions working on different things, it tends to go of in each region and become very regionalized rather than global. And the challenge is to maintain that global perspective versus a regional perspective.

Negotiating a set of global requirements implies that individuals must be flexible about how global needs could be satisfied and must be willing to compromise:

We standardized it.. . .Terminology was the major thing. I mean, in [the U.S.], certain things are called diferently than from Asia and from Europe. So we had to agree to a common terminology. We had to give up some: Asia had to give up some, Europeans had to give up some, [the U.S.] too (Manager of Analysis & Q/A, Credit).

Successful negotiation required that the stakeholders, especially the meeting facilitator, have an understanding of when to ‘give’ and when to ‘take’:

You can’t push your standardization on everything. Instead, you give and take, and the key things, you know you have to very rigorous about those, but the others, you don’t have to (IM Manager, Metrics).

Though the result may not have perfectly fit any one unit’s requirements, it was something that all units could agree to: ‘‘It was a compromise solution because we wanted buy-in’’ (Senior IS Manager, Credit).

Maintaining consensus on requirements required vigilance and efort as well. Respondents from both firms commented on the dificulty of maintaining common, global requirements as additional needs surfaced or additional business units become involved in the project. At Credit, one manager commented:

If there’s something that needs to be changed. . .we’ll send them pieces of information so they can look at it and give us their feedback.. . .So the users have looked at the system. They have given us their observations, comments.. . .We work with each other to get it done as quickly as possible. Once the [U.S.] users approve it, then it will go out globally. . .for them to take a look at it. They may come back and say that it’s not a global need and, therefore, we don’t want this part in the system. Or they may come back and say well, good thought here, but I would like to add this, this, and this in the process. Then we add that and we get everybody’s agreement on that (Manager of Analysis & QA, Credit).

## At Metrics, the Business Project Sponsor likened the process to a boxing match:

You can never relax. It’s a little bit like a boxing match. You can’t say, ‘Well, I can punch the other person twice and then I can relax.’ Because once you put your arms down, boom, you’re going to get hit and you’re on the canvas. So you can never give up because there is always a new employee who is new or a new manager.

Diferent communication styles sometimes hampered the negotiation process. The User Manager at Metrics worked very hard to facilitate efective communication by ensuring that all views were represented: ‘I think remaining objective and giving everyone an equal voice was really tough’. He noted that the Europeans were accustomed to dealing with people from multiple countries and their presence helped ensure cooperation. He felt that Americans, in particular, learned a lot from the European approach to cooperation. Despite his eforts, the User Manager found it dificult to elicit input from all. For example, he found the Japanese representative particularly reticent. He took a couple of steps to rectify this. He began an email conversation with just the Japanese representative, and he requested that the Australian delegate, who was comfortable interacting in this setting, also represent the Japanese interest. Explaining this latter step, he commented:

That sort of highly interactive, confrontational, throw-it-out-in-the-room style that we had with the Europeans and the Americans, we wouldn’t have had with the Japanese.

The negotiation process was far less orderly than the knowledge acquisition process. The User Manager at Metrics explained: ‘‘It was kind of primitive. We all got in a room and we fought it out’’. He further commented:

Not that it was easy [to reach consensus through negotiation]. It was exhausting. It was grueling, it was painful, it was exhausting. . .organized chaos.

Though the Credit stakeholders did not come together for an intense 3-day meeting as the Metrics stakeholders did, they still found the process of negotiating requirements long and arduous. The Manager of Analysis & Q/A explained it this way:

There’s a quick buy-in process [to the vision of a common global system], then we spent six months, eight months, or a year to get to that agreement,. . .the agreement of functional specs. The agreement on all those things took some time.

## 5. Discussion and implications

The results suggest that determining global requirements for a common system is a twostage process consisting of both knowledge acquisition and negotiation. Turning the global vision into a local reality meant that all representatives had to commit to a common global system and define common global requirements. It was not enough for the partic ipants to agree to a common system in concept only. Rather, they had to identify an agreed-upon set of concrete requirements. Ultimately, both project teams in this study reached a consensus by iterating between knowledge acquisition and negotiation until they agreed on common global requirements. However, despite their ultimate successes, both project teams found the requirements determination process arduous, challenging, and time-consuming.

In Fig. 1, a model of requirements determination for common, global systems is proposed based on the results of this study. The model is explained and elaborated below.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the results suggest that the goal of the requirements determination process for common global systems is not simply to determine a set of detailed requirements. Rather, the goal is also to achieve and maintain consensus and buy-in on a set of detailed requirements that both meet the needs of the organization as a whole, and are acceptable to the local regions. Both the Credit and Metrics project leadership placed significant emphasis on reaching consensus and buy-in by the local regions, reflecting the likely importance of such buy-in to the successful implementation of the systems. Indeed, the Metrics project was preceded by an earlier attempt to standardize metrics that ended in failure when local regions simply coded around the standards. This earlier failure may have sensitized management to the need for consensus and buy-in. The attention to consensus and buy-in highlights the importance of a negotiation stage of the requirements determination process, and suggests that a more sophisticated view of the political nature of requirements negotiations is needed, one that goes beyond the view that the most pow erful group in the negotiations will get its way.

In this study, project stakeholders achieved these twin goals (of common requirements and stakeholder buy-in) via knowledge acquisition and negotiation processes. The pre dominant technique for learning about local domains and identifying potential require ments during knowledge acquisition was the interview. This finding is consistent with other studies that have identified the interview as a major approach to requirements determination (e.g. Agarwal and Tanniru, 1990). Entering into this process, the regional representatives were aware of the push for global standards and publicly agreed with it. Knowledge acquisition, at both firms, was analytic and systematic, relying on face-to-face interactions that had an air of civility and collaboration. Thus, this stage can be charac terized as a rational process in which participants agree in principle to a new system and agree to participate in the knowledge acquisition process (Robey and Markus, 1984).

While interviewing may have surfaced local needs and desired functionality, it did not result in a set of common global requirements or stakeholder buy-in. Indeed, even though individuals agreed in concept to a new system and were aware of the global initiative for standardization, they were not always willing to compromise on their own, local vision of a system. Reaching consensus on requirements and fostering buy-in among the stakeholders involved a negotiation stage, which can best be described as a dialectical process in which intense interactions, debates, and discussions occur. Working through these diferent interests to negotiate a common solution requires a determined, focused, and unrelenting efort that depends, in part, on the skills and roles of key stakeholders. In comparison to knowledge acquisition, negotiation can be described as a political process, with diferent parties lobbying for solutions that best meet their local needs (Robey and Markus, 1984). This behavior is not unusual in negotiation: the literature often characterizes individual behavior during negotiation in terms of the amount of attention given to the positions and outcomes of self versus others (Bendersky, 2003; Brett and Okumura, 1998; Pruitt and Rubin, 1986).

The finding that requirements determination involves both knowledge acquisition as well as negotiation, combining interviews with dialectic interactions, is consistent with other studies that have found that determining requirements is not simply a matter of asking users what they want, but rather an iterative, emergent process of multiple methods with varying degrees of structure (Alvarez, 2002; Davidson, 2002; Marakas and Elam, 1998; Banbury, 1987; Davis, 1982). Further, characterizing requirements determination as both rational and political is consistent with prior research that has noted how both rational and political processes can co-exist during systems design and development (Robey and Markus, 1984). The air of civility and agreement during knowledge acquisition may in fact mask political agendas, that is, a desire for a ‘global’ system that fits the individual needs of the local region may be lurking below the stated agreement on the need for a common standard.

In both the knowledge acquisition and negotiation stages, the facilitators played important roles. The facilitators were observed pursuing two diferent goals. One goal was encouraging and facilitating participation so that each local region would have a voice in the process. A second goal was to narrow the focus of the requirements and push consensus. For example, at Metrics, the User Manager, as facilitator, recognized that the Japanese participant was not comfortable in the environment, and he took steps to make sure those views were surfaced. He also had a key role in narrowing the focus of the metrics requirements gathering by identifying and focusing on common interests across the local regions.

The leaders who acted as facilitators also were observed to gain credibility by their role as boundary spanners. The Manager of Analysis & Q/A at Credit was instrumental in leading the process; his credibility as a ‘business’ (as opposed to an IS) manager paved the way for participation. At Metrics, many of the members of the IM department, which had primary responsibility for the Metrics project, had years of experience working closely with the business and IT communities. These findings reinforce prior studies that have highlighted the importance of individual skill and organizational position in determining requirements (e.g. Guinan et al., 1998; Marakas and Elam, 1998). Some researchers have suggested that trusting one another may be critical to successful negotiation (e.g. Lewicki et al., 1998). Other researchers have also noted the impact of individual diferences in successfully negotiating outcomes (Brett et al., 1999; Neale and Bazerman, 1983). Finally, researchers have pointed to the importance of having key project personnel who span organizational boundaries because their insight into both business and IT needs helps them to integrate knowledge from these areas, and because of their ability to overcome communication barriers among organizational units (Curtis et al., 1988).

In addition to highlighting the importance of both knowledge acquisition and negotiation to the requirements determination process and the importance of the facilitator role, the research results also suggest richer views of three important IS phenomena: stakeholder roles and relationships in the requirements determination process, conflict and agreement and the political nature of negotiations, and the nature of system building. Research on requirements determination frequently focuses on the key roles of the user and the analyst and the relationship between them (Pitts and Browne, 2004; Kirsch and Beath, 1996). The users are supposed to have domain knowledge of the business process that the system is being designed to facilitate, and the analysts are supposed to have knowledge of the capabilities and limitations of system components. In the typical view, the users and analysts come together, share their knowledge with each other, and build requirements. The set of roles and relationships exhibited in the two cases in this study, however, is considerably more complex. Knowledge of the business goals of the new system exists in several groups, as does system knowledge. The business users and IT groups in the local regions provide knowledge of their existing systems and business processes to representatives of central management, who communicate the vision for the business goals of the system, which were developed through the collaborative efort of central management, central IT, and regional business leaders. Clearly this is a more complex set of relationships than that typically described between systems analysts and users.

Conflict and agreement are also observed to co-exist and be interrelated. Stakeholders came to the requirements determination process understanding the global initiative to standardize, and agreed with the goal of building and deploying a global system. This overarching goal proved to be too ambiguous and amorphous to base a system design on; the stakeholders had to identify precise requirements and had to compromise on the final solution. However, the persistent shared agreement is sustained throughout the negotiation process, even while conflicts over implementation details are being addressed and stakeholders are compromising on specific requirements. Thus, the intent of the negotiation was not for individuals to ‘get what they want’, but rather was a time of ‘goal discov ery’ in which participants ‘discover what to want’ (Brett et al., 1999, p. 437). These results provide some evidence that stakeholder goals can change and evolve during negotiation, influencing the requirements determination process. This is important since much prior research ‘neglect(s) the dynamic, interactive nature of negotiation’ (Brett et al., 1999, p. 435). Davidson’s (2002) study provides additional evidence about the evolution of requirements over time, demonstrating, for example, how changes in participation can result in changes in requirements.

The research findings also suggest that it may be useful to begin to categorize system development eforts based on the primary project sponsor and whether the project most resembles system building, system replacement, or system merging and consolidation. In both the Credit and Metrics cases, the new project more closely resembled a standardization or merger efort than building a new system. In both organizations, local regions have existing systems that will be replaced by the global common system. Thus, in the negotiation phase, dificulty arises from disputes over current features that must be given up rather than what features the new system will have. This type of a situation may be more contentious than when a new system is built and there is no existing system. The Metrics project User Manager commented:

When we got to new territory, that was very easy to come to agreement, because we were starting without history, without bias, without existing processes, and we could very quickly come to agreement on what’s the ideal.

Another factor that may have contributed to the dificulty of the negotiation stage is that, in both cases, the vision and motivation for the common global system originated in a central part of the organization, either central management or central IT. Then, the vision was pushed out to the local regions. Although representatives of the local regions agreed to the global vision, this situation contrasts with the common situation in which business users request a system that is subsequently designed and built by an IT function with input from the business users.

Finally, the finding that requirements determination consists of a two-stage approach of knowledge acquisition and negotiation builds on and elaborates earlier studies. In particular, Newman and Noble (1990) proposed a multi-stage model in which user involvement varies over diferent development stages. They suggest that early development is characterized as one-way learning, in which users learn about systems and articulate their requirements for a new system. Once designs were produced, Newman and Noble observed open conflict between users and IS specialists, as well as political persuasion and negotiation. Later in the development process, after conflicts were resolved, the authors noted mutual learning and adaptation on the part of users and IS specialists. The Newman and Noble study is important as it highlights how IS-user interactions change over the development life cycle. This study also documents changing interactions, but it examines in more depth the requirements determination stage, noting that not only does ‘one-way learning’ (knowledge acquisition) occur during this stage, but also negotiation. In addition, in the Newman and Noble study, conflict arose after designs were produced because of the user’s perception that the designs did not match the specified requirements. In this study, conflict arises during the requirements determination process over the requirements themselves, not over whether the system matches the requirements. This study also highlights that the negotiation takes place among users as they try to reach consensus on global requirements, and not just between IS specialists and users, which has been the focus of many earlier studies. As other researchers have noted (Davidson, 2002; Kuljis et al., 1998; Walz et al., 1993), when multiple user groups are involved in the project, learning about multiple domains and building consensus about requirements are very challenging.

## 5.1. Limitations and implications for practice

Before discussing the implications for practice, limitations of this study should be noted. One potential limitation is that the findings are derived from just two case studies. Though this is a small number of cases, the data collection process resulted in more than 300 pages of transcripts, project documentation, and field notes. Moreover, the sites provided rich insight into requirements determination as this process played a critical role in both firms eforts to build and implement these common solutions. Second, this study focused on a specific type of requirements determination—the identification of common global requirements—and may, therefore, be limited in its relevance. However, many firms are deploying large common systems, and regardless of whether they are implementing a package or custom developing a solution, they typically go through the requirements determination process. The findings reported here are salient for these firms. A third limitation is that the retrospective nature of the study may result in incomplete or inaccurate data. Numerous steps were taken to establish reliability and validity, however, mitigating this concern. In addition, because project participants were still engaged in installing the systems in various locations at the time of the study, project facts appeared to be quite fresh in their minds.

The findings presented here have several implications for managers. First, the study highlights the tension between a vision for a global common system and the reality of satisfying local needs and desires. Even though stakeholders may agree to the need for a common system, they may be reluctant to agree to specific common requirements. The paper describes the way in which two firms successfully surfaced and resolved (at least some) global–local diferences utilizing a mix of structured approaches and intense discourse and debate. It is important that managers recognize that though the knowledge acquisition process may be relatively straightforward, negotiation will be messy and will require patience, time, and diplomacy. Further, the findings suggest that common requirements and system design cannot be mandated (cf. Markus and Keil, 1994): the failure of the early project at Metrics illustrates this phenomenon. Instead, management must develop the commitment and buy-in of the local regions to a global solution. Finally, the findings emphasize the need for specialized training for project participants, especially the facilitator who plays a crucial role in forging global requirements. Skills in interviewing techniques, negotiation, compromise, conflict resolution, and consensus-building are critical to successfully determine global requirements for large common systems and to engender commitment and buy-in to the global solution.

## 6. Conclusions

This study investigated the requirements determination process for large common systems that are deployed globally. The results suggest that requirements determination consists of two stages: knowledge acquisition and negotiation. The knowledge acquisition process is comprised of learning about the local domains and identifying potential requirements. Nego tiation encompasses forging agreement to a global system, building commitment to a global system, compromising on requirements, and fostering efective communication. The knowledge acquisition and negotiation processes are influenced by a common, global vision; needs and priorities of the local regions; and the skills and organizational roles of key stakeholders. Successful knowledge acquisition and negotiation result in a set of common, global requirements as well as consensus and buy-in to the global system on the part of the local regions.

The results of this study suggest several avenues for future research. In highlighting the importance to participants of reaching consensus and buy-in, the research results go beyond a simplistic view of the political nature of the requirements determination process in which the most powerful group gets its way. Rather, this emphasis suggests that it is very dificult to mandate system use. Consensus and buy-in are critical to ensuring the system will be used as designed and that the common global requirements will be maintained. Future research could examine whether buy-in is as important as suggested by this study, as well as the processes that lead to consensus and buy-in. It would also be interesting to examine potential barriers to consensus, such as incentive structures that are not aligned with the proposed new system and the new business process it is being designed to support.

As these two cases have suggested, the facilitator is very important. Future research could examine the role of the facilitator in both stages of the requirements determination process (knowledge acquisition and negotiation), and investigate the relative value of techniques by which the facilitator structures and controls the process. It would also be useful to attempt to identify the personal and social network characteristics of an efective facilitator.

It may also be useful for future research to examine the similarities and diferences among IS projects according to a taxonomy that categorizes development eforts based on who requested the development, users or management, and whether the new system represents a totally new system, replacement of an existing system, or consolidation of existing systems.

Finally, though this study focused on the development of a common global system, the results may be applicable to domestic systems designed to meet the needs of multiple units within or across organizations. Future research is needed to expand and elaborate the model in other contexts.
