# HOW TECHNOLOGY-AFFORDED PRACTICES AT THE MICRO-LEVEL CAN GENERATE CHANGE AT THE FIELD LEVEL: THEORIZING THE RECURSIVE MECHANISM ACTUALIZED IN SWEDISH RHEUMATOLOGY 2000–2014<sup>1</sup>

Anna Essén House of Innovation, Stockholm School of Economics, Box 6501, SE-113 83 Stockholm, SWEDEN {anna.essen@hhs.se}

Sara Winterstorm Värlander

Center for Advanced Studies in Leadership, Stockholm School of Economics, Box 6501, SE-113 83 Stockholm, SWEDEN {sara.winterstorm.varlander@hhs.se}

The information systems literature has paid a great deal of attention to how macro-level structures shape local technology enactments. Less research has focused on the mutual shaping of situated technology enactments and such extra-organizational structures. This study explores how technology-afforded human action at the micro-level may transform field-level social structures and thus generate institutional change. We use a critical realist approach and institutional logics lens and draw on empirical data about the development and implementation of an e-health service in Swedish rheumatology between 2000 and 2014. We identify a recursive mechanism consisting of three recursive practices—material reconstruction, discursive reconstruction, and emergent use—that fostered a shift in the field’s constellation of institutional logics, moving from a competitive to an additive relationship between logics.

Keywords: Recursive mechanism, micro-level enactments, technology affordances, healthcare, longitudina case study, field-level change, institutional logics, critical realism

## Introduction

A large body of studies in the information systems (IS) field has investigated how institutions shape the way organizational members use technology in situ (Boudreau and Robey 2005; Chu and Robey 2008; Hultin and Mähring 2014; Leonardi and Barley 2010; Orlikowski and Barley 2001). As argued by several scholars (Leonardi and Barley 2010; Orlikowski and Barley 2001), there are however few empirically substantiated conceptualizations of the mutual shaping of institutional structures at the field-level and users’ situated deployment of technology. With the exception of a limited number of studies (Barrett and Walsham 1999; Barrett et al. 2013; Scarbrough et al. 2015), IS studies tend to focus on how field-level institutions unilaterally shape, rather than being shaped by, the situated use of technology at the micro-level (e.g., Azad and King 2012; Berente 2009; Boudreau and Robey 2005; Cho et al. 2007; Davidson and Chismar 2007; Hultin and Mähring 2014; Lim et al. 2011; Yeow 2011; Seidel and Berente 2013).

We argue that the lack of theorization about the potential of micro-level technology enactments to reshape and even transform structures at the macro-level is problematic because we “cannot adequately explain the microsocial dynamics of technological change without considering the macrosocial processes of technological change (and vice versa)” (Leonardi and Barley 2010, p. 41). Against this background, this paper attempts to answer how technology-afforded human action occurring at the micro-level may transform social structures at the field level and thus generate institutional change. To answer this question, we combine a critical realist (CR) approach with an institutional logics lens and draw on data from a case study of the development and implementation of an e-health service in Swedish rheumatology between the years 2000 and 2014. We outline how the competitive constellation of logics (Goodrick and Reay 2011; Thornton and Ocasio 1999) governing the field of rheumatology shifted into an additive constellation during the study period. An additive relationship between logics implied that work practices were influenced by multiple logics and needed to be in order to be legitimate and considered appropriate (Goodrick and Reay 2011). In line with our CR approach, we also explain the recursive mechanism driving this change toward reconciled logics in rheumatology. Specifically, we identify three practices occurring at the micro-level—material reconstruction, discursive reconstruction, and emergent use—which together formed a recursive mechanism that generated the institutional transformation observed. These insights pave the way for future research initiatives aimed at explaining the micro– macro mechanisms through which situated technology enactments may generate institutional change in healthcare and beyond.

The paper is structured as follows. First, we selectively outline the state of the art in research on the mutual shaping of technology, social structure, and human action. We then present our institutional logics lens followed by an introduction of our CR approach. This section is followed by an outline of our method. Subsequently, we introduce our findings, followed by a discussion of our inductively derived model of how recursive practices lead to institutional change. The paper ends with conclusions and suggestions for further research.

## Mutual Shaping of Technology, Local Technology Enactments, and Social Structures In the IS Literature

A limited but significant body of studies within the IS literature attempts to understand the process through which technology, local technology enactments, and social structures mutually constitute each other. The diverse studies located within this body of research have focused on different dimensions of the co-constitutive process, and draw on different ontological approaches, including CR and/or imbrication theory (Leonardi 2011, 2013), institutional logics, or structuration theory (Barley 1986; Hultin and Mähring 2014; Leonardi and Barley 2011), and the sociomaterial approach (Orlikowski 2007). As visually summarized in Figure 1, studies using CR or imbrication theory (left side) separate material structures from organizational practices but typically exclude field-level structures. Studies drawing on institutional logics and/or a structuration lens (middle) typically distinguish between social structures and micro-level practices but do not separate material structures from micro-level practices. The sociomaterial view (right side) assumes there are no structures preceding or external to practice (structures are actualized in practice only).

While acknowledging their differences, we will hereafter refer to the streams of research abstracted in Figure 1 as recursiveness studies, because they all exhibit an interest in if and how situated heterogeneous appropriations, interpretations, or enactments of technology may, over time, lead to a convergence of (new) practices, (new) formal structures, or (new) shared understandings.

An analysis of the foci of recursiveness studies (see detailed overview in Appendix A), however, revealed that they tend to be limited to studying processes of co-constitution within certain organizations (i.e. intra-organizational levels) (Barley 1986, 1990; Leonardi 2011, 2013; Orlikowski 1992, 2000; Orlikowski et al. 1995). Further, the few studies that expand the scope to consider the links between extra-organizational social structures and local technology deployment typically highlight how technology use reproduces rather than transforms extra-organizational structures (e.g., Azad and King 2012; Bartelt and Dennis 2014; Boudreau and Robey 2005; Cho et al. 2007; Chu and Robey 2008; Davidson and Chismar 2007; Lim et al. 2011; Seidel and Berente 2013; Su 2015) and emphasize the constraining influences of institutional structures on individuals’ actions and IS use patterns. For example, professional norms influenced users’ enactment of technology, leading users to avoid features that could “interfere with traditional work practices” (Chu and Robey 2008, p. 94), and local responses to an enterprise system appeared “to align with society’s broader institutions that guide microlevel behavior” (Berente 2009, p. viii). Notably, Barrett et al. (2013) and Scarbrough et al. (2015) touched upon the mutual shaping of technology and social structures at the field level without theorizing how the development and use of technology at the micro-level can foster field-level institutional change. Further, Hultin and Mähring (2014) and Raviola and Norbäck (2013) defined social structures as multiple coexisting institutional logics. Although they showed that logics were variably invoked in local technology enactments, they did not address how this variability at the local level may evolve into new or transformed institutional logics at the field level.

In sum, current IS scholarship highlights the flexibility of technology enactments but pays less attention to the flexibility of field-level social structures. Although most scholars assume, in theory, that institutional structures and local technology enactments mutually shape each other, conceptualizations of the mechanisms through which this mutual constitution occurs is lacking. This provides a limited view of human agency and marginalizes the potential of technology to provide possibilities for action, which may evolve into social change at the macro-level. In the next section, we present the institutional logics lens that constitutes the first building block in the development of our approach to study the recursiveness between technology, human action, and social structures at the field level. The institutional-logics lens allows us to treat social structures as multiple and variably invoked by individuals in their development and use of technology. This lens thus enables us to conceptualize, in a more fine-grained way, the multiplicity of what is sometimes referred to monolithically as “work ethos” (Azad and King 2012, p. 362), or “institutional properties” (Orlikowski 1992, p. 410) in the IS literature.

## Unpacking Field-Level Social Structures with Institutional Logics

Institutional theory recently acknowledged the importance of bridging micro-level practices and macro-level structures to understand institutional enactment and change (Lounsbury and Crumley 2007; Smets et al. 2012). Institutional logics have been defined as macro-level belief systems that shape actors’ cognitions and actions at the field level (Friedland and

Alford 1991). An organizational field is defined as a set of interdependent populations and organizations participating in the same cultural and social subsystem (Scott and Davis 2007). “Logics provide a frame of reference that preconditions actors’ sensemaking choices” (Thornton et al. 2012, p. 54). Thus, logics act as “taken-for-granted social prescriptions” (Battilana and Dorado 2010, p. 1420). The benefit of the logics approach is that it acknowledges that organizations are immersed in environments where multiple, more-or-less compatible, logics guide members of a field (Friedland and Alford 1991; Kraatz and Block 2008). Although multiple logics may impose competing demands and pressures on organizations and their members (Greenwood et al. 2010; Jarzabkowski et al. 2013), multiple logics can also coexist in a less competitive relationship by informing organizational members simultaneously. Work may be segmented between different logics, and “some parts of professional work [may] reflect attributes from one logic while other parts reflect attributes from different logics” (Goodrick and Reay 2011, p. 379). Based on this assumption, Goodrick and Reay (2011) introduced the notion of a constellation of logics to describe how several logics can combine to influence professional work practices at a given point in time, whereas the relative influence of the different logics in the constellation may differ over time. In an environment with multiple logics at play, studies have also shown that individuals can use logics strategically as a “tool kit” (Swidler 1986) and to achieve certain goals (Smets et al. 2012). Thus, institutional logics are produced and reproduced in everyday practice as individuals selectively enact and combine logics in their variable performances in situ (McPherson and Sauder 2013).

While advancing our knowledge about how multiple logics may be enacted in daily practice, and that this may change over time, institutional logics research only recently started to acknowledge technology as part of institutional processes. For example, it has been suggested that technology and artifacts should be viewed as part of logics (Cloutier and Langley 2013), implying that logics prescribe a particular technology infrastructure and that technology is simply to be viewed as a manifestation of the values and strategies imposed by the logic. This, however, suggests that technologies are mere “carriers” of logics rather than actively involved in processes of institutionalization.

In summary, the institutional logics lens provides a remedy to the lack of conceptualizations and empirical illustrations of the coexistence of multiple, flexible, social structures in current IS studies. However, the institutional logics lens lacks analytic tools for detangling the particular role of material structures in processes of institutionalization (Figure 1, middle). The institutional logics literature has also been criticized for lacking clarity regarding the ontological status of logics (although logics are frequently referred to as shaping human action, they are sometimes defined as realized in action only), and analysts call for more explicit definitions of the relationship between structure and action to solve this paradox of institutional embedded agency (Leca and Naccache 2006). Against this background, we combine the institutional logics lens with the ontological CR approach described below.

## Bringing in Technology: A Critical Realist Approach

The CR view has been suggested as a promising approach to studies of IS phenomena (Leonardi 2013; Mingers 2004; Smith 2006), in part because it assumes that outcomes are influenced by an interplay between individual actors (their interpretations and use of technology, and technology itself) its particular inherent properties, as well as the social context. CR acknowledges the existence of structures that both constrain and enable social activities (Archer 1995; Bhaskar 1998). Human action, on the other hand, is endowed with both the reproduction and transformation of practices and structures (Ekström 1992). Hence, CR emphasizes that structures and actors’ actions are separate and related entities that can neither collapse nor conflate into each other (Archer 1995; Sayer 2000). Thus, CR provides an answer to how actors can be influenced by the very structures that their actions can change (the paradox of institutionally embedded agency; Leca and Naccache 2006).

From a CR view, social structures, which we conceptualize as logics, are real, despite being immaterial entities, because they have causal efficacy and have an effect on behavior (Fleetwood 2005). The assumption is that social structures preexist individuals’ current activity whereas individuals may invoke logics heterogeneously in situ. The same applies to technology; that is, material structure. Technology is a structure in the sense that it predates actors’ actions and perceptions; when technology is introduced into context, it comes with a preconfigured materiality. Thus, technology is considered “real” in the sense that it has properties “which may have causal potential, where causal potential is understood to mean that a property may be a necessary condition for some outcome to occur” (Markus and Silver 2008, p. 621). Gibson’s (1979) concept of affordance is useful as a way to conceptualize the causal power of technology. Volkoff and Strong (2013) define an affordance as “the potential for behaviors associated with achieving an immediate concrete outcome and arising from the relation between an object (e.g., an IT artifact) and a goal-oriented actor or actors” (p. 823). This suggests that affordances are potentials for action rather than the action itself and that affordances refer to the relational aspect between an object and actor. A technology can afford different things for different actors depending on their goals and the ways in which they combine the various features of the technological artifact (Leonardi 2011). The nature of and extent to which an affordance is actualized hence depends on the presence of a variety of “enabling, stimulating and releasing conditions … as well as other mechanisms arising from broader organizational and environmental structures” (Volkoff and Strong 2013, p. 828). We argue that logics provide a lens through which we can understand the relationship between such environmental structures and technological affordances.

Figure 2 illustrates our analytical framework, which offers a lens for studying the mutually constitutive but non-conflated relationship among material structure (hardware and software features of technology), human action (technology-afforded behaviors), and social structure (institutional logics) over time. The assumption is that social and material structures predate and afford certain human actions at $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 } .$ In turn, the structurally afforded human actions at $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 }$ can lead to modification of the material features of the technology. In such cases, a new material structure emerges in $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 } ,$ which can be understood as a phase that is qualitatively different from $\mathrm { T _ { 1 } }$ (Barley 1986; Langley 1999). The technology afforded human behaviors at $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 }$ can also imply transformation of the social structures in $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 } .$ . Human action at $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 }$ can, however, also imply reproduction of the social structures and no modification of the material structures. That is, the structures in $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 }$ may remain the same or change, depending on human action (situated behavior at the micro-level). ## Method

We draw on an in-depth single case study methodology, which allows the use of multiple sources of data to build an “explanatory theory that matches the empirical facts as closely as possible” and is well suited for understanding multilevel interactions (Wynn and Williams 2012, p. 804). It is also suitable for longitudinal studies where explanations are sought (Dobson 2001).

The present case study was part of a larger project focused on the development, implementation, and usage of the Swedish rheumatology quality registry (the Registry) in Swedish rheumatology between 1995 and 2014. The Registry was initiated by the Swedish Society for Rheumatologists (SSR) in 1995. Rheumatologists used it voluntarily to document drug prescriptions and patient health outcomes and continuously added new features to the Registry in parallel with its diffusion. The present study focuses on the development and implementation of one such feature, namely an e-service added to the Registry in 2001: the Patient sElf Registration service (PER). The process was purposively sampled because it provided us with the possibility to explain how an e-service that was initially perceived as controversial was adopted by a majority of rheumatologists a decade later, and how this changed the institutional field of Swedish rheumatology. We followed this process through interviews and observations from 2009 to 2014, while drawing on archival data tracing its historical development from 2000 onward.

Although the rheumatologists involved in the process worked at clinics spread across all 20 regions and counties in Sweden, they shared a background in specialist training and they all faced similar tasks revolving around the diagnosis and monitoring of chronically ill patients on a regular basis over long time periods. Further, virtually all the Swedish rheumatologists were members of the SSR and other interorganizational and even international professional rheumatology associations that developed medical guidelines targeting their practice. Rheumatologists experienced pressure to comply with the guidelines in their daily practice, to use the research insights provided by the community, and to engage in research activities in parallel with their clinical work. Thus, Swedish rheumatology can be conceptualized as a profession (Friedson 1970) governed by its particular definitions of “professional” practice.

## Data Collection

Because CR assumes a reality composed of many types of structures, different methods are required to create knowledge about them (Wynn and Williams 2012). Our use of a mix of documentation, interviews, and observations reduces the risk of bias and compensates for various weaknesses of each method, including informants’ potentially distorted views of reality (Mingers 2004), and it provides different and complementary perspectives (see more details about this principle in Appendix B).

## Archival Data

We started the data collection by gathering extensive documentation. Medical guidelines, governmental reports, and PER grant applications provided an understanding of how the professional and healthcare context changed over time. It also provided input to the interview protocols; for instance, enabling us to trace important events in the development, implementation, and use of the PER. Triangulating archival data with the retrospective accounts from the interviews enabled us to craft a historical narrative of the development, diffusion, and use of the PER system from 2000 to 2014.

## Interviews

We conducted 80 semi-structured interviews with the broad goal of learning about respondents’ experiences with the PER between 2009 and 2014 (see Table 1). In addition, we conducted 48 interviews with rheumatologists, nurses, and clinical managers, most of whom were PER users but had been nonusers in the past. Some were still nonusers at the time of the interview. We also conducted 10 interviews with 8 members of the Registry committee that developed the PER, many of whom were also user rheumatologists. Hence, among our Registry committee members, there was no clear boundary between “developer” and “user.” These interviews were geared more at understanding the development process. Finally, we interviewed 19 external stakeholders (on 22 occasions) to reach a better understanding of the ambitions of external actors in the institutional field; that is, the broader environment in which the everyday practice of rheumatology was embedded. Ethical approval was gained from the regional ethical vetting board of Stockholm (which is responsible for vetting the ethics of research involving sensitive information about people or personal information) in 2010 and again in 2014 to enable follow-up interviews. Access to rheumatologists and patients was facilitated by the Registry administration.

To gather rich interview data, we drew on a technique referred to as laddering (Schultze and Avital 2011), wherein the interviewees are invited to elaborate on the links between constructs that emerge in the interview. For example, when a respondent said he or she did not use the PER because it was not aligned with the perceived needs for evidence, we asked what “real evidence” meant and invited the respondent to elaborate on why it was not aligned. This allowed us to understand the means–ends chains and the values that guided particular practices. Because part of the interview questions aimed at how the respondent had engaged with PER over time, we also used bounded recall, an artificial restriction of the reference time period (Auriat 1993) that enhances interviewees’ ability to recall specific events. We asked respondents to talk about whether and how they used the PER when it was made available nationally in 2003; after it had been available for some time (anchoring this question in relation to national events and PER modifications); and, finally, their current experiences of the PER. The interviews lasted 40 to 90 minutes (approximately 110 hours in total). The first author conducted, recorded, and transcribed the interviews.

## Observations

To reach a deeper understanding of rheumatologists’ interactions with the technology in situ, the first author conducted nonparticipant observations of 15 patient–rheumatologist interactions at two clinics using the PER in two Swedish counties (preceded by informed consent provided by patients and physicians). The first author also performed participant observations of more than 80 professional meetings where the Registry and PER were discussed. Field notes were taken during all observations and expanded after each session in order to fill in as much as possible what may have been left out due to the pressure of keeping pace with the ongoing interactions. ## Data Analysis

Our focus at the outset of the study was broadly to explore technology implementation in organizations with a focus on the variation in system usage. One issue that emerged as salient during the data collection was that rheumatologists perception of the PER had been motivated by seemingly different ideals about how to perform their work between 2000 and 2014. The ideals also seemed to change as a result of their use of the PER over time. The subsequent analysis of the data can be described as an iterative process aimed at making sense of (1) the nature of this change in ideals and (2) how it had come about. We describe the major analytic steps below.

We analyzed the data following the approach of thematic analysis. Thematic analysis is “a method for identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns (themes) within data” (Braun and Clarke 2006, p. 79). First, we performed an initial coding of the entire data set. This first round enabled us to uncover prevailing themes in the data set. One salient theme from this first round of coding was micro–macro interactions; that is, a change in rheumatologists’ norms and values, which seemed to intertwine with preceding technology implementation and use. To make sense of this interaction, we resorted to the literature on system usage and IS implementation. However, its capacity to help us unpack the complex and changing meaning systems at the institutional level that appeared so strongly in our data was limited. For example, we attempted to apply conceptualizations from scholarship on organizational routines, imbrication theory, and versions of sociomateriality but realized these theories did not capture the dynamic nature of, and interplay among, the material structure of technology, human action, and social structures at the field level. This helped us outline the gap in the literature, as depicted in Figure 1. Engaging with the literature on institutional theory, particularly that on logics and critical realism, helped us identify our specific research question (Figure 2). Hence, as is often the case in thematic analysis, our research question evolved from the initial coding of the data (Braun and Clarke 2006).

## Identifying the Outcome to be Explained

With the help of the institutional logics approach, we theorized the empirically observed theme of a “change in values” noted in 2014 as being a reflection of a change in the institutional logics existing in the domain of the real. We coded empirical instances where rheumatologists referred to “how things were to be done” or “typically done” and shared values such as “most of us” or “none of us” as manifestations of field-level logics. We iterated between our emerging definitions of the logics and the definition of logics found in previous studies on healthcare (Dunn and Jones 2010; Reay and Hinings 2009). Our data confirmed the multiple logics outlined in these studies: a logic of science that emphasized providing care based on evidence and maximizing health outcomes and patient safety; a logic of care that prescribed a focus on providing care catered to patients’ holistic needs; and a logic of management that prescribed cost-effective care provisioning. The exact content and way in which the logics were enacted were, however, specific to our study setting. Also, similar to these studies, we found that the relationship between logics changed, becoming less competitive over time (Dunn and Jones 2010; Reay and Hinings 2009). ## Identifying the Mechanism Producing a Shift in the Constellation of Institutional Logics

With the goal to identify how, and the period during which, the change at the field level had come about, we identified two empirical events as critical (Wynn and Williams 2012): First, the accomplishment of the first physical prototype of the PER in 2000, and second, the implementation of the PER at a majority of clinics in Sweden (as suggested by annual registry reports and user logs) and rheumatologists using the PER for multiple purposes and the associated change in fieldlevel values (as suggested by interviews) in early 2009 (Phase 3). In line with CR, the key task was then to identify the mechanisms explaining the process of transition between these two key events. That is, the assumption was that the PER did not alone determine the change but that it was one among several components in the domain of the real that afforded human action; that is, certain social processes that, in turn, caused the new state from early 2009 (Hedström and Swedberg 1996; see Appendix B for more details on how we adhered to the principles of CR studies outlined by Wynn and Williams).

From the empirical domain, we then aimed to identify the mechanism that had been at work during the period between the two critical events and that created the outcomes at the real level (Leca and Naccache 2006; Wynn and Williams 2012) in the form of a changed constellation of logics. To this end, we engaged in retroduction and empirical corroboration (Wynn and Williams 2012; described in more detail in Appendix B). We created different time lines, chronological narratives, and data figures with the major events at the micro-level and the constellation of field-level logics that our material suggested at each point in time (Appendix C). These timelines made the recursive pattern between the material structure of the technology, micro-level use of the technology, and field-level changes clearer. Specifically, we applied broad-ranged temporal bracketing (Pozzebon and Pinsonneault 2005), which is a suitable method to detect mechanisms that drive social transformations (Barrett and Walsham 1999; Pozzebon and Pinsonneault 2005). Figure 3 provides a simplified timeline of the chronological sequence of events. It shows the three significantly and qualitatively different periods we bracketed, where the first and third periods demonstrate different constellations of logics in Phase 1 and Phase 3 respectively (also see Appendix C), whereas Phase 2 was a phase of transition, in which the interrelationships between logics shifted, over time changing from a competitive to an additive constellation of logics. This is the focal period of our longitudinal analysis, and it is treated in depth through the case narrative and the analysis. ## Explaining Institutional Change in Swedish Rheumatology: Tracing the Recursive Mechanism

In 1995, the SSR introduced the patient health assessment form (Patient form) as an add-on to the Registry. The Patient form, being the predecessor of the PER, included standardized questions about patients’ subjectively perceived functional ability, pain, and overall health. Hence, the Patient form was intended for rheumatologists but required input from patients. In 1995, the Patient form was paper based, and rheumatologists manually entered the patients’ answers into the registry via a computer. Rheumatologists in the registry steering committee administered the Patient form and encouraged their peers to use it. However, between 1995 and 2000, the majority of rheumatologists refrained from using the Patient form. Many were hesitant about the value of the “evidence” it generated. The salience of this view in our material indicated that underlying the initial nonuse of the Patient form was the widespread evidence-based ideal (logic of science), that coexisted with the logics of care and management in a competitive constellation in 2000, as outlined in Table 2. A competitive relationship between the logics implied that “one logic’s ‘win’ is another logic’s ‘loss’; that is, increases in the strength of one logic occur with a corresponding decrease in another logic” (Goodrick and Reay 2011, p. 401). Such a competitive relationship between the logics was expressed by a rheumatologist recalling this period in time:

We used to say there were two kinds of doctors. Those interested in research on the one hand, and those interested in patient care on the other. And then there were managers, who were interested in cutting costs .… Reconciling these interests was not easy.

The Patient form was digitized in 2001 and labeled “the Patient sElf Registration service” (PER). The motivations behind this initiative included making the registration of patient data easier and quicker by saving administration work related to the paper-based Patient form, creating more environment-friendly, paperless clinical offices, and digitizing care generally. The PER was adopted by only a few rheumatologists in 2001–2004 (Phase 1); slowly but successively adopted by more rheumatologists from 2005 to 2008 (Phase 2, which we conceptualize as a transition period); and, from early 2009 on, used at a majority of rheumatology clinics in Sweden (Phase 3). At this time, an institutional shift had occurred, wherein the competing relationship between the logics of science, care, and management in Swedish rheumatology had evolved into an additive relationship (Table 2). Again, as stated in the introduction, an additive relationship, as defined by Goodrick and Reay (2011), implies that work practices are influenced by multiple logics and need to be in order to be legitimate and considered appropriate. In our case, this involved a win–win situation, in which the enactment of one logic could simultaneously reinforce another logic.

Below, we present the three practices that together composed the recursive mechanism generating this shift in the constellation of logics in the Swedish rheumatology field between

2000 and 2014. The three practices were all mobilized around PER.

## Practice 1: Material Reconstruction

The first practice that had a salient role in contributing to the institutional change was what we refer to as material reconstruction. In this practice, individual or groups of rheumatologists deliberately or unintentionally challenged the competitive relationships between the logics, as established on the field level, by managing the design of and adding new functionality to the PER. An illustrative example occurred in 2001, when a rheumatologist at a large university hospital acquired a research grant for developing the PER and became the leader of the project. The PER was incorporated as a module in the national registry in 2003 and became a project administered by a few individuals in the Registry steering committee, hereafter referred to as the PER Team (composed of the leader, a few other rheumatologists, coordinators, and IT specialists). The PER was an electronic imitation of the paper-based Patient form. However, PER also included a set of new questions asking patients to make a self-assessment of their swollen and tender joints by using a standardized questionnaire. Rheumatologists had previously performed and documented this assessment in face-to-face interactions with patients because it required a certain technique of manually examining each joint. As described by a rheumatologist:

With its particular user rights, PER guided individuals logged in as “patients” in how to assess and report their joint status into the Registry. The system asked patients to evaluate an aspect of their symptoms that they had never before been asked or allowed to evaluate formally.

Hence, the PER made it technologically possible for patients to formally evaluate a broader range of health outcomes than before. Further, the PER constrained rheumatologists’ possibilities to double check or modify the patient’s assessment before it was stored in the Registry. This was due to the PER making it possible for patients to enter their assessment data directly into the computer in the waiting room, bypassing the rheumatologist. A salient view was that this functionality challenged the understanding that professionals or researchers—with an objective perspective of what was measured—should generate Registry data, which in turn was used as evidence in scientific studies. This was illustratively expressed by the IT designer:

PER technologically opened up the door between patients and the registry, which was the scientific database. Rheumatologists found this direct interface between the patient and the formal health IT systems scary—would it threaten the quality of the data?

Another example of material reconstruction was when, in 2004, Swedish health agencies launched a national web portal enabling patients to schedule visits and renew prescriptions. Shortly after, the PER Team acquired research grants that funded an integration of the PER with this portal. The PER Team’s intention behind the integration was to improve the “patient centeredness” of care by allowing patients more flexible online opportunities to continuously use data. The integration offered patients, independently of where they lived, the possibility to assess and analyze their health changes in the PER from their own computers in their own homes. The existence of these material possibilities, whether or not they were used, suggested that engaging patients in new forms of standardized health analysis and improvement was feasible and a way to achieve patient-centered care. This challenged the established view that rheumatologists were the ones to achieve patient centeredness and care improvement, and that this required empathy, nonstandardized qualitative inquiry, and face-to-face communication. As phrased by a rheumatologist:

[Patient-centered care] was seen as a matter of being mindful of one’s own behavior, that it was important for us to listen and adjust to patients’ multidimensional needs and holistic situation[s] .… In contrast, the PER–portal link enabled a change in the role of patients, offering them possibilities to do some of the work … beyond the walls of the clinic and its open hours.

By enabling patients to use structured, quantitative data from their own homes in ways that improved care from their point of view, the integration of PER with the portal hence also put into question the established dichotomy between care and science (Table 2).

The PER system design and functionality were continuously reconstructed between 2003 and 2014 (see Figure 3 for a time line of events and Appendix D for more empirical examples of material reconstruction), which generated a steady stream of new material affordances that challenged the competitive relationship between the ideals in the rheumatologist community. We discuss below how certain rheumatologists realized these material potentials in their daily practice.

## Practice 2: Emergent Use

From 2000 to 2004, the majority of rheumatologists rejected PER because they were reluctant to spend time on a “patient procedure” not supported by evidence proving its contribution to effective medical practice. However, a small group of rheumatologists in the PER Team and their close network challenged this established interpretation of the evidencebased ideal (logic of science). They let their patients interact via touch screens and a printer in the waiting room to fill in the PER and print their assessment before the care encounter. The rheumatologists then imported the patient’s reported data into the Registry, performed manual examinations of the patient, and filled in the additional fields in the patient’s account in the Registry during the meeting. We refer to this kind of usage patterns as examples of emergent use. In this first example, the early rheumatologist users (i.e., rheumatology physicians) experienced that the PER afforded possibilities to generate more evidence they could use in their own research studies. The early users also unexpectedly found the quality of the patient-generated evidence superior to the data previously collected through the paper-based Patient form or not collected at all. As an early user noted:

I was surprised at how patients sat in the waiting room and frowned, analyzing how they had really felt lately. When I asked them, they often answered a bit sloppy, “Oh, I’ve been as usual, no big change,” but when the system asked them, they really tried to be more precise.

Thus, as this was discovered, early users began to see the PER as aligned with rather than as threatening the science logic. Moreover, early usage patterns started to challenge the established competitive relationship between science and care. Some rheumatologists perceived that PER afforded patients the possibilities to learn about their health in ways they had not envisioned. For example, they observed how their patients started to bring their printed assessment summaries to clinical appointments, asking more about certain values and probing for more information about the relationships among values. The rheumatologists frequently mentioned their surprise at seeing patients use evidence in the PER to understand, articulate, and communicate their unique needs to the doctor. Thus, the patient centeredness afforded by the PER also led to patients becoming more familiar with the use of data and evidence. The early usage patterns also implied that physicians used evidence in a patient-centered way. One rheumatologist stated:

The patients … seemed more confident … when having numbers, some kind of proof of what they wanted to communicate. This posed and poses new demands on me as a doctor. I have to be more explicit in why I make certain decisions rather than others .… But I also need to remember to relate to the specific patient, like, “In your specific case, we may want to do this instead, considering this score.”

Sometimes, patients also collected the printed forms at home to put together longitudinal analyses of their own health. What emerged from these uses was the view that the PER could enable patients to influence care in the direction they wanted by using the evidence they themselves had generated. This challenged the view that improving care and science were two antithetical aims.

As another example of emerging usage, rheumatologists at three clinics began improvising with the PER to adjust appointment scheduling to the variation in patients’ care needs over time from 2004 onward. This challenged the established practice of scheduling all appointments with the same standardized frequency. To understand variation in care needs, the rheumatologists used accumulated data from the Registry and PER to identify two types of patients: unstable and stable. Unstable patients were scheduled on frequent visits and could also be received on short notice to calibrate their therapy. Stable patients, on the other hand, were asked to initiate visits themselves, should they experience a flare-up in symptoms. The rheumatologists using this so-called Open– Tight scheduling model experienced that patients were not only capable but also appreciative of the opportunity to determine when they needed to visit. A few rural clinics adopted versions of the new scheduling model and experienced that it helped them deal with the lack of rheumatologists in their region because it freed up time from rheumatologists calendars. A nurse at one of the rural clinics explained:

We can now [with the Open–Tight model] serve a larger group of patients with the same amount of specialists without endangering their health outcomes.

Thus, these emergent experiences brought to the forefront a new purpose of the PER: to enable redistribution of tasks and economize scarce resources at the clinical level, an ambition legitimate within neither the logics of science nor care, as established at the field level. The endeavor to ensure resource efficiency was seen as tied to the logic of management, which a majority viewed as in conflict with the logics of science and care (Table 2). However, early adopters of the Open–Tight practice experienced that cost savings were not always in conflict with patient needs. The Open–Tight clinic was rather an example of how the different goals became additive. A rheumatologist working at a rural clinic explained:

Reducing costs is not always bad from a medical or patient perspective. Patients often prefer a phone call over a visit as long as they feel we monitor them [via PER].

Several emergent usage patterns, which engaged a minority of rheumatologists while puzzling the larger rheumatology community, surfaced over time (Figure 4 and Appendix D). These usage patterns challenged the competitive relationship between the logics of science, care, and management in subtle as well as radical ways by suggesting the goals tied to these logics could be reconciled. What the rheumatology community saw as challenging, however, changed over time. In 2014, those mentioned usage patterns had partly or completely diffused across the field, and a majority of rheumatologists saw them as quite legitimate and appropriate ways of conducting practice. As noted by a health informatics researcher in 2014:

These are common practices nowadays. Many specialties now offer their patients self-rating services that are structurally similar to PER, and using patient-reported data to evaluate new processes and interventions on a regular basis is not controversial anymore. I doubt the PER Team needs to explain the PER service and the Open–Tight clinic extensively now. Compare this to the early days, when they [the PER team] were forced to explain and explain and explain how PER could be used.

The frequent discussions about the PER contributed to this change, as discussed in the next section.

## Practice 3: Discursive Reconstruction

Verbal descriptions of the PER often implied a concurrent reframing of the values underlying rheumatologist practice. We refer to this third practice as discursive reconstruction. It occurred in the many peer-to-peer discussions about the PER within the rheumatologist professional community. For example, our informants recalled that the addition of new questions to the PER in 2003 triggered several discussions in which some rheumatologists expressed concern about patients’ abilities to assess their joints in a reliable way. In response, other rheumatologists referred to the PER as a means to secure evidence-based medicine in a patientcentered way. The PER “defenders” invoked nascent evidence indicating patient ability to assess their own joints reliably with the specific instrument used in the PER. They also referred to how their patients used PER data to more accurately communicate their needs, thus suggesting the PER could contribute to goals tied to the logics of both science and care.

Rheumatologists’ use of the PER to reorganize their scheduling processes in Open–Tight models from 2005 onward was further initially met with skeptical comments from other rheumatologists. The critics feared the labor-saving potential of the PER could lead to an underproduction of service, which in turn could threaten safe, evidence-based, as well as patientcentered, practice. Interestingly, only a few years later, the PER was viewed as aligned with the ideals of science, management, and care—and seen as affording increased safety and patient-centeredness due to the time savings it enabled. The quote of a rheumatologist at a seminar in 2012 illustrates this view:

The point of releasing time through PER is to make room for more individualized care. As PER takes care of routine questions before the meeting, the patient and I can then focus on things that are unique for that patient, that day, things that are not captured in PER during the meeting .… Hence, this is not about cutting service or automating care. It is rheumatology 2.0; it is the future .… Using technologies like PER to improve care is what modern rheumatologist practice should be about.

Another rheumatologist also explained how the rhetoric tied to PER changed over time to increasingly align the logics of care and management:

At first, the PER seemed like a fun thing people used in order to claim they were into the “e-patient” thing .… But listening to this guy, he explained that because of the strains at his clinic—they were lacking rheumatologists—they used the PER and found it made them more efficient and their patients happier .… This made me rethink the PER … and my own responsibility.

Similarly, other rheumatologists justified their use of the PER to reorganize care and evaluate such reorganizations by arguing this was a matter of expanding rather than compromising evidence-based thinking. Thus, PER was framed as affording practices aligned with the logics of science, care, and management simultaneously.

## One rheumatologist argued during a 2012seminar:

We have a long tradition of using medical evidence for evaluating different drugs. Now, we also need to see the way we work, who does what, as in need of evidence-based evaluation to ensure we reorganize our processes on the basis of relevant data.

Many rheumatologists underlined that the discussions were important to achieve a shared view of the purpose of the PER and the goals of rheumatology practice. The fact that rheumatologist users and nonusers continuously exchanged verbal descriptions of how the PER could (or could not) contribute to several aims simultaneously contributed to a convergence of rheumatologists’ views of the PER over time. In turn, this contributed to adoption of the PER by a majority of rheumatologists from early 2009. This development was further closely intertwined with the majority of rheumatologists growing understanding of the relationships among the ideals governing their practice as mutually reinforcing. That is, the continuous discursive reconstruction of the PER contributed to normalization and nationwide use of the PER for purposes related to the logics of science, care, and management simultaneously and to a reconciliation of these historically competitive logics.

## How the Recursive Mechanism Generates Institutional Change: A Conceptual Model

Our inductively derived model shows how three recursive practices—material reconstruction, discursive reconstruction, and emergent use—together formed a mechanism (Bhaskar 1998; Volkoff and Strong 2013; Wynn and Williams 2012) that generated institutional change in the field of Swedish rheumatology from 2000 to 2014. Figure 5 provides an abstracted visual illustration of the model. We define and discuss the general operation of the three recursive practices below.

## Defining the Three Recursive Practices

At a general level, the practice of material reconstruction refers to the process through which humans transform the meanings of and relationships among prevailing logics by shaping what the technology is (its artifactually real dimensions; Fleetwood 2005). Thus, the result of this practice is the modification of a technology’s material structure (Leonardi 2013). Material reconstruction differs from inscription, which suggests that values are hardwired or encoded into technology (DeSanctis and Poole 1994; Latour 1992),<sup>2</sup> by including also the ability of material modifications to provoke and, as one interviewed policy maker said, “produce exclamation and question marks” among members of a field. Our data provide several examples of how rheumatologists added new material features to the PER, which, simply by existing, suggested new ways of reaching and reconciling the goals tied to the different prevailing logics, thus consistently providing capacities and potential for change (Strong et al. 2014; Volkoff and Strong

2013). Hence, in our case, the practice of material reconstruction was associated with a persistent evolution of the material structure of the PER from 2000 to 2014. This stubborn operation of the observed practice of material reconstruction is noteworthy if compared to the majority of IS studies that tend to portray technology structures as given (Leonardi 2013).

Emergent use refers to humans’ variable hands-on deployment of the technological artifact in situ and highlights the relational aspect between an object and actor (Volkoff and Strong 2013; see also Chu and Robey 2008; Orlikowski 2000; Yamauchi and Swanson 2010). Whereas we define technology-afforded behavioral patterns as aligned with the constellation of logics prevailing at the field level, thus representing institutionalized “accepted” behaviors, emergent use refers to parallel and “pioneering” technology-afforded behaviors that involve a minority of field members and challenge the relationships among logics as established at the field level. In our case, such behaviors emerged thanks to the rheumatologists’ improvised efforts to integrate the PER in their daily interactions, wherein rheumatologists found that the technology afforded them (new) possibilities to respond to situational (e.g., patient needs) and contextual exigencies (e.g., resource scarcity) in an improvised manner (Yamauchi and Swanson 2010). For example, as our findings show, a few rheumatologists began using the PER to change sched uling practices (Open–Tight clinic) as a response to long wait times. This improvised use provided a concrete example of the feasibility of using the PER for several purposes: generating evidence (logic of science), delegating decision-making power to patients (logic of care), and increasing clinical resource efficiency (logic of management), which in turn contributed to their understanding of these purposes (logics) as additive. Emergent uses in our case were typically not strategically aimed at changing the relationship among established logics (Orlikowski 2000; Smets et al. 2012). However, they produced hands-on, partly nondiscursive experiences and concrete, convincing examples of the PER’s ability to cater to several logics simultaneously in daily clinical practice, thus challenging the established competitive relationship among the logics in the field.

Finally, discursive reconstruction refers to the formulation of verbal descriptions of the technology and its use in ways that question shared views of the relationships among field-level logics. This practice can hence be understood as a specific type of micro-level framing (Gray et al. 2015; Strang and Meyer 1993) because it is tied to a technological artifact and, by definition, challenges the relationships among logics in a field. Note that the practice of discursive reconstruction does not literally reconstruct the material structure of the technology (its artifactually real dimensions). Rather, discursive

Thus, the conceptually mediated aspects may or may not have a material referent. In our definition, discursive reconstruction realizes the flexible potential of both logics and technology to a greater extent than material reconstruction, which is limited by the availability of physical technological opportunities. Scholars have referred to this as projective agency, which

For example, rheumatologists would invoke radical framings of the PER, arguing it was a tool that would move rheumatology toward “modern medicine.” Such discursive reconstructions forced other rheumatologists to reflect on and defend their view of the PER, whether they were users or nonusers. Over time, the increasing exchange of verbal descriptions contributed to the development of a “shared” view of the PER’s role and an associated convergence of rheumatologists’ views of the goals of their practice.

Although each of the practices had a distinct nature and outcome, they all exhibited a recursive ability. Not only did they challenge the prevailing competitive constellation of field-level logics by suggesting the goals tied to the logics could facilitate each other, but also all the practices were shaped by the constellation of logics at the field level and involved a significant portion of reinforcement of the prevailing logics. For instance, PER additions included only standardized questionnaires as dictated by the logic of science (material reconstruction), the verbal descriptions of the PER repeatedly underlined goals tied to the logic of care (discursive reconstructions), and the use of PER data in research (emergent use) followed several procedures dictated by the logic of science. The practices were similarly enabled and constrained by the prevailing material structures. Indeed, our data suggests that the PER team deliberately engaged in technological path reinforcement rather than disruption by, for instance, adjusting the PER interface and features so that the PER could communicate with adjacent systems such as the Registry and the national health portal (material reconstruction). This parallel reinforcement and rebuilding of prevailing surrounding material structures played an important role in making the PER sustainable over time.

The practices interacted in multidirectional ways, suggesting there may be several alternative entry points and paths through the mechanism. For instance, in some cases, new material features triggered new practices of discursive reconstruction, which in turn triggered emergent usage practices. However, emergent usage practices also triggered new discursive practices and a parallel modification of material features. Hence, we refrain from establishing a specific serial pattern between the practices. Instead, we conclude that their combined operation constituted the mechanism that generated institutional change in Phase 3.

In summary, the proposed mechanism is transformational (micro–macro) (Hedström and Swedberg 1996) because it explains how human practices occurring at the micro-level can generate macro-level change. However, it also has a built-in macro–micro link because the human practices are inevitably shaped by the preexisting constellation of logics and the material structures implemented. Hence, it is a recursive mechanism that builds on the flexible nature of both logics (Thornton et al. 2012) and technology (Leonardi 2011); this is what creates a capacity for change. Human actors, however, are the key purposive agents in the model, possessing the “‘timeless capacity or ability to exercise free will; to think and act creatively; to do novel things” (Fleetwood 2005, p. 216). In our case, however, their acts often generated emergent benefits and outcomes that were not initially anticipated.

## Outcomes of the Recursive Mechanism

The model depicts how the activation of the recursive mechanism in one period of time generates institutional change in terms of a transformed constellation of logics (Goodrick and Reay 2011) within a field in a subsequent period of time. This dynamic potentially repeats itself as the prevailing constellation of logics and the material structure of the technology in the field are fed into the recursive mechanism at any point in time. In our case, we observed a transformed constellation of logics in terms of a shift from a competitive constellation of logics toward a constellation of logics in which practices and goals of the logics were overlapping and additive (Goodrick and Reay 2011; Table 2). Hence, the observed shift did not involve an entire change of all elements of the logics of care, science, and management (i.e., their goals, audiences, and practices) or the abandonment of one logic in favor of another. The change rather implied that a larger set of practices were “allowed” within each logic and that new, more flexible means–ends chains (additive relationships) between the goals tied to each logic were constructed. This change was reflected in the mindset and practices of rheumatologists, among whom a majority used the PER to better reach goals tied to all three logics, simultaneously or in a serial manner, in the third phase As this way of using the PER was seen as acceptable and appropriate in the field of rheumatology, we conceptualize this as new shared technology-afforded behavioral patterns. Finally, the material structure in itself was also transformed, which fed into the recursive mechanism in a new round of recursiveness.

## Contextual Conditions Enabling the Recursive Mechanism

The model is based on the CR assumption that the recursive mechanism, regardless of its perceptibility (Mingers 2004), exists in the domain of the real. Whether or not the mechanism is actualized, however, depends on the contextual conditions of the setting studied (Volkoff and Strong 2013). Contextual conditions act as “lubricants” for the mechanism rather than driving the outcome and may “support or restrict the start of the actualization process” (Volkoff and Strong 2013, p. 822). We identified three enabling contextual conditions in our case that were present during the whole process and thus differ from “triggers,” which may be present only in the initial phase of a process: first, the organizational structure at Swedish hospitals, which allowed rheumatologists discretion regarding what tools to adopt and how to use them in their research and patient interaction (control over local practice formation [decentralized]); second, the availability of funding which rheumatologists (users) could seek to initiate technology-development projects (resources for technology development [user driven]); and third, the strong professional influence of the rheumatologist association (Anand and Watson 2004; Greenwood et al. 2002; King et al. 1994; Lampel and Meyer 2008; Robertson et al. 1996; Swan and Newell 1995). In our case, the cohesiveness of the community was partly a result of the rheumatologists’ shared and relentless drive to accumulate data for research, the underlying ambition being to incrementally uncover the unsolved mystery at the core of their practice: the pathogenesis and possible cure for rheumatoid disease. This constant desire for more knowledge about the disease, its manifestations, and its relationship with interventions motivated rheumatologists to join forces and invent new ways of systematically generating and using data, which in turn facilitated the persistent actualization of the recursive mechanism.

## Limitations

Our empirical account does not cover the international developments that most likely shaped the change we observed in the Swedish rheumatologyfield. Global movements including the quantification of society, self-tracking, patient participation, citizenship, value-based care, and personalized medicine could, in a sense, be viewed as contextual conditions that also enable the practices, or as the outcome of parallel mechanisms that triggered the actualization of the recursive mechanism identified here. (See further details about how we identified the recursive mechanism as the most adequate explanation in Appendix B).

We are further not claiming that the relationships among logics changed in every area of the healthcare system in which rheumatology was situated. Although we refer to field-level change in Swedish rheumatology, our analysis concerns those realms of Swedish rheumatology where the PER’s affordances were drawn upon; that is, clinical and research practice. In areas such as budgeting, recruiting, and the formation of career paths, the relationship between the logics may still be enacted as competitive. This suggests that the concrete use of technology and the enmeshing of technology within practices are central to changing norms—and, consequently, that changes of norms might not extend into realms beyond the reach of the technology.

## Contributions

We provide two major contributions to the literature. First, our study of recursiveness between micro-level technology use and field-level structures highlights the powerful role of technology in institutional change processes. As we show, in the context of a complex institutional domain encompassing multiple institutional logics, evolving technology affordances and micro-level technology use can reshape how established logics are related on the field level, thus producing institutional change. Our conceptualization of the recursive mechanism involved in this process adds to the IS literature, where studies tend to focus on how institutions shape instantiations of technology use, often in organizational settings, but where few, if any, studies have addressed how instantiations of technology use might, over time, contribute to changing institutions at the field level (Barrett et al. 2013; Barrett and Walsham 1999; Scarbrough et al. 2015) (see Appendix A). By bringing the material structures of technology to the fore and placing technology-afforded practices at the center of our model, we also contribute to institutional theorizing, which, as noted by several scholars, has remained relatively silent about the role of the materiality in institutional processes (Leonardi and Barley 2010; Meyer et al. 2013; Monteiro and Nicolini 2015; Ramus et al. 2017). Our study adds to this literature by offering an alternative theorization emphasizing how a recursive mechanism, which integrates and highlights the critical role of materiality in these processes, reshapes constellations of logics.

Second, by underpinning our institutional logics lens with the ontological approach of CR, we explicitly and coherently define the relationship between material and social structure and human action. We thus provide a much-requested (Mutch 2013) alternative to the widespread “inseparability camp” within IS research about sociomateriality, in which structures are only considered enacted and realized in action and where preexisting structures are thus neglected as a unit of analysis (Orlikowski 2007), as depicted in Figure 1. Our approach also extends studies inspired by Giddens (1984), which, despite claims of recursiveness, have remained largely static in their portrayal of structures (see Appendix A). We show the dynamic nature of macro-level structures and unpack how not only organizational roles and interaction orders change (Barley 1986) as a result of new tools and practices, but also deeply held norms within an institutional field.

Finally, by separating the contextual conditions from the recursive practices, we were able to identify a mechanism that is generalizable rather than contextual. As Mingers (2004) noted, separating the contextual conditions enables us to better theorize the process through which IT can make an impact apart from the context.

Our study also provides implications for healthcare policy makers. Although the recursive practices build on the inherent dynamic capacities of social and material structures, the actualization of this flexible ability is far from common practice in healthcare. Whereas it is easy to find local examples of emergent use, the technological and social infrastructure of healthcare systems exhibit inertial tendencies, partly as a result of the silos between different actors and conflicting views of what healthcare improvement is and is not. Policy makers seeking transformation at the ecosystem level should, therefore, encourage recursive practices that reconcile ideals such as evidence-based care, managerialism, and patient-centered care. By allowing professionals to engage simultaneously in finding new ways of using, modifying, and reframing technologies over long time periods, thus combining technological with medical and organizational innovations, new ambitions and means-ends relationships can become visible and legitimate. Specifically, policy makers can provide financial support to long-term and interdisciplinary IT development projects that encourage practices of material inscription, and modify inflexible reimbursement and staffing regulations discouraging emergent, experimental uses of technology in which tasks are reshuffled across technology, patients, and professionals.

## Conclusion

The study reported here set out to explore how technologyafforded behavior at the micro-level can generate institutional change at the field level. Based on our findings, we conclude that the introduction and evolution of a technological artifact in an institutional setting contributes, under specified conditions and through a recursive mechanism, to change not only at the local level but also institutionally at the field level.

Consistent with our CR perspective, we emphasize that the outcome of the recursive mechanism is contextual. Future studies may provide new perspectives on how the recursive mechanism and its practices operate in relation to other technologies. For instance, the recursive mechanism would most likely be actualized and operate differently in relation to cross-sectorial material structures, which may connect diverse audiences and professional communities, thus potentially generating institutional changes across fields. Further, in our case, the mechanism persisted over an extended, multiyear period and involved not only a persistent challenging of the relationships among logics, but also a significant dose of reinforcement of prevailing logics. It is possible that the mechanism may generate more radical transformations of established constellations of logics, in which logics may be more completely changed or replaced in terms of their content and role, under other contextual conditions or if actualized simultaneously with other mechanisms. We hope our study will enable future research detangling these and other questions related to the micro-level processes through which technology can transform not only organizational but also larger-scale social structures.

## Appendix B

## Assumptions Underlying the CR Approach

Critical realism conceives reality as stratified in three nested domains. The real domain consists of physical and social objects (such as technological and social structures) and the causal powers inherent in them. Objects in the real domain create capacities for behavior called mechanisms. If activated, mechanisms generate events and experiences in the actual domain. The third layer, the empirical domain, is a subset of the actual and includes events and experiences that may be observed through perception or measurement (Archer 1995; Sayer 2000). A key feature of CR is to explain the mechanisms (capacities) that generate a certain event that has been empirically observed.

## CR Principles for Data Analysis

In the following, we describe how we executed the five principles characterizing well-executed CR studies as outlined by Wynn and Williams (2012).

## Explication of Events

Using the interview transcripts, observational notes, and documents, we determined key events in the empirical domain (e.g., the initial introduction of the PER, stages of PER development and adoption, physician and patient PER-usage patterns, or changes in values). We deployed bracketing (Pozzebon and Pinsonneault 2005) to note the main events on a time line and to detect qualitatively the different phases across the period. The phases were different in terms of the rheumatologist’s view and adoption of the PER, which in turn reflected a different constellation of logics at the field level when comparing Phase 1 with Phase 3. (This is shown in more detail in Appendix C.) In Phase 1 (2000–2004), a majority of rheumatologists rejected the PER, and our material pointed at a competitive relationship between logics at the field level. In Phase 2 (2005–2008), diffusion of the PER took off, and the number of rheumatologists who used the PER for multiple purposes increased. The field-level constellation of logics was shifting in this period according to our data. Variable enactments rather than one agreed upon, established field-level constellation of logic were visible. We conceptualize this as a transition period where logics were in flux. In Phase 3 (2009–2014), a majority of rheumatologist clinics had implemented the PER for multiple purposes, and it was no longer seen as controversial, reflecting an additive constellation of logics at the field level.

It was not clear from the outset how the events in the timeline should most adequately be abstracted, as this depends on the theoretical lens chosen (Wynn and Williams 2012). Initially, it was also unclear which events should be considered to constitute the outcome to be explained. Drawing on institutional logics, we realized that the outcome was broader than local changes in routines and actually implied a field-level shift in the ways in which rheumatologists perceived and exercised their profession. That is, we theorized the change in expressed values noted in 2014 as reflecting a change in the institutional logics existing in the domain of the real.

## Explication of Structure and Context

We identified the components of the social and physical structure, the contextual environment, and the relationships between them (Bygstad et al. 2016). As noted by Wynn and Williams, the context could be described infinitely, in detail and complexity; hence, trade-offs need to be made. We left out those social and material components that were only anecdotally evident in our empirical material. We acknowledged that the change observed encompassed a larger context than any one single organization. Rather, the change was enacted by rheumatologists al over Sweden. Drawing on institutional theory, we conceptualized Swedish rheumatology as a “field” (Scott and Davis 2007) separate from (but related to) other fields. The rheumatology field was hypothesized as a structure consisting of several interrelated (real) social and materia components, including immaterial ideals, material structures, and human actors. We used institutional logics theory and CR to place these components and their capacities into a theoretical perspective (as suggested by Wynn and Williams). For instance, we described field-level norms as several different logics (Thornton et al. 2012) capable of shaping and being shaped by human action; rheumatologists as human actors, capable of shaping and being shaped by logics, technology, and the situational context; and technology as a temporarily stable structure capable of affording and being shaped by human action. All these capacities were related.

## Retroduction

The challenge in our longitudinal data was to theorize how the components had caused a majority of rheumatologists to perceive the logic of science, care, and business as additive rather than competing. This outcome was neither intended by any of the actors nor the result of any single event. Rather, it emerged successively during the course of 10 years as a result of several interacting processes which we attempted to detangle and abstract into more general, albeit directly unobservable, mechanisms. In search for these, we coded the data in several rounds. Afte numerous rounds of recoding, clustering, and abstracting of the micro-level activities observed (see Figure 4 and Appendix D for our coding), the analysis pointed at three processes present in all three phases of our time line, and which together constituted the “recursive” mechanism at play. None of the practices could explain the outcomes alone but, taken together, they exhibited the capacity to drive institutional change. Thus, the recursive mechanism builds on the combined theorized capacity of material and social structures (the PER and logics) to shape and be shaped by (afford but not determine) human action, and human actors’ capacity to not only reproduce but also transform such structures These capacities are indeed the essence of the components and what makes the field of rheumatology what it is (conceptually, mechanisms are “nothing other than the ways of acting of things”; Bhaskar 1998, p. 38). Mechanisms are inherent to physical and social structures, enabling or limiting what can happen within a given context (Sayer 2000; Smith 2006). We found three contextual conditions that enabled the realization of the three micro-level practices: resources for user-driven technology development, decentralized decision-making, and a strong professiona community. We identified the contextual conditions by analyzing the relationship between key events and attributes of the field in all three bracketed phases identified. The contextual conditions were present through all phases, and the empirical examples of the three practices could be traced to the conditions through interview transcripts, documentation, and observations. Note that the CR assumption is that the specific outcomes observed here could be generated through other mechanisms in another context (equifinality). Further, the recursive mechanism identified here may generate other outcomes in another context (multifinality).

## Empirical Corroboration

Wynn and William’s fourth principle is important to ensure that the proposed mechanism(s) have causal power and have better explanatory power than alternatives. For example, this can be done through a comparative analysis of the candidate mechanisms to determine the one with the strongest explanatory power (Bygstad 2010). We outlined several alternative causes for the change observed; for instance, exogenous “shocks” such as political agendas or managerial incentives. However, empirical events reflecting such activities seemed temporally to resul from and reflect rather than cause the change in rheumatologists’ practices and values. For instance, none of the interviews referred to the nationwide spread of the PER or the use of PER for multiple purposes as a result of new regulations, the activities of policy makers, or managerial incentives. Rather, they referred to managers and policy makers as “being clueless,” “leaving us largely alone,” and “not interfering in the process.” Hence, these candidate mechanisms did not seem to have stronger explanatory power in relation to the institutional change observed. The possibility that the shift in rheumatology was merely a result of a more overarching transformation of healthcare at a global leve was also considered. This would imply that the recursive practices reflect rather than cause a transformation of logics. Many healthcare studies point to a change in healthcare, moving from being impregnated by one dominant logic to multiple competing ones (e.g., Reay and Hinings

2009). The specific ways in which the logics were enacted and transformed, changing the relationship of the constellation of logics from competitive to additive, however, highlighted the salient role of technology in our recursive practices. Without the practices involving the PER, the specific technology-afforded behaviors and field-level logics would have looked different in the third phase. Hence, we view the practices as a necessary condition for the specific outcomes observed. Worded differently, the general shifts at the societal level needed to be operationalized, mobilized, and translated through the technology-afforded practices in order to have an effect in the domain of the real in the field of Swedish rheumatology

The many roles and locations of respondents further allowed us to view the outcomes from different perspectives. We capitalized on this in our attempt to empirically corroborate, for instance, the perspective of the county council employees and the rheumatologists. Both groups frequently referred to the change as driven by a collective and continuous reinvention of tools, practices, and terms. Our longitudinal analysis further revealed that the mechanism was tied to a change and a “temporal unfolding of events” (Wynn and William 2012, p. 802) not in onl one but in all three phases.

## Triangulation and Multimethods

Finally, the importance of using triangulation or multimethods is twofold (Wynn and William 2012). First, because CR assumes a reality composed of many types of structures, our use of different methods was required to create knowledge about them and compensate for various weaknesses in each method. For instance, we juxtaposed interviewees’ descriptions of events with the events surfacing during debates at seminars and with the events mentioned in reports and official applications. Because causal analysis lies at the core of CR, multiple sources also allow for a solid understanding of mechanisms and outcomes and provide different and complementary perspectives instead of a limited viewpoint (Mingers 2004). Being two researchers, making sense of the data individually and jointly also provides validity to the conclusions and enabled us to reduce personal biases. We also used triangulation of theories. We found similarities between the meaning systems evident in our data and the institutional logics in other healthcare studies. We also found support for some of the practices in the literature, although there was no comprehensive framework conceptualizing how together they can generate institutional change (see Figure 2).
