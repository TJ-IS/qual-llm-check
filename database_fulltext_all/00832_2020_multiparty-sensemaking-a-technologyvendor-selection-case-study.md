# Multiparty sensemaking: A technology-vendor selection case study

Adrian Yong Kwang Yeow<sup>1</sup> | Cecil Eng Huang Chua<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Business, Singapore University of Social Sciences, Singapore

<sup>2</sup>Department of Business and Information Technology, University of Missouri Science and Technology, Rolla, Missouri

## Correspondence

Cecil Eng Huang Chua, University of Missour Science and Technology, Rolla, MO. Email: cecq8z@mst.edu

## Abstract

Information system (IS) procurement history is replete with poorly executed, multimillion dollar procurement decisions. Yet we barely understand what effective IS procurement should look like. IS procurement is highly challenging, as it requires the client to simultaneously select a technology and vendor. This paper explores the technology-vendor selection process through the sensemaking perspective. Our study develops a sensemaking model for technologyvendor selection that connects the multiple rounds of client-vendor communicative actions with the client's sensemaking process. We show how the client reconciles fragmented and sometimes conflicting cues and information through three intertwined cycles: immediate, retrospective, and decision. Sensebreaking occurs as a separate process (and not a communicative action) when disruptive cues occur persistently and from different vendors over multiple rounds of sensemaking. We derive a set of critical factors, on the basis of the sensemaking perspective, for selecting an appropriate vendor and technical solution. These insights in turn help explain many poorly executed IS procurement decisions.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Poor vendor selection has contributed to unsatisfactory outcomes and some spectacular information system (IS) pro ject failures (Argyres & Mayer, 2007; Dey, Fan, & Zhang, 2010; Gschwendtner, Kahl, & Urbach, 2017). For example the complex \$200+ million Novopay teacher payroll system contract went to a 2-year-old, managed services com pany (rather than a software development firm) with no history of implementing complex payroll systems who produced a much-delayed, overbudget system that failed to pay teachers the correct amount on time (New Zealan Government, 2013). Vendor selection is widely recognized as an important project risk factor (Liu & Yuliani, 2016); fully half of IS outsourcing agreements lead to disputes (Michell & Fitzgerald, 1997). Yet we have little understanding of what constitutes good and bad IS procurement practice.

IS procurement is a unique IS business process. It is one of the few interorganizational interactions where an IS department engages concurrently with multiple external parties (vendors). Also, the client makes two simultaneou decisions of choosing the specific technology to implement and a vendor to manage the chosen technology. To emphasize the joint technology and vendor decision, we employ the term “technology-vendor selection” forthwith

Clearly, the way the client engages vendors shapes its understanding of which technology is best and affects its final choice of vendor (Cullen, Seddon, & Willcocks, 2005). However, the process by which the client makes sense of what competing vendors say and how that shapes the client's decision-making has hitherto been underexplored

Our study examines this process from the sensemaking perspective (Weick, 1988, 1993; Weick, Sutcliffe, & Obstfeld, 2005). IS researchers have adopted sensemaking to understand how organizational actors develop shared meaning and understanding of technology and technology-enabled work through communicative actions. Technol ogy-vendor selection is interesting theoretically because most research on sensemaking occurs in collaborative con ditions, but in technology-vendor selection, clients make sense of competing vendor claims as vendors strategicall leverage information asymmetry to manage the client's sensemaking and vendor selection.

Our study investigates the question: “How do multiparty interactions affect clients' sensemaking and how doe this process in turn affect clients' technology-vendor decision-making?” Specifically, we studied the request for pro posal (RFP) process for a New Zealand law firm's attempt to move to cloud computing.

Our study produces three key insights. First, we provide a sensemaking model for technology-vendor selection that connects the multiple rounds of client-vendor communicative actions with the client's sensemaking. Unlik extant studies of sensemaking in work teams, our model reveals that a challenge the client faces is the reconciliation of fragmented and sometimes conflicting cues with information gained from parallel client-vendor communicative actions. The client accomplishes this through three intertwined cycles: immediate, retrospective, and decision. We also find that sensebreaking does not occur as distinct communicative actions but as a separate process wher sensebreaking cues occur persistently and from different vendors over multiple rounds of sensemaking.

Second, we derive three critical factors for selecting an appropriate vendor and technical solution: The client must (a) build requisite variety to overcome the temptation to simplify interpretations, (b) enact resilient and improvi sational actions after sensebreaking, and (c) engage in prospective sensemaking by aligning the technical solutio and vendor aspect of its ideal narrative.

Finally, we contribute to sensemaking by elaborating the role of artefacts. Specifically, we show how, as embod ied cues, artefacts enable the client to gain experiential understanding of abstract concepts. Their tactile and sensor nature strengthens client-vendor communicative actions. Embodied cues in artefacts are more salient than othe cues (e.g., the things vendors say) and can become integral to the client's ideal narrative.

## 2 | THEORY

## 2.1 | Information technology systems and the vendor selection problem

Information technology (IT) systems are equivocal (Weick, 1990) as they are highly malleable, open to multiple inter pretations, and are reconfigured and adapted to different uses (Orlikowski, 2000; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008; Weick 1990). As such, Weick (1990) proposes that sensemaking is required to acquire, implement, and manage IT. IT solu tions are often unfinished, and clients need to make sense of IT systems to adapt them to their own situation, history capabilities, and needs (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004). IT vendors are expected to help address client requirements b adapting and modifying IT solutions. This may range from vendors having custom programme libraries for implementing particular IT platforms to having proprietary tools to facilitate migration of data from Microsoft Exce to Oracle databases (Chua, Lim, Sia, & Soh, 2012). Where customized IT solutions are designed as a suite of modular systems implemented by different IT vendors, a client may engage multiple vendors to work together (Cullen et al., 2005; Gawer & Cusumano, 2002; Schneider & Sunyaev, 2016).

Technology-vendor selection is thus an inherently complex form of work (Lang, Wiesche, & Krcmar, 2018; Loebbecke & Huyskens, 2006; Willcocks & Lacity, 2006). Even for relatively standardized technology-vendor selec tion, such as implementing an IS infrastructure, the client must factor in multiple contextual issues (Castelluccio 2004; Pollock & Hyysalo, 2014). Surfacing different evaluation variables for less standardized and complex technol ogy is even more challenging (Snir & Hitt, 2003).

The criteria for judging and arriving at the best technology-vendor are not always ascertained in an objective optimized process but are socially constructed through dynamic interactions among organizational actors (Garud & Ahlstrom, 1997; Moe, Newman, & Sein, 2017; Tingling & Parent, 2004). Internal teams often engage in discursive actions, politics, and negotiations to evaluate technology and vendors (Howcroft & Light, 2010). To navigate the complexity of technology and the social dynamics of technology-vendor selection, clients must organize and mak sense of the fragmented competing information produced by vendors, journalists, researchers, and industry players (Boonstra & van Offenbeek, 2018; Pollock & Williams, 2009; Swanson & Ramiller, 1997; Swanson & Ramiller, 2004 Wang, 2009). The client must use inputs from multiple vendors to concurrently refine and update its perception and interpretations of both technology and vendors (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). This process is recursive (Tingling & Parent, 2004), and vendors can influence the selection process by demonstrating their capabilities and cultivating ties with client organizations' senior management (Howcroft & Light, 2010; Pollock & Williams, 2009; Pollock & Williams 2007; Verville & Halingten, 2002).

While prior research informs us about the internal social processes involved, they have not fully considered how multiple vendor interactions with the client shape the clients' interpretation and evaluation of technology-vendors and their solutions (Garud & Ahlstrom, 1997; Garud & Rappa, 1994). Having a holistic understanding of how vendors interactions influence clients' decisions is critical (Zerbini & Borghini, 2015). History is replete with multimillion dolla projects that failed largely because of poor vendor selection. Examples include the London Ambulance Service Com puter Aided Dispatch System (Finkelstein, 1996), and New Zealand Novopay (New Zealand Government, 2013) where complex projects were awarded to inexperienced vendors despite the presence of more experienced bidding vendors. Vendors have also been recruited to develop (at that time) technologically infeasible systems. Examples include CONFIRM, the combined Hotel/Car Rental/Airline reservation system (Oz, 1994), and the Secure Border Ini tiative, a sensor studded border security system that was defeated by the harsh climate and terrain at the US-Mexican border (Hite, 2010). One vendor's summary of the Secure Border Initiative illustrates typical errors made—“W can't do it; we're bound by the laws of physics. So you'll have to find some other company that isn't bound by the law of physics” (Bump, 2016). It is therefore critical to understand how interactions with vendors shape and encourage decision-making errors that, in hindsight, could have been avoided. To do so, we use the sensemaking perspective.

## 2.2 | Sensemaking perspective in IS research

Weick (1995b) introduced sensemaking as an organizational process that allows organizational members to create and maintain a coherent understanding of new or changing environments so as to enable collective action. Maitlis and Christianson (2014, p. 67) define sensemaking as “a process, prompted by violated expectations, that involves attending to and bracketing cues in the environment, creating intersubjective meaning through cycles of interpreta tion and action, and thereby enacting a more ordered environment from which further cues can be drawn.” While most research focuses on sensemaking novel events, such as unexpected crises, recent research has also used sen: semaking in more conventional contexts that have issues of high ambiguity and uncertainty (Abolafia, 2010; Haas 2006; Vlaar, Van den Bosch, & Volberda, 2006; Vlaar, van Fenema, & Tiwari, 2008).

Weick (1995b) proposed that the seven properties of sensemaking (identity construction, retrospection, focus on extracted cues, driven by plausibility, enactive of environment, social, and ongoing nature of sensemaking) are embedded in three key sensemaking processes: Enactment deals with reciprocal exchanges between the organization and environment, and the activities of noticing and bracketing, selection attempts to generate and choose tentativ plausible narratives through retrospective attention to and narrative reduction of bracketed material, and retentio solidifies plausible narratives by relating them to past experience and/or significant identities and using them for fur ther action and interpretation (Weick et al., 2005).

Recent refinement to the sensemaking model focuses on explaining why some cues are more plausible than others (Mills, Thurlow, & Mills, 2010). Specifically, Abolafia's (2010) three-stage process of abduction, plotting, and selective retention explicates when certain cues are important. According to Abolafia (2010), the focal actor first finds a model from their experience that best fits the cues (abduction), arranges the cues to fit the model (plotting), and then interprets cues on the basis of this rearrangement (selective retention), often dropping cues that do not fit in the process. This is helpful to understand technology-vendor selection as it shows how the client may determin what is plausible for technology and/or vendor.

Work on high reliability organizations argues that in uncertain, dynamic, and emergent conditions, organizations may have inappropriate or incorrect models of their environment (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2007). Organizations must put in place processes that develop their capability so as to discover and manage unexpected cues or events and adjust existing mental models. One process applicable to technology-vendor selection is building “reguisite variety (Schulman, 1993), i.e., having divergent perspectives and models of the technology or processes being outsourced Requisite variety enables an organization to hold a broader set of assumptions to sensitize it to a greater variety of inputs and interpretations. Organizations with requisite variety are less willing to oversimplify their interpretation and models, be constantly alert to discrepant or unexpected cues, and be equipped to detect anomalies in their envi ronment and act accordingly (Bigley & Roberts, 2001; Weick et al., 2005). Complementary to requisite variety is the ability to act on unexpected anomalies through improvisation of routines, rules, and artefacts (Weick, 1993). Thi ability enables the organization to not only detect unexpected changes but also to utilize them to move forward.

Institutions and politics can influence the construction of plausible narratives and are especially pertinent for technology-vendor selection, given that client and vendor interactions occur within a specific social context of power (eg, certain vendors may be larger and more powerful than others) and rules (industry guidelines) that privilege cer tain vendor inputs and interactions (Maitlis & Christianson, 2014; Sandberg & Tsoukas, 2015). The field discourse created by large clients with market power, journalists, academic researchers, industry pundits, and major vendors which Swanson and Ramiller (1997) refer to as organizing vision,<sup>1</sup> also shapes how clients make sense of new tech nologies and decide whether and how to adopt and implement new IT innovations (Pollock & Williams, 2009; Swanson & Ramiller, 1997; Swanson & Ramiller, 2004; Wang, 2009)

In IS, sensemaking has been used in two main ways. Much research adopts sensemaking as a cognitive process, explaining how users react to and make sense of novel technologies during implementation and use (Bansler & Havn 2004; Griffith, 1999; Jensen, Kjærgaard, & Svejvig, 2009; Vaast & Walsham, 2005). A smaller stream adopts sensemaking as a process of social construction where sensemaking takes place among different organizational actors through communicative actions (Aanestad & Jensen, 2016; Vlaar et al., 2008). Vlaar et al. (2008) used the latter approach to understand how geographically distributed IT teams develop shared and actionable understanding through sensemaking and associated communicative actions. Aanestad and Jensen (2016) apply the same approach to explore how managers engage in sensemaking during postimplementation adaptation.

The emphasis in this second stream shifts attention to the social nature of organizational sensemaking and the construction of intersubjective understanding among different actors and actors' subsequent actions (Maitlis & Christianson, 2014; Sandberg & Tsoukas, 2015). As such, this stream of IS research not only looks at the enactment selection, and retention processes but also analyses how actors' communicative actions of sensegiving sensedemanding, and sensebreaking (Aanestad & Jensen, 2016; Maitlis, 2005; Vlaar et al., 2008) interact with the sen semaking process.

Sensegiving is a “process of attempting to influence the sensemaking and meaning construction of others toward a preferred organizational reality” (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991, p. 442). While most sensegiving researc focuses on leader behaviour, Maitlis (2005) and Vlaar et al. (2008) show that nonleaders, like stakeholders and team members, also engage in sensegiving (Maitlis & Christianson, 2014). Examples of sensegiving actions include offering justifications, offering descriptions and explanations, providing training and manuals of work procedures and creating credible narratives (Aanestad & Jensen, 2016; Vlaar et al., 2008).

Sensedemanding involves proactive attempts to acquire and process information from other stakeholders so as to establish quick tentative accounts in order to reduce uncertainty. Examples include requesting clarification and confirmation, cross-checking with other participants, performing inquiries, and seeking direct assistance (Ashford Blatt, & Van de Walle, 2003; Hargadon & Bechky, 2006; Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007 ; Vlaar et al., 2008).

Sensebreaking is the communicative act whereby meaning is broken or destroyed (Maitlis & Christianson, 2014 Pratt, 2000). In sensebreaking, problems with one's existing understanding are identified and one questions the basi and foundation of current enactments and actions (Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007; Vlaar et al., 2008). Specifically, through sensebreaking, people are motivated to reconsider the sense they have already made, to question the validity of underlying assumptions, and to reexamine their course of action (Lawrence & Maitlis, 2005).

We note in technology-vendor selection that the client engages in sensemaking with multiple external partie (i.e., competing vendors) in an ambiguous and uncertain process. Clients face many “solutions” in the form of ven dors and malleable IT systems and seek to absorb multiple inputs from external parties to build credible narratives

In addition, given recent calls to theorize the material aspects of sensemaking (Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012) and th role of technology in sensemaking (Sandberg & Tsoukas, 2015), we also focus on the role of artefacts as part of thi constructivist process of technology-vendor selection. First, given the complexity of technology, we suspect its rol in sensemaking will be significant, albeit unclear. Some argue artefacts' key sensemaking role is to support actors construction of new meaning as external repositories of information (Kaplan & Orlikowski, 2013; Sandberg & Tsoukas, 2015; Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012). For example, Stigliani and Ravasi (2012) showed how material practice worked with communicative practices to develop new understandings. Others conceptualize artefacts as embodie cues—a form of sensemaking resource (Gephart, 1993)—that designers engage with to develop abstract categories and build new meanings.

Our focus thus is on the communicative actions (sensegiving, sensedemanding, and sensebreaking) of the client and vendors and their interactions with artefacts as they go through iterative cycles of refining tentative narratives (Maitlis & Christianson, 2014; Vlaar, Van den Bosch, & Volberda, 2007; Zerbini & Borghini, 2015). We also recognize the client's decision involves its enactment, selection, and retention processes. These cycles may be divided into epi sodes of “temporary stability, provisional resting points …” (Weick, 2012, p. 146), each triggered by events in the con text and/or interactions among client and vendors and ending when the client's sensemaking achieves temporary stability.

While these concepts form our study's theoretical foundation, there are still gaps when they are applied to technology-vendor selection. Most studies explore sensemaking among actors working in collaborative contexts (Weick 1993; Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutcliffe, 2007). While organizational teams collaborate in complex, uncertain, and volatile environments, the goal of these teams is still aligned. Hence, there is an assumption that communi cative actions result in convergence of the group's intersubjective meaning and understanding. However technology-vendor selection involves actors (vendors) in direct competition. Also, significant information asymmetry exists between client and vendors. Thus, there is the possibility that the initial tentative narrative that the client uses to determine the appropriate technology-vendor may not be accurate or appropriate, and those narratives may evo lve and change on the basis of contextual factors and vendor actions. Unfortunately, it is unclear how such competi tive dynamics and information asymmetry influence the unfolding of sensemaking so that the client can arrive at th most “appropriate” technology and vendor. As such, we conducted a case study and built a theoretical model to provide insights to these gaps.

## 3 | METHODS

## 3.1 | Research design and study setting

We conducted an interpretive theory-building case study of an IT RFP initiated by a New Zealand legal firm (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995). We constantly moved back and forth between the theory and our interrogation of the data to refine our model (Klein & Myers, 1999). Upon understanding the process as socially constructed, we adopted an interpretive epistemology (Sarker, Sarker, Sahaym, & Bjorn-Andersen, 2012)

The client is one of New Zealand's oldest law firms and employs approximately 100 employees. The client initi ated the RFP to migrate its IT infrastructure to cloud computing. The firm's small size and small IT department means it regularly outsources IT work. To help manage this RFP, the client hired an external, independent consultant (Larse & Myers, 1999; Verville & Halingten, 2002) with more than 20 years of experience in IT procurement. The consultant had been engaged previously by the client and was recruited principally for his experience managing RFP processes He was not a technical IT expert.

Vendor selection proceeded from October 2009 to end-May 2010. Through the process, the client discovered cloud computing was not appropriate for the organization. Instead, its problems could be attributed to incompetenc by its local hosting vendor. Thus, the client shelved cloud computing and arranged to change its local hosting vendor See Table 1 for key events

We selected this project for a number of reasons. First, the case exemplifies a properly conducted vendor selection process. The consultant worked in IT procurement for many years and built substantial competence. Second, the consultant stated the vendor selection was (for him) a typical RFP. Even the unexpected outcome was typical. In the consultant's experience, one rarely ended with the product one initially expected because of the high uncertaint inherent in the IT RFP environment. Third, the case is unusually rich. Ordinarily, it is hard to get quality retrospectiv data in case studies, especially since most rely on retrospective interviews. This case, however, was well docu mented; we obtained a substantive archive of emails, meeting minutes, and documents. Finally, from a technologica perspective, the case is novel—it is one of the earliest documented attempts in New Zealand to migrate to clou computing.

## 3.2 | Data collection

The IT consultant maintained an extensive and comprehensive archive of all email, telephone, and face-to-face inter actions and activities between the client, consultant, and vendors. For telephone and face-to-face interactions, th consultant took minutes shortly after they occurred. He also maintained a comprehensive archive of RFP drafts specifications, vendor responses, and related reports. See Table 2 for archival data used in our study

The materials present a detailed account of the RFP from inception to completion, including both mundane and significant events. While some materials capture minor issues (eg, emails to schedule a meeting), many others pres ented detailed explanations of vendor and client's opinions. For example, one email contained a three-paragraph summary of the consultant's impression of a cloud conference and simultaneously described a conversation with vendor whom the consultant met at the conference. Table 2 only takes into account relevant archival material whil our actual data set comprised more than 400 emails, minutes, and other documents.

Archival material is primary data in other disciplines (eg, History) and often has desirable properties interviews do not (Myers, 1997). Specifically, archival materials are more detailed and relatively precise as they capture event when they occurred and are not dependent on human memory and retrospection. Furthermore, they are unobtrusiv and nonreactive as they are not adjusted by subjects to please an interviewer. Finally, they are less contingent tha interviews, which may be affected by time constraints and other distractions (Denzin, 1989; Huber & Power, 1985).

We clarified and corroborated events and issues with the consultant and the client's management team. We also conducted respondent validation to ensure our interpretations corresponded to events that actually occurred (Mason, 1996). In addition, we conducted retrospective interviews with the external consultant and client to triangulate key events and decisions, and confirm the client's agenda and perceptions of vendors, and the industry as a whole. Thus, retrospective interviews helped confirm the accuracy and completeness of our data as well as the cli ent's sensemaking.

While the consultant collected the data, much of it (eg, emails and vendors' responses to the RFP) originates from the sources themselves. Thus, while some consultant bias exists (eg, reports are written by consultant), most of th data are unbiased.

Furthermore, the following factors mitigated consultant bias. First, the consultant wanted the client to achieve a positive outcome as that encourages repeated business. Second, the consultant was not affiliated with any vendor. Third, the client oversaw the consultant's work, and we saw no evidence that they differed in opinion or were dissat isfied with the consultant's performance. Fourth, the client abrogated no responsibility in hiring the consultant. Th consultant provided advice and facilitated meetings and the RFP process. The client attended all meetings and inter acted with vendors. Thus, from the perspective of technology-vendor selection, we argue that the consultant's per spective was fully aligned with the client's.

## 3.3 | Data analysis

As we focused on the “how” question, we adopted a four-step (Pentland, 1999) process approach for analysis (Lang ley, 1999). First, we used a narrative strategy. This was followed by temporal bracketing. Third, we coded the brack eted data using concepts from sensemaking. Finally, we uncovered theoretical themes, which form the bulk of our discussion. The narrative strategy involves the development of a detailed chronological story from the raw data. This was important, given that processes occur in a temporal order (Langley, 1999). We constructed an in-depth narrative of vendor selection, which formed the basis for summarizing the archival data. This narrative was also used as a vali dation tool (Gephart, 2004; Golden-Biddle & Locke, 1993; Yin, 2017).

Next, we used the narrative to identify each round of sensemaking, following the temporal bracketing strategy (Langley, 1999, p. 707) where one breaks a chronological story into segments on the basis of events demarcating log ical breakpoints in interactions. Temporal bracketing provides a practical way to compare client-vendor actions and analyse how they influence client decisions across each segment. Following extant IS process studies, each segment is a round of interactions (cf Newman & Robey, 1992). We identified four rounds: initial query, response to initia query, refined query, and resolution. Each round followed a common pattern. Specifically, the client (and consultant first interacted with vendors, and each round ended with intense consultation between the client and consultant after which a set of actions was identified (eg, drop a vendor).

Third, we analysed sensemaking interactions within each round. Our level of analysis is individual communication (ie, individual emails or reports). We treated each communication as representative of the source organization. For example, a sales representative's email is representative of the sales representative's organization. Within each round, we first coded client-vendor interactions (eg, email and logged conversation) for source and target of interac tion and identified the interaction as sensebreaking, sensegiving, or sensedemanding. This third step allowed us to systematically structure data for subsequent comparative analysis.

We initially wanted to code interactions as “sensebreaking” if they radically altered an existing conversation However, we found few instances of sensebreaking as a communicative act. Indeed, what seemed to alter the conversation was the client's reflection during sensemaking. This formed one of our contributions. We coded “sensegiving” interactions on the basis of prior studies that included actions such as offering justifications, offering descriptions and explanations, and providing documentation (Aanestad & Jensen, 2016; Vlaar et al., 2008). Sensegiving, thus, refers to actions that provided information to influence meaning construction (Vlaar et al., 2008) An example of sensegiving is identifying problems with the client's infrastructure. On the other hand, in extant literature, sensedemanding includes actions such as checking one's interpretations with others (Maitlis & Lawrence, 2007), help seeking (Hargadon & Bechky, 2006), or actively asking questions (Ashford et al., 2003). One example is requesting for clarifications on the RFP. See tables in the Appendix for more coding examples. As part of our analysis of sensegiving and sensedemanding interactions, we observed that technological artefacts played an important role in the process, which we will elaborate on in the discussion.

For sensemaking, we focused our coding on interactions and discussions among the client's team, specifically at the end of each round when the consultant and client discuss what they had learned. This was often encapsulated in email exchanges and meeting summaries drafted by the consultant. While most client communication processes involved the different client-initiated interactions (ie. sensegiving and sensedemanding). we also coded the client's decisions on whether to drop or communicate with specific vendors. For the selection process, we coded how the client and consultant categorized multiple narrative fragments and cues to reconcile and harmonize them into tenta tive vendor narratives. Lastly, for the retention process, we coded how the client and consultant linked tentativ vendor narratives to existing ideal vendor and technical solution narrative and evaluated them

After iterating with the codes, we observed that the client's sensemaking processes of selection and retention were more complex and differentiated than the theoretical framework allowed (see Figure 1). These complexitie were qualitative rather than quantitative in nature. For example, contrary to the literature, we found we could not map individual actions of sensedemanding or sensegiving directly to the client's sensemaking. We observed, fo example, how the client made sense of the same piece of information differently across episodes. Similarly, w observed that sensebreaking occurred in a fundamentally different way from the literature, which depict sensebreaking as occurring with a single communicative action. Instead, we found sensebreaking to be a more grad ual process where multiple sensebreaking cues (encapsulated as sensegiving) were necessary to actually caus sensebreaking to occur. We thereby developed a nuanced model of technology-vendor selection sensemaking and sensebreaking processes (see Figure 2).

## 4 | FINDINGS AND ANALYSIS

Because of space constraints, we have opted for a summary, rather than a detailed presentation, of our case findings Supporting quotes are in the analysis proper.

## 4.1 | Summary

The client originally employed a managed services model where it kept IT infrastructure in-house but hired an exter nal vendor to maintain it. The client faced capacity issues with the infrastructure system, support service issues with the incumbent vendor, as well as problems of increasing costs. The client's initial sensemaking led them to consider it impractical to continue acquiring new infrastructure, especially since IT infrastructure was not the firm's core busi ness. The client also suspected it was overpaying for IT infrastructure support. Drawing cues from trade press arti cles, the client considered cloud computing in October 2009, ie, hosting applications in a professionally managed data centre (DC), believing this would simultaneously lower costs and transform the sunk cost of hardware purchase into a variable running cost.

The client invited six vendors to the RFP, including its incumbent managed services vendor. When invited, th incumbent apologized to the client and acknowledged it had not been performing its managed services role well. The client also visited cloud computing conferences to get a sense of the landscape. There were five vendors who replied to the RFP, all of which submitted with a partner. The partners' roles were split into that of front-end vendor (who managed the client relationship, support, and software) and back-end vendor (who managed the physical cloud infra structure). Vendors 1, 2, and 5 (all front-end) introduced new partners, while vendor 3 (front-end) partnered with vendor 4 (back-end). The client was initially most impressed with vendor 5 because they asked the most technically relevant questions and was least impressed with the incumbent (vendor 1) because its submission was interpreted a essentially the same high-cost, managed service operation in a cloud environment. All bids but one (the incumbent's failed to include a price component. So the client asked all vendors to rebid (in round 2).

During the rebid, the client emphasized to the incumbent that its bid was the weakest, prompting the incumbent to invite the client on a tour of its back-end partner's site. The client was greatly impressed by the back-end vendor (vendor 7). In private negotiations with vendor 7, the client learned that vendor 7 felt the incumbent was locked in an old model of service delivery. The incumbent advised the client that cloud computing was not something the cli ent should do. While the incumbent's analysis was later proven correct, at that time, the client felt the incumbent was spreading fear, uncertainty, and doubt (FUD), just like many older vendors the client encountered at cloud conferences.

Through ongoing interactions with the vendors, the client made comparisons and found that vendor 5 acted more like a back-end vendor—technically savvy, but weak on customer engagement. Furthermore, vendor 5's back-end partner (vendor 8) had very poor infrastructure, as compared with vendor 7. On the other hand, vendor 2 was viewed as sophisticated and expensive, while vendor 3 told the client its back-end partner (vendor 4) was not interested in the RFP. At the end of round 2, the most attractive vendors were vendors 2 and 3 (front-end and vendor 7 (back-end).

The client was looking for a cloud services solution lower in cost than its current managed services operation However, no vendor delivered this. The client thus asked vendors to give a new bid (in round 3), although, this tim round, it dropped vendors 1 (the incumbent) and 4 and kept vendor 7.

During the third round, vendor 2 became unresponsive and submitted a late bid. The client heard rumours that vendor 2 was being acquired by its back-end partner, and subsequently, vendor 2 withdrew from the bidding. At th same time, the client was invited for a second tour of vendor 7's site.

All bids for round 3 were above the client's price point, leading it to conclude that the incumbent was correct— cloud computing was not feasible for the client. At this point, the client did not know how to proceed.

Vendor 2 then introduced vendor 9, a direct competitor to the incumbent. Vendor 9 reviewed the client's configuration and realized vendor 1 was managing the client's infrastructure poorly, something that vendor 1 acknowledged at the beginning of the RFP. For example, vendor 1 was running 33 Citrix servers for the client a 100-person law firm. The client asked vendor 9 to put in a cloud services bid with vendor 7, and when tha did not prove cost-effective, the client recruited vendor 9 to replace vendor 1 as its managed services partner.

## 4.2.2 | Retrospective cycle

In this cycle, which occurs at the end of each RFP round, the client reflects on different vendor narratives to update its ideal vendor and technical solution narrative. While the retrospective cycle is similar to the general sensemaking notion of retrospective attention (Weick et al., 2005) and selective retention (Abolafia, 2010), there are nuanced dif ferences as it is not only about comparing current cues and narratives with existing information (eg, industry news). Importantly, it is also about comparing cues and narratives across parallel vendor interactions during the selection process. The client observes emerging themes on the basis of two kinds of cues—recurring and contrasting. Som themes emerge from recurring cues across vendors. For example, after round 1, the client and consultant realized that almost all vendor RFP submissions included partner vendors—a front-end service and a back-end technical infra structure vendor. Below, the client invites vendors to the second RFP using the terms “front” and “back-end” ven dors, which they did not use previously.

[The client] have determined the parties with which they wish to continue the RFP conversation through Phase 2 are: [vendor 2] (Front and Back), [vendor 3] (Front-end), [vendor 7] (Backend), [vendor 5] (Front and Back). (Email, client invitation to vendors to move forward with RFP)

In other cases, themes emerge as contrasting cues across vendor narratives. For example, the client noticed the distinct quality contrast between vendor 7's tier 3 and vendor 8's tier 1 data facility.

In the retention process, the client draws relationships among these themes and updates its ideal vendor and technical solution narrative. One such theme emerged from evolving persistent cues (a point raised in Abolafia (2010)) For example, the client received numerous signals, from the beginning, that what it wanted was not workable:

[Vendor 1] … expressed concerns about embracing the cloud to [sp] early and particularly the risks associated with running [the client]'s complex app mix in cloud outside of a known server environment. (Client summary of telephone call)

But in early rounds of the retrospective cycle, the client dismissed these cues as traditional vendors spreading FUD:

… at an IDC conference on “Cloud Computing for the Enterprise” at which there was lots of defensive FUD and redefinition to protect the status quo from traditional suppliers. (Consultant email to client

However, these cues were reincorporated in rounds 3 and 4 when the persistent pricing cues across all vendors led the client to finally realize their ideal vendor and technical solution narrative was not consistent with cost savings Below is the client's assessment of the final vendor 9-vendor 7 joint bid

… it was useful because the proposed pricing (that was consistent with other offers received) had assisted [client]'s growing appreciation that the Business Case for acting now [ie, going to the cloud] did not stack up.

At the end of the process, the client updated and refined its provisional ideal vendor and technical solution narra tive; ie, the ideal technical solution was managed services, and the right vendor was vendor 9.

The retrospective cycle requires the client to not only attend to cues from a single vendor interaction but also attend to recurring, contrasting, and linking cues that are insignificant in one context but evolve to become signifi cant later. This is in contrast to existing sensemaking research that finds that discrepant facts may be reinterpreted to fit existing models (Abolafia, 2010; Maitlis & Christianson, 2014). Thus, the client must resist the temptation to quickly oversimplify their narrative for the ideal vendor and technical solution and be open to discrepant cues from sensegiving actions that lead to sensebreaking over time (Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutcliffe, 2007). Oversimplification of the ideal technical solution occurred early on in our case. When vendor 1 and vendors at the confer ence claimed that cloud computing was not right for the client, the client dismissed the claims as vendor 1's attempt to keep its business and conference vendors' way of spreading “fear, uncertainty, and doubt.” While the client's interpretation of these cues was correct, it was incomplete and oversimplified.

## 4.2.3 | Decision cycle

This occurs after the retrospective cycle, when the client reflects on multiple and provisional narratives by comparing and evaluating each vendor narrative against its own updated and provisional ideal vendor and technical solution narrative (Abolafia, 2010; Weick, 1995a). Unlike the prior cycles, this cycle is more in line with “prospective sensemaking,” where the focus is on constructing a new understanding of the environment to “structure the future” (Gioia & Mehra 1996 p 1229) so actors can develop ways to relate to the imagined future state (Kaplan & Orlikowski, 2013; Sandberg & Tsoukas, 2015; Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012). Like recent work on prospective sensemaking (Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012), our decision cycle shows the client engaging in additional work to develop its provisiona understandings (ie, its ideal vendor and technical solution narratives) in the earlier cycles and, subsequently, integrat ing and connecting the different provisional understandings. Specifically, the client first engages in selection, where it evaluates each vendor narrative, noting discrepancies and misalignments between vendor and ideal narratives. The more alignment, the better the evaluation. Then, in retention, the client compares the evaluated vendor narratives (discrepancies or alignment) with other vendors' narratives. This results in a tentative closure where the client decides whether to cease or continue further interactions with particular vendors or reach a decision.

Because these narratives are future oriented and thus novel and ambiguous, the client has to rely on sufficiency (rather than comprehensiveness) and plausibility (rather than accuracy) (Weick, 1995a; Weick & Sutcliffe, 2007) to make its decision. In contrast, industry IT procurement practices assume clients' decision-making is based on th most comprehensive and accurate data (Chai, Liu, & Ngai, 2013). As such, the client's ideal vendor and technica solution narrative may change across multiple rounds and, in turn, so will its evaluation of vendors across rounds. Consider how vendor 2 was a favoured vendor in the early rounds:

Agreed order of preference for [client] Project RFP1 responses was; 1: [vendor 2], 2: [vendor 3], 3: [vendor 5], 4: [vendor 1] … . Action: [consultant] to invite [vendor 2] to discreetly engage with [client] for discovery. (Meeting of client team)

However, it subsequently dropped in the client's evaluation because of poor engagement:

Also—no surprise that there was no RFP2 Response from [vendor 2] by the formal deadline. (Email consul tant to client)

Similarly, vendor 5 was initially well favoured but later dropped. Likewise, the client had planned to dismiss ven dor 7 with vendor 1 but began to favour vendor 7 late in the iterations. Finally, at the end, a vendor not even consid ered during the initial stages—vendor 9—won because the technical narrative changed from cloud to managed services.

## 4.3 | Sensebreaking in vendor selection

While the literature tends to portray sensebreaking as distinct episodes where actors' existing conceptions and understanding are questioned, reframed, and problematized (Lawrence & Maitlis, 2005; Vlaar et al., 2008), we found that sensebreaking did not occur over a single episode, but as a process that unfolded incrementally over time, wher discrepant cues were gradually aggregated from multiple sources.

Observe how in Table 3, interactions are principally sensegiving and sensedemanding. No statement by a vendor radically alters the client's sense of cloud computing at the point of utterance. Even vendor 7's demonstration of a proper back-end vendor set-up serves to further the client's understanding rather than radically alter it. Neverthe less, the client does go through sensebreaking—vendors' rankings shift radically and near the end of the RFP, the cli ent finally questions the viability of cloud computing. While sensebreaking occurs, it is not as a communicative act by the client or vendors. Rather, sensebreaking is internal to the client and caused by an accumulation of information obtained through communicative acts. Discrepant cues were present at the beginning of the technology-vendor selection process, when existing conference vendors and vendor 1 warned the client about the unsuitability of cloud computing. Furthermore, at the end of the second RFP round, all vendors submitted bids above the client's antici pated costs—a major discrepant cue as one aspect of the technical solution narrative was cost savings. While it might be argued that such incidents should be coded as sensebreaking, the client did not change its perspective toward cloud computing in any of these communications. Instead, such cues were interpreted as vendors misunderstandin the client. At the time of utterance, the cue only provided information (ie, sensegiving) but did not generate a radica rethink (ie, not sensebreaking).

Our experience … definitely confirmed that the traditional tech headspace of an IT vendor requires some distinct re-adjustment to really appreciate the revolutionary concept that we are actually interested in More-IT-for-Less-\$s rather than More-IT-for-More-\$s. (Email consultant to client)

Thus, for sensebreaking to occur, discrepant cues have to occur persistently across the technology-vendor selec tion process. This is because assumptions of the technical solution are reinforced by vendor sensegiving and the cli ent's bias towards the suitability of the technology. While vendors compete, they focus on delivering the client's choice of technical solution, and thus, most narratives and cues support that choice. The sources of discrepant cue have to be varied to lend credibility to sensebreaking. For example, when existing vendors and vendor 1 warned th client about cloud computing, the client viewed their narratives sceptically. It was only after the client received simi lar discrepant cues from other vendors' sensebreaking actions (eg, vendor 9) that the client finally questioned its idea narrative.

We could observe sensebreaking because the qualitative nature of client sensedemanding changed. For example once the client recognized cloud computing was not effective, it made inquiries about a new managed services solu tion, specifically what it would look like. In effect, a sensebreaking alteration of the client's technical solution narra tive changed the client's sensedemanding. Below, the client explicitly analyses what vendor 9 said about its curren managed services set-up in order to envision what a new set-up would look like:

I should let you know I had the [vendor 9] guys in this morning taking a look at our system. A few things gave them cause for some concern—mention of load balancing in Citrix … also the switching setup to floors, and our login script sent shivers up their spines (as it does mine). (Client email to client and consultant)

## 5 | DISCUSSION

Our case study and sensemaking model of technology-vendor selection provides insights as to how technology-ven dors are selected and some potential explanations as to why so many cases involve inappropriate selection of tech nology-vendors. Specifically, an important issue is how sensebreaking occurs within the client—not through direct communicative actions but as separate cognitive processes triggered by the accumulation of discrepant cues (se Figure 2). We discuss these in detail below.

## 5.1 | Overview of contributions

Our sensemaking model of technology-vendor selection contributes to the literature by unpacking the sensemakin process that client organizations undertake through immediate, retrospective, and decision cycles. Our analysi above shows how they differ significantly from traditional sensemaking, where organizational teams collaborate to achieve common goals. In the immediate cycle, we show how each provisional vendor narrative is assembled an refined over successive cycles from fragmented cues provided directly by the vendor as well as indirectly from indus try and other competing vendors and the client's existing ideal narrative. Unlike other forms of sensemaking, thes vendor narratives do not necessarily converge—the client may interpret and include cues unaligned with specific vendor communications. This is especially so when clients suspect vendors are acting in ways that are not beneficia to them.

In the retrospective cycle, we show how, again, unlike other sensemaking contexts, the client compares across multiple vendor narratives to observe recurring and contrasting cues that are then retained to update its ideal narra tive. More importantly, persistent discrepant cues may eventually emerge—these can be linked to the separate pro cess of sensebreaking (see Figure 2).

In the decision cycle, we observe the client shifting to prospective sensemaking. Unlike typical sensemaking, th client has to rely on sufficiency (rather than comprehensiveness) and plausibility (rather than accuracy) (Weick 1995a; Weick & Sutcliffe, 2007). Whereas industry IT procurement assumes that client decision-making is based on the most comprehensive and accurate data (Chai et al., 2013), from a sensemaking perspective, the client's ideal ven dor and technical solution narrative may not be totally comprehensive or aligned and often will change across multi ple rounds.

This explains classic cases of vendor selection failure like the London Ambulance Service, Novopay, CONFIRM and the Secure Border Initiative. In all these cases, the client converged on a vendor and technical solution narrativ before sufficient cues could be obtained for sensebreaking to occur. Armchair analysts with 20/20 hindsight can point to cues in all these projects that suggested the proposals were infeasible. However, these discrepant cues, aris ing principally from nonwinning or nonparticipating vendors, were overwhelmed by cues, from both participating and winning vendors, that suggested feasibility. If more consideration was given to each project, the additional time could have encouraged additional cues to enter the immediate cycle, which in turn might have prompted a shift in the retrospective cycle, thus leading to a different (and more correct) decision.

## 5.2 | Critical factors for selecting an appropriate vendor and technical solution

Our study thus reveals three critical factors that enable the client to select the appropriate vendor and technica solution. One, the client was able to build requisite variety (Weick et al., 1999; Schulman, 1993), which helped it overcome the temptation to simplify its interpretation early on in the sensemaking process. Two, the client was able to act in a resilient and improvised manner once it switched from sensemaking to sensebreaking. Three, it was abl to manage prospective sensemaking in its decision cycle by finally aligning the technical solution aspect with the vendor aspect of its ideal narrative.

As discussed in Section 2, Weick et al. (1999) identified requisite variety as a key factor helping organizations hold a broader set of perspectives, assumptions, and models so they can be alert to unexpected cues, inputs, and interpretations. However, requisite variety in vendor selection is qualitatively different. One, it occurs in the inter organizational setting where divergent perspectives come from the different vendor narratives that the client develops over multiple REP rounds. Two, reguisite variety in the form of vendor narratives provides the client with both different perspectives of the client's current technical situation as well as each vendor's capabilities. These perspectives are not negotiated (as in traditional theory) but are collated by the client. Third, requisite variety occurs for a different reason in vendor selection; it occurs so long as the client's procurement goals are not perceived as met (because of the lack of alignment between the technical solution and vendor aspects of the ideal narrative). This led the client to conduct multiple rounds of RFP, which increased the requisite variety and in turn increased the salienc of discrepant cues. Requisite variety is especially important for technology-vendor selection since existing researc shows that organizations fail to leverage on discrepant cues to question their own assumptions but instead reinter pret discrepant facts to fit with existing models (Abolafia, 2010; Maitlis & Christianson, 2014).

Put together, requisite variety is a key factor for helping the client stay alert to discrepant or unexpected cue and be equipped for detecting environmental anomalies. Requisite variety thus allowed the client in our study to switch to sensebreaking, thus leading it to realize its initial mistake in choosing cloud computing as its ideal technica solution.

In a similar way, the London Ambulance Service, Novopay, CONFIRM, and Secure Border Initiative failures dem onstrated clients' fixation with particular solutions to problems. Unlike our case, requisite variety was never created because vendors claimed to meet the client's procurement goals early in the RFP

Connected to requisite variety is the client's ability to be resilient and improvise. Resilient organizations need to not just detect unexpected changes but be able to cope by utilizing and acting on those changes through constrained improvisations (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2007). Such improvisations are structured around rules, routines, and even arte facts (Weick, 1993). This is consistent with our findings on sensebreaking—once the client realized the issue of cost savings was not tenable, they were not just willing to change their ideal vendor and technical solution narrative but also began improvising the rules of the RFP process. Although they rejected vendor 1's criticism that cloud comput ing was not viable in the first and second rounds, they were willing to engage vendor 9 on this same point later on. Rather than sticking with their prior rules of only looking at cloud computing and interpreting vendor 9 as a solely front-end provider, the client discussed with vendor 9 about ways of improving their current managed services configuration. These constrained improvisations retained the original rules and routines of engaging with vendors but also changed how and what the client was communicating with vendors. This resonates with the view of Weick et al. (1999) that such improvisations are novel recombination of actions already in the organization's existing reper toire. The important insight here is that the novelty of the recombined actions is guided by the narrative developed during sensebreaking. In this way, we show how requisite variety complements and works together with constrained improvisation to help the client understand what the appropriate vendor and technical solution is.

In contrast, a lack of adaptability in the client's actions often manifests in cases of inappropriate vendor selection. In the cases of Novopay and CONFIRM, the respective clients were not willing to adapt their requirements despit obvious issues. For Novopay, there was an insistence on automating all pay rules, despite obvious inconsistencie and contradictions across pay contracts (the legacy system Novopay replaced allowed for manual processing). Wit regard to CONFIRM, there was an insistence that the base code came from the SABRE airline reservation system despite radical differences between airplane and hotel and car bookings

The final factor critical to the client's sensemaking is the client's ability to manage its decision cycle so as to ensure alignment between the ideal technical solution and the vendor aspect of the narrative. Whereas most sensemaking research focuses on retrospective elements, our case of technology-vendor selection—especially in the decision cycle—is closer to prospective sensemaking (eg, strategy making, entrepreneurship, and innovation) as much of the client's ideal vendor and technical solution narrative is relatively unknown and there is no clear “past experience” to depend on. The challenge for the client is not only to choose the right vendor and technical solution under such conditions but also to choose them to match its imagined future state. As discussed in studies focusing on prospective sensemaking (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991; Sandberg & Tsoukas, 2015; Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012), it is important for the client to navigate this new understanding of its environment by continually articulating and elabo rating tentative narratives held by its organizational members (Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012). In our case, the client needed to elaborate and link emergent (but sometimes incomplete) vendor narratives so as to refine its ideal vendor, and technical solution narrative. This allowed the client to establish plausible criteria (of its future state) for evaluating vendors.

While such studies have focused on practices that support the iterative refinement and linkages among tentative narratives, our study shifts attention to the articulation and elaboration of the two aspects of the ideal narrative, ie technical solution and vendor aspects, and its impact on vendor selection decision-making (Abolafia, 2010; Boje 1995). In our case, the vendor aspect of the ideal narrative was continually refined over multiple rounds, but the cli ent did not reach any decision because there was no clear closure for the technical solution aspect of the ideal narra tive. As such, prospective sensemaking in technology-vendor selection requires the two aspects of the idea narrative—vendor and technical solution—to be aligned before the client can achieve a stable decision. Our finding also show the ideal technical solution aspect is more significant as it strongly influences the client's ideal vendor nar rative (Leonardi & Barley, 2008; Yeow & Faraj, 2014). For example, in our case, the cost saving attribute of the technical solution narrative was an issue no “ideal” vendor could resolve, so there was no alignment between their idea vendor and ideal technical solution narrative when the RFP process formally ended. This contrasts with the cases of failed vendor selection discussed earlier, where either a single vendor claimed to meet the client's demands (Londo Ambulance Service and Novopay) or the vendor approached the client (CONFIRM), thereby leading prematurely to potential alignment between the vendor and technical solution in their ideal narrative. Hence, it is imperative the cli ent carefully navigates prospective sensemaking in its decision cycle by taking into account the interplay betwee the two aspects of an ideal narrative in order to decide on the right vendor and technology.

## 5.3 | Role of technological artefacts

We find technological artefacts strengthen communicative actions during client-vendor interactions. As per extant research, we find technological artefacts embody abstract concepts and novel understanding as there are limitations to how client and vendors can process abstract cognitive information, eg, cloud computing as a working concept. While extant research finds that material artefacts help actors visualize information and see how information is linked (Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012), the technological artefacts in our study are physical instantiations of the abstract concepts that enable actors to physically engage with and provide experiential understanding of these abstract con cepts. For example, we observed how artefacts supported vendor 7's sensegiving when they provided the client a tour of their server hosting facilities. It was only when the client saw the servers, software, etc, in action that the appreciated and understood the abstract concepts they read about in magazine articles and the RFP. Likewise, arte facts supported the client's sensegiving actions when vendor 9 physically accessed the client's server system and viewed its login scripts to study the technical configuration of its managed service. It was only when vendor 9 real ized that the client's scripts were misconfigured that it could offer the client a way to resolve the RFP. In both cases artefacts provided embodied cues through experience that reinforced sensegiving actions. Thus, in vendor 7's case the client was not discursively processing the vendor's claim about its ability to perform but its actual performance In vendor's 9 case, vendor 9's inspection of the client's systems gave it insights into the inefficiencies in the client' IT operations (ie, misconfigured scripts and excessive number of operational servers) beyond what the client was able to express discursively.

Furthermore, these artefacts (as embodied cues) assist in the client's internal sensemaking by becoming an inte gral part of the provisional ideal technical solution narrative. Embodied cues in the form of concrete and contextualized experiences enable the client to anchor abstract ideas within the narrative more effectively than with discursive cues. For example, we observed that although vendor 3 conducted a workshop for the client to help it understand its cloud computing capability, this did not change the client's view of cloud computing as much as vendor 7's sit tour did. The client only understood what “faxing to the desktop” meant after the vendor 7 site tour because they had experienced that concept physically. Similarly, the vendor 8 site tour put the final nail in vendor 5's coffin, and vendor 9 was able to propose and describe how their technical solution fit in with the client's needs using embodied cues gained from the site visit. This technical solution, in turn, was selected and retained as part of the client's idea vendor and technical solution narrative.

## 5.4 | Limitations and practical implications

This study is based on a single in-depth case, and therefore, our model has limited generalizability. Nevertheless, it has generated a useful theory that is testable elsewhere (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). This case involved a small organization whose RFP project requirements were complicated, dealt with complex technologies, and had significant levels of uncertainty and ambiguity (Brynjolfsson, 1994; Perrow, 1984). Future research needs to explore how appli cable this model is for RFPs conducted by organizations of varying sizes, complexity, and governance structures. Fur thermore, data are mainly drawn from archival and retrospective interviews. We have mitigated against data source bias by triangulating between the archival and interview data. However, more research should consider other ways to enrich the credibility of the data, eg, through direct observations.

Practically, our findings suggest several insights into technology-vendor selection. First, a certain amount of adaptability is necessary in the client's goals and objectives. Contrary to principles articulated in existing industry practices (Chai et al., 2013; Sen, Subramaniam, & Nelson, 2008/2009; Sun, Dong, Hussain, Hussain, & Chang, 2014), vendor selection is not just about comparing vendor offerings against objective criteria but is also about comparing vendor offerings with each other in an emergent way. For example, vendor 7 was favoured because it had features like faxing to the desktop that other vendors did not articulate. As the client learns what the industry offers, it shoul expand its own vendor selection criteria. Existing industry practices fail to take into account that criteria for technol ogy and vendor selection are mutually constructed by the client and vendors. Thus, adaptability is important as tech: nology-vendor selection is not just about processing what one knows and does not know but about “what one does not know one does not know.”

Second, our findings concerning requisite variety suggest potential vendors should be retained for as long as it is cost-effective because of the information they could potentially provide. In our case, the client only eliminated two vendors (1 and 4). More effective sensemaking occurs when diverse information sources are available. Vendors, even those with no hope of winning, are sources of information. The vendors that the client felt would not deliver, ven: dors 5/8, for example, were retained over subsequent rounds and became useful in highlighting the advantages of vendor 7. Of course, retaining vendors increases costs for the client, and if such costs are onerous, vendors should be dropped. However, the key here is that decisions to drop vendors should not be based on vendors' capabilit alone but on vendors' ability to provide information as well. Also, costs of retaining additional information sources should be weighed against the problems incurred from making the wrong decision. In other words, if the cost of maintaining a vendor in the RFP is relatively small, it would be greatly offset by the reduced risk of making a mistake.

## 6 | CONCLUSION

One of the biggest puzzles in IT procurement practice is why clients sometimes choose less qualified vendors to lead novel IT projects that, as a result, end in spectacular disasters. Through an in-depth case study of a law firm's successful technology-vendor selection, we developed a sensemaking model of technology-vendor selection that shows how the client reconciles fragmented and conflicting vendor cues through three intertwined cycles—immediate, ret rospective, and decision. More importantly, we observed the three critical factors from the model and case stud that enabled the client to eventually shift from their original (incorrect) technical solution selection to an appropriate vendor and technical solution choice. As discussed above, these factors are (a) build requisite variety to overcome the temptation to simplify interpretations, (b) enact resilient and improvisational actions after sensebreaking, and (c engage in prospective sensemaking by aligning the technical solution and vendor aspect of its ideal narrative.

Our findings provide a positive step in an important research stream that remains relatively small. Outsourcing is now commonplace, and while there is much research on how currently contracted clients and vendors should engage with each other, there is very little research on how clients should initiate and manage the procurement process. As this research illustrates, IT procurement is not simple or linear. Substantial research must be done to develop theor on IT procurement to help clients make better decisions.
