## Abstract

Diversity issues in management information systems (MIS) research have received abundant attention over the last 20 years. This paper extends the debate on the limited diversity in the research methods of MIS researchers to include the global research community. The argument of methodological exclusiveness on a global scale is argued to be the result of educational influence on faculty in each region. Leading universities throughout three major regions of the world were evaluated to show the educational influence of faculty causing the expansion of methodological exclusion on a global scale. The paper concludes with recommendations for addressing the issues discussed to further facilitate collaboration among researchers internationally.

## Introduction

he field of management information systems (MIS) is relatively young compared to other academic disciplines. Research in MIS has borrowed theories from various disciplines, adding to the diverse nature of the field. Few topics have received the degree of attention garnered by diversity in information systems (IS) research. Previous debates concerning diverse issues in MIS research have focussed on topics such as rigor vs relevance (Zmud, 1996; Benbasat and Zmud, 1999; Moody, 2000; Ben-Menachem, 2001; Boudreau et al., 2001; Dennis, 2001; Kohli and Health, 2001; Mathieson and Ryan, 2001; Olfman, 2001; Chen and Hirschheim, 2004; Pearson et al., 2005), design science vs behavioral science (Hevner et al., 2004; Niehaves and Stahl, 2006), positivist vs interpretivist (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991; Weber, 2004; Westland, 2004), and the identity of the discipline (Baskett, 1973; Banville et al., 1989; Orlikowski and Iacono, 2001; Alter, 2003; DeSanctis, 2003; Lyytinen and King, 2004). Figure 1 contains a delineation of the topics and periods in which they were debated.

To further the debate on diversity in research methods used in MIS, Davidson and Martinsons (2011) discuss the trends of methodological exclusivity in MIS research. They discuss actions to improve the relevance and holistic understanding of theories as they apply to organizational settings. This discussion can be extended to research being conducted internationally throughout different regions of the world. With trends in the organizational environment rapidly moving toward multinationalism (Straub, 1994), it is logical for academic research to follow suit. Historically, MIS research has been heavily influenced by American universities (Palvia et al., 2007). The type of research conducted and methods employed is arbitrated by the interests and perspectives of the various stakeholders such as editors, reviewers, and the researchers themselves (Koh, 2003). Periodically, stakeholders possess contrasting views and interests that could prevent research from being published.

While North American research has been dominated by local institutions likely to have homogeneous interests and perspectives, there are an increasing number of international researchers publishing in academic journals (Palvia et al., 2007). Furthermore, the number of foreign students attending North American doctoral programs and then returning to their home country as professors has also increased (Aslanbeigui and Montecinos, 1998). It has been proposed that this trend of international students attending North American institutions is correlated to the increase in international faculty members publishing in North American journals. While this has increased the diversity of researchers in academic journals, it is argued that a lack of diversity in the methods being employed still remains. International faculty members who obtained their doctorate degree from North American institutions are trained to publish using identical techniques employed by researchers at North American institutions. Therefore, the issues of methodological exclusiveness argued by Davidson and Martinsons (2011) can be broadened to an international phenomenon.

While academia has taken many steps toward the unification of multinationalistic research, there is still much amelioration to be achieved. This paper looks beyond the opinions on diversity and its place in the field of information systems to establish a theoretical approach demonstrating the benefits of diversity to the discipline of MIS. First, the sociological and psychological perspectives of diversity are reviewed to ascertain how diversity can be applied to academic research. Second, an analytical discussion of international diversity and research influences among different regions of the world, such as North America, Europe, and Continental Asia, is conducted. Implications and arguments for addressing concerns of methodological exclusiveness and lack of diversity on an international scale are then presented. The paper concludes with recommendations for addressing the issues among researchers internationally.

## Theoretical perspective of diversity

Diversity is a broad concept fundamentally meaning that something is different from something else. There has been a plethora of research in psychology and sociology discussing the effects of diversity, along with extensive research throughout the management literature on the business school. Diversity is often referred to as ‘any differences in attributes that lead to perceptions that one individual is different from another’ (Jackson, 1992; Triandis et al., 1994; Williams and O’Reilly, 1998). Extensive research has been conducted that evaluates diversity on attributes such as age, gender, race ethnicity, functional background, education, and tenure (Milliken and Martins, 1996; Williams and O’Reilly, 1998; Jackson et al., 2003). Diversity is a comprehensive theory containing many characteristics not entirely expedient to the research topic discussed here. To identify facets of diversity well suited to MIS research in the context of relevance and publication methodologies, a review of diversity literature must be conducted to ascertain the aspects of diversity that can be applied.

Evaluating diversity from a broad perspective, it has been shown that diversity plays an important role in economic development (Thomas and Darnton, 2006). Overall economic productivity is improved by the existence of cultural diversity in society (Ottaviano and Peri, 2004). This supports the idea that diversity benefits society by consolidating a multitude of task-relevant knowledge, skills, and abilities that are distinct and can be used to ameliorate society as a whole. This concept can also be applied to the field of MIS because researchers from diverse backgrounds, collectively, can provide insights and knowledge that will advance the discipline much further than a homogeneous set of researchers could. A negative perspective of diversity also exists that must be given equal respect and managed in order to reap the benefits of diversity. While proliferation of diversity may benefit a group (Bantel, 1994), diversity can also have adverse effects on performance among a group of people (Guzzo and Dickson, 1996; Milliken and Martins, 1996; Williams and O’Reilly, 1998).

There are many different classifications and traits of diversity that can be explored. However, three primary theoretical perspectives have been established in literature: the similarity-attraction paradigm, self and social categorization, and information processing (Mannix and Neale, 2005). The similarity-attraction paradigm describes individuals who seek out others with likeness to them, having high attraction among individuals who share similarity in attitudes (Byrne, 1971), socioeconomic background (Byrne et al., 1966), competency (Baskett, 1973), or social activities (Werner and Parmelee, 1979), and low attraction among dissimilar individuals (Rosenbaum, 1986). Self and social categorization theory concerns race, ethnicity, gender, religion, tenure, etc., in the development of groups and social structures (Mannix and Neale, 2005). In social categorization, an individual’s role or social position plays a critical role in determining whether they are perceived positively or negatively. Similarity-attraction and social categorization often depict a negative view of diversity, that individuals are more attracted to others with similar characteristics (Mannix and Neale, 2005).

The third paradigm, information processing, presents a positive view of diversity, creating an atmosphere that results in higher performance within groups (Jehn et al., 1999; Mannix and Neale, 2005). Information-processing diversity encompasses the intellectual differences among individuals, such as education, experiences, knowledge, and expertise (Jehn et al., 1999; Mannix and Neale, 2005). Information-processing diversity research commonly focuses on the direct impact of information diversity on social and cognitive processes within groups (Priem et al., 1999; Pitcher and Smith, 2000) in terms of the benefits gained from information, educational background, functional background, or expertise (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992; Winquist and Larson, 1998). This form of diversity is influenced by deep-level differences in knowledge without concern for surface-level diversity encountered with social categorization and similarity-attraction paradigms (Harrison et al., 1998). To gain benefits from informationprocessing diversity, it is imperative that members of the group possess heterogeneous knowledge, experiences, and perspectives (Gruenfeld et al., 1996) to stimulate thinking and advance knowledge. The theory attests that diversity in knowledge will improve performance by stimulating and enhancing creativity in the group to develop innovative solutions and search for new information (Levine et al., 1993; Jehn, 1995). The added information results in the improvement of group outcomes (Gruenfeld et al., 1996; Phillips et al., 2004). Table 1 shows the theoretical perspectives of diversity and the attributes associated with each category.

In applying these theories to MIS research, it is imperative to understand the influence that each diversity category has on publication productivity. It can be argued that the effects of similarity-attraction and social categorization are minimal due to faculty’s ability to choose their co-authors. This would result in less conflict and fewer negative outcomes due to diversity. However, the influence that these two paradigms possess relates to limiting diversity and collaboration between co-authors who are dissimilar. The most influential paradigm of diversity that must be harvested is known as information-processing diversity. This paradigm is concerned with functional knowledge, skills, experience, and information that are critical to the development and progression of knowledge in the field of MIS. This perspective creates a positive outlook on diversity that is useful for the creation of new ideas resulting in a multitude of topics that can expand the field of MIS. While there is obvious diversity among researchers publishing in quality journals, it is argued that there is a lack of diversity in the methods used for conducting research (Davidson and Martinsons, 2011). The next section reviews the influences of researchers from different regions to evaluate the effects of education on cultural diversity. It is contended that methodological exclusiveness on a global scale is a result of the influence by the region where the researcher obtained their terminal degree.

## Educational influence of international diversity

Studies have been performed that evaluate diversity in research methodologies being used and topics being discussed. It has also been argued that a significant degree of methodological exclusiveness exists within MIS research (Davidson and Martinsons, 2011), with the primary methods of investigation over a 38-year period (1968–2006) consisting of surveys, case studies, laboratory experiments, and field experiments (Riedl and Rueckel, 2011). However, further research should be conducted in evaluating the effects of educational influence internationally that result in a lack of diversity on a global scale. This section evaluates educational influences on researchers from three distinct regions: North American, Europe, and Continental Asia. It is argued that researchers are influenced by the location where they obtained their terminal degree. This educational influence, in turn, is the driving factor for methods employed, resulting in methodological exclusiveness on a global scale.

This study accumulated data from leading universities in each of the three regions being evaluated by aggregating the number of IS professors at each university based on the location where they obtained their terminal degree. The universities selected for this study were obtained from several sources that evaluate and rank universities based on research activity (Athey and Plotnicki, 2000; Baden-Fuller et al., 2000; Yonezawa et al., 2002; Liu and Liu, 2005; Chau and Kuan, 2007; Lee and Liang, 2007; Lee and Yoo, 2007; Tan and Chan, 2007; Venkatesh, 2010). The website of each university evaluated was searched to obtain the number of faculty and the region where they obtained their terminal degree. To ensure validity, several universities were randomly selected for re-evaluation to ensure that the data were accurate. Each region will be discussed separately to show the influence of education on the research being conducted.

## North America

Research conducted in North America typically focuses on the IT artifact and human–computer interactions (Galliers and Whitley, 2007), consisting of quantitative techniques with empirical results for evaluation (Walsham, 1995; Chen and Hirschheim, 2004). It has been shown that researchers in North America most commonly publish their work in American journals (Galliers and Meadows, 2003). This inclination is prognosticated due to researchers in the US and Canada obtaining their terminal degrees from North American institutions where they learn skills required for publishing in local journals. Based on this assumption, it was predicted that the majority of professors in the US and Canada obtained their Ph.D. from North American institutions and continue to work locally upon completion of their terminal degree. To verify this assumption, a list of the leading IS schools in North America was obtained based on research productivity (Athey and Plotnicki, 2000; Venkatesh, 2010) in order to evaluate the faculty’s educational influence. Each university website was searched to obtain the number of faculty and where they obtained their terminal degree, which is current as of winter 2010. The results of this search are shown in Table 2, with universities listed in alphabetical order displaying summated totals of regional educational influence.

Lecturers and doctoral students were excluded from the study due to not having completed their terminal degree to date. The results show that 116 out of 120 (96.7%) researchers in North America obtained their terminal degrees locally. While some North American institutions teach doctoral students qualitative methodologies, junior faculty and doctoral students are often encouraged to utilize quantitative research methods that are deemed less risky when publishing in American journals (Davidson and Martinsons, 2011). The understanding of qualitative methods among researchers in North America is increasing; however, there are still great strides to be made in the execution and implementation of these methods in order to provide a more holistic understanding of organizational intricacies.

## Europe

Research conducted in Europe commonly focuses on social, organizational, strategic, economic, and marketing aspects of information systems (Galliers and Whitley, 2007), consisting of qualitative techniques for evaluation (Evaristo and Karahanna, 1997). This trend is deduced due to researchers in Europe obtaining their terminal degree from European institutions that focus on teaching research methods commonly used in local journals. To verify the assumption that researchers at European institutions obtain their terminal degree from local institutions, the IS departments of several universities throughout Europe were evaluated. Due to the strong influence of qualitative research conducted in Europe, a quantifiable list of top IS schools in Europe was unobtainable. Alternatively, several European institutions listed in the top 30 from a list of top 100 universities worldwide were selected (Venkatesh, 2010). This list was combined with a quantitative list ranking the top Management schools in Europe (Baden-Fuller et al., 2000) due to the close nature of these two disciplines. The IS department from each school was reviewed and data were extracted from the university departmental website. Data from a few universities were unobtainable due to a non-existing or non-identifiable IS department, or because an English version of the website was unavailable from which to collect data. The results of this search are shown in Table 3, with universities listed in alphabetical order displaying summated totals of regional educational influence.

There were a total of 80 faculty members obtained from eight universities within five different countries. From the faculty evaluated, 75 (93.8%) of these researchers obtained their terminal degree from local institutions. With local academic journals predominantly publishing qualitative research, it is assumed that doctoral students are taught methods required for publishing in local outlets. The fact that professors are trained at European institutions and then work locally alludes to the lack of diversity and limited exposure to quantitative methods in this region of the world.

## Continental Asian countries

Various countries throughout Continental Asia are influenced by a number of universities across Europe and North America. Traditionally, Japanese business schools have been influenced by European institutions, and MIS is no exception. However, numerous Japanese institutions do not offer MIS curriculum at business schools. Alternatively, the majority of institutions offer either ‘Information Science’ or ‘Information Engineering.’ Many professors in the school of business at Japanese Universities receive their terminal degree from Japanese or European institutions, rather than North American institutions. However, other countries such as South Korea, China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan show a more considerable influence from North American institutions. To evaluate the influence from North American and European institutions, several universities throughout Continental Asia were selected to evaluate the educational influence on their faculty. The institutions selected in this study were obtained from published university rankings identifying the top universities from each country evaluated (Yonezawa et al., 2002; Liu and Liu, 2005; Chau and Kuan, 2007; Lee and Liang, 2007; Lee and Yoo, 2007; Tan and Chan, 2007; Venkatesh, 2010). The IS departmental website from each university selected was evaluated to obtain data about educational influence of the faculty. A detailed view of each university in alphabetical order sorted by country can be found in the Appendix. Summarized data showing each country and summated totals of educational influence are given in Table 4. The value in parentheses next to the country name indicates the number of universities included in the study for each respective country.

As indicated previously, South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and Hong Kong display a strong educational influence from North American institutions. South Korea displays the highest influence, with over 88% of IS faculty obtaining their terminal degree in North America. Alternatively, faculty in Japan and China appear to possess less educational influence from foreign institutions. More than half of the IS faculty in China received their terminal degrees from local institutions, while the single university evaluated in Japan had all of its faculty obtaining their terminal degree locally. Overall, these results display a significant influence from Western institutions on IS faculty in Continental Asia. As a result, many researchers in Continental Asia employ research methods and theories developed in the American context without applying them to local contexts relevant in Asian countries. March (2004) argues that research in China could prove beneficial to the international research community by applying developed theories in local Chinese contexts, exposing the limitations of context-free ideas that reflect political or cultural differences. This concept can also be applied to other countries throughout Continental Asia.

## Implications of multinational diversity

As discussed previously, there is a divide among research being conducted internationally, particularly between North American and European countries. A review of research published in European IS journals (Galliers and Meadows, 2003) and European Ph.D. dissertations (Evaristo and Karahanna, 1997) shows that research published in European journals tends to be more qualitative in nature, while North American IS journals typically publish very little qualitative research (Walsham, 1995; Chen and Hirschheim, 2004). The North American research community consists predominantly of positivist researchers, compared to a strong interpretive research tradition in Europe (Evaristo and Karahanna, 1997). An analysis of research over a 38-year period from 1968 to 2006 revealed that half of the research conducted in North America used survey, case study, laboratory experiment, and field experiment methodologies, with 24% of the research evaluated consisting of surveys (Riedl and Rueckel, 2011). These differences can lead to issues in reviewing and publishing research across international boundaries. Researchers at European institutions most frequently publish research in European journals, while researchers at North American institutions predominantly publish in North American journals (Galliers and Meadows, 2003). Researchers in Continental Asian countries commonly focus on publishing in international journals, which tend to have contradictory goals to local issues, resulting in a lack of relevance to business in Asia (Meyer, 2006). While North America and Europe tend to have research traditions consistent with other institutions throughout their perspective regions, research in Asia varies depending on the education influences of the professor. As shown, researchers who obtained their terminal degree in North America tend to publish in North American journals, with the same being true for researchers who are influenced by European education.

Another issue when evaluating research productivity internationally is the ranking system and standard used for measuring the quality of academic journals. Due to differences in the methods used and research focus, many European and Asian journals have been excluded from journal ranking and IS research productivity studies (Gallivan and Benbunan-Fich, 2007). ## Recommendations and conclusion

It has been argued that research in MIS suffers from methodological exclusion (Davidson and Martinsons, 2011). The current research argues that diversity within the field should be expanded towards the international community to help overcome some of the limitations in MIS research. This will contribute to the evolution of theories through multiple contexts containing an array of political and cultural perspectives. International contribution and collaboration can enhance information-processing diversity along with pluralist methodologies, giving a more holistic understanding of business problems from a global perspective. It has been argued that many junior professors and doctoral students in North America lack the quantitative skills necessary and are often discouraged from conducting such studies (Davidson and Martinsons, 2011). European research has a contradictory dilemma often being criticized that research lacks rigor often seen in North American research. Continental Asia appears to be divided on this conundrum depending on the educational influence of professors in each country. While a pluralist approach is recommended in order to further expand the holistic understanding of research (Mingers, 2001), very few researchers possess the methodological capabilities to conduct these types of studies. MIS research could achieve these results through expanded collaboration among researchers in different regions of the world. By combining methodological capabilities of researchers in different regions, a richer understanding of business within global contexts can be achieved. This would not only further increase the diversity of methods being employed, but also allow for the application of research questions in multiple contexts beyond the locality in which the questions were asked (DeSanctis, 2003).

Information systems research has acknowledged the need for diversity in research methods. The editor-in-chief of MISQ has contributed to the solution of methodological inclusion by removing publishing constraints in MIS Quarterly, allowing well-executed qualitative work to be accepted (Straub, 2008). While this step contributes to the growth of MIS research, there are other actionable procedures that can be put in place to further advance the methodological diversity of MIS research. The current research provides suggestions to help achieve the goal of methodological diversity and further advance the field of MIS research.

## International exchange programs for doctoral students

The development of international exchange programs for doctoral students could prove beneficial for learning the techniques required to conduct pluralistic research. It has been argued that few professors in North America are qualified to teach doctoral students deep knowledge needed to perform qualitative studies. It has also been demonstrated that European institutions commonly perform qualitative research, and therefore many North American students could benefit from a semester in Europe learning and applying these techniques. The alternative could be true for European students who would partake in a semester exchange program at North American institutions. This could further be extended to include institutions in Continental Asia to create a joint learning environment where students learn in a global environment.

Faculty exchange and international sabbatical research exchange The benefits of doctoral students studying abroad could further be extended to include professors and post-doctoral positions. Invited summer research positions between universities from various countries could further enhance collaboration and learning of multiple methods when conducting research. These benefits could be further realized through exposure to international contexts and collaborative efforts between researchers possessing complementary skills. Senior professors preparing for sabbatical could also benefit from international exposure and collaboration efforts with other senior professors around the world.

Exposure and inclusion of internal journals in world rankings Expanding journal rankings to include more international journals could increase exposure to research conducted on a global scale. The inclusion of journals conducting quality research using qualitative methods would help enhance the holistic understanding of business problems. The addition of journals throughout Europe and Continental Asia would help further the understanding of research problem in various contexts throughout the world. Creating an international special column or forum of leading journals could further expand international collaboration and mixed-methods research.

Online forums discussing topics of interest such as diversity There are numerous online social networks and forums on the Internet associated with various topics of interest. It is recommended that an online forum for professors and doctoral students be established where the IS community can debate issues such as diversity in MIS and collaborate together in sharing ideas that could help advance the discipline. A specialized social network, forum, or online community for members of the IS community could prove beneficial for addressing current issues and increasing international collaboration. Currently, the Association of Information Systems (AIS) website (www.AISnet.org) contains a community discussion forum; however, there is little activity, with the majority of comments dating back to 2008. Invitations to join the online forum could be sent to all existing MIS professors to encourage use and collaboration. Current professors could encourage their doctoral students to participate in online discussions appropriate for their education and career development. This could facilitate a cultural norm where future professor begin their career possessing ideas of knowledge-sharing and collaboration internationally.

## Translations of published research

It has been noted in this paper that there are limitations to collaboration among non-English-speaking countries. There are many non-English-based journals developing theory that researchers publishing in English are unable to take advantage of. Through the development of a global academic community offering translated research results, further collaboration can be facilitated that has previously been unobtainable. The world continues to globalize and the influence of technology is increasing exponentially. Among the various disciplines within a university, it would be logical for MIS to become the pioneer in developing means of translating research to enhance the sharing of knowledge on a global platform. For example, Japan and South Korea are world leaders in the efforts of mobile telecommunications, with abundant research being conducted by faculties in these countries. Many domestic journals are published in local languages, limiting the accessibility of research results by English speakers. Providing translations of research conducted could further enhance a global understanding of these theoretical contributions and further advance the field of MIS.

These recommendations could lead to further collaboration within the global IS community and could encourage diversity within the methods employed for research. Taking advantage of strengths from different regions would help overcome limitations such as methodological exclusion experienced by North American research and lack of rigor affecting European research. Furthermore, these efforts will expand the intellectual interactivity and pervasiveness of information communication and technology. Future research should expand this study on diversity to the Middle East, South America, Africa, New Zealand, Australia, the Caribbean, and other regions of the world to develop a more comprehensive evaluation the global IS community.
