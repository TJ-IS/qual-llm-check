# Unraveling Grievance Formation in Online Movement Research: Insights from the Amnesty International Sex Worker Policy Protest

Lisen Selander,<sup>1</sup> Sirkka L. Jarvenpaa<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Applied IT, University of Gothenburg, Sweden, lisen.selander@ait.gu.se <sup>2</sup>McCombs School of Business, University of Texas at Austin, USA, sirkka.jarvenpaa@mccombs.utexas.edu

## Abstract

Many moments of political expression on social media networks fail to become online movements. However, studies on these failures and their causes are conspicuously absent in the literature. We had the opportunity to trace such a failure when a contentious policy advocating for the decriminalization of sex work failed to materialize into a cohesive online movement. However, uncovering the reasons behind this failure proved challenging: Why didn’t mobilization happen despite favorable political opportunity structures? Eventually, we turned to existing research on what motivates movement participation and the role of grievances. Shared understandings of grievances are essential for social movements to emerge and be sustained over time. However, the focus on grievance formation has taken the back seat in research on contemporary movements and collective action. Through our interpretation of the sex worker policy protest and by examining existing research on online movements, we advance propositions of the challenges involved in online grievance formation. The propositions contribute to the pursuit of a theory to explain failure in online movements.

Keywords: Social Media, Social Movement, Grievances, Online Movements, Social Injustice

Varun Grover was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on October 7, 2021 and underwent four revisions.

## 1 Introduction

The information systems (IS) field has raised many doubts about the effectiveness of online movements in reshaping democratic practices and calls for investigations into their role in driving social change (Young et al., 2019). For example, Syed and Silva (2023) wrote: “What remains to be known is whether movements sustained on social media translate into any opportunity for a substantive social, political, or policy change.”

For IS research focusing on online movements, it is crucial to be sensitive not only to how mobilization manifests online but also to the specific grievances that trigger and sustain such movements. Grievances are the central claims and conceptions of a social movement (Simmons, 2014). Such claims pertain to the “practices, policies, or phenomena that movement members claim they are working to change (or preserve)” (Simmons, 2014, p. 515). In this way, grievances aim to answer the question of “what is at stake.”

In the social movement literature, it is well-accepted that shared grievances are critical for motivating mobilization and for movement sustenance (Almeida, 2019; Klandermans, 1984; Simmons, 2014; Snow, 2013; Snow & Soule, 2010; Pinard, 2011). While rooted in individual sentiments and/or experiences, grievances are also imbued with macrolevel understandings, such as the historical and contextual roots of the claims. Attention to grievances requires exploring such understandings and how people interpret their circumstances (Simmons, 2014). However, grievance formation has often been overlooked in IS research on online movements, including studies grounded in the perspective of “connective action” (Bennett & Segerberg, 2012). Within this stream, research has made many essential advances in explaining how movements spread and scale online via personal action frames<sup>1</sup> (e.g., Syed & Silva, 2023), critical findings around their organization, and the technological affordances associated with such organizing (Leong et al., 2019; Sæbø et al., 2020; Vaast et al., 2017). This perspective, however, largely overlooks the very formation of the underlying claims and conceptions that drive these movements — including their macrolevel roots. For instance, in their three-year study of the women’s movement, Syed and Silva (2023) found very few tweets that addressed grievance formation.

Engaging deeply in grievance formation is necessary to understand the motivation and sustenance of any social movement (Klandermans, 1984; Simmons, 2014; Snow, 2013; Snow & Soule, 2010). According to Snow and Soule (2010, p. 23), “none of the various sets of conditions necessary for the emergence and operation of social movements is more important tha[n] the generation of deeply felt shared grievances.” Attention to grievances can help us better understand not only online movements and how they emerge and morph over time but can also add depth to matters of social justice, marginalization, and collective action — areas increasingly addressed in IS research (Aanestad et al., 2021; Young et al., 2019). For example, understanding grievances and their macrolevel roots can help us better explain why claims of injustice(s) are much more potent at certain times. In this research, we ask the following question:

## RQ: How do shared grievances form and evolve in online movements?

The current paper is a conceptual contribution based on the existing social movement literature on grievances and online movement studies found in IS. We illustrate our argument through a policy on the decriminalization of sex work developed by Amnesty International that caused substantial protests, which were, however, never strong enough to overturn the policy. We draw on this illustration and online movement research to explain failures in online movements. In the next section, we review the online movement literature to problematize the lack of attention to grievances. We then review the grievance literature in the field of social movement studies (Almeida, 2019; Pinard, 2011; Simmons, 2014; Snow, 2013; Snow & Soule, 2010) and direct attention to three conditions critical for grievance formation. Next, we challenge the conditions as they manifest online. Finally, we summarize the challenges in the form of propositions. The propositions extend the current understanding of how social media use can contribute to failures in grievance formation in online movements. We conclude with implications for research and practice.

## 2 Conceptual Foundations

This section discusses two theoretical frameworks: connective action and grievance theory. Connective action, based on Bennett and Segerberg’s (2012) work, explains how social media can facilitate mobilization but largely overlooks the formation of the underlying claims and conceptions that drive these movements. On the other hand, grievance theory, from the social movement literature (Pinard, 2011; Simmons, 2014), highlights the importance of grievance formation for the emergence of social movement activities (Almeida, 2019) but says little, if anything, about the role of social media in that process.

## 2.1 The Connective Action Perspective

Research on online movements in the IS field (see Appendix B) is substantially based on the work of Bennett and Segerberg (2012) and their “logic of connective action” (e.g., Braccini et al., 2019; Ghobadi & Clegg, 2015; Leong et al., 2015, 2020; Stewart & Schultze, 2019; Syed & Silva, 2023; Vaast et al., 2017). This perspective suggests that social media enables individuals with a wide range of personal views to join a movement “without having to enter into complex negotiation of personal versus collective politics” (Papacharissi, 2016, p. 8). Social media expands the repertoires of political contention (Rennick, 2013; Selander & Jarvenpaa, 2016; Stewart & Schultze, 2019) and allows new forms of expression and engagement to emerge at an unprecedented pace (Ghobadi & Clegg, 2015; Oh et al., 2015). The new repertoires broaden the diversity of individuals engaged in social movements and the range of reactions they exhibit.

The logic of connective action suggests that social media has the organizing capacity to scale up and stabilize communication between such diverse and distributed actors. Social media features, such as hashtags, retweets, and likes, can be used to coordinate resources and individuals, and deconstruct formal leadership roles (Oh et al., 2015; Vaast et al., 2017)— even in dynamic contexts with diverse interests. For example, Leong et al. (2019, p. 189) argued that:

social media, characterized by direct connections among individuals, including strangers and unfamiliar others, provides an organizing platform that prevents the stiffening of barriers to contributing resources, such as additional efforts to connect with an agency. It is the autonomy of individual actors that serves as a basis for the coordination of resources.

In this way, diversity is not viewed as a threat but as an opportunity to expand a movement to include supporters with different interests (Bennet & Segerberg, 2012). However, IS and other scholars have noted the challenges associated with social media coordinated mobilization and the lack of formal organization and leadership (Leong et al., 2020; Pond & Lewis, 2019; Tufekci, 2014). Scholars have suggested that technological features such as hashtags may not be as radical or politically dense as they may have first appeared (Shahin & Ng, 2022).

Instead of shared expressions, the logic of connective action emphasizes what Bennett and Segerberg (2012) referred to as “personal action frames”—that is, the importance of individual networks, emotions, and the “personal take” on issues at hand. The use of social media features can help coordinate the coproduction and co-distribution of personal messages to scale up the movement.

The connected action literature rarely engages in how grievances are formed, but rather in how individual sentiments are shared. It appears to rest on the implicit assumption that there is a preexisting shared understanding of grievances in online movements.<sup>2</sup> Bennett and Segerberg (2012, p. 744) wrote that online movements “require little in the way of persuasion, reason, or reframing to bridge differences with how others may feel about a common problem.” For the most part, the literature considers the immediate microcontext of the movement only (i.e., the personal action frames) and largely neglects the potential impact of the macrocontext, such as how to interpret grievances in particular times and contexts. This includes the cultural, historical, and political origins of the claims of movements that shape the movement trajectories.

We argue that such lack of attention to the macrocontext can be problematic for online movements unless individuals act from similar geographical areas (Stewart & Schultze, 2019), share political and contextual understandings (Braccini et al., 2019), or share social positions, such as a diaspora acting on behalf of a particular group (Tye et al., 2018). We next turn to the field of social movement studies and the work of Simmons (2014, 2021) to explain the nature of grievances, shared grievances, and the role of the macrocontext.

## 2.2 Grievance Theory

Even within the traditional social movement literature, Simmons (2014, p. 2) asserted that “grievances remain under-theorized.” According to Snow (2013, p. 1):

When individuals collectively challenge authorities via social movements, they typically do so over matters about which they are deeply troubled, have considerable concern, and feel passionately. These troublesome matters or conditions, and the feelings associated with them—such as dissatisfaction, fear, indignation, resentment, and moral shock—can be thought of as grievances.

Grievances can be triggered by shocks and shock effects, such as natural crises strong enough to generate mobilization without preexisting mobilization networks (Jasper, 1997; Van Stekelenburg & Klandermans, 2013). Grievances may also emerge to recognize injustices and illegitimate treatment. Such grievances can rely on existing networks but also introduce new ones as awareness of inhumane treatment spreads. Another form of grievance is related to violations of ideological principles or policy changes, as was the case with our work on the sex worker policy protests. Such violations can render the situation unacceptable and trigger action (Simon et al., 1998; Van Stekelenburg & Klandermans, 2013).

Individual and shared grievances: Snow (2013, p. 1) separates individual grievances from shared grievances. Individual grievances pertain to individual experiences, and/or feelings of discontent or injustices. However, these feelings and experiences, which one might experience daily, “rarely congeal into collectively shared grievances.” Shared grievances, on the other hand, are collectively interpreted grievances considered serious enough to motivate some “corrective, collective action” (Snow & Soule, 2010, p. 24). Shared grievances are not merely expressions of individual discontent; rather, they carry with them the historical, contextual, and cultural context (Simmons, 2014) of the matters or conditions at stake. According to Snow (2013, p. 2):

Unlike individual grievances, mobilizing [shared] grievances are not everyday phenomena that materialize on a regular basis. Put metaphorically, mobilizing [shared] grievances are more like mushrooms after a spring rainfall than weeds; they do not flourish continuously and everywhere, but only under specifiable conditions.

Grievances are separate from political opportunity structures. Political opportunity structures are external conditions, such as laws or external allies, that become a catalyst for, facilitate, or intensify grievances (Meyer & Minkoff, 2004). Much of the research subsumes grievances as part of political opportunity structures or argues that grievances are stable rather than evolving and hence explains little variance in their formation and evolution (Simmons, 2014). This is a misconception, as grievances are dynamic and likely to be reshaped throughout a movement. Grievances are claims that signal how a situation or problem is understood, while political opportunities are the structural conditions that can signal to a community that collective action is possible (Almeida, 2019; Pinard, 2011).

Grievance formation: Simmons (2014, 2021) suggested three generalized critical conditions of grievances that motivate social movements. The first condition is attention to the interpretations of grievances in a particular time and context. Her argument—that how individuals interpret grievances depends on their social worlds—requires one to attend to their historical, contextual, and cultural roots. She writes: “We must ask how do people interpret their grievances before the imposition of a particular grievance and how might that interpretation inform their response.” (Simmons, 2014, p. 523).

For example, Simmons (2014) analyzed the 2000 Bolivian Water War, during which thousands of people protested in the streets. A new regulation had shifted the ownership of wells, including communal wells, to a private firm. The issue at stake was not only access to affordable water but also, more broadly, a continuing decline in the standard of living. To understand the contextual conditions of the resistance, Simmons provided a detailed historical account of the economic shifts that occurred back in 1985, including conversations among engineers, architects, economists, and environmentalists on feasible solutions to ensure water availability to address the history of drought. The interpretation of the grievance in this case was grounded in a shared understanding of the cultural and historical meaning of water.

The second condition of grievance formation is that individually felt injustices need to accumulate into shared understandings. Snow (2013) also emphasized this condition, highlighting the significance of collectively shared grievances in the formation of social movements. The process of transforming individual grievances into a collective force necessitates negotiation among activists to address ambiguities and reconcile different thought worlds within the movement (Simmons, 2014). Simmons (2014) argued that without such negotiations, a shared grievance will not emerge with sufficient emotional energy to sustain action toward collective ends. There is a need to actively stimulate negotiations that connect to emotions (Benford, 1993; Cress & Snow, 1996).

In the case of the Bolivian Water War, Simmons (2014, p. 536) described the importance of the coordinadora, an organization seeking to defend “water and life” that acted as a coordinator stimulating negotiations between actor groups. Based on their understanding of the history and cultural interpretation of water in the region the members of the coordinadora identified themselves as members of the region. They were able to broker discussions and redirect negotiations from what it means for “me” to what it means for “us” Simmons (2014).

The third condition for grievance formation is the importance of emotions. Powerful emotional reactions, such as anger, solidarity, and vulnerability, are critical for the formation of grievances (Goodwin et al., 2000; Jasper, 1997, 2011). As suggested by Jasper (1997, p. 126), “grievances are only grievances because they upset or outrage people.” Emotions shape people’s understanding of grievances and propel them to act in certain ways and not in others (Polletta & Jasper, 2001). More so, emotions can unify individuals and groups who belong to different communities.

During the Bolivian Water War, the grievances needed to translate across heterogeneous individuals and groups: “cross-class, cross-sector or occupation, crossgender, cross-ethic, cross-location—urban, peri-urban, and rural” to motivate movement participation (Simmons, 2014, p. 515). While early participation involved heterogeneous voices reflecting concerns of “me” and “my unique ways of life,” the emotions were what glued individual voices together. Strong emotions from these diverse social groups were in concert because the grievance had evolved and morphed from claims of economic hardship, water, and material existence to the issue of national identity.

## 3 The Policy on Sex Work Protests at Amnesty International

The importance of attending to grievance formation only became clear to us when we struggled to explain a failed movement related to a policy on “decriminalizing sex work” from Amnesty International (Appendix A provides a timeline of key events in the Sex Worker Policy Protest). The policy sparked online protests and a flood of personal sentiments, but these sentiments failed to connect into a coherent shared voice strong enough to overturn the policy despite favorable political opportunity structures. Not only was there a national law on the side of the grievance, but there was also intense media coverage.

The uprising followed an Amnesty International (hereafter “Amnesty”) policy draft on decriminalizing sex work leaked to Daily Mail, a UK news magazine. The policy draft referred to sex workers as “empowered workers” (Hulusjö, 2013) and emphasized safe working conditions and workers’ rights. It portrayed sex work as an economic transaction with mutual gain (equivalent to other types of service work). In the Swedish Amnesty organization and its social media channels, the policy and its underlying “economic” principles caused considerable unease, and the social media space rapidly scaled with messages contesting the policy. The ideas in the policy presented a threat to the perceptions of sex work shared by many Swedish Amnesty members—i.e., that sex work is a form of oppression, not work, resulting from patriarchal gender relations (Weitzer, 2009). <sup>3</sup> This view was also manifested in the Swedish sex purchase law, <sup>4</sup> which prohibits and penalizes buyers in the prostitution marketplace but does not criminalize sex workers, as they are seen as victims of the people who abuse them (Jeffreys, 2009).

Around the time that the policy document was leaked, we followed Amnesty’s Swedish Facebook page and gathered input from Amnesty’s Swedish branch staff as part of a longitudinal study. At that time, Facebook was the organization’s most active social media space. The Facebook group was “open,” which meant that all messages were visible to the public (including those not following the site). The page had about 60,000 followers, with about 350,000 to 550,000 views per month.

The logic of “connective action” guided our initial analysis of the protest. We observed massive amounts of “personal action frames” (Bennett & Segerberg, 2012) but were bewildered by the disconnected and fragmented nature of the messages. Personal action frames included issues such as trafficking, rape, abortion, and even organ trade, which were highly emotional and included threats to the organization.

The use of social media features, such as likes and comments, did not help to organize the collective action, as the logic of connective action would suggest. Messages were redundant or seemed disconnected. Even the Amnesty social media staff expressed how “unproductive” the dialogue was online: “I would say that only two or three people were engaged in a nuanced discussion—most people just repeated their messages over and over again.”

It was not until we removed ourselves from the online activism literature and returned to the social movement literature that we believed we had identified the underlying problem: the lack of a shared grievance. We had seen individual grievances but not the formation of a collective voice. However, even as we strove to understand the grievances in the sex worker policy case, we were too focused on what was behaviorally visible in the Facebook messages. This microperspective prevented us from perceiving the macrocontext—that is, the entangled nature of grievances and their cultural and historical roots. We could not, for example, connect the messages of organ trade or trafficking to a coherent claim, failing to see their historical roots. We allowed ourselves to be enticed by a particular “moment” and forwent “complex negotiations” (Papacharissi, 2016, p. 8), which would have involved bringing relevant knowledge about the broader ideological, cultural, and historical context of the women’s movement and the various recent entanglements to decipher and render the messages on Facebook meaningful.<sup>5</sup> This caused us to infer how social media imposes challenges on the conditions developed by Simmons (2014) on grievance formation, as well as challenges associated with researching such formations as they manifest online.

## 4 Extending the Grievance Theory to Online Movements

While the three conditions of grievance formation from Simmons’s (2014) work remain intact, this section discusses how social media imposes challenges on the conditions that can blur and even immobilize the formation and evolution of a movement. In this section, for each condition, we (1) discuss the challenge, (2) relate the challenge to the sex worker policy protests, and (3) triangulate with examples from online movement studies. The propositions on challenges are in the pursuit of a theory to explain failure in online movements.

## 4.1 First Condition: Grievances Are Imbued with Contextual Understandings

Simmons (2014) emphasized the significance of “the local” and the “location” (p. 2021) of grievances. To her, “how the problems themselves are understood” (p. 519) at a certain time and in a particular context is critical for understanding how grievances are formed. We must ask: “How do people interpret their circumstances … and how might that interpretation inform their response.” (Simmons, 2014, p. 523). Any movement—online or offline—can encompass not only individuals who have experienced suppression, violations, or adversity but also supporters who may lack awareness of the context, history, or cultural developments surrounding a claim. The observation of an event, as well as connecting and relating to it, differs depending on one’s relationship to the specific event.

Sex worker policy protests: In online movements, there can be rapid and wide transcendence of messages across diverse contexts and times, making the interpretation of circumstances very difficult. The networked structure of social media brings new voices to movements and increases the risk of displacing “initial intentions” (Massa & O’Mahony, 2021, p. 1072). At the time that the sex work policy was leaked to the press, the first messages on the Facebook group started with requests for verification about Amnesty’s intentions: “I have a question. I read in the Metro [Swedish newspaper] this morning that Amnesty International is working to legalize sex purchases. What does this mean to the Swedish Amnesty section? What is your position? … What do you think about this and how will you act?” (Facebook Member). “Is it true? Are you legalizing sex work?” (Facebook Member). Some even engaged by adding resources such as suggesting additional reading and clarifying the implications of the Swedish sex purchase law: “These issues need to be put into their historical context. We cannot start from scratch with this debate. … This should not even be a controversial issue. It was 20 years since we debated this in Sweden. The Swedish sex purchase law was ratified with a wide majority. Many of us welcomed the law and were active in the debate.” (Facebook Member). Some made references and problematized the ideological foundations of the policy: “Why don’t you see all the power structures that are in this policy. Would not aspects such as gender violence, racism, and class be of importance?” (Facebook Member). A few—active and retired—sex workers engaged in the discussion, adding detail to the issue at hand: “As an exited prostituted person I am dismayed and wounded by your actions to have prostitution legalized.”

Yet as soon as the Swedish and international news media picked up on the issue, the Facebook group rapidly scaled, and about 4,000 members joined over a few days. With the rapid scaling of the space, we no longer observed verification requests or background resources, as the messages on Facebook looked more like a social media firestorm (Matook et al., 2022). The richer understanding of “what was at stake,” the formation of a shared grievance, originally found in the members of the Facebook group became blurred by the rapid influx of new members and their messages: “These were new people to us, skilled people, and even organizations that were mobilizing against us.” (Amnesty Representative, interview). The influx of new members also attracted individuals who voiced approval of the policy: “In the first days, we only had negative reactions. But it also attracted people that agreed on the policy draft … these were different people from our regular members, and mostly men” (Amnesty Representative, interview).

With the escalation of members, messages drifted and became more distant and ambiguous in terms of the claims that were made. It was difficult to interpret messages and contextualize them. The messages did not use a coherent voice. To illustrate, the following quotes (mostly) contain the perspectives of those critical of the policy. However, in the middle of the message thread (in bold), a member posts a message that reflects an opposite position of the policy; this position was, however, not accounted for in the subsequent messages.

1/29/2014 8:20:22 PM: I, or anyone that I know will not donate anything to Amnesty nor sign any of your petitions again! This is disgusting. I am in shock!

1/29/2014 8:35:29 PM: Why are you doing this? It is ridiculous and you [Amnesty] seem to try and cover it up by claiming a “democratic discussion” that is just sad. What have we learned from the Swedish sex purchase law? Has research proved it wrong? I honestly don’t understand a thing.

1/29/2014 8:52:46 PM: I think that this shows that Amnesty can think and act wisely. Why shouldn’t prostitutes have the same [worker] rights as the rest of us? It doesn’t help anyone to judge or try to pretend as if prostitution doesn’t exist. I think you all should think beyond what is considered politically correct.

1/29/2014 9:41:46 PM: I really hope that this suggestion will not be ratified. It is like going backward. It is such a strange feeling that Amnesty is even thinking about this. I am surprised.

1/29/2014 11:20:34 PM: I will leave Amnesty if this [policy] suggestion is ratified!

Essentially, these emotionally charged messages only captured instances or moments in time but did not generate follow-up negotiations that would have rendered a deeper shared contextual understanding of the issue at hand.

Online social movement studies: In the existing online movement literature, Miranda et al. (2016), in their study of the Stop Online Piracy Act (SOPA) discourse, also noticed the “competing social constructions of the meanings surrounding an issue” and emphasized the hegemonic nature of social media. Similarly, Selander and Jarvenpaa (2020) delved into the challenges associated with transferring contextual understandings online in their study of the “Decoder” crowdsourcing platform at Amnesty. They noticed how activists with situated awareness and local understandings engaged more deeply and understood the tasks more fully. Zheng and Yu (2016) noted, for example, how shared understandings and even bodily memories of hunger and food deprivation were critical for the rapid scaling of the Free Lunch for Children online campaign. Our point here is that what people choose to represent in their messages and how those messages travel and are interpreted by others online vary significantly based on people’s relationship to “the local.”

Proposition 1: In online movements, grievances may transcend diverse local contexts, but such transcendence can reduce coherence without shared contextual understandings and thus pose challenges for the formation of shared grievances.

## 4.2 Second Condition: Brokers help Translate Individual Sentiments into Shared Grievances

Crossing interpretative differences may require brokering and guidance. It is not just the severity of the grievances but also how they coalesce that matters (Jasper, 1997; Klandermans, 1984; Simmons, 2014; Zald, 1991). Shared grievance formation can be less problematic when “a large fraction of some population is exposed to clear change or change in their living conditions” (Zald, 1991, p. 349). Even though the relative severity of such a change would vary, others who are outside the population (i.e., outsiders or peripheral actors) can still “imagine the moment” and understand its political significance and context. Yet in instances when such shared experiences are not present, a broker may be required to translate individual sentiments, political significance, and context in ways that generate the necessary internal motivation for outsiders and peripheral actors to participate in the movement. The translation needs to convey the grievance cognitively but, even more importantly, in sentiment—“feeling aggrieved” (Pinard, 201, p. 29). For shared grievance formation the broker acts as a “culturally legitimated theorist”<sup>6</sup> that can align emotions and contextual understandings.

Sex worker policy protests: At Amnesty, the professional staff members are expert brokers—they know how to draw from the empathic feelings of their supporters to motivate and sustain their financial and moral support for different human rights campaigns. Many of the campaigns involve victims in faraway places facing adversity that supporters would not be likely to experience themselves. Amnesty bridges the lack of social proximity and shared social context. For example, newsletters are carefully crafted to allow supporters to put themselves in the position of victims and imagine the context of the injustices they face— but without “risks of vicarious distress and burnout” (Scheffer et al., 2022, p. 2) that could emotionally tax and turn off supporters. However, at the time of the sex work policy protest, these skills had not been fully transferred to Amnesty’s social media spaces.

In the sex worker protests, messages were rapidly forking, and the very amount of claims was posing a significant challenge for the social media staff: “We responded to about half of the [online] messages, we redirected them to our web pages so that people could read [a formal statement] … we received critique for sounding like robots, but honestly, we had nothing more to say.” Even in instances where individuals seemed to share a standpoint, there was no sense of a coherent voice or of “being united.”

1/30/2014 11:12:02 AM: I think this [the policy] is really good, I hope that it will pass. This is one of the reasons that I am a proud Amnesty member, Amnesty is defending human rights even when not everyone agree <3

## 1/30/2014 11:34:28 AM: Are you drunk?

1/30/2014 12:01:52 PM: I have been looking for an organization that I can engage in that follows democratic principles and that allows for critical discussions… I will join Amnesty from now on.

1/30/2014 12:35:45 PM: Today the law is indifferent to whether you buy sex from a consenting adult or not, it is the same crime. If we decriminalize sex workers, there would be less trafficking.

We saw few signs of “productive dialogue” (Tsoukas, 2009) or brokering between messages capable of providing the guidance needed for shared grievance formation. This caused us to raise questions about the challenges associated with translating individual sentiments, political significance, urgency, and context in diverse social media spaces. Without a human broker, the Facebook group seemed to lack the capacity to create the necessary understanding to allow a shared grievance to emerge from the co-opted and divergent messages.

Online social movement studies: In online movements, a broker can take on many forms—both human and technological. Brokers are needed to provide the guidance and understanding necessary to interpret social media messages (see Miranda et al. 2016). In the realm of online movements, transnational constituents, specialized language, or feature use, can lead to insufficient connections between messages. To grow into a shared grievance, messages and claims require content that can connect sentiments with the macrocontext. The specific words used in the messages matter. For example, whether a message uses the word “sex worker” or “prostitute” speaks differently to social groups, their context, and their ideological convictions.

The use of technological features, such as hashtags and likes, may help but also fail to fulfill the broker’s role. They can either be too specialized or too abstract to form a connection. Messages may then become prone to morphing as individuals bring their personal networks and understandings of “what is at stake” to the protests. In this way, grievances can lose their coherence, particularly when messages come from communities with interpretative differences. For instance, Mundt et al. (2018, p. 11), studying the Black Lives Matter (BLM) movement, highlighted the challenges and obstacles caused by such interpretative differences. They noted the significant amount of time needed for brokering and protecting online spaces:

constantly [monitor] the space [Facebook], diverting resources away from amplifying the movement’s central messages. In fact, the BLM group administrators spoke [about] the immense amount of time they dedicate to moderating their online social media profiles, in large part to stay on the defensive front against unsavory narratives or outright criticism.

Similarly, in their study on the Anonymous hacktivist movement, Massa and O’Mahony (2021, p. 1066) underscored the challenges associated with diversity in interpretations of the movement’s central claim:

When larger numbers of participants join open forums, diverse newcomers unaware of established norms may expand upon or reinterpret the initial ambition, giving rise to chaos: disjointed and divergent ways to engage in protest that can undermine coordinated collective action. … This is particularly likely when there is no well-articulated collective identity story and no strong leader to tell that story (e.g., Wry et al., 2011).

Social media use also poses challenges related to conflicting claims. As with the sex worker policy case, the arrival of new members at different times caused messages to be redundant and there was no shared understanding of possible or desired future action.

Proposition 2: In online movements without human brokers, individual claims are prone to morph and may lack co-presence, which poses challenges to shared grievance formation.

## 4.3 Third Condition: Emotional Reactions Motivate Shared Grievance Formation

Emotional reactions, such as anger, solidarity, and vulnerability, are important resources in grievance formation (Simmons, 2014). They shape people’s understanding of grievances and propel them to act in certain ways and not in others (Jasper, 1997; Simmons, 2014). Although in the field of social movement studies, there is wide recognition that emotions can mobilize but also demobilize movements, there has been much less discussion of how grievances can shape and be shaped by emotions (Pinard, 2011).

Sex worker policy protests: “Experience sharing” is made possible when there is congruence with others affective valences (Scheffer et al., 2022). In the Facebook messages, a minimal number of participants identified themselves as sex workers, while the majority had little or no direct personal stake in the cause. Instead, the individual grievances stemmed from ideological threats, and emotions were unregulated due to the perceived absence of risks or retribution. The messages articulated a range of emotions, including being “extremely upset,” experiencing “emotional shock,” or feeling “sad” or “embarrassed.” Some individuals expressed threats, such as refusing to sign Amnesty petitions in the future or considering dropping their membership if they proceeded with the policy. The news media also contributed to the escalation by intensifying their reporting. A member of staff shared their experience: “At some point, a journalist called, and I was sick, but I took the call. I was so relieved that he wanted to talk about some critical developments in [Country X] [and not the sex work policy]. I almost felt like crying.”

Online social media studies: The literature on online social movements (Miranda et al., 2016) and connective action has also widely recognized the role of emotions, especially in terms of their potential to amplify movements (Leong et al., 2015). Social media platforms facilitate the different expressions and forms of emotions, be it through imaging, performances, or memetic figures (Ghobadi & Clegg, 2015; Khosravi Ooryad, 2023). For example, in the case of the My Stealthy Freedom (MySF) online movement (Stewart & Schultze, 2019) in Iran, people reacted to a policy threat—a law mandating compulsory hijab use—and used social media to protest and show solidarity with the movement. Stewart and Schultze (2019, p. 11-12) wrote:

… the images [published on Facebook] nevertheless contained a number of elements that rendered them representative of the movement. These included (i) holding the headscarf in the wind like a flag of freedom (Fig. 1) or dropping it like a discarded tissue (Fig. 2); (ii) being located in a public space (from deserted locations to populous religious sites); and (iii) enacting gestures of freedom (e.g., jumping, throwing arms into the air, letting hair flow in the wind, and smiling broadly). … The removal of the headscarf in a public space was an experience that MySF activists shared, albeit in a highly individuated manner.

The visibility and possibility for imagination triggered high emotions and solidarity that became vehicles for the movement’s shared grievance formation.

However, emotions can “both make or mar a movement” (Shahin & Ng, 2022, p. 534). High-arousal emotions, such as anger and fear, risk becoming distorted or misinterpreted as they travel across different contexts. For example, Shahin and Ng (2022) pointed out that sharing personal grievances online risks causing a negative and short-lived emotional culture characterized by negative sentiment, fear, and shouting in the dark. They wrote: “If individuals participate in a social movement with the motivation of sharing their personal stories, their individual objectives are already met by tweeting once or twice about the issue” (p. 543).

What is at stake is the risk of demotivated participation or even immobilization (Pinard, 2011). Demotivated participation may come from weak emotions, but highly aroused emotions, such as anger, may also trigger fear among movement insiders due to perceived risks of retribution. Moreover, individuals who are contextually distant from the grievance would find it challenging to relate to these intense emotions, ultimately diminishing the potential for shared grievance formation. When high-arousal emotions are constantly before one’s eyes, emotional brokering might be needed to regulate, filter, and interpret messages and lower the costs of engagement. This may be particularly evident in situations where contextual understandings or historical trials are not necessarily shared.

Proposition 3: In online movements, grievances shape emotions, but those emotions could also shape grievances to the point of immobilization and pose challenges for the formation of shared grievances.

## 5 Discussion and Implications for Future Research

The current paper is a conceptual contribution based on existing literature, with an illustration to explain failure in online movements. We contribute to the literature in three ways by (1) shedding light on the notable gap regarding grievance formation within online movement literature, (2) presenting challenges to the conditions that foster shared grievance formation on social media, and (3) reflecting on our interpretative difficulties in understanding (and seeing) shared grievances and providing insights for future research endeavors aiming to explore grievances within contemporary movements.

Our first contribution is to shed light on the absence of attention to grievances within the literature on online movements. In their study, Syed and Silva (2023) acknowledged that only a few tweets could be attributed to grievance formation. However, they did not delve deeper into why this was the case. We illustrate the void of attention to grievances in the online movement literature. We argue that this neglect stems partly from the lack of empirical studies on the reasons why some online movements fail. Scholars have prioritized the exploration of seemingly successful movements without problematizing grievance formation. The lack of attention to grievance formation can also be traced to the widespread reliance on the connective action perspective (Bennett & Segerberg, 2012) in which grievance formation is not a central notion. The connective action perspective rests on the assumption that “personally expressive content is shared with, and recognized by, others who, in turn, repeat these networked sharing activities” (Bennett & Segerberg, 2012, p. 752). Yet, as pointed out by online movement scholars and as illustrated in the sex worker policy protest, messages in open social media spaces are often diverse, emotionally charged, and at high risk of cooptation.

Our second contribution is the examination and extension of Simmons’s (2014) conditions for grievance formation within the realm of online movements. We problematize these conditions in the online context and provide propositions. We recognize that online movements are not a distinct category of social movements but are extensions of the modernization processes that have evolved over the past two centuries (Tarrow, 2011). The emergence of social media networks has transformed the movement landscape, allowing individual grievances to reach broad audiences across geographical boundaries and localities. It is necessary to understand how the conditions for shared grievance formation are challenged in the online context. Such an understanding could help explain failures but also provide insights into how movements become powerful enough to alter political developments.

The three propositions address conditions that can result in an online movement failure. In a nutshell,<sup>7</sup> the first proposition suggests that the broad reach of social media allows outsiders to contribute to public discourse without a strong connection to the cause or grievance and that these outsiders diversify movement messages such that the grievance is difficult for outside observers or peripheral activists to distill. The second proposition suggests that on social media, messages can morph and be hijacked, resulting in dynamic or incoherent claims. Finally, the third proposition suggests that high emotional intensity in online movement messages may result in immobilization.

The propositions are an extension of Simmons’s (2014) theory and aim to address failure in shared grievance formation in online movements. To reduce the potential of failure, it is important to understand the locality of grievances and the individual “starting positions” of the claimants. Although these initial positions may be understandable within specific local contexts (Stewart & Schultze, 2019), individually felt grievances intertwined with culture and ideology face challenges in coalescing into shared grievances when spanning different contexts, increasing risks of co-optation and fragmentation (Mundt et al., 2018; Shahin & Ng, 2022). Emotions play a vital role as amplifiers in this process, but on social media, emotions may also hinder progress or even immobilize the movement. In online movements, human or technological brokers are needed for meaningful translations across contexts, for foreseeing the future (what possible action or inaction might lead to), and for regulating emotions.

The third contribution is sharing our own interpretive difficulties in explaining online movement failure and providing insights for future research endeavors aiming to explore grievances within contemporary movements. Our initial focus was on comprehending the sex worker policy protest as an independent and later failed movement, but we did this without adequately connecting the protest to its macrocontext—its historical, cultural, and ideological roots. We overlooked the fact that people did not share “contextual understandings” in their Facebook messages. We also failed to understand the historical roots of the women’s rights movement, and missed, for example, claims about “trafficking” and “organ smuggling” as part of a shared grievance on women’s rights. We view our failure as both a cautionary tale and a catalyst for approaching grievances in the study of online movements.

To reduce such interpretative blind spots, researchers are encouraged to recognize their respective starting positions when studying online movements and grievances, in particular. This self-reflexivity is critical for studying grievances and for the interpretative challenges associated with moving between the macroand microlevels. Stewart and Schultze (2019, p. 8) demonstrated such exemplary research responsibility. They recognized how they themselves needed to communicate their understanding of the grievances in interactions with claimants:

…the first author—an Iranian native who conducted all the interviews—recorded a short WhatsApp video message in which she introduced herself. She sent this clip as part of her request to interview a given candidate. … she talked about her experience with hijab and feelings towards the law in her video.

As researchers of contemporary movements, studying social media networks and the messages posted on them is not enough. Rather, researchers need to move away from finding explanations in technological affordances in the microcontext to tracing the genealogy of grievances in the macrocontext. Failure to do so poses the risk of disregarding grievances that are culturally endorsed.

Without a “macro” perspective, there is a risk of misinterpreting movements as “failed” because it cannot be seen how grievances are interconnected and may potentially spin off into new movements. This is certainly also true for our study. Here, we infer a failure, but we do not know what the future might hold for the policy and whether messages of the Amnesty sex worker protest will be reinvigorated in other movements. Grievances can flow into other spaces and times (Khosravi Ooryad, 2024). For example, the phrase “I can’t breathe” originated with the killing of Eric Garner in 2014 and gained further prominence through its association with George Floyd’s death in 2020. This powerful expression played a pivotal role in reinvigorating the BLM movement (Apata, 2020). Subsequently, it evolved into “We can’t breathe” during protests following the poisoning of Iranian schoolgirls in 2023. The grievance—the bodily experience of suffocation —traveled across time and different movements.

In future research, we urge for further theoretical and empirical investigation into grievances within online movements. Additionally, exploring the influence of social media in shaping potential “disjunctures” between these movements should be a focal point. Such research is crucial for expanding the existing literature beyond framing and mobilization (Simmons, 2014) and incorporating the study of grievance formation. Future research should delve into the dynamics between movements and examine what happens to grievances as they travel through them. How do material, temporal, and ideological resources contribute to the transformation of grievances within and across different social media networks? We hope for future studies that are not based on single illustrations or cases but consider multiple online movements to increase the generalizability of the findings.

Research is also needed on participation barriers in grievance formation. How is the shared grievance formation process impacted when those suffering injustice are not visible or heard? How can we understand their starting positions and their desired ends? What can we say about movement impact when those impacted are not voiced in the data material we study or in our study design? In the case of the sex worker policy, few sex workers were visible to us in the Facebook messages. The Facebook page did not afford a protective space or “shelter” where shared understandings and collective support could be developed (Cress & Snow, 1996; Vaast & Levina, 2015).

Mundt et al. (2018, p. 11) stated that social media, such as Facebook, “can significantly exacerbate physical risks already associated with nonviolent civil disobedience and activism.” To what extent is there a need for the study of not only public social media networks but also private spaces? The importance of private spaces for the interpretation and formation of grievances was also emphasized by Massa and O’Mahony (2021), Stewart and Schultze (2019), and Kavada (2015). Private spaces, in all these studies, are not only tied to recurrent interpretative work and organizing but also to “protection” and risk mitigation. For example, the MySF campaign in Iran (Stewart & Schultze, 2019) was heavily brokered by the originator of the movement, a single person with a deep understanding of the political and historical context. Future research should ask questions about the role of private spaces in social media platforms for shared grievance formation.

For practice, we particularly highlight the role of brokering in the emergence and maintenance of online movements. Brokering is essential for negotiations around claims and conceptions, creating a sense of directionality, and motivating action. Our research also infers the immobilization risk when emotional effects are not regulated (DeCelles et al. 2020). Emotions are tightly related to grievance formation, yet high-arousal emotions also risk obscure shared understandings of “what is at stake”.

## 6 Conclusion

In this conceptual piece, we explain online movement failure by delving into the notion of shared grievance formation. We follow the social movement literature and conceive of grievances as grounded in a particular time and context—and infer how grievances are formed and become challenged when they materialize online. We suggest research propositions to extend the grievance theory to online movements and move toward a theory of online movement failure. Paying attention to grievances at both macro- and microlevels is critical as the IS field increasingly engages in questions related to injustice, marginalization, and politics. Our research is an attempt at elaborating how shared grievances operate in online spaces.

## About the Authors

Lisen Selander serves as a professor of information systems at Gothenburg University, Sweden. Her scholarly work engages with digital transformation, social (in)justice, and contemporary collective action. She is one of the founding members of TechnAct—a research cluster on gender, sexualities, emergent communities, and technocultural assemblages.

Sirkka L. Jarvenpaa is a professor of information systems and the Bayless/Rauscher Pierce Refsnes Chair in Business Administration at the McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin. She is a recipient of the Association of Information Systems Fellow and LEO Awards (LEO is awarded for the lifetime achievement of exceptional global contributions in the field of information systems). Her research focuses on interorganizational and interpersonal collaboration and innovation in fast-paced and technologically advanced data and knowledge environments with regulatory and policy implications. Her work has appeared in information systems, management, engineering, accounting, marketing, psychology, and anthropology journals.
