# Visual Background Music: Creativity Support Systems with Priming

RANDALL K. MINAS AND ALAN R. DENNIS

RANDALL K. MINAS (rminas@hawaii.edu) is an assistant professor and the Hon Kau and Alice Lee Faculty Fellow at the Shidler College of Business at University of Hawai’i at Manoa. His research centers on influencing cognition through design. He is co-founder of the Hawaii Interdisciplinary Neurobehavioral and Technology (HINT) Lab at that university. Dr. Minas has been published in such journals as the Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Sciences, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, AIS Transactions on Human Computer Interaction, and others. He has received Best Paper awards at the Hawaiian International Conference on System Sciences and the Human-Computer Interaction International Conference.

ALAN R. DENNIS (ardennis@indiana.edu; corresponding author) is Professor of Information Systems and holds the John T. Chambers Chair of Internet Systems in the Kelley School of Business at Indiana University. Dr. Dennis has written more than 150 research papers and has won numerous awards for his theoretical and applied research. His research work focuses on three main themes: team collaboration, fake news on social media, and information security. He is President Elect of the Association for Information Systems (AIS), a Fellow of AIS, and co-Editor-in-Chief of AIS Transactions on Replication Research. Dr. Dennis also has written four books (two on data communications and networking and two on systems analysis and design).

ABSTRACT: Developing creative solutions to problems is often a key element of organizational decision making. Much research has been done on systems to support individual creativity, called Creativity Support Systems (CSS). Commercially available CSS and past CSS research have offered either a structured approach to the problem or provided stimuli to help the user think about the problem in a different way. We conducted two experiments testing an approach that builds on priming research by providing stimuli related to achievement on the screen as users worked, as what we call visual background music. Our findings show that this approach increased an individual’s creative output by 10–40% depending upon the experiment and outcome measure. Under the conditions of our studies, the priming stimuli we used had the strongest effect on the flexibility pathway by broadening the user’s consideration of the solution space, rather than the persistence pathway of encouraging more ideas within a topic category. A key implication of this study for CSS design is to add a banner that has rotating pictures about achievement to existing CSS.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: priming, creativity, creativity support systems, idea generation, dual pathway model, stimuli provider, visual background music, priming creativity.

## Introduction

Creativity is important to most modern organizations because the generation of a wide variety of ideas and alternatives is often a key step in problem solving and decision making [60, 66, 68]. The study of information systems to support creativity has a relatively long history [21, 29, 45, 53] and is an active area of research [1, 2, 51, 75]. These systems, called Creativity Support Systems (CSS) [68], typically support the creative process in two different ways [50, 51]. The most common approach is to provide task-specific information or other stimuli to encourage the user to take a different perspective on the task [50]. A second common approach is to provide a structured process that guides the user to think about the task in different ways [21, 29]. Some CSS use both approaches [34]. Both stimuli providers and process guides are commercially available [2], with stimuli providers having the strongest effects on performance [2].

In this paper, we propose a different approach to CSS design, an approach that uses priming [8, 9, 37]. Priming is the presentation of stimuli that make specific emotions or concepts more accessible to an individual’s working memory and can influence both cognition and behavior without the individual’s conscious awareness [8, 9, 37]. Many different forms of priming exist (e.g., supraliminal, subliminal, affective); we use supraliminal priming (i.e., “above threshold”) in which the user is aware of the stimuli, but not necessarily aware of their intended effects [8, 9, 37]. Unlike stimulus providing CSS or structure providing CSS (both of which overtly strive to direct the user’s deliberate cognition in certain ways), supraliminal priming does not require direct conscious cognition and is more like metaphorical background music while we work: we know it is there, but it seems to have no direct connection to our work.

We conducted two separate experiments examining the effects of a CSS designed to use supraliminal priming that presented images associated with the concept of achievement on the side of the screen as the user worked to generate ideas. The findings indicate that the use of this type of CSS improves both the quantity and creativity of ideas. We believe this contributes to research on CSS by theorizing and testing a new approach to CSS design and showing that the theoretical mechanism by which it works is primarily through the flexibility pathway (i.e., broadening users’ consideration of the solution space so that they generate ideas in more topic categories), rather than the persistence pathway (i.e., more ideas within the topic categories). These findings also have implications for the design of future CSS. One simple first step is to add a banner that has rotating pictures about achievement to existing CSS.

## Prior Theory

## Creativity

“Creativity typically emerges from discovering new associations between previously disparate things” [50, p. 719]. One of the best theoretical models for understanding idea generation is the Cognitive Network Model (CNM) [64]. The CNM builds on classic cognitive science research showing that there are at least two components to human memory (see also [54]). The first is working memory, which has limited capacity and is only capable of storing information for a limited time [64]. Deliberate conscious cognition occurs in working memory, as concepts are loaded for consideration and use.

The second component is long term memory which stores an individual’s fundamental knowledge [54, 64]. Long term memory has essentially unlimited capacity and is persistent, although it can degrade with aging [54]. Long term memory is organized into bundles of related concepts [64]. When a bundle is accessed by working memory (or even just one concept within a bundle), it creates ready access to all the concepts within the bundle. For example, if one accesses the bundle of a picnic, then the concepts within it (e.g., blanket, hamper, sunshine) also become easily accessible with little extra cognitive effort [64].

Bundles are linked to other bundles [54, 64]. For example, tree may be linked to shade, fire, and paper. There is often considerable overlap between bundles and the boundaries are fuzzy, especially for concepts that share many linkages to each other and to other concepts [54]. When one bundle is activated in working memory, it often activates linked bundles, and activation gradually spreads [64]. We have no control over what bundles are activated by external stimuli, because the process is automatic and based on the linkages among bundles [64].

Idea generation is a two-step process [54]. First, an individual activates bundles or concepts in long term memory, loading them into working memory [54]. Second, working memory is utilized as the individual tries to generate a new idea by combining concepts, making new connections or applying the concepts in the bundle to a new domain [54]. These two processes are iterative in that the output from one influences the other [54]. Ideas produced in step two serve as cues to bundle activation in step one. As an individual generates an idea, it influences what concepts and bundles are further activated and brought into working memory for use in generating more ideas. Thus, ideas may exhibit cognitive inertia as an individual explores the same solution space in greater depth, overlooking other parts of the solution space [26, 64].

The combination of concepts sparks the generation of a new idea [64]. When two unrelated concepts are connected, there is the potential for a new idea [41, 48]. Greater disparity between bundles in working memory may increase the likelihood that a new idea is produced, and that the idea is more creative [41, 64]. Individuals are usually poor at idea generation, because they tend to rely on routines and fail to use disparate bundles of knowledge [64].

External stimuli, such as those from CSS, can activate an unrelated bundle of concepts in long term memory and cause it to be accessible by working memory [64]. Activation by external stimuli is automatic and not under the deliberate control of the individual [5, 8, 10, 64]. As we previously noted, once a bundle is activated, the activation spreads through its connections to other unrelated bundles [64]. Thus idea generation can be a somewhat random walk through the solution space, shaped by both deliberate effortful focused cognition, and the automatic activation of bundles triggered by external stimuli [64]. Thus idea generation exhibits both inertia (as an individual explores the one part of the solution space in greater depth) and switching (as the activation of new knowledge bundles trigger new directions) [26, 64].

The way we construct a problem in our minds can influence the nature of bundles that are activated. For example, suppose you get a flat tire while on the way to an important meeting and discover the car has no jack [62]. The bundles we activate are different if we construct the problem as a search for something to lift the car, a search for someone to change the tire, or a search for alternate transportation. The activation of different bundles will lead to very to different ideas. The more ways we can construct a problem, the more likely we are to activate more bundles, and the more likely we are to generate more ideas and more creative ideas [4, 62].

The dual pathway model of creativity argues there are two routes to a set of ideas: flexibility and persistence [23, 55, 58]. Flexibility is the search for a breadth of different categories within the solution space (i.e., more bundles), while persistence refers to the pursuit of categories in depth (i.e., more ideas within a category) [23, 55, 58]. Persistence is “hard work, perseverance, and more or less deliberate, persistent, and in-depth exploration of a few cognitive categories and perspectives” [23, p. 740], while flexibility “manifests itself in the use of different cognitive categories and perspectives” [23, p. 740].

The way in which the ideas are distributed across the distinct categories within the solution space is an indicator of which pathway dominated an individual’s cognitive processes [23]. Greater use of the persistence pathway is indicated by a set of ideas in only a few categories in the solution space (e.g., many ideas in each category), while greater use of the flexibility pathway is indicated by the dispersion of ideas across categories (e.g., many categories) [23].

The choice of pathway (flexibility or persistence) can be influenced. For example, flexibility can be increased by providing more diverse cues in the task description which trigger ideas across more categories [4, 54]. An individual’s mood, which can be manipulated, also affects flexibility and persistence [23]. Mood is typically assessed on two dimensions: valence (positive or negative) and arousal (how intense or activating) [15]. When individuals have high arousal and positive valence, flexibility increases; conversely, when arousal is high and valence is negative, perseverance increases [23]. Thus, arousal is key to the production of ideas and valence influences which pathway is used [23].

## Creativity Support Systems

The study of creativity and CSS in the IS discipline has a long history [21, 29, 45]. There are many different types of CSS that support the creative process in a variety of ways [44, 50, 51]. The most common approach taken by many CSS is to support divergent thinking by proving task-specific stimuli, a guided process, or combination of both [2, 50, 51].

CSS that provide task-specific stimuli attempt to induce individuals to improve their creative productivity via the flexibility pathway. They offer stimuli related to a task that are intended to trigger thoughts of other bundles that would otherwise be overlooked. For example, both the CreativeFlow and MUSE systems have modules that provide visual stimuli from past projects and enable Web-based searches for stimuli [75]. LEAPS is a case-based reasoning system that provides a knowledge base of task-related knowledge that presents solutions to related problems that can be used to trigger new ideas [1]. IdeaFisher has an idea bank with over 700,000 associations of topics, phrases, and words as stimuli for generating ideas [47].

CSS that provide a guided process can vary greatly in the nature of the process that they support; some are specific to a general category of tasks, while others are general purpose [2]. Many CSS process guides support a process that also focuses on the flexibility pathway by encouraging individuals to find additional idea categories from within their own knowledge by using techniques suggesting different vantage points from which to consider the problem. For example, the guided fantasy technique encourages individuals to think of a fantasy world distant from the task and use elements of it in generating ideas [21, 22]. The GENI system has the user first identify problem elements (e.g., goals, beliefs, objects, and processes) and then presents a series of structured combinations of randomly selected problem elements to help the user find associations among them to generate more ideas [45]. The ods/Consultant system offers a variety of processes that can be mixed and matched to guide the user through problem assessment, assessing validity and reliability of known facts, developing and testing explanations for why the problem is the way it is, identifying resources to address the problem, and determining objectives [29, 46]. Ideatree provides a hierarchical (or lateral) process for developing ideas and then expounding on them in more detail [47]. The Axon Idea Processor encourages users to diagram objects and the relationships among them [31]. A group process introduced by Santanen et al. [64] had a facilitator interrupt participants every two minutes to focus their attention on five general concepts related to the task (more effective, faster, cheaper, easier, and simpler) and found that prompting led to more creative solutions than no prompting.

Our conclusion is that the use of CSS (stimuli providers or process guides) improves the number and creativity of ideas produced—sometimes modestly, sometimes substantially—but the effects are not uniform; some people and problems benefit more from CSS than others, and some find no benefit [50, 51, 68]. CSS that provide task-related stimuli tend to have a stronger effect on performance than process guides that rely on users’ own knowledge, but the evidence is not overwhelming [2]. Thus, the current received wisdom for CSS design is to include modules that provide task-specific stimuli and/or processes that encourage divergent thinking [50].

## Using Supraliminal Priming to Increase Creativity

We examine a different theoretical approach that does not use process or taskspecific cues to increase number and creativity of ideas but, rather, influences automatic cognition to increase creative flexibility and/or persistence. This type of theoretically driven approach could be both universally useful (i.e., since it is not dependent on a specific process or task) and combined with other process and taskstimuli approaches to design future CSS.

Priming is the activation of concepts or bundles of knowledge in an attempt to influence subsequent behavior [6]. Priming is usually delivered through stimuli presented before measuring behavior on a task of interest (which is why it is called priming, as in priming the pump) [67]. There are many types of priming, all variants on the same theme of initial stimuli that affect future behavior. Previous research has used subliminal, supraliminal, negative, and syntactic, among others [6]. Some types of priming are far afield from the current study; for example, negative priming focuses on selective attention [49, 73]. It uses a “distractor” stimulus that is supposed to be ignored during the priming phase. In the subsequent task, a target stimulus is shown in the area that contained the distractor stimulus [73]; therefore, healthy individuals take longer to recognize the target stimulus [49], creating a “negative priming effect” [49].

We used supraliminal, or “above threshold,” priming. With this priming, individuals are consciously aware of the stimulus, but not the intent behind it, unlike subliminal, or “below threshold,” priming [6, 25]. Even if the individual becomes aware of the intent of the priming stimulus, it still can influence behavior [7]. A variety of supraliminal priming techniques have been used, including commercials [3], posters [57], scenarios [20], and word games [6].

Supraliminal priming works by presenting stimuli designed to activate a specific target concept that is stored in the individual’s long term memory and move it into working memory. Once a concept becomes activated in working memory, its accessibility in long term memory is heightened [72]. Accessible concepts can influence cognitive processes such as selective attention or information retrieval [77]. When the target concept is active in working memory, it can affect how a person interprets new information and thus, can change behavior [5, 63].

Priming will only induce the desired behavior if the individual’s memory contains the target concept, if the priming stimuli activates the concept, if the link between the concept and the desired behavior is strong, and if the individual is in a context where the behavior is relevant [33]. There are many “ifs” in the theoretical chain that must be true for priming to have an impact, so its effects are not uniform across all individuals [8, 30, 71]. Nonetheless, priming has been shown to influence a wide variety of individual behavior, attitudes, and beliefs [8, 30, 71].

Priming can also influence individual behavior by affecting mood as well as by affecting cognition [52]. Most priming research on emotions has focused on the effect of priming on positive and negative emotional valence, rather than arousal [79]. Repeated exposure to words with positive valence or negative valence has been found to create a positive or negative emotional state, which in turn influences an individual’s subsequent behavior [18].

Emotional and cognitive reactions to priming are not mutually exclusive; emotions and cognition often interact and both influence behavior [78]. Defining priming stimuli as solely emotional or cognitive oversimplifies the actual influence of the prime [78]; it is more reasonable to assume that cognitive and emotional response to priming stimuli are related [78]. For example, the use of the concept of achievement has proven successful in prior studies, in part because achievement oriented concepts are typically high in positive valence and arousal. Therefore, priming the concept of achievement will likely both shape cognition and induce emotional states that are high in positive valance and arousal [11, 39]. Emotional states high in arousal increase perseverance and the production of ideas [23]. There also is some evidence that high arousal coupled with positive valence increase flexibility, leading to more creative ideas, while high arousal coupled negative valence increases perseverance, leading to more ideas overall [23].

There has been some initial research that attempts to understand if priming can be used to influence creativity. Friedman et al. [32] primed participants in two studies with either a broad or a narrow scope by having them look at a small area or wide area on the screen prior to the task; one study showed increased creativity for the broad priming, but the other did not. Brennan [12] primed participants in two studies by including or omitting the Apple Computer logo on task materials, and found no effects in either study. Sassenberg et al. [65] primed participants in two studies by asking them to first describe three creative activities or three precise activities; those receiving the creative priming were more creative in both tasks. Lewis et al. [43] primed participants in two studies by showing them positive images, negative images, and neutral images prior to the task, and found mixed effects on creativity in the first study for both positive and negative images, and only effects from negative images in the second study. Dennis et al. [27] primed groups of five participants with a word game using achievement words or neutral words and found groups receiving achievement priming were more creative.

As we look across past empirical research, we see two patterns. First, many different approaches to priming have been examined. Different stimuli (e.g., numbers, games, images, writing statements) intended to prime a variety of concepts (creative, novelty, broad, achievement) or affects (positive or negative) have been presented prior to a wide range of creativity tasks. Second, the effects on creative outputs have been equally diverse, with some studies showing priming to improve creative outputs and others showing no effect.

At best, we might say that priming may have potential to influence creative outputs; skeptics might say the erratic pattern shows a random walk. Priming can produce different effects in different individuals because each individual is likely to have a somewhat different set of concepts in long term memory; not all priming stimuli will activate the same concepts and trigger the same behaviors in everyone exposed to the same stimuli [17, 28, 70]; as we argued above, there is a long chains of “if” statements that need to be true for priming to work.

## Concurrent Non-Task-Related Supraliminal Image Priming

This study uses a type of supraliminal priming that we call concurrent non-taskrelated supraliminal image priming, in which the priming stimuli (images) that are not directly related to the task (non-task-related) are placed in the visual field of the user during the task itself (concurrent). We consider each of these three design elements in turn (concurrent, images, non-task-related). For simplicity of language, we will refer to this as concurrent priming for the rest of the paper.

## Concurrent

Priming has traditionally been done in an a priori fashion, meaning an individual is primed first and then works on the task (an exception is [43] who made a library of stimuli available to users during the task, but did not encourage or require them to access it). Since the prime is removed before the task starts, the priming effect begins to decay as the task commences [25]. Priming effects dissipate at a decay rate determined by a number of factors such as duration of priming session and stimuli used [25]. As a result, the efficacy of the prime is most pronounced at the beginning of a task and diminishes as time elapses.

From a theoretical perspective, this pattern is the opposite of what fits idea generation. Individuals usually begin idea generation with many ideas and produce a flurry of ideas as they start [64, 69]. Once they produce their initial ideas, idea generation becomes more difficult and they must either persist within the topics they have considered or exhibit flexibility and find new topics within the problem space. Traditional a priori priming may be limited in its effectiveness because the priming effect is wearing off in latter stages of the task just when it is most needed.

From a practical perspective, a priori priming also presents a potentially important limitation. Because it is delivered prior to the task, individuals must take time away from the idea generation task to receive priming, before they can begin working. Thus, the use of a priori priming in an organizational setting may be limited because individuals may not want to perform the priming task, as it takes time away from the more important idea generation task.

Concurrent priming delivers the priming stimuli at the same time as the individuals are performing the idea generation task. From a theoretical perspective, it is likely to have a stronger priming effect than traditional a priori priming, because the priming stimuli continue to act throughout the duration of the task and are active in the later part of the task when individuals have the greatest need for help generating ideas. From a practical perspective, concurrent priming is easier to implement because it occurs simultaneously with the task; the individual no longer needs to waste time performing a priming task prior to beginning to generate ideas.

However, with concurrent priming, individuals will be using cognitive resources to perform the brainstorming task at the same time as they need to process the priming stimuli. It is important that the priming stimuli do not require too much cognitive processing so that they do not constitute a distraction that impairs performance; for example, processing text messages while performing a task, impairs performance [74]. Interestingly, one meta-analysis found that modestly increasing cognitive load increased the strength of priming effects because when cognitive load is higher, individuals have less ability to actively resist priming [25].

## Images

One concern with concurrent priming is the need to devote cognitive resources to processing the priming stimuli at the same time as you perform the task. Processing images uses a different memory and processing system than verbal information [59], so they are less likely to present a distraction that inhibits the generation of idea of ideas expressed in verbal or written words. Priming research has found that individuals do not need to consciously process the stimuli for them to influence behavior [18]. Many stimuli are present in our visual field, and conscious processing does not occur for all stimuli. Priming stimuli presented in the visual field that are readily apparent influence behavior even if the individual does not recall them [18].

Images have been shown to be as effective as words in eliciting priming effects [16, 35], and also have been used in CSS [50]. Image primes activate semantic networks in a similar way to priming with words by eliciting semantic interpretations of the images [35, 50]. For example, an image of an individual crossing a finish line in first place creates a semantic interpretation of “that individual just won the race.” This semantic interpretation activates mental representations related to winning in the same manner as words related to winning [35, 50].

## Non-Task-Related

Many stimuli-proving CSS provide task-related stimuli because, as argued by CNM [64], it is the connection between concepts in different bundles—especially disparate bundles—that leads to creative ideas [1, 47]. In contrast, most priming-based approaches have used non-task-specific stimuli designed to prime positive affect or general concepts intended work across a variety of tasks (e.g., broad, creative, achievement).

One key challenge in priming lies in selecting an appropriate concept to prime [27]. One concept that has been shown to have broad impact is achievement. Achievement priming works through a cognitive route by activating semantic networks related to achievement, which motivates behavior by creating an expectation of success and an increased desire to achieve [7, 27]. Motivation to perform a task is a function of the individual’s perception of the importance of that task and the expectancy of success [76], so if one can positively influence either, motivation should increase [27, 76]. If we can increase motivation, then idea generation performance should improve because increased motivation leads to increased idea production [23].

Research on achievement priming’s effects on the persistence and flexibility pathways of idea generation are mixed. One study has shown that achievement priming decreases perseverance and increases flexibility, but the task did not involve creative idea generation [36]. In another study, individuals primed with achievement continued to work on the task after they were instructed to stop, indicating that achievement priming induces perseverance behaviors [7]. They also performed better in subsequent word puzzle or impression formation tasks [7], but this study did not examine idea generation. Priming achievement may also influence emotional mood, because achievement-oriented words tend to have positive valence and be high in arousal. Participants with high arousal and positive valence are more flexible in idea generation [23].

## Hypotheses

In summary, we argue that based on theory, concurrently priming achievement during an idea generation task will increase motivation and change mood (increased arousal and more positive valence), which together can work through the flexibility and/or perseverance pathways to improve individual idea generation. There is some empirical evidence for tasks other than idea generation that supports our theoretical arguments that achievement priming will increase both flexibility and perseverance. Given the theory and prior empirical evidence, we hypothesize that achievement priming will affect both theoretical pathways:

Hypothesis 1a: (Flexibility) Concurrent priming of the concept of achievement will induce individuals to generate ideas in a greater number of categories in the solution space, compared to a control condition designed to have no impact.

Hypothesis 1b: (Perseverance) Concurrent priming of the concept of achievement will induce individuals to generate more ideas within the categories they consider, compared to a control condition designed to have no impact.

There are many ways to assess creative output. The two most common ways are counting the number of non-redundant ideas produced, and an assessment of creativity, which has been conceived as originality, feasibility, relevancy, or some other aspect of creativity [13, 14, 24, 27, 56, 61]. Based on a review of prior research, Dean et al. [24] conclude that the creativity of an idea is comprised of three distinct dimensions: novelty, workability, and relevance. Novelty addresses originality (the degree to which the idea is imaginative) and paradigm (the ability of the idea to change the way a problem is viewed). Workability reflects acceptability (the level to which an idea does not violate known constraints) and implementability (the ease with which the idea solves the problem). Relevance captures applicability (how clearly the idea relates to part of the stated problem) and effectiveness (how well the idea solves all or part of the stated problem). Each of these three dimensions is theoretically distinct, and while there is some correlation among them, they are separate (although workability and relevance are highly correlated and could be collapsed into one dimension) [24].

Researchers have long argued that quantity and creativity are related to each other [56]. The empirical evidence on the effects of priming suggests that priming sometimes affects the number of ideas differently than some measures of idea characteristics, such as originality. Bounded Ideation Theory (BIT) argues that the relationship between quantity and creative quality is a function of the task, individuals, and work process [14]. Small tasks (for which individuals are likely to produce all viable ideas in less than the time available) or very large tasks (where participants work until sleep-deprived) are special cases that set some limit beyond which the two are not related. Omitting these special cases, BIT argues idea quality and creativity is primarily a function of individuals’ ability and understanding of the task. Someone with a perfect ability and understanding would only produce good creative ideas [14]. Someone with less ability or understanding would produce a mix of good and bad ideas [14].

BIT argues that the number of creative ideas depends upon flexibility and perseverance [14]. In general, each topic area contains creative ideas, and less creative ideas. Some ideas in a topic area are obvious ideas that come readily to mind when the topic is first considered [23, 55]. Thus increasing flexibility should lead to more ideas in a given time period, because these obvious ideas are easy to produce [61]. These ideas are not likely to be novel but they may be quite implementable [23, 55], so whether they are creative ideas may depend on whether the goal is to produce novel ideas or workable ones [24]. Ideas that are good across all dimensions of creativity often lie deep in the topic space and require motivated attention to produce; thus processes that increase perseverance have stronger effects on the number of creative ideas [14].

We theorized above that priming achievement will increase flexibility and persistence (H1). Increased flexibility leads to more ideas, and increased perseverance leads to more novel and workable ideas, so we hypothesize that achievement priming will increase both the number of ideas and the creativity of ideas. The creativity of an idea is the extent to which it is novel, workable, and relevant [24]. Some ideas may be novel but not workable or relevant. Similarly, workable and relevant ideas might not be that novel (or any combination of these). In this study, we consider all three creativity dimensions simultaneously, as the most creative ideas are simultaneously novel, workable, and relevant. Thus:

Hypothesis 2: (Quantity) Concurrent priming of the concept achievement will increase the number of non-redundant ideas generated, compared to a control condition designed to have no impact.

Hypothesis 3: (Creativity) Concurrent priming of the concept achievement will increase the number of creative ideas generated, compared to a control condition designed to have no impact.

## Study 1

## Method

## Participants

The study was a repeated measures experiment in which participants participated in two treatments. Forty-three students (56% male) were recruited from a business core course offered to sophomores and juniors at a US state university. Age ranged from 18 to 23; average of 20.

## Tasks

This study used two 15-minute tasks from prior research [27]. One task was generating ideas to increase tourism within the state, the instructions were: “Please generate as many ideas as you can to increase the number of tourists that visit the state of Indiana. Please consider tourists from inside the state who visit other parts of the state, as well as tourists from other parts of the US and those from other countries.” The other task was generating ideas to reduce pollution, the instructions were: “Please generate as many ideas as you can that will reduce pollution. Please consider ideas to reduce air pollution, water pollution, and ground pollution (e.g., garbage and landfills).” In both tasks the participants were instructed to “Try to generate as many ideas as possible. Do not criticize anyone’s ideas. All ideas are welcome, no matter how silly or far out they seem. Try to build on and improve the ideas of others.”

## Treatments

There were two within-subject treatments: achievement priming and neutral priming. Participants generated ideas using notepad software with a banner on the right side of the screen to display the images (400 x 300 pixels). The achievement priming condition consisted of a set of 30 images designed to prime the concept of “achievement,” while the neutral priming condition consisted of 30 images designed to have no effect (see Appendix A for example images). The banner presented a new image every 30 seconds.

One important consideration is ensuring the two sets of images actually differed in perceptions of achievement. We began by searching a stock photo service for neutral images and images related to four achievement themes: sports win, personal victory, graduation, and award. We derived these themes based on examination of a wide variety of achievement-related images. Through an iterative process, we selected a set of 40 images we believed were related to achievement, and another 40 images that were neutral and intended to have no effect.

We then assessed whether our perceptions of the images matched those of the participant population. Each image was rated by 25 students drawn from the same participant pool (but who were not part of the study). The set of 80 images was presented in random order on a computer screen in the same size and dimensions as the treatment images. After viewing each image for 10 seconds, the students were asked to rate the image on achievement, valence, and arousal using a 7-point scale. We selected 30 images from each set of achievement and neutral images based on mean achievement ratings and low standard deviation (achievement: M = 5.54. SD = .51, neutral: M = 3.92, SD = .60). The image sets significantly differed on achievement (t(58) = 7.87, p < .001), arousal (t $( 5 8 ) = 4 . 7 1 , \mathrm { p } < . 0 0 1 )$ ), and valence $( \mathrm { t } ( 5 8 ) = 3 . 7 5 , \mathrm { p } < . 0 0 1 )$

## Dependent Variables

The dependent variable was idea generation performance, conceptualized in two ways: quantity and creativity. Quantity was the number ideas, defined as the number of ideas that provide a solution regardless of quality, where each idea is counted only once (i.e., duplicate mentions of the same idea are not counted). Ideas were disaggregated if multiple ideas were provided in one statement (e.g., “Reduce the amount of materials used, reuse old materials, and recycle other materials” would be counted as three ideas as long as they were not previously stated by that individual).The number of ideas was counted by one rater who created a master idea list of all ideas. A second rater independently counted the ideas generated by a randomly selected subset of 20% of the participants and matched those ideas to the master idea list. Inter-rater reliability was 95%, calculated as the number of ideas identified by both raters divided by the number of ideas identified by either rater (i.e., number of agreements/total codings). There were a total of 734 pollution ideas and 792 tourism ideas.

The second dependent measure was the number of creative ideas. We used the approach outlined by Dean et al. [24] and conceptualized creativity as a multidimensional construct, consisting of three separate dimensions: novelty, workability, and relevance. Each dimension consisted of two sub-dimensions ranked on a scale from 1 to 4, with higher numbers indicating the idea has more of that attribute. Novelty is comprised of originality and paradigm breaking. The workability dimension has the two sub-dimensions of implementability and acceptability. Relevance consists of the sub-dimensions of applicability and effectiveness. One rater rated each idea on the master list on all six sub-dimensions. A second rater independently rated a random subset of 20% of the ideas. Inter-rater reliability was adequate: Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85.

There are several ways of calculating creativity (e.g., calculating a total creative score, counting the number of creative idea) [61]. We measured creativity as the number of ideas rated as high in creativity [61]. To be considered a creative idea, an idea had to be rated as a 3 or 4 on all six sub-dimensions of the novel, workable and relevant scales. Thus, creative ideas are simultaneously novel, workable, and relevant. About 20.1% of the pollution ideas were classified as creative, as were 14.4% of the tourism ideas.

Flexibility was assessed by examining the number of topic categories within the problem space that the ideas considered [23]. The tourism task had 11 categories (e.g., advertise, laws, attractions, festivals) while the pollution task had ten (e.g., education, laws, incentives, social responsibility). Persistence was the average number of ideas within each category [23].

## Procedure

Participants completed the experimental procedure after providing informed consent. The study was conducted in a 16-seat computer classroom lit by fluorescent lights that provided an individual study carrel with a Windows computer for each participant. The carrels were arranged into two rows of eight against opposite walls. When seated, participants could not see other participants’ computer screens. The four treatment-task combinations were randomly distributed throughout the room at each session. The number of participants at each session ranged from 8 to 16. All tasks and instructions were presented on paper. The computer screens presented the Windows notepad software and the software that presented the images (which did not start until the participant pressed play); the experimenter verified that all participants had pressed play in each session. Participants were randomly assigned to either the neutral or achievement priming condition first, and either the tourism or pollution task first. Participants worked on the first task in the first treatment. Next, they completed a short survey that asked demographic questions and several distractor questions designed to provide time for the initial treatment effects to dissipate. Participants then performed the second task with the second treatment, and completed a second survey. Finally, they were debriefed and released.

If most individuals have a similar conceptual schemas of achievement then we would expect a normal distribution of its effects. If schemas are not similar, then the priming images are likely to influence some individuals, but have no effect on others, which will lead to a bimodal distribution, with one mode for those affected and a second mode for those not affected.

## Discussion

We used a repeated measures design to compare a CSS that used images to deliver concurrent priming of the concept of achievement to one that presented images designed to have no impact. When users used the priming CSS, they generated more ideas and more creative ideas that were novel, workable, and relevant. Priming worked primarily through the flexibility pathway by inducing participants to consider more topic categories.

Priming has been the subject of much debate in psychology [38, 40]. Some studies have found priming effects, while others have not. When some studies have been replicated, the replication studies have sometimes found similar results and sometimes have found different results [38, 40]. Therefore, when studying priming, it behooves researchers to run a second study to replicate their results. Therefore, we conducted a second study.

## Study 2

Prior priming research has used a control treatment that matches the priming treatment in structure [5, 7]. Both the control treatment and priming treatment present the user with pre-task stimuli (e.g., images, or a word game) but in the control treatment, the content of the stimuli is designed to be a placebo; it is a set of words, images, and so forth, designed to have no effect. This enables the researcher to be sure that the priming effects are not from the pre-task stimuli such as a game, but rather from the priming content. If no pre-task stimuli were used, then we could not rule out the alternative explanation that playing a game—any game—prior to the task produced the results. Thus, prior priming research has used a placebo treatment as the control treatment.

The control treatment in Study 1 included such a placebo treatment by presenting neutral images designed to have no effect. This enabled us to rule out the alternative explanation that providing images—not the specific priming images—is what caused the differences.

However, the use of concurrent priming adds an additional issue. Because the priming stimuli are delivered at the same time as the idea generation task, they have the potential to distract. Any stimuli that appear in the visual field will automatically draw the user’s attention because our eyes are drawn to movement or changes in the visual field and this may trigger our conscious attention [37]. The stimuli in Study 1 were designed to be of neutral valence and non-arousing, but it is possible they drew the users’ conscious attention. Stimuli that distract attention can reduce performance [74]. Thus, one could argue that an alternative control treatment would be no images of any kind that would mimic unaided idea generation. Therefore, a control treatment without images has ecological validity.

In Study 1, a placebo condition with neutral priming images was used, as is traditional in priming research, but this does not enable us to determine how the priming CSS compares to unaided idea generation. Therefore, in Study 2, we chose to use two different control treatments. One was the same placebo control treatment of neutral images used in Study 1. The other was what we call an “ecological control” condition of unaided idea generation.

## Design

The hypotheses were the same as Study 1. The design was identical to Study 1 (same tasks, measures, procedures, and analyses) but an additional ecological control (i.e., no priming) treatment was added. One control treatment (which we call the placebo control treatment) was identical to Study 1. The other control treatment (which we call the ecological control treatment) was unaided idea generation; this treatment removed the images; as a result, participants had only a text window in which to type ideas.

The participants were 225 students (54% male) recruited from a business core course offered to sophomores and juniors at a US state university. There was no overlap in participants between Study 1 and Study 2.

Study 2 was conducted in a 48-seat computer classroom lit by fluorescent lights that provided an individual study carrel with a Windows computer for each participant. The carrels were arranged into six rows of eight carrels. When seated, participants could not see other participants’ computer screens. The four treatmenttask combinations were randomly distributed throughout the room at each session. The number of participants at each session ranged from 10 to 48. All tasks and instructions were presented on the computer screen using a web interface (using Google chrome). Participants clicked an on-screen button to advance from step to step within the software. A timer was used on each task segment to prevent participants from leaving the idea generation task prior to the time allotted.

## Discussion

The use of CSS priming prompted individuals to generate more ideas and more creative ideas. The results were the same regardless of the use of a placebo control (neutral images) or an ecological control (no images). Priming worked primarily through the flexibility pathway by increasing the number of categories considered, rather than the persistence pathway.

The pattern of results in Study 2 matches those in Study 1; the replication study produced the same results. One difference from Study 1 was our use of two different control treatments, a placebo priming treatment that matches the control treatments in prior theoretical research and an ecological control treatment that matches unaided idea generation common in practice. We found no meaningful differences due to the use of one or the other control treatment.

## General Discussion

We used two studies to examine the impacts of a CSS using concurrent non-taskrelated supraliminal image priming on idea generation. This form of priming places images not directly related to the task in the visual field of the user during the idea generation task. The images are essentially visual background music as the user generates ideas. The users are aware of the images, but not necessarily their purpose and do not need to focus attention on them.

Both studies found that individuals generated more ideas, and more creative ideas (ideas simultaneously novel, workable, and relevant) when exposed to images designed to prime the concepts of achievement concurrently with the idea generation task as compared to a placebo or ecological control treatment, when used in this manner.

The results across both studies show that the primary theoretical pathway through which this form of priming acts is the flexibility pathway rather than the persistence pathway, when used in this manner. The priming stimuli induced participants broaden their thinking about the task and consider significantly more categories than the control treatment but had no effects on the number of ideas per category. These findings are the first to identify the theoretical pathway through which achievement priming acts. The priming effects were mostly normally distributed throughout our participant population, counter to our expectations that the priming stimuli might affect some participants but not others (which would lead to a bimodal distribution).

We draw three conclusions from these results. First, achievement priming, delivered concurrently via images as an individual performs an idea generation task in this manner, induces individuals to produce more ideas and more creative ideas. The priming effect works through the flexibility theoretical pathway, rather than the persistence theoretical pathway. Specifically, achievement priming broadens individuals’ thinking about the solution space, triggering them to become more flexible in the range of ideas they generate. Interestingly, our study found no evidence that achievement priming affected the persistence pathway for idea generation tasks, indicating that it does not induce individuals to generate ideas more deeply within their selected parts of the solution space. The concept of achievement is task independent, unlike many CSS stimuli providers, so this approach can be applied to many different tasks.

Second, concurrent achievement priming works at the individual level in an individual idea generation setting. In prior research that involved teams, achievement priming was shown to work at the team level [27]. However, that study was unable to determine whether the priming effect was due to the nature of the team interaction or based on each individual generating more ideas. Our study shows that achievement priming works at the individual level increasing both the quantity of ideas and the number of creative ideas.

Third, the fact that this approach is concurrent (unlike traditional a priori priming) suggests it can be a useful complement to existing CSS. Achievement priming can be embedded within CSS to increase creative generation for individuals. The achievement prime is not context-dependent (i.e., it does not need to be altered based on the specific topic of the idea generation task), nor does it rely on altering the process of the individual during idea generation consciously. Rather, this study shows that CSS can be designed with achievement priming to modestly affect idea generation productivity without requiring alterations based on the nature of the task or drastically changing an individual’s process of idea generation.

## Limitations

This research suffers from the usual limitations of experimental research. We studied undergraduate participants working on short, artificial tasks. Student subjects are considered appropriate for testing theories applicable to the general population [19, 42], as our theory is intended to be. Age and socioeconomic-status are generally considered to be the key differences between students and organizational populations [19]; here, neither is theorized to interact with the experimental conditions. Undergraduate students are also representative of future employees, since they will join organizations within a span of 2-3 years [19]. Our tasks were only 15 minutes in duration, which is typical of laboratory studies.

Likewise, we did not test a fully developed prototype software of our proposed CSS approach. Much like the original studies at the University of Arizona that investigated electronic brainstorming, our goal was to see if there was “something here” before designers invest time and effort in a proper prototype or commercial system. The “CSS” we used in Study 1 was two different commercial packages presented in separate windows. The “CSS” we used in Study 2 was a purpose-built Web-based system coded using a scripting language.

Despite these limitations, we believe that this study presents insights for CSS research. We found that a relatively simple manipulation produced significant effects on ideation performance that can be readily incorporated into existing CSS. We believe that these results have important implications for both research and practice, pending additional work in the field.

## Implications for Research

First and foremost, our results show that concurrent supraliminal priming using achievement images improves individual idea generation performance when used in this manner. This complements prior research that found group idea performance was improved when group members were exposed to a priori priming of the concept of achievement via a word game [27]. Our research extends this research by showing that priming delivered concurrently via images improves individual idea generation in much the same way that an 8-minute a priori priming session with a word game improves group idea generation. We believe our results call for more research on concurrent priming in individual idea generation.

Second, our results show that the concurrent priming of achievement in this manner acts primarily through the flexibility pathway. This theoretical pathway leads to more ideas and more creative ideas when individuals broaden their solution space, rather than delving deeper within a specific category. This finding is important because it suggests that this approach works in a manner similar to CSS stimuli providers and many process guides that strive to trigger divergent thinking [2, 50, 51]. Thus, situations that are amendable to these forms of CSS should also be amenable to concurrent priming with achievement because they share similar theoretical foundations. There also may be specific idea generation tasks wherein a broad and flexible range of ideas are needed, and other tasks wherein many ideas must be generated in a specific category (i.e., perseverance). Priming with achievement may ideally suited for some tasks. Conversely, if idea generation tasks that require increased perseverance rather than flexibility, then a different priming concept may be required to produce increased perseverance.

Third, it is difficult to classify this approach as being a CSS stimuli provider or process guide. Structurally, it is similar to CSS stimuli providers in that it provides images and works via the flexibility pathway. However, CSS stimuli providers often use stimuli that are task-specific and designed to activate taskrelevant associations in long-term memory than can be brought into short-term memory and used as the building blocks of ideas. In contrast, our approach works by using the concept of achievement to change the expectancy of success, and thus acts in a non-task specific manner. We believe that it is more useful to think of this approach as suggesting the development of a new class of CSS called priming stimuli providers.

Such a classification immediately prompts the question of what other priming concepts could be employed. Achievement is a fairly robust concept, having effects on a number of behaviors [7], but there are numerous other concepts. For example, a priori priming of the concepts of “mindfulness,” “attentiveness,” and “rudeness” have been found to affect behavior [6]. Would priming the concept of “creativity” lead to more ideas, or more ideas that are highly creative? Could a different prime work through the persistence pathway instead of the flexibility pathway, thereby being more appropriate when the idea generation task requires delving deeply within a category? Would priming concepts related to the idea generation task improve performance (e.g., for the tourism task, water sports, Web advertising, low costs)? Since most current CSS work through the flexibility pathway, priming concepts designed to increase persistence may have stronger effects when combined with current commercial CSS. We need more research to better understand how different priming concepts affect idea generation.

Fourth, the effects of priming could be bimodal, affecting some individuals but not others, because of the underlying differences in their long term memories [17, 28, 70]. However, our post-hoc analysis found no such bimodal effects. We believe this calls for more research on concurrent supraliminal priming and its effects on individual idea generation. Were the long-term memories and conceptual schemas of the participants in our study relatively consistent with regard to the concept of achievement and the images we used to prime it? This study was conducted on second and third year undergraduates in a business school at a large US state university, so their conceptual schema related to achievement could be similar and, thus normally distributed. Most of the participants were US citizens, but 30% were international. Perhaps a bimodal priming effect would be observed in a more diverse population, or perhaps memories and conceptual schema related to achievement are somewhat consistent across different cultures at this age. Future research should examine individuals with different backgrounds and goals and probe more deeply into their effects on priming for idea generation.

Fifth, we used images as the concurrent supraliminal priming stimuli. Would other forms of stimuli be effective? Priming has often been done with words, but words require cognitive processing and thus have the potential to interfere with task performance when presented at the same time [74]. However, we know of no research that has investigated this. We need research to understand whether concurrent word primes cause sufficient interference to reduce performance. There are other sensory modalities that might cause less interference, such as sound or smell [70]. Would these alternative delivery methods have the same effects?

Finally, these implications may extend beyond the CSS realm. Information systems are generally easy to reconfigure, so it is possible to incorporating concurrent priming into many different information systems. For example, could we prime individuals to be more mindful of information security practices? Or prime them to be more satisfied with a system or to be more likely to buy products on an ecommerce Web site? Our results suggest that concurrent priming may be added to any of these scenarios as there is nothing limiting concurrent priming to idea generation tasks. Future research should examine concurrent priming in other information systems areas to see where priming can improve productivity, safety, or satisfaction.

## Implications for Practice

This study has three implications for practice. First, the finding that concurrent priming of achievement improves idea generation suggests that designers of CSS should consider incorporating concurrent achievement priming into their software. Unlike the stimuli used in other types of CSS stimuli providers, the concept of achievement is task independent. There is no need to reconfigure a CSS using achievement priming for different tasks. We implemented concurrent priming by displaying the priming images in an unobtrusive window beside the window for entering ideas.

Existing CSS, whether stimuli providers or process guides, could easily implement this design by adding a banner at the top or side of the screen that presents achievement-oriented images. We used four categories of achievement: (1) winning in sports, (2) personal victory, (3) graduation, and (4) awards. These four categories provide a good starting for selecting images to include, but are not exhaustive. The CSS could be designed to present a banner that has a set of semi-randomized pictures drawn from these four categories or other categories related to achievement. These images should change about every 15 seconds throughout the brainstorming session and switch among different categories related to achievement (i.e., awards, personal victory, etc.) so that two images from the same category do not appear back to back. Software developers could also test other images related to achievement to create a CSS that improves brainstorming performance and increases creative generation for the organization.

Second, even before CSS vendors add this to their software, it would be easy for users to implement this by opening a new window on their screen (or second screen, as many individuals use dual screen computer configurations) and using it to present a slideshow of achievement-oriented images as they work. This also suggests that individuals wishing to be more creative could change the background images on their screens to display achievement images.

Finally, organizations could adopt more creative images as background images for their standard desktop builds. This would be unobtrusive to most employees, but could provide significant benefits in enhanced creativity in all activities employees perform.

## Conclusion

For decades, information systems research has examined ways to improve creativity with technology. Creativity Support Systems typically support the creative process by providing information and/or a structured process to help the user take a different perspective on the task [34]. Users are guided to change the way they think about the problem. Many commercial CSS are available [2].

Our research suggests a different approach to CSS design that can significantly improve idea generation: a pattern of images displayed as users work. This visual background music requires no direct attention by the users and no changes to way users’ work. There is no new process to learn. Instead, the images play quietly in the background of users’ cognition, subtly influencing them to be more creative.
