# PRIVACY CONCERNS AND DATA SHARING IN THE INTERNET OF THINGS: MIXED METHODS EVIDENCE FROM CONNECTED CARS<sup>1</sup>

Patrick Cichy Bern University of Applied Sciences, Institute for Applied Data Science and Finance, Bern, SWITZERLAND {patrick.cichy@bfh.ch}

Torsten Oliver Salge RWTH Aachen University, TIME Research Area, Institute for Technology and Innovation Management, Aachen, GERMANY {salge@time.rwth-aachen.de}

Rajiv Kohli Raymond A. Mason School of Business, William & Mary, Williamsburg, VA, U.S.A. {rajiv.kohli@mason.wm.edu}

The Internet of Things (IoT) is increasingly transforming the way we work, live, and travel. IoT devices collect, store, analyze, and act upon a continuous stream of data as a by-product of everyday use. However, IoT devices need unrestricted data access to fully function. As such, they invade users’ virtual and physical space and raise far-reaching privacy challenges that are unlike those examined in other contexts. As advanced IoT devices, connected cars offer a unique setting to review and extend established theory and evidence on privacy and data sharing. Employing a sequential mixed methods design, we conducted an interview study (n=120), a survey study (n=333), and a field experiment (n=324) among car drivers to develop and validate a contextualized model of individuals’ data sharing decisions. Our findings from the three studies highlight the interplay between virtual and physical risks in shaping drivers’ privacy concerns and data sharing decisions—with information privacy and data security emerging as discrete yet closely interrelated concepts. Our findings also highlight the importance of psychological ownership, conceptualized as drivers’ feelings of possession toward their driving data, as an important addition to established privacy calculus models of data sharing. This novel perspective explains why individuals are reluctant to share even low-sensitivity data that do not raise privacy concerns. The psychological ownership perspective has implications for designing incentives for data-enabled services in ways that augment drivers’ self-efficacy and psychological ownership and thereby encourage them to share driving data. These insights help reconcile a fundamental tension among IoT users—how to avail the benefits of dataenabled IoT devices while reducing the psychological costs associated with the sharing of personal data.

Keywords: Privacy, privacy concerns, cybersecurity, psychological ownership, data sharing, data disclosure, Internet of Things, IoT, connected car, smart products

## Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT) is increasingly pervading and transforming the way we work, live, and travel. Consumer IoT is reshaping fields as diverse as fitness, housekeeping, home automation and security, and personal mobility. IoT devices such as fitness trackers, robot vacuum cleaners, smart meters, smart cameras, and connected cars have become constant companions that promise automation, personalization, and comfort for everyday activities (Beverungen et al. 2019; Raff et al. 2020).

This value proposition, however, relies on IoT devices collecting, storing, analyzing, and acting upon a continuous stream of data generated by increasingly manifold and powerful sensors. As a result, data streams are collected as an inevitable by-product of IoT devices’ everyday usage—even though not all users are fully aware of the actual scale and scope of such practices. In fact, IoT devices can capture important information about the device itself, its user, proximal or distant others, as well as the local context. When analyzed jointly, IoT data can reveal individuals’ behavior, preferences, and personality with potentially negative consequences for them. Data from connected cars, for instance, can provide insights into drivers’ attention, risktaking behavior, and mobility patterns—all of which can be of considerable interest for actors in the broader connected car ecosystem including manufacturers, insurers, and regulators. Hence, data privacy and security concerns are likely to impact users’ IoT adoption and data sharing decisions. As such, IoT devices pose a novel, almost paradoxical challenge to users: The core value proposition of IoT devices is inexorably linked to the unrestricted sharing of user data. Yet sharing of that data in the absence of adequate privacy controls exposes the user to substantial virtual and physical risks. Extant privacy studies conducted in contexts as diverse as online shopping (e.g., Dinev and Hart 2006), location-based services (e.g., Crossler and Bélanger, 2019), electronic health records (e.g., Anderson and Agarwal 2011), and online social networks (e.g., Krasnova et al. 2010) consistently point to the importance of privacy concerns in shaping individual data sharing decisions and highlight the role of data sensitivity, trusting beliefs, and personalization benefits. However, none of these previous studies have explored privacy concerns and data sharing in the emerging IoT context with its unique privacy challenges.

But is the emerging IoT context sufficiently distinct to raise new privacy challenges, limit the applicability of established theory and evidence, and warrant dedicated research attention? Four key properties of IoT set it apart from the previously examined contexts. IoT devices (1) tend to be “always on” and generate continuous data streams, (2) give users little or no power to control the data flows, (3) require unrestricted data access to fully function, and (4) invade users’ virtual and physical space given the increasingly powerful actuators—components that transform electric impulses into physical actions—they are equipped with. These four properties alter the very nature of both the privacy risks that IoT users are exposed to and those users’ decision-making with regard to data sharing.

In response, we adopt a privacy perspective relating to data sharing to explore the rapidly expanding context of consumer IoT in general and connected cars in particular. Employing a sequential mixed methods design (Venkatesh et al. 2013; 2016), we dive deep into car drivers’ privacy assessments and their data sharing decisions. We seek to (1) uncover the key determinants of individuals’ data sharing decisions with a focus on privacy concerns and psychological ownership, that is, feelings of ownership toward their driving data; (2) explicate the interplay of those determinants in the presence and absence of data-enabled services as incentives; and (3) explore how to incentivize IoT users to share data for service innovation.

The connected car is an ideal IoT exemplar for our purposes, as it exhibits high levels of all four distinguishing properties of IoT devices. Car manufacturers and other players in the growing connected car ecosystem (e.g., insurers, car rental companies, car repair shops, and providers of navigation and infotainment services) have recognized car data as an essential source of future value creation and service innovation (McKinsey, 2016)—and are jointly transforming the connected car into a “rolling self-surveillance machine” (The Washington Post, 2020). The connected car is not only an important technological stepping stone toward autonomous driving, but also a window into a possible future of consumer IoT more broadly. It provides a unique opportunity to engage in contextualized theorizing (Hong et al. 2014) and to review and extend established theory and evidence on privacy and data sharing in light of the emerging realities of the IoT. After all, in the history of privacy research, technological advances have played a vital role in pushing the boundaries of and stimulating theory development (Smith et al. 2011).

Given the contextual novelty of our research, we adopted a sequential mixed methods design to guide the interplay of our studies (Venkatesh et al. 2013). Below, we describe three consecutive studies—arranged in a way so that the findings of one study can inform the next—which allowed us to develop and validate a model of individuals’ data sharing decisions. First, short, semistructured interviews (Study 1) provided rich insights into drivers’ data sharing decisions and helped identify salient constructs. Second, our survey (Study 2) formally tested and supported drivers’ privacy concerns as mediating the relationship between psychological ownership of their driving data and their data sharing intentions. Third, our field study (Study 3) employed a plug-in telematics device and associated app to turn ordinary cars into connected cars in order to move from drivers’ intentions to their actual data disclosure decisions and associated contingency factors (relational trust, security beliefs, data sensitivity, and incentive design).

## Conceptual Background

## Privacy and Individuals’ Data Sharing Decisions

Privacy is commonly defined as the ability to control information about oneself (Stone et al. 1983).<sup>2</sup> Empirical studies seeking to understand individuals’ decisions about the maintenance or loss of this ability have traditionally relied on the privacy calculus as the dominant analytical framework (e.g., Jiang et al. 2013; Xu et al. 2009). This perspective considers opposing forces that jointly influence individuals in their decisions regarding whether to reveal or to conceal personal information (Culnan 1993). Individuals are assumed to weigh the positive and negative consequences of any particular disclosure of information—i.e., to perform a costbenefit analysis (Culnan 1993; Dinev and Hart 2006) and to act in a way that will result in the most favorable net outcome (Stone et al. 1983). This rationale is in line with theories of human decision-making, such as utility maximization theory (Awad and Krishnan 2006), expectancy theory of motivation (Stone and Stone 1990) and expectancy-value theory (Ajzen 1991), which serve as a psychological foundation of the privacy calculus (Li 2012).

While the perceived benefits of information sharing might take the form of financial rewards (Hui et al. 2007) or personalization (Awad and Krishnan 2006), the perceived costs are assumed to originate first and foremost from the endangered privacy of individuals (i.e., the partial loss of the ability to control personal information), manifested in their privacy concerns (Malhotra et al. 2004; Smith et al. 1996). Privacy concerns, viewed as a measurable proxy for privacy itself, constitute the central construct in empirical studies on privacy-related behavior. They have been identified as the most influential determinant of individuals’ willingness to share personal information (Malhotra et al. 2004; Stewart and Segars 2002). In line with the privacy calculus perspective on sharing decisions, Dinev and Hart (2006) observed that in most cases, individuals do not strive to retain absolute privacy, but are willing to partially sacrifice it for certain benefits that arise from data sharing.

Privacy is said to be a highly context-sensitive concept (Altman 1975; Margulis 2003; Westin 1967), meaning that the perceived costs and benefits individuals have to trade off against each other can differ significantly across situations. With this in mind, scholars have examined how privacyrelated behavior interacts with contextual factors, such as what type of information is being requested, who is requesting it, and what it is to be used for (e.g., Anderson and Agarwal 2010; Malhotra et al. 2004). With the rise of e-commerce, for example, consumers have been asked to share their names and addresses with online vendors (Dinev and Hart 2006). In addition, they have had to come to terms with the fact that their search and buying patterns are tracked by online vendors who increasingly personalize websites to their customers’ individual product interests (Xiao and Benbasat 2007). Similarly, the digitization of medical data promised to enhance the quality and efficiency of care (Agarwal et al. 2010) yet continues to rely on patients’ willingness to share their personal health data with healthcare providers, pharmaceutical companies, and public health agencies (Anderson and Agarwal 2011). As far as the Internet of Things is concerned, Sheng et al. (2008) were among the first to shape our understanding of privacy implications associated with the vision of ubiquitous computing. They predicted privacy concerns to emerge because enabling technologies like RFID and sensor networks collect data in a continuous fashion and often without individuals’ active consent (e.g., in case of RFID-enabled carts in supermarkets that continuously track and report customers’ location). Interestingly, such tracking is commonplace for many of today’s IoT devices, which creates new opportunities and challenges for research on users’ information privacy (Lowry et al. 2017).

In retrospect, it becomes apparent that technological innovation is one of the main triggers for new privacy studies. Novel technologies have often stimulated privacy research and catalyzed theory development. The privacy implications of IoT devices and the vision of a fully connected, smart world they are associated with, have, however, not yet been adequately examined (see Yun et al. 2018). Given the rapid diffusion of IoT devices across many areas of life, ranging from fitness to housing to mobility, there is now a pressing need for research on privacy and data sharing in the context of the IoT (Lowry et al. 2017).

## Privacy in the IoT Context

The increasingly ubiquitous and expanding class of consumer IoT possesses several distinct properties with important privacy implications. First, IoT devices have previously unseen abilities to sense their environment (e.g., the shape, position, and movement of nearby objects, as well as contextual factors such as air temperature and air quality) and their inner workings (e.g., mechanical wear and tear and usage patterns). Depending on the number and types of sensors they are equipped with, data collection by IoT devices is characterized not only by its volume but also by its specificity and continuity. Indeed, most IoT devices, such as wearables, smart home appliances, and connected cars, collect a continuous stream of data when in use (Oberländer et al. 2018). The decisive factor that determines their level of intrusion into individuals’ informational space is determined by the range and activity of sensors embedded in a particular IoT device, which clearly differ across the various fields of consumer IoT, as illustrated in Table 1. Equipped with probably the most comprehensive sensors in consumer IoT, a connected car captures more diverse and more revealing information about its driver than, for example, a smart running shoe does about its wearer. The most valuable insights about individuals often emerge not from single points of data but from continuous streams over time (Shim et al. 2020). Acceleration and braking data, for example, provide insights into individuals’ general driving style, their mood, and their attitude toward the environment. The frequency of refueling and of tire pressure adjustments, in turn, may serve as indicators of drivers’ general safety needs. Recurring patterns in the routes traveled reveal not only frequently visited places, but also how much time the driver spent there. It might be possible to infer from personal driving data where a person works, what restaurants are preferred, or what leisure activities the driver engages in, with the accuracy of such inferences increasing with the amount of available data (De Montjoye et al. 2015).

Second, users of IoT devices are often left in the dark as to how precisely their data is being collected, integrated, and analyzed. This characteristic is observable across all fields of consumer IoT and may be partly due to the fact that IoT devices—in contrast to personal computers—are equipped with tiny screens or else entirely lack components that would allow them to display rich information. Users instead must rely on a second device, such as a smartphone, or a smart speaker, to interact with the IoT device. Adapted to these circumstances, IoT devices’ user interfaces tend to offer only limited information and control options regarding personal data flows (Bahirat et al. 2018). Moreover, associated software components, for example a smartphone app that enables the steering and monitoring of the IoT device, often cannot be replaced or supplemented with more privacy-friendly versions. While users have access to various alternatives for software running on their generalpurpose computing machines and can select the software that best meets their privacy preferences (e.g., various messenger and navigation apps for smartphones), IoT devices are often one-purpose computing machines and are, in most cases, inherently linked to the firmware they are shipped with. IoT device users hence often have to live with whatever level of transparency and control over personal data is granted by the manufacturer.

Third, the value proposition of IoT devices is inherently connected to the unconstrained collection, integration, and analysis of data. A robot vacuum cleaner, for example, would simply be unable to operate without a camera and computer vision algorithms that allow it to find its way around furniture and other obstacles. Without creating realtime indoor maps, it would be unable to document the cleaning process and outcome. Similarly, the core functionalities and services of connected cars, such as remote car control, predictive car maintenance, driving style assistants, and pay-how-you-drive insurance schemes are reliant on unrestricted access to car data (Seiberth and Gruendinger 2018). This inherent reliance on car data is likely to sharply increase over the next decade, during which time vast amounts of driving data will be needed to train and validate autonomous driving algorithms (Eady 2019). For consumer IoT in general, the functionality available to the user sharply degrades in the presence of data constraints. In other words, sharing personal data is a necessary precondition for customer value creation in consumer IoT.

Finally, IoT devices and their increasingly powerful actuators<sup>3</sup> go beyond previous technologies in that they invade not only users’ virtual space, but also their physical space. This adds a new dimension to the consequences of privacy-related decisions that users of IoT devices need to include in their calculus (Dinev and Hart 2006). Outside the IoT context, privacy risks stem primarily from unsolicited outflows of user information in combination with the exploitation of that information in undesired ways. Examples include individuals online purchase histories, which are sold to third-party marketers, social media profiles that are considered during the selection of job applicants, and personal passwords that end up in the hands of cybercriminals. Within the IoT context, in contrast, privacy risks result not only from unsolicited outflows but also from unsolicited inflows of data. Consider how faulty signals from sensors or the insertion of malicious data from the outside may induce general malfunctioning or even threatening behaviors from an IoT device. The severity of such threats relates primarily to the IoT device’s physical impact (i.e., its ability to manipulate the environment and the amount of mass it can set in motion) and its physical proximity (i.e., the extent to which it can invade the physical space of an individual or open it up for intrusion by others).

As illustrated in Table 1, the connected car ranks high in both of these categories and is hence associated with the most severe data-induced threats to individuals’ physical safety of all consumer IoT technologies present today. In summary, the IoT radically changes the nature of the privacy threats that users are exposed to because of several factors: (1) the unprecedented volume, specificity, and continuity of data streams collected by IoT devices—with many IoT devices being “always on”; (2) the opacity of data collection, integration, and analysis, coupled with the fact that the user has little or no power to control data flows; (3) the degradation of functionality in the presence of data constraints; and (4) the ability of IoT devices to invade users’ virtual and physical spaces. Factors (2) and (3) are mostly manufacturer-specific, irrespective of the particular type of consumer IoT. For example, among electronics manufacturers, Apple incorporates more privacy-friendly defaults and more granular privacy controls than Amazon does regarding products of the same type, e.g., smart speakers (Kelly 2019). Factors (1) and (4), in contrast, are specific to the type of consumer IoT. This makes it possible to arrange IoT devices like smart shoes, smart fridges, and connected cars on a continuum from low to high according to their ability to sense and to (physically) act (see Table 1).

Once again, discontinuous technological developments are set to challenge and extend existing theory on privacy and data sharing decisions. To help privacy research engage with the emerging realities of the IoT, we explore the specific context of connected cars and the specific data types that these novel IT artifacts collect. This is an ideal context for our purposes, given the presence in connected cars of all four distinguishing properties of IoT devices, which will be compounded as we advance toward the vision of autonomous driving.

## Mixed Methods Research Design

Mixed methods research generates rich insights by combining qualitative and quantitative methods in the same inquiry. Mixed methods allow for deeper and more robust analyses of a phenomenon by leveraging the complementary strengths of both approaches (Johnson and Onwuegbuzie 2004). While contextsensitive research is depicted as a promising endeavor to advance theory in general (Hong et al. 2014; Johns 2006), Venkatesh et al. (2013) suggest that mixed methods research is particularly well-suited to examine IS-related phenomena that closely interact with context, i.e., with the IT artifact. This is especially true when existing findings are fragmented, inconclusive, or equivocal (Venkatesh et al. 2016). Mixed methods research is not only frequently called for (Ågerfalk 2013; Venkatesh et al. 2013) but also increasingly adopted in research practice—with IS taking a leading role (e.g., Srivastava and Chandra 2018; Wunderlich et al. 2019).

## Figure 1. Mixed Methods Approach and Research Questions

The execution and interplay of qualitative and quantitative research in mixed methods designs can be manifold and should be guided by the objectives of the particular research endeavor. The overall objective of our work was to derive a holistic understanding of individuals’ data sharing decisions in IoT contexts, as exemplified by the case of connected cars. Toward this goal, and following the methodological guidelines put forward by Venkatesh et al. (2013, 2016), we conducted three consecutive studies such that the findings of one study could inform the next. While an inductive qualitative interview study (Study 1) helped contextualize our theorizing, two subsequent deductive quantitative studies— one a survey (Study 2) and the other a field study (Study 3)— provided validation for our emerging contextualized model. This is in line with Venkatesh et al.’s (2013, p. 25) proposal that “interviews, a qualitative data collection approach, can provide depth in a research inquiry by allowing researchers to gain deep insights from rich narratives, and surveys, a quantitative data collection approach, can bring breadth to a study by helping researchers to gather data about different aspects of a phenomenon from many participants.” The overall purpose of our sequential mixed methods research design could be depicted as being primarily developmental with an element of confirmation and completeness according to Venkatesh et al.’s classification. To further enhance the richness and practical relevance of our work, we followed the guidelines for context-specific theorizing put forward by Hong et al. (2014) in executing our quantitative studies, which included, among other things, formulating context-sensitive versions of our measurement instruments. ## Study 1: Data Sharing in the Context of Connected Cars Methods

Design: In Study 1, we explored the determinants that shape individuals’ sharing decisions in the context of connected cars. In particular, our objective was to discover the origins, manifestations, and consequences of privacy concerns in this specific IoT context and to identify additional factors that influence individuals in their decision to share driving data with third parties. Given the novelty of connected cars and the unique privacy challenges associated with them, we employed an inductive qualitative approach using interviews (Charmaz 2006).

Procedure: We recruited a sample of 120 car drivers in Germany for our interview study (age: M = 29.2 years, SD = 13.8; gender: 47% female; higher education = 34%; active use of online social networks = 68%). We ensured that participants represented a broad range of personal mobility habits and driving experiences. On average, recruited participants covered a distance of 10,438 km/year (SD = 9,430; Min = 800; Max = 55,000) and 28% of them had done so with a car that they said they did not share with any other person. Following Flick’s (2014) guidelines, we used short briefings as introductions to the interview. These were meant to provide participants with information on connected cars, including indications of their potential to collect, process, and transmit driving data to enable a broad array of novel services without framing this in either a positive or a negative way. The introduction ensured that participants had a basic understanding of the technologies and applications of the connected car. Brief, semistructured interviews (length: M = 5.4 minutes, SD = 1.9) allowed participants to elaborate on their beliefs about and associations with the connected car, as well as their reasons for or against sharing driving data. At the end of the interview, study participants completed a short questionnaire on their personal characteristics and their mobility habits. All interviews were conducted in person, digitally recorded, fully transcribed, and analyzed for content. The transcribed interview material amounted to a total of 31,946 words or 114 typewritten pages.

Analysis: Given our goal to arrive at a better understanding of privacy concerns and data sharing decisions in the emerging IoT context of connected cars, we employed an inductive approach in interpreting our interview material, similar to extant qualitative studies in privacy research (e.g., Karwatzki et al. 2017a; Krasnova et al. 2009, Parks et al. 2017). In the first round, we used open coding to label the emotional reactions, associations, and opinions that the participants expressed. The first author and an experienced research assistant did so independently, using both in vivo codes and provisional category names (Charmaz 2006). In an iterative process, we then refined, merged, and extended initially assigned codes into conceptual categories. This step was characterized by moving back and forth between data, codes, and relevant literature, and was accompanied by ongoing discussions among the three authors (Charmaz 2006). The coding procedure was supported by ATLAS.ti software. As an additional validation step, and to derive reliable quantitative statements regarding the perception of negative consequences of sharing driving data, a previously uninvolved research assistant reanalyzed this particular category of codes, using a coding scheme and having no knowledge of the previously assigned labels (Miles and Huberman 1994). Results were compared and differences in the assigned codes were resolved by discussion among the authors.

## Findings and Discussion

Privacy concerns and negative consequences associated with individuals sharing driving data: We observed that interviewees viewed the adoption of connected cars and the subsequent sharing of driving data with other parties as a potential threat to their privacy. 41% of them articulated concerns over the loss of their privacy, with reactions ranging from a slight sense of discomfort to serious fears. Answers indicated that sharing of personal driving data with the car manufacturer and other service providers creates a sense of losing the ability to control such data. One participant said: “There is no way for me to verify what my car manufacturer really does with this data. I don’t know if they keep their promises.” Another participant commented: “Let’s say I transmit the data to my car manufacturer, for example. In this case, I actually do not really know what will happen to it. I can’t control it anymore. This somehow scares me.” Participants came up with various negative consequences originating from a loss of their ability to control personal driving data. Table 2 lists these negative consequences in the order of their prevalence in the interview material.

All negative consequences revealed during our interviews can be assigned to one or more of the privacy concern dimensions proposed by Smith et al. (1996). While the basic dimensionality of individuals’ privacy concerns appears to hold across technological contexts, our results underline the need for contextualizing the specific content of these concerns. First, similar to previous findings on individuals fears when providing access to personal information (Karwatzki et al. 2017a), data sharing in connected cars is associated with the risk of financial harm (e.g., Items 2, 5, 10, and 14 in Table 2), social harm (e.g., Items 6, 12, and 15 in Table 2), and psychological harm (e.g., Items 6, 11, and 13 in Table 2) to the sharing individual (Moon 2000). However, the manifestation of data-induced threats to users’ physical safety (e.g., Items 3 and 7 in Table 2) distinguishes the IoT context of connected cars from other areas in which privacy is a concern.

Personal driving data might reveal not only where individuals live and work, but also their daily schedules and routines. One participant stated, “The problem with the collection of driving data is, really, that they see that from Monday until Friday I leave home at 7 a.m. and return at 6 p.m. And there might be someone who takes advantage of the fact that nobody is at home … and robs your house.” He went on, “They can exactly see where I am and when I am coming back … [and] they know if I’m in the car with buddies from football or if my child is with me.” The presence of a child can indeed be detected by the car through sensors of the seat belt reminder system—a standard feature in modern cars. As noted above, in the context of connected cars and IoT devices in general, it is usually not the individual data point that reveals potentially sensitive information about users and their habits, but the inferences made based on recurring patterns in data from a variety of sources and from multiple points in time.

The individual user typically has little choice in regulating the content or the quantity of data streaming from the connected car, unless the user chooses to discontinue connectivity services altogether, accepting a drastic degradation in functionality. For instance, only through sharing wear-andtear data with the workshop can drivers benefit from predictive maintenance services.

Similarly, only through sharing data on driving style with a car insurance company can drivers obtain discounts for safe driving. Because of this fact, some participants seem to relate the adoption of connected cars to the inevitable sharing of driving data with other parties, for example, “I would never get into one of those [connected] cars…. My data belongs to me.” This seems understandable, as privacy-enhancing strategies, like those observed in individuals’ responses to data requests on the web (e.g., intentionally entering false information; see Son and Kim 2008), are not transferable to the context of connected cars and other IoT devices. The inevitability of data collection and the lack of control over data flows make drivers of connected cars feel they are being observed and exposed (see Item 6 in Table 2).

Study participants also imagined direct threats to their vehicles and to their physical safety when driving a connected car (Item 7 in Table 2). One participant mentioned that he was concerned that the manipulation of driving data might cause the engine to overheat. Another stated that she was afraid that someone could hack into the system and remotely apply or lock the brakes. Presumably, those fears stem from media reports describing how attackers managed to remotely manipulate connected cars in multiple ways, such as the case of a Jeep Cherokee, which was intercepted and driven off the road (Wired.com 2015). Despite manufacturers’ efforts to prevent such incidents through security systems, the fear of losing physical control over the vehicle will continue to influence individuals perceptions and the adoption of connected cars.

Looking at the statements referring to negative consequences associated with connected cars, we made two further meaningful observations. First, notable risk spillovers may occur, such that negative consequences are not limited to the drivers, but instead also affect individuals in close physical proximity (e.g., family members, co-workers, car passengers or other drivers as well as pedestrians) and even society at large (e.g., collective surveillance) (see Items 6 and 15 in Table 2). Related to this argument, some negative consequences emerge when IoT devices, especially those with high acquisition costs, are shared with others, and the data become incorrectly associated with another individual (see Item 9 in Table 2). Second, particularly revealing or consequential data streams need not necessarily be fueled by the output of embedded sensors of connected cars only. Instead, data streams combined from a multitude of IoT devices can make individuals particularly vulnerable to unsolicited contacts by commercial entities and attacks by hackers. Privacy-related risks might hence accumulate and potentiate within IoT ecosystems (e.g., see Item 3 in Table 2 for home security technologies).

However, it was also insightful to learn that—consistent with the notion of privacy calculus—participants weighed the consequential loss of control of personal driving data against the benefits they could receive in return. In regard to sharing data with the car manufacturer, one participant stated, “I must admit, I would be somewhat concerned. But if it would pay off, I would think about it.” Another said “I don’t want to become fully transparent, to be honest. At least not if it doesn't come with any benefits for me.” Study participants mentioned a broad set of potential benefits that could result from sharing personal driving data. Among the most cited were monetary payments, supplementary personalized services, technical improvements of vehicles, emotional benefits resulting from supporting research and development projects, and direct contributions to road safety or improved traffic flows.

## Determinants of Negative Consequences

Whether or not connected car drivers will actually face one or more of the negative consequences identified above will ultimately be determined by (1) the intentional and unintentional actions of the party with whom driving data are shared, (2) the level of data security embedded in connected car technology, and (3) the types of driving data that are captured and shared. Drivers seem to make judgments concerning these three factors through assessing the trustworthiness of the party receiving the data, the expected level of data security, and the sensitivity of shared data. For the participants, trust in the other party meant believing that any shared personal driving data would be handled appropriately and not used for purposes other than those for which they were granted permission. Interestingly, many participants—even though they were consciously aware of the potential negative consequences of data sharing—were convinced that they, themselves, would remain unaffected. The main reason for attributing a low likelihood to negative scenarios was, presumably, their trust in the data-requesting party who they believed “will do everything right.” We found particularly high levels of trust in cases where participants imagined that the party receiving data was the manufacturer of their car: “With the car manufacturer? Of course I’d share my data, because, after all, I trust them.” In contrast, less trusting participants stated that other parties “don’t keep their promises” and generally questioned the integrity of data requesting parties. Some of these participants were especially suspicious of internet companies that already engage in intensive data collection in other contexts. One participant stated, “Google is already observing everything I do online. I really would not like them to monitor my driving as well.”

Participants also pointed to the technical issues related to the protection of driving data. Statements referring to information security of connected cars were usually negative: participants stated that data cannot be sufficiently protected. One participant said: “It’s like the computer. No system is 100 percent secure. Nobody can guarantee you full security.” Regarding their overall privacy concerns, participants reflected on the sensitivity of their driving data. Interestingly, they came up with very different answers. One participant said, “I don't care about my driving style. I don’t view this as sensitive data that should be protected.” Another participant stated the opposite: “I think information on my driving style is as sensitive as data can get. I would be extremely concerned if somebody got access to this data.”

## Feelings of Ownership toward Driving Data

Participants’ responses indicated that the feelings of data ownership may play an influential role in their sharing decisions. Participants often emphasized words such as “my,” “mine,” and “not theirs” in their answers, and, directly or indirectly, claimed ownership of the types of data that connected cars can capture and transmit. Such claims came with a dismissive attitude toward the sharing of personal driving data in general and a strong desire to exclusively control such data in particular: “No one gets my data, simply as a matter of principle”; “In the end, this is my data, right? Companies should keep their hands off it.” In contrast to their more detailed elaborations on their privacy concerns, individuals rarely differentiated between the types of driving data they claimed possession of. In their statements, participants instead referred to personal driving data as a whole. The interviews were also helpful in shedding light on why individuals wanted to preserve their feelings of possession toward their driving data.

Interviewees indicated that preferred car settings, radio channels, routes traveled, and driving style are indicative of who they are as individuals. One respondent said, “I think I have some kind of attachment to my driving data, just as I have to any other kind of personal information. [Having control over driving data] is important to me, because it reflects what kind of driver I am.” Moreover, driving data seem to help people to take care of their vehicle and operate it safely and skillfully. One respondent expressed this in the following way: “The data is important because it can help me to drive better … For example, if I do not shift gears correctly, I might damage the engine. So, the driving data can help me to protect my engine.” Another participant said, “[Driving data] helps me to better understand how my car behaves.” Emphasizing a different aspect, another participant stated, “The way how people drive can be improved by looking at the data. For example, it can become more environmentally friendly.” These statements express feelings of self-efficacy and self-identity, motives that are said to cause individuals’ desire to experience possession (Pierce et al 2003). While the state of psychological ownership relates to a cognitive perception (e.g., “It’s my private car and my private data is in it”), individuals seem to experience it as an emotional and affective sensation. Such feelings of ownership appear to determine participants’ desire to control access to their driving data.

Consistent with Venkatesh et al.’s (2013) statement that interviews “can provide depth in a research inquiry by allowing researchers to gain deep insights from rich narratives” (p. 25), Study 1 complemented extant privacy research and informed our exploration of data sharing decisions in IoT contexts. First, we were able to confirm that privacy concerns play a significant role in data sharing decisions in the context of connected cars and we shed light on the nature of those concerns. Most importantly, our findings highlight the fact that IoT devices introduce an additional category of the negative consequences of data sharing—namely, direct threats to users’ physical safety. These fears originate from the fact that IoT devices are equipped with actuators that manipulate their immediate environment based on data signals. It is this intrusion into users’ informational and physical space that renders the privacy implications of IoT devices different from those related to online social networks (Krasnova et al. 2009) or online services in general (Karwatzki et al. 2017a). Second, our qualitative study points to the so-called psychology of possession and property (Peck and Shu 2009) as a fruitful avenue to arrive at a more holistic understanding of data sharing decisions in IoT contexts. This is particularly promising, as psychological ownership might even be a factor in the sharing of low-sensitivity data, such as information on cars’ engine oil levels and engine temperatures. Lastly, our qualitative study highlighted the continued relevance of the privacy calculus perspective as a basic theoretical framework—which, however, needs to be expanded into a contextualized model of sharing decisions that incorporates psychological ownership effects and contextual risk factors such as trust, data sensitivity, and data security.

## Emergent Conceptual Model

## Psychological Ownership

Individuals are known to feel a connection with various objects of possession—including, and especially, homes and cars (Dittmar 1992). Feelings of ownership can also emerge regarding nonphysical objects such as ideas, creative endeavors, and personal data (Anderson and Agarwal 2010; Pierce et al. 2003). This is also the case for driving data, as observed in our exploratory interview study (Study 1). The concept of psychological ownership generally refers to a state in which individuals feel as though the object is “theirs” (Pierce et al. 2003) without presupposing legal ownership (Furby 1980). Ownership feelings develop through individual experiences of controlling the object, getting to know the object intimately, and investing the self into the object (Pierce et al. 2001). Such feelings are meaningful in practice as they are shaped by—and, in turn, shape—individuals’ self-concept and perceived self-efficacy (Belk 1988). Consistent with this notion, Pierce and colleagues (2001) proposed that psychological ownership is rooted in a set of various motives, namely efficacy, effectance,<sup>4</sup> self-identity, and home (i.e., the state of having a physical space to inhabit). Considering the fundamental role of perceived possessions in human lives (Belk 1988), it is not surprising that psychological ownership has been found to affect individual behavior in significant ways. Such behavioral consequences include protective behaviors vis-à-vis the object (Brown et al. 2005) and greater stewardship (Korman 1970). Anderson and Agarwal (2010) demonstrated that psychological ownership toward computers increases users’ intention to protect them by means of antivirus software or firewalls. Other scholars observed that individuals with feelings of ownership over their ideas resist sharing them with colleagues (Webster et al. 2008) and seek to retain exclusive control over them (Choi and Levine 2004; Pierce et al. 2009). These behaviors appear because of emotional distress from losing control over objects that individuals perceive to own (Bartunek 1993) and the feeling of loss of pleasure (Heidegger 1967).

Feelings of ownership toward personal driving data vary between drivers, not least as a function of whether they own, rent, or share their car with others (one respondent commented: “It’s a company car anyway, I don’t care about the data”). However, consistent with the literature on psychological ownership and our insights from Study 1, we expect car drivers’ feelings of ownership toward data collected by the various sensors of their connected cars to shape their data sharing decisions. Drivers with strong feelings of ownership regarding their driving data are likely to resist any request for data sharing, irrespective of privacy concerns. Given the theoretical arguments and empirical evidence as well as our inductive insights from Study 1, we expect psychological ownership of driving data to trigger protective behaviors thereby reducing data sharing. Thus,

Hypothesis 1: The stronger the drivers’ psychological ownership of driving data, the less likely they will be willing to share that data.

## The Mediating Role of Privacy Concerns

We expect privacy concerns to mediate the negative relationship between feelings of ownership of driving data and the likelihood of sharing it with other parties. Exhibiting control over an object was identified as one of the most important paths to the development of psychological ownership toward the object (Pierce et al. 2003). In general, the greater the control over an object, the greater the perceived loss when control is diminished (Pierce et al. 2003). Applied to our context, losing the ability to control personal data would prevent drivers from maintaining and affirming their emotional attachment to their data. Losing control over personal data, as we have seen, might also entail more direct negative consequences, the likelihood and severity of which individuals anticipate in their overall level of privacy concerns (Smith et al. 1996). Individuals hence seem to benefit from not disclosing personal data in two ways: it allows them to preserve their emotional attachment toward their data and it reduces their privacy concerns. This is why we expect that individuals with stronger feelings of data ownership will also express more concerns about privacy when faced with a request to share their driving data.

Conceptually, psychological ownership describes a state of emotional attachment to an object (e.g., driving data) associated with the desire to maintain control over it and can exist regardless of legal ownership (Pierce et al. 2003). Privacy concerns, in contrast, pertain to the negative consequences an individual associates with a loss of the ability to control personal information as a result of sharing it with another party (Xu et al. 2012). In line with their role as the predominant cost dimension in the privacy calculus, privacy concerns have been found to negatively affect individuals willingness to share personal data (Culnan and Armstrong 1999; Dinev and Hart 2006). Studied extensively in privacy research, this relationship has been successfully reproduced across various contexts (see synthesis in Li 2011). Indeed, some scholars claim privacy concerns to be the single most important factor in individuals’ disclosure decisions (Malhotra et al. 2004; Stewart and Segars 2002). Linking the concepts of psychological ownership and privacy concerns in shaping individuals’ sharing decisions, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2: Privacy concerns will mediate the negative relationship between drivers’ psychological ownership of driving data and their likelihood of sharing that data.

## The Role of Contextual Privacy Risk Factors in Shaping Privacy Concerns

We conceptualize contextual privacy risk factors as important, context-informed antecedents of individuals’ privacy concerns. Behavioral or technological in nature, these risk factors jointly determine how likely and severe individuals expect the negative consequences of personal data sharing to be. The three privacy risk factors that emerged as particularly relevant in our interviews were relational trust, data sensitivity and data security.

Consistent with our exploratory insights from Study 1, privacy literature suggests that relational trust plays an important role in diminishing privacy concerns (e.g., Gefen et al. 2003; Krasnova et al. 2010; Pavlou et. Al 2007). Relational trust is the belief that the other party in a relationship possesses characteristics that inhibit it from engaging in opportunistic behavior (Dinev and Hart 2006; McKnight et al. 2002). In line with this conceptualization, individuals depict data sharing with a trusted stakeholder as less risky because intentional data misuse is perceived to be less likely (Gefen et al. 2003). Because relying on trust is an effective human strategy for dealing with uncertainty in general (Luhmann 1979), it is likely to be a part of the decision heuristics that are relevant to data sharing decisions in IoT settings as well. In the case of the connected car, as with many other IoT devices, sharing data with another party is usually not a single, one-time transmission. Instead, individuals typically agree to continuous or repeated transmissions of data. In the case of telematic insurance, for example, drivers let insurers monitor their driving behavior to receive discounts for careful driving (Vaia et al. 2012). In this case, it is difficult for the individual driver to assess ex ante the extent to which the ongoing transmission of sensor-generated data could become a point of concern in the future. For example, a car might record and transmit speeding data, which when combined with other information, will reveal sensitive insights through the use of advanced analytics. Drivers’ privacy concerns are hence likely to be shaped not only by the type of data to be shared with another party but also by their expectations about how this party will handle their data once shared.

Though studies conducted in other settings also point to the role of trust, they do not provide consistent suggestions on the positioning of trust in empirical models to explain individuals’ data sharing decisions. While some scholars have suggested that trust enhances sharing intentions directly (e.g., Dinev et al. 2006), others view trust as an antecedent (e.g., Krasnova et al. 2010) or outcome of privacy concerns (e.g., Bansal et al. 2010). These differences, however, seem to be based on how the risk-mitigating factor was conceptualized, namely as a general (general institutional trust, as in Dinev et al. 2006) or context-specific belief (relational trust, as in Krasnova et al. 2010). Adopting the concept of relational trust, we argue that trust in the requesting stakeholder will directly impact individuals’ privacy concerns in a given disclosure situation. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: The higher the level of trust in the datarequesting party, the lower drivers’ privacy concerns will be.

A second contextual privacy risk factor that our interviews revealed is the perceived sensitivity of personal driving data, which might be low for some types of data (e.g., outside temperature) and high for other types of data (e.g., acceleration patterns). Data sensitivity can be defined as the extent to which information, if released or shared, could cause harm to the subject of the information (Gandy 1993): for example, in the form of social stigma (being viewed as a poor driver; see Item 13 in Table 2), discrimination (being charged higher rates for car rental because one’s driving style was assessed as risky; see Item 5 in Table 2), criminal prosecution (in case of serious speeding; see Item 2 in Table 2), or loss of property (being robbed in times when the car’s location indicates that nobody is home; see Item 3 in Table 2). Studies have repeatedly shown that individuals’ willingness to share decreases as data sensitivity increases (e.g., Nowak and Phelps 1992; Phelps et al. 2000). Smith et al. (2011) conclude that the level of data sensitivity might be the most important contextual factor in individuals’ sharing decisions. As the sensitivity of data directly relates to the severity of negative consequences associated with disclosure (Mothersbaugh et al. 2012), it can be conceptualized as a further antecedent of context-specific privacy concerns (Kehr et al. 2010).

Especially in the IoT context, there is substantial variance in the sensitivity that individuals assign to different data types being collected. This simply reflects the broad range of data types captured by the multitude of sensors that IoT devices such as connected cars are equipped with. Most types of data collected are likely to be of low perceived sensitivity, especially those that pertain to the device’s inner workings (e.g., engine temperature, mechanical wear and tear) and do not allow inferences to be drawn about individual users preferences and behaviors. Findings from basic research on human cognition suggest that the observed variance in data sensitivity ratings might also stem from systematic over- and underestimations. The cognitive biases that lead to such a phenomenon have been repeatedly observed in situations in which individuals are exposed to high levels of uncertainty (Reyna 2004). Indeed, most drivers might struggle to recognize what different types of sensor-generated data can reveal about them, especially when combined from multiple sources and analyzed with advanced machine learning techniques. The complexity of such data sensitivity assessments is arguably much higher in the IoT domain than in contexts that users are familiar with, such as data on health status as documented in medical records or profile data from social network sites.

Overall, we expect users’ privacy concerns to increase with the perceived sensitivity of the data to be collected and shared. We thus propose:

Hypothesis 4: The higher the level of perceived data sensitivity, the higher drivers’ privacy concerns will be.

A final relevant observation from Study 1 regarding contextual risk factors pertains to individuals’ perception of data security. Overall, 24 of our interviewees (20% of the total sample) mentioned concerns about nefarious actors like hackers and cybercriminals, evoking the substantial security challenge that IoT technologies present (Poudel 2016). Indeed, interconnected systems that involve various devices, computers, and transmission technologies (e.g., Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, mobile broadband) offer multiple entry points for attacks. Given that the vulnerability of a system is determined by its weakest link, ensuring security in the IoT is particularly challenging (Lowry et al. 2017). Cybersecurity challenges are a particular concern in the domain of connected cars, not least because of their potential physical impact (NHTSA 2018). Governments around the globe are increasingly aware of such security threats and are enacting legislation and voluntary measures to tackle security issues (Lim and Taeihagh 2018).

As scholars have frequently noted (e.g., Belanger et al. 2002; Landwehr et al. 2012), while information privacy and security are conceptually related, they are two distinct concepts. However, this distinction is inadequately reflected in models of disclosure decisions (Smith et al. 2011). While privacy refers to the level of control an individual can exert over the collection and use of information tied to his or her identity, security refers to the protection of data from unauthorized access as well as damage (Belanger et al. 2002) and is associated with three goals (Von Solms and Van Niekerk

2013): assurance of integrity (information should not be altered during transmission and storage), authentication (the identity of the parties involved and their eligibility to access data should be verified), and confidentiality (data access and use should be confined to authorized parties and purposes only). Meeting these three security goals is a necessary precondition for privacy (Acquisti et al. 2016; Smith et al. 2011). Indeed, when personal data are protected from, for example, third-party access, meaningful control over their usage becomes possible. Since the findings of Study 1 revealed that drivers are aware of the link between security and privacy, we expect perceived data security to act as an important contextual risk factor, along with trust and data sensitivity, for shaping privacy concerns in the IoT. Thus, we predict:

Hypothesis 5: The lower the level of perceived data security, the higher drivers’ privacy concerns will be.

## The Moderating Role of Incentive Design for Data Sharing

Notwithstanding the protective tendencies associated with psychological ownership, individuals are typically willing to give up (perceived) ownership of an object in return for some form of compensation (Belk 2010). Virtually all objects of ownership, whether material or nonmaterial, are exchangeable for a corresponding countervalue. Offering a countervalue (e.g., a monetary payment or a service) in this regard typically serves as compensation for reducing feelings of ownership. In our final hypotheses, we propose an alternative managerial strategy to mitigate drivers’ protective tendencies toward their driving data—namely by understanding and appealing to those motives that explain why individuals develop feelings of ownership in the first place (Pierce et al. 2003).

Possessions help individuals define themselves, express their self-identity to others, and maintain continuity of the self (Price et al. 2000). This is the case even when the possession is personal data. As cited earlier, drivers we interviewed indicated attachment to their driving data. Further, possessions help their owners achieve desired outcomes, both socially and physically (Dittmar 1992). As a case in point, our interviewees viewed their driving data as a means to better understand their car, avoid damaging it, and, ultimately, become a better driver.

Pierce et al. (2003) concluded that psychological ownership must serve the motives of self-identity as well as efficacy and effectance. It is thus not surprising that some suggest that a loss of perceived ownership can bring “a sense of shrinkage of our personality, a partial conversion of ourselves to nothingness” (James 1890, p. 293). Loss of possessions can trigger feelings of depression (Formanek 1991). This raises the question of whether personal data could be used in such a way that the motives of self-identity and effectance are enhanced rather than threatened. In that case, the effect of psychological ownership on individuals’ data-sharing decisions might weaken or even change direction, thus increasing data sharing rather than decreasing it. Such a change could occur as a result of introducing carefully designed incentives that enable individuals to enhance their self-efficacy and contribute to their self-identity as a direct consequence of disclosing their personal data. Consider the case of sharing your driving data in exchange for obtaining access to real-time feedback on your driving style that helps you improve your driving and confirms your self-image of being a skillful driver. While the idea of compensation rests on inhibiting feelings of ownership, such subtler incentives help to foster psychological ownership as an emotional state through sharing, thereby redirecting the influence of psychological ownership on individuals’ behavior. In this case, both the negative effect of psychological ownership and the need for compensation might dissipate.

Hui et al. (2006) identified self-enhancement, defined as the improvement of the self-concept in relation to others, as a major motivation to share personal data. They found that incentives that build up one’s self-concept—such as a premier membership that reflects a certain status in an online community—increased individuals’ intention to share personal information. Perhaps the most compelling evidence for the validity of our arguments, however, is the massive selfdisclosure observable on online social networks such as Facebook (Chen 2013). Despite expressed privacy concerns (Krasnova et al. 2010; Xu et al. 2008), large-scale data leaks (e.g., the Facebook—Cambridge Analytica controversy in 2018), and perceived psychological ownership (Spiekermann et al. 2012), individuals continue to share highly personal data on online platforms, with the expression and enhancement of their identity being the key motivators (Schau and Gilly 2003).

Early privacy research found that individuals do not usually receive direct benefits from sharing personal data on the internet but receive indirect benefits when the sharing enables them to conduct transactions online (Dinev and Hart 2006). Revealing one’s shipping address and credit card information to e-merchants, for example, makes it possible to order products offered in online stores. Similarly, sharing personal financial information with a banking institution makes it possible to use e-banking services. Beyond being a prerequisite for the use of various digital services offered today, disclosing personal information allows individuals to increase their ability to derive value from digital services while decreasing their effort. For example, when registering for Pinterest, users are asked to specify topics they are most interested in. This ensures that only relevant content will be shown to them during their future visits to the image-based social network. Other companies infer users’ interests directly from past interactions with their service: for example, the viewing histories of YouTube users determine future video recommendations. Whether the process is enabled by deliberate data sharing or not, the exploitation of personal data to make a digital service more relevant allows individuals to reach their goals more efficiently—e.g., quickly finding videos that fit their preferences. Some privacy scholars have tried to capture individuals’ perception of this form of selfefficacy enhancement via gains in convenience while performing certain online tasks. Hui et al. (2006), for example, found that expected time savings can motivate consumers to share personal information on the web. Similarly, Hann et al. (2007) showed that users are willing to give up some of their privacy to gain more convenience when using websites that are personalized to their preferences. These self-efficacy enhancing effects are likely to be even stronger for IoT devices and services that offer data-driven personalization advantages—for example, smart shoes that suggest the most effective training plan based on its wearers past running history and vital signs.

Synthesizing our theoretical arguments, we expect the design of incentives that appeal to motives of psychological ownership to be a particularly effective and legitimate managerial strategy for encouraging data sharing in the face of feelings of ownership. Thus, we posit:

Hypothesis 6a: Incentive design that enhances drivers’ selfefficacy will moderate the relationship between psychological ownership of driving data and the likelihood of data sharing, such that the higher the self-efficacy enhancement associated with the incentive employed, the weaker the negative effect will be.

Hypothesis 6b: Incentive design that is congruent with drivers’ self-image will moderate the relationship between psychological ownership of driving data and the likelihood of data sharing, such that the higher the congruence of the incentive employed with drivers’ self-image, the weaker the negative effect will be.

We show our full conceptual model in Figure 2. While the focus of Study 2 is on testing for mediation (Hypotheses 1 and 2), Study 3 tests the effects of contextual risk factors (Hypotheses 3 and 4) as well as the moderating role of incentive design (Hypotheses 6a and 6b). In sum, our conceptual model provides a sense of continuity with extant privacy research but also introduces extensions to existing models of disclosure decision-making that emerged from our deep engagement with the context of IoT in general and connected cars in particular. Regarding the determinants of data sharing that have been detected by studies in other settings (i.e., relational trust, data sensitivity), we advance current knowledge by explicating their critical role in IoT contexts. Regarding factors that have been underexplored or not yet considered by privacy scholars (i.e., data security and psychological ownership), we provide new insights. These highlight the important role of individuals’ perceptions of data security and psychological ownership in shaping their data sharing behavior. As such, they also illustrate the potential of a more nuanced view of incentives for data sharing that connect to more subtle human needs.

## Study 2: Psychological Ownership and Privacy Concerns

## Methods

Design: In Study 2, we collected survey data and conducted structural equation modeling (SEM) to examine the extent to which psychological ownership negatively affects drivers willingness to share driving data (Hypothesis 1) and whether this effect is mediated by privacy concerns (Hypothesis 2). To test our hypotheses, we measured our core variables after exposing subjects to a hypothetical data sharing scenario (Rosenthal and Rosnow 2006). Scenario-based approaches are appropriate for studying novel technology contexts (Sheng et al. 2008) and are commonly used in the privacy literature (e.g., Anderson and Agarwal 2011; Malhotra et al. 2004; Xu et al. 2009). Accounting for the situational sensitivity of privacy decision-making (e.g., Anderson and Agarwal 2011; Acquisti et al. 2012), we provided all subjects with specific information on contextual factors that have been shown in previous literature to influence individuals’ sharing intentions. These comprise the type of data (Phelps et al. 2000), the requesting stakeholder, and the declared purpose of data usage (Anderson and Agarwal 2011). We carefully selected contextual factors to create a realistic scenario related to the use of connected cars, while minimizing trigger effects on both the positive (e.g., arguments for sharing) and the negative (e.g., concerns) side, which could otherwise bias our findings (see Figure A1 in the Appendix). To further reduce ambiguity, the scenario description also contained information on connected car technology.

Procedure: We administered a paper-based questionnaire, in person, to 333 car drivers in Germany (age: M = 32.3 years, SD = 15.6; gender: 45% female; car usage: M = 12,765 km/year, SD = 11,448; legal ownership of car: 46%; Facebook users: 70 %). This data collection approach allowed us to include in our sample individuals who would normally not participate in standard mail or online surveys (see Basi 1999), increasing the variety of views in our study.

CFIP is conceptualized as a reflective second-order factor comprising four first-order components: collection of personal information, unauthorized secondary use of personal information, errors in personal information, and improper access to personal information. Agreement to statements was measured on a 7-point scale (where 1 = strongly disagree and 7 = strongly agree). The construct of psychological ownership originally formulated by Pierce et al. (2001) was captured by a well-established four-item measure employed in previous studies (Anderson and Agarwal 2010; Peck and Shu 2009). Table A4 in the Appendix lists all measurement items and their respective sources. In addition to the core constructs, the survey also captured relevant control variables, including age, gender, education, higher education, car usage (in km/year), and whether the participant is an active user of online social networks (OSN usage).

Analysis: We used covariance-based SEM techniques, as implemented in AMOS 25, to test the proposed relationships. Following a two-step approach, we first assessed the quality of our measures through the measurement model and then tested hypotheses through the structural model. Maximum likelihood estimations were employed for the model assessment. To establish mediation, we followed the procedure proposed by Preacher and Hayes (2004) and examined the significance of the indirect effect with bootstrapped data.

## Findings and Discussion

Results of a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supported the convergent and discriminant validity of all multi-item scales. The reliability of all scales exceeded the generally accepted threshold of a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.7 (Nunnally 1978). Model fit indices suggested that the proposed factor structure has a reasonably good fit with the data (CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, SRMR = 0.04, and RMSEA = 0.04) (Hu and Bentler 1999). The unstandardized regression weights of the structural model provided support for the hypothesized relationships (Table 3). Consistent with the theoretical arguments, we found a statistically significant negative effect of psychological ownership on individuals’ willingness to share driving data (Model 1: B = - 0.568 p < 0.01). Hypothesis 1 was thus supported.

To test whether this effect is mediated by a third variable, as expressed in Hypothesis 2, we estimated indirect effects. In this regard, bootstrap analyses with 5000 replications revealed that the negative effect of psychological ownership on willingness to share is indeed mediated by privacy concerns, as indicated by a significant indirect effect (Model 2: B = - 0.316, 95% BC CI [-0.480, -0.179]). Thus, Hypothesis 2 was supported. The weakened but detectable direct effect of psychological ownership on willingness to share (Model 2: B = -0.253, p < 0.01) suggests a complementary mediation (Zhao et al. 2010). We replicated the empirical findings in support of H1 and H2 across two additional independent samples. One sample (n =131) was composed of active users of Facebook, the other of technically educated employees of a major German car manufacturer (n =730). In the light of arguments that, for example, digital natives or individuals with technical knowledge might systematically differ in their disclosure decision-making (Dinev and Hart 2006; Marwick et al. 2010), the successful replication of our findings increased our confidence in their validity.

After establishing the basic mediation of our proposed research model, we designed Study 3 to examine the role of incentives and contextual privacy risk factors in individuals sharing decisions. As such, Study 3 benefits from and builds upon Study 2, which explicated the fundamental interplay between psychological ownership, privacy concerns, and data sharing, in order to establish a more comprehensive model of individuals’ sharing decisions.

## Study 3: Privacy Risks and Service Design in Data Sharing Decisions

## Methods

Design: In Study 3, we examined how contextual privacy risk factors affect individuals’ privacy concerns (Hypotheses 3-5) and the extent to which incentive design can mitigate the protective tendencies associated with strong feelings of ownership (Hypotheses 6a and 6b: Moderation). To test these extensions of our previously established mediation model, we conducted a field study designed to observe drivers’ actual data sharing decisions in the presence of an incentive.

We conducted a survey-based pilot study to inform the design of our field experiment. We surveyed car drivers in Germany (n=346) to identify incentive mechanisms that would mitigate the negative effect of psychological ownership on drivers data sharing decisions. Consistent with our theoretical perspective, only those incentives that appealed to the motives of psychological ownership neutralized the negative effect of psychological ownership. This was especially true of the dataenabled service of real-time driving analysis (digital driving assistant), which can potentially enhance both one’s selfidentity as a skilled driver and one’s perceived self-efficacy of being able to control the car at all times. To translate these insights from our survey-based pilot study into our field study, we established a research collaboration with TomTom Telematics, a leading automobile device company, to obtain access to a retrofittable plugin telematics device known as the TomTom Curfer along with its associated mobile app.

Procedure: In the first step, through an online survey, we identified 324 individuals who met our selection criteria of (1) owning or having regular access to a car built in 2004 or later and (2) having a smartphone with a mobile data plan. Our final sample (age: M = 32.2 years, SD = 15.0; gender: 43% female; frequent driving: 80%; legal ownership of car: 58%; online social network users: 76%) contained car drivers who were all capable of making use of the telematics service we offered from a technical viewpoint. In the second step, the 324 selected participants were asked to provide information on key constructs described below and to decide whether or not to enroll as a test driver and share driving data through the TomTom Curfer with real-time driving analysis capabilities. The device they would then receive is easy to install and transmits personal driving data<sup>6</sup> to TomTom, the service provider. In return for agreeing to share their driving data, participants obtained full access to all functionalities of the digital driving assistant—real-time feedback on acceleration, cornering and braking, insights into car performance, trip review, and driving style badges—through the TomTom Curfer smartphone app. To enable informed decisions, we provided individuals with background information on the underlying technology and the associated data flows.

Our dependent variable, sharing of driving data, constitutes a discrete choice and was hence measured by a binary variable. This variable takes the value of “1” if a participant agreed to share her driving data and physically installed the telematics device in her car and of “0” otherwise. As such, Study 3 is one of the first in the field of privacy research to go beyond intentions to share and has captured actual data sharing decisions in a field setting.

To measure privacy concerns associated with the digital driving assistant, we relied on the same scale as in Study 2 (i.e., CFIP, again modeled as a reflective second-order factor). We also used the same instrument to measure psychological ownership toward the requested personal driving data. We captured participants’ perception of the service provider’s trustworthiness (relational trust) with a four-item scale (Tax et al. 1998) with 7-point Likert-type response options (1 = strongly disagree and 7 = strongly agree). We asked participants to rate the sensitivity of the requested data (data sensitivity) on a scale of 1 (not sensitive) to 7 (very sensitive) (Kehr et al. 2015). To assess how much the driving analytic service that was offered appealed to participants’ self-identity, we used the self-image congruency scale developed by Sirgy et al. (1997). Selfefficacy enhancement referred to how much the drivers believed that using the digital driving assistant would improve their driving ability; our measurement here was a reformulated scale that originally assessed how well a specific website supports an individual in online shopping (Childers et al. 2001). In the absence of suitable established scales for individuals’ perceptions of data security that are sufficiently distinct from privacy concerns, we developed a new instrument that captures the extent to which individuals consider their personal data in a specific setting to be at risk of being corrupted, manipulated, deleted, or accessed without authorization during data collection, storage, or transfer. As part of the scale development process, we generated an item pool based on prior data security literature. We tested this item pool among a student sample (n=159; detailed results are available from the authors upon request). Based on results from exploratory factor analyses, we eliminated items with low loadings and retained four items (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.83).

We also captured relevant control variables pertaining to personal characteristics including age, gender, education, higher education, and active use of online social networks (OSN usage). Additionally, we captured information on whether an individual used his or her car frequently (frequent driver = more than two rides per week) and exclusively (exclusive driver).

Analysis: Given the binary nature of our dependent variable, we used Bayesian structural equation modeling (BSEM) as implemented in IBM SPSS Amos 25 to test our hypotheses (Byrne 2016). In contrast to the frequentist approach, the Bayesian method views parameters (e.g., the mean) as variables instead of constants. This requires making fewer asymptotic assumptions than, for example, maximum likelihood estimation (Muthén and Asparouhov 2012). We set noninformative priors and used the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm to draw random values of parameters from a high-dimensional joint posterior distribution (Gelman et al. 2004). To test our moderating hypotheses, we specified latent interactions in our model using the matched pairs approach (Marsh et al. 2004).

## Findings and Discussion

A CFA supported the convergent validity for all multi-item measures (see Table A1 in the Appendix). The discriminant validity of our measurements is supported in all cases except one. In the case of self-image congruency and self-efficacy enhancement, the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) exhibits a slightly lower value than the interconstruct correlation. Testing the hypothesized moderators by estimating one model only is hence not feasible (Hair et al. 2010). The reliability of all scales exceeded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.7 (Nunnally 1978). Model fit indices suggest that the proposed factor structure has a good fit with the data (CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, SRMR = 0.04, and RMSEA = 0.05) (Hu and Bentler 1999).

Table 4 summarizes the median estimates of the structural relationships and indicates whether their 90%, 95%, and 99% posterior intervals contain zero. The results for both models were obtained with 500 burn-in samples and 50,000 iterations. Relying on the measure suggested by Gelman et al. (2004), our analysis exhibited a sufficient convergence of the chains with final values reaching 1.0014 (Model 1) and 1.0018 (Model 2).

Consistent with the theoretical arguments, Bayesian estimates indicated that perceptions of the trustworthiness of the requesting stakeholder—the service provider—were negatively related to individuals’ privacy concerns (Model 1: B = -0.243; Model 2: -0.249; 99.5% of posterior draws are negative). Thus, Hypothesis 3 was supported. We further found that the more sensitive the requested data were, the higher the individuals’ privacy concerns tended to be (Model 1: B = 0.190; Model 2: 0.192; 99.5% of posterior draws are positive). Thus, Hypothesis 4 was supported. Regarding our third contextual privacy risk factor, we found that an individual’s perceptions of data security significantly affected his or her privacy concerns. The more negative perceived data security was, the higher the privacy concerns reported by the study participants (Model 1: B = -0.505/ Model 2: -0.501; 99.5% of posterior draws are negative). Thus, Hypothesis 5 was supported.

Among the study participants, 24 participants (7.4%) agreed to install the telematics device in their car and transmitted verifiable personal driving data to the service provider. The results of the BSEM explaining such sharing of driving data are consistent with findings from Study 2 on sharing intentions. They indicate that privacy concerns significantly reduce the likelihood that a driver will decide to share personal driving data (Model 1: B = -0.819; / Model 2: - 0.754; 99.5% of posterior draws are negative).

Our moderation Hypotheses 6a and 6b suggested that incentives—in our case, digital driving assistants—that enhance drivers’ self-efficacy or are congruent with their self-image may contain the negative effect of psychological ownership on data sharing. High correlation between selfefficacy enhancement and self-image congruency (r = 0.82) and VIFs above commonly recommended thresholds suggested that results would be more reliable if the hypothesized moderating effects of both variables were tested separately (see Model 1 and Model 2) (Hair et al. 1995). The coefficient for the interaction term of selfefficacy enhancement and psychological ownership was positive and significant (Model 1: B= 0.649; 97.5% of posterior draws are positive), lending support for Hypothesis 6a. The nonsignificant coefficient for self-image congruency x psychological ownership (Model 2: B = 0.201, 95% of posterior draws are positive and negative), on the other hand, does not indicate a moderating effect. Hence, Hypothesis 6a was supported and Hypothesis 6b was not supported. This might result from the fact that the design of the digital driving assistant was more effective at enhancing drivers’ self-efficacy in the driving task (Mean= 3.65, SD=1.54) than at facilitating the expression of their self-identity (Mean= 2.85, SD= 1.46). One possible explanation for rather low average ratings of the digital driving style assistant’s level of self-image congruency lies in the design of its user interface. It could be described as rather neutral in the sense that it did not contain visual cues that would evoke associations with appealing themes in the context of personal mobility (e.g., a helmet for motorsport or a leaf for environmental awareness). Apart from this, Carter and Grover (2015) suggest that an IS artifact, in general, will be viewed as integral to a person’s sense of self when it is woven into personal and social routines, which might apply less to the digital driving assistant employed in this study.

## General Discussion

The IoT, in general, and connected cars, in particular, are important and rapidly expanding contexts in which the physical and the virtual converge. This introduces novel privacy challenges that are both increasingly prevalent and still largely unexplored. We contribute to a deeper understanding of data sharing decisions and possible managerial options to enhance data sharing for service innovation. The model of data sharing decisions that we developed and validated in three complementary studies provides new insights into the nature and interplay of psychological ownership, privacy concerns, incentive design, and data sharing in the context of connected cars.

## Implications for Research

Our findings contribute to the interdisciplinary field of privacy research with contextual, theoretical, and methodological extensions of the literature on individual data sharing.

First, regarding our connected car context, we respond to calls for extending the boundaries of privacy research by exploring novel empirical settings (Smith et al. 2011). The four characteristics we identified to be determining for the nature of the privacy threats that users are exposed to while interacting with IoT devices (see section “Privacy in the IoT context”) underscore the need for dedicated privacy research—both empirical and conceptual—in this domain (Lowry et al. 2017; Shim et al. 2020). These characteristics exemplify both similarities to and differences from what has been detected in other settings. For example, our findings point out that individuals’ privacy concerns in the domain of IoT are triggered not only by virtual risks but also by physical ones like exposure to burglars, manipulation of vehicle functions, and car hijacking through hacker attacks. This amalgamation of the virtual and the physical in the IoT points to the need to treat informational and physical privacy as interdependent rather than independent concepts (Smith et al. 2011). Similarly, the technological developments associated with the IoT increase the potential for cross-fertilization between research on behavioral aspects of privacy and technical aspects of security (Shim et al. 2020). Our Studies 2 and 3 provide quantitative evidence for the link between privacy and cybersecurity in the connected car context, with privacy concerns increasing as data security decreased. We also argue that data sharing and device usability are uniquely intertwined in the IoT. Typically, IoT users only benefit from the value proposition if they engage in unconstrained data sharing despite the virtual and physical risks as well as the psychological costs associated with it. This dilemma makes the question of appropriate compensation and incentive mechanisms for users, as well as our theory and evidence in this regard, particularly relevant in the IoT. We argue that such insights extracted from connected cars might well generalize to the broader class of IoT devices.

Second, with regard to theory, our research makes several additions to extant conceptual models of data sharing decisions that may well enhance the explanatory power and relevance of privacy research against the emerging realities of the IoT. This is of particular value in light of the growing criticism of the privacy calculus perspective and its focus on privacy concerns as the predominant, if not the only, perceived cost assumed to affect individuals’ sharing decisions (Acquisti et al. 2015; Dinev et al. 2015; Keith et al.

2012). Indeed, individuals are often reluctant to share their data even under conditions of anonymity, when privacy concerns are unlikely to be at play (Chellappa and Sin 2005; Coy 2001). Hence, extant privacy-centered models are unable to sufficiently explain the sharing of anonymized and seemingly low-sensitivity data, the kind of data that is of practical relevance to service innovation in the IoT. In this regard, our qualitative analyses from Study 1 suggest that the perceptions about the costs of data sharing are more diverse than previously assumed. In that regard and similar to previous findings, we show that individuals exhibit strong feelings of ownership not only with regard to physical IT artifacts (like a computer as in Anderson and Agarwal 2010), but also in terms of the data that those artifacts process (like social network profile information, as in Spiekerman et al. 2012). The sharing of personal data—even if less sensitive— might thus be perceived as a loss of psychological ownership that threatens individuals’ emotional attachment to their data. Consistent with these arguments, our quantitative findings reveal that feelings of ownership have important behavioral implications in the context of data sharing decisions— especially when legal ownership is ambiguous, as is still the case in many IoT domains (Kohler and Colbert-Taylor 2014; Zmud et al. 2016). Our results also indicate that a deeper understanding of the motives underpinning psychological ownership can inform the design of effective incentives to compensate individuals for sharing their data. Incentives in the form of data-enabled services can be designed so that they appeal to the fundamental motives that underpin psychological ownership (e.g., self-efficacy), letting the act of data sharing enhance rather than threaten this desired emotional state.

Third, regarding methods, our study illustrates the complementary nature of qualitative and quantitative research methods. Qualitative methods provide context-specific insights into salient factors that influence key behaviors or outcomes, which can then be measured through quantitative methods. For instance, without context-specific understanding through qualitative analysis, we may not have identified psychological ownership as a main determinant of individuals’ willingness to share driving data. Our mixed methods approach and the opportunities for methodological triangulation it provides, however, also put us in the position to contribute to two methodological debates among privacy scholars. The first debate pertains to the potential biases induced by survey studies, the predominant approach in privacy research. There is growing awareness among privacy scholars that participants’ exposure to a privacy concern scale might trigger such concern in the first place, given the emotional and cognitive processes it initiates (Alashoor et al. 2017). The established multidimensional scales (e.g., Smith et al. 1996; Malhotra et al. 2004) might draw participants’ attention to areas of personal data misuse that they might not have thought about before (e.g., excessive collection or unauthorized access to data). That said, our qualitative and quantitative studies converge in highlighting the significance of privacy concerns regarding connected cars. Perhaps most importantly, 41% of participants in Study 1 associated connected cars with a threat to their privacy. The strong privacy concerns observed across our three studies are, hence, not a methodological artifact of the priming effect of survey instruments. Our work also has implications for a second methodological debate that encourages privacy researchers to advance from explaining data sharing intentions to observing actual data sharing behavior (e.g., Belanger and Crossler 2011; Dinev et al. 2015; Smith et al. 2011). As the first attempt to examine both outcome variables in the same empirical context—data sharing intentions in Study 2 and data sharing behavior in Study 3—we found that our results converge across the two outcome variables.

## Implications for Practice and Policy

Our research also contributes to the ongoing public discussion on how to balance organizations’ interests in collecting and monetizing personal data with users’ interests in controlling and benefitting from that data. Several IS scholars have called for research to advance the understanding of privacy and cybersecurity by addressing these controversial and unresolved issues (Lowry et al. 2017) rather than trying to fill gaps in literature without making any practically relevant contributions (Rai 2017). Indeed, engaged IS scholarship has an important role to play, not only in developing a better understanding the perceived and actual vulnerabilities of IoT users, but also in deriving countermeasures and incentive mechanisms that enable value creation in IoT ecosystems. As such, our work helps address a practical issue that ranks among the most persistent barriers to the adoption of connected car services, such as telematic insurance, digital driving assistants, and predictive maintenance (Foley 2017; Jean 2020).

The contextual privacy risk factors that we uncovered offer multiple points of departure for managerial action. As our findings indicate, building relational trust with users helps to alleviate their privacy concerns and ultimately enhances data sharing, the central precondition for connected car services. Data-requesting stakeholders could signal their integrity and reduce perceived risks of data sharing through transparent data usage policies, user-friendly privacy settings, and external guarantees of privacy through privacy seals provided by thirdparty organizations (Rifon et al. 2005; Xu et al. 2011). Moreover, car manufacturers and technology providers would be well-advised to take seriously drivers’ beliefs about security, which are closely intertwined with privacy concerns in IoT-related contexts, as our study shows. This will involve embedding resilient security systems into IoT devices, informing users of any residual security risks, and educating them on how best to contain them. For the latter two, the negative consequences revealed in our interviews represent possible starting points for better communication with drivers. In addition, policy makers could implement certifications that stipulate a sufficient level of data security as a precondition for the launch of an IoT product. Our analyses of contextual privacy risk factors also revealed that privacy concerns—and subsequently data sharing—varied notably across the levels of perceived data sensitivity. Hence, rather than simply depriving car drivers of their privacy and maximizing the collection and use of connected car data, service providers may adopt more selective and tailored approaches to design data-enabled services that stand out from the competition by offering the best possible ratio of customer benefit to data consumption, especially at the high sensitivity end of the data spectrum. Alternatively, sensitive data could be processed only locally, i.e., on users’ IoT devices without transferring the data to the cloud and hence shielding it from further central storage and analysis (see edge computing, Shi and Dustdar 2016). Our findings also underline the promise of “privacyby-design” principles (Cavoukian 2011) when designing and evaluating solutions to create value from the data generated by connected cars and other smart and interconnected devices.

Perhaps most profoundly, our findings reveal that, despite strong feelings of ownership, drivers may be willing to share their data in exchange for appropriate compensation in the form of offers that address the motives underlying their feelings of ownership. As illustrated in our Study 3, this compensation can take the form of a digital driving assistant that helps users learn about their driving style. Rather than attempting to suppress users’ feelings of ownership toward their data, stakeholders in the connected car and broader IoT arena could partner with users in an open innovation environment to design services that appeal to drivers’ self-identity, enhance perceptions of selfefficacy, and further enforce drivers’ psychological ownership regarding the data that their connected vehicles and other IoT devices generate. As a case in point, it would be interesting to consider how gamification elements could further enhance the experience of self-efficacy (Hamari et al. 2014) and be integrated in services such as the driving style assistant employed in our Study 3. We believe that psychological ownership will gain in relevance as policy makers seek to empower users and provide them with greater control over the collection, storage, and use of their data. The 2018 reform of European Union’s (EU) General Data Protection Rules (GDPR) has empowered users and strengthened their bargaining position in the emerging data economy. Overall, we hope that our findings will help practitioners and policy makers better understand drivers’ concerns about sharing driving data, placing organizations in a better position to recognize their digital responsibility and design technologies, services, and policies centered on users’ privacy and ownership needs.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

Our study has theoretical and empirical limitations that offer several meaningful opportunities for future research. First, as with all self-reported data, our research is exposed to a possible common method bias, as in our Study 2, which uses data sharing intentions as a dependent variable. We sought to reduce this threat ex ante by implementing procedural remedies recommended by Podsakoff et al. (2003), including guaranteeing the anonymity of survey participants, providing contextual information and definitions to reduce ambiguity, and informing participants that there were no right or wrong answers. Moreover, results from Harman’s single-factor test, the unmeasured latent method construct approach (Liang et al. 2007), and the specific bias test (Serrano Archimi et al. 2018) jointly suggested that common method bias was unlikely to affect the results of Study 2. Future research could go a step further and measure data sharing intentions at a later point in time using a different instrument.

Second, the design of our field study, Study 3, may have limited our ability to detect the moderating effect associated with increased self-image congruency (Hypothesis 6b). As outlined above, it might well be that the digital driving assistant did not allow for a meaningful expression of drivers’ self-identity, given its limited integration into the drivers’ daily life (Carter and Grover 2015). We encourage IS scholars to refine our study design and further examine the effect that incentives with high self-image congruency could have in terms of redirecting the effect of psychological ownership on data sharing.

Third, our empirical focus on the data sharing decisions of car drivers in Germany, who are known to be particularly hesitant to share data with connected cars (McKinsey, 2016), allowed for local theorizing and interpretation (Chiasson and Davidson 2005) but might constrain the generalizability of our empirical findings (Seddon and Scheepers 2011). Future replication studies could seek to establish the statistical generalizability of our empirical findings to other geographical settings that differ in terms of cultural beliefs, prevalent attitudes toward possession, and privacy norms (Dinev et al. 2006; Lowry et al. 2011). However, we expect our theoretical model to be generalizable to a broad set of smart, connected devices in the IoT that generate continuous streams of data as by-products of everyday use (Porter and Heppelmann 2014).

Lastly, mixed methods research is associated with additional sources of bias that pose a threat to the inference quality of such study designs. One of these limitations results from making statistical generalizations from a sample population to a larger population (Onwuegbuzie and Johnson 2006). It is possible that our interview participants and the participants recruited for our quantitative Studies 2 and 3 were not representative of the population. This would limit the transferability of findings between studies and would affect the meta-inferences that emerge from our mixed methods design. However, the concerns associated with the integration of multiple samples are mitigated as our data are from a relatively large number of interviewees (n=120) and survey respondents (>300). Study participants were recruited through the same channels and exhibited similar sociodemographic characteristics across all our studies. A second potential limitation of our mixed methods design pertains to a possible bias that originates from the sequence in which our studies were conducted (Onwuegbuzie and Johnson 2006). It is possible that some of our findings are dependent on the order in which the studies were administered and that our interpretation could be different if the order of the quantitative and qualitative phases had been reversed.

## Conclusion

The connected car represents a critical stepping stone toward the vision of a fully interconnected and enriched experience of personal mobility. As a significant IS artifact in consumer IoT, the connected car simultaneously presents substantial opportunities for data-enabled value creation and unprecedented threats to drivers’ informational and physical privacy. This calls for refined models of data sharing that account for the emerging realities of the connected car and the broader IoT. Against this backdrop, we conducted three complementary studies to develop and validate a conceptual model based on the interplay of psychological ownership, privacy concerns, and data sharing. We found evidence that drivers exhibit feelings of data ownership that reduced their data sharing propensity, both directly and indirectly, because of heightened concerns about privacy. Privacy concerns, in turn, were shaped by relational trust toward the data-requesting party, perceptions of data security, and the perceived sensitivity of the data requested. We demonstrated that drivers with high psychological ownership were willing to share their driving data if they were offered adequate compensation in exchange. We found that incentives that appealed to the motives of psychological ownership (enhancing self-efficacy or contributing to self-identity) alleviated the negative effect that psychological ownership had on individuals’ decisions to share personal data. Our conceptual model and empirical findings promise to promote data sharing with adequate compensation of drivers and to encourage future privacy research in the IoT context. Only then will privacy research be able to keep up with the technological advances and contribute to sustained value creation in the IoT era.
