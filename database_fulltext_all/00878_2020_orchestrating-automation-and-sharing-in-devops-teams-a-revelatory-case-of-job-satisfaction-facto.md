# Orchestrating automation and sharing in DevOps teams: a revelatory case of job satisfaction factors, risk and work conditions

Aymeric Hemon-Hildgen <sup>a,b</sup>, Frantz Rowe <sup>b,c</sup> and Laetitia Monnier-Senicourt <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Operation Management & Decision Science Department, ESSCA School of Management, Angers, France; <sup>b</sup>IAE Faculty, LEMNA research center, University of Nantes, Nantes, France; <sup>c</sup>SKEMA KTO research center, SKEMA Business School - Campus De Sophia Antipolis, Sophi Antipolis, France

## ABSTRACT

Agile can be seen as an accelerator to develop software, yet it sufers from bottlenecks between development and operations. DevOps can overcome bottlenecks between agile development teams and operations, notably by providing continuous integration, delivery and deployment, thus improving end-to-end processes between development and operations functions. However, literature on DevOps efects is scant. Using job design characteristics theory and Herzberg’s job satisfaction theory, this paper investigates perceptions of job (dis) satisfaction, risks and work conditions among 59 employees working within 12 agile and DevOps teams in the same firm. Findings show that DevOps provides greater job satisfaction than agile alone. However, this case study also unveiled an efect of risk amplification with DevOps, and the greater need to orchestrate automation and sharing, contingent on work conditions. We theorise that orchestration of interactions between automation and sharing and related risks must be managed to improve job satisfaction when agile teams transition to DevOps.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Since the 1970’s, in large firms, the Information Systems (IS) function has been organised around a division of labour between the Development and Operations functions. In the 1990's, agile software development methods were progressively adopted, deeply afecting processes of the Development function but not those of the Operations function. In 2008, during the Agile Conference in Toronto, Debois called on IT people to create cross functional teams between agile development teams and operations to “break the agile development by including infrastructure people in the team” (P. Debois, 2008). Debois coined the term DevOps, indicating the need to bring the two functions together, when he named and organised the first DevOpsDays Conference in Ghent in 2009. P. Debois (2009a) put forth practices and values supporting DevOps but did not define it (P. Debois, 2009b). Subsequently DevOps has been depicted as an approach, a philosophy or a method (Lwakatare, Kuvaja et al., 2016). In this paper we define DevOps teams as a collective of both software development and operations roles that strive to systematically and eficiently collaborate in order to improve the quality and speed of software development and deployment processes. In these teams, operations members are associated with application development from the beginning of the development process so that development members rapidly integrate deployment constraints in production environments (A. Hemon et al., 2019a). Full DevOps allows continuous delivery and deployment and eliminates bottlenecks between development and operations (Humble & Farley, 2010; Kim et al., 2016). DevOps follows the agile principles of collaborative work (rather than a sequence of individual jobs) and of iterative development in short sprints, yet it extends these principles to a broader perimeter than agile. Organisations are increasingly adopting DevOps (Dybå & Dingsøyr, 2008; Forsgren et al., 2017; Version One, 2018) in a quest to push performance beyond levels observed with agile (Chen, 2017), which has demonstrated superior potential to plan-driven methods. However, the promise of DevOps is not evident. IS development and IS operations had, until very recently, been kept separate because they had to fulfill diferent objectives and reflected very diferent values. Beyond the risks of culture clashes, organisation theory argues that decoupling diferent functions or systems can be beneficial (Berente & Yoo, 2012; Orton & Weick, 1990). By requiring IS development to create code without having to integrate production rules, and having IS operations focus on robustness, decoupling may promote both agility and reliability. However, such decoupling may be advantageous only in the short term and may incur technical debt (Cunningham, 1992; Keller et al., 2019). Conversely, because DevOps implies tight coupling between IS development and IS operations, it may force the IS function to focus on reducing its technical debt, at the cost of creativity and agility. Further, bigger teams are often harder to manage and self-organise (Putnam, 1978; Rodríguez et al., 2012). Whether grouping development and operations in one team enhances team member satisfaction and overall performance remains an open question. In particular, the question of team member satisfaction is worth investigating because the logic of optimising the IS function as a whole may run against the perception of each of the two functions, which may see the status quo as more advantageous because they have diferent priorities or values and change is costly and risky for them.

Whereas the scientific literature on agile adoption abounds (Korhonen, 2009; Sureshchandra & Shrinivasavadhani, 2008), and most often reports job satisfaction (Melnik & Maurer, 2006; Tripp et al., 2016), the literature on DevOps phenomena is scant (Erich et al., 2014; Smeds et al., 2015).

Knowledge of the risks organisations face when moving to DevOps is limited (Lwakatare, Karvonen et al., 2016; Nybom et al., 2016; Shropshire et al., 2017). Specifically, we lack knowledge of the risks employees perceive in DevOps teams and whether and why their (dis)satisfaction increases with DevOps compared with agile teams. This knowledge is important to successfully overhaul the IS function, as part of DevOps, and to make expectations about the potential of DevOps more realistic. Consequently, this study explores employees’ risk perceptions and job (dis)satisfaction when using DevOps practices, and examines the extent to which they difer from agile practices. The study of risk perception combined with an analysis of the sources of satisfaction and dissatisfaction could clarify the impact of DevOps use on job satisfaction, because these perceptions play a major role in IS staf retention, especially in a context of scarce resources in the IS job market. Successful new methods and practices should not only bring adopting firms major gains but must also be seen as progress by users of these methods and practices.

In DevOps, team collaboration between development and operations raises new management challenges and orchestration needs. The literature on assets or resource orchestration and the capabilities needed in a dynamic environment is nascent (Helfa et al., 2009; Sirmon et al., 2007, 2011). Resource orchestration combines resource management and asset orchestration (Sirmon et al., 2011) and refers to the “comprehensive process of structuring the firm’s resource portfolio, bundling the resources to build capabilities, and leveraging those capabilities with the purpose of creating and maintaining value for customers and owners” (Sirmon et al., 2007, p. 273). According to Helfat et al. (2009), orchestration is essential to achieving new combinations and coalignment of resources and assets in a dynamic environment, like the software development industry. Specifically, the meaning of team orchestration in the DevOps context and how it difers from the agile context remains underexplored.

Hence, our three research questions: Q1) How do DevOps team members perceive job (dis)satisfaction and risks? Q2) Do these perceptions between Agile and DevOps difer, and why? Q3) How can these diferences between Agile and DevOps reveal team orchestration needs?

Answering these questions could help organisations make better decisions regarding DevOps adoption and management. The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 presents the literature on DevOps and agility, and on satisfaction. Section 3 specifies our embedded case study research methodology. Section 4 states and illustrates our findings. Finally, Section 5 discusses the findings relative to the literature, suggests theoretical implications, and outlines the limitations of this research along with research avenues.

## 2. LITERATURE BACKGROUND AND THEORETICAL LENSES

In this section we first clarify DevOps raison d’être, provide a definition and diferentiate it from agile. We then review the literature on risks related to agile and DevOps practices and how those enable job satisfaction or dissatisfaction. Finally, we present the two theoretical lenses we will use to investigate the phenomenon.

## 2.1. DevOps: raison d’être, definition and diferentiation from agile

## 2.1.1. DevOps Breaks Silos between Development and Operations

DevOps stems from the enduring need to break silos within the IS function. DevOps emerged to resolve the bottleneck problem in the flow between an accelerated and incremental development process based on agile practices and the slower delivery processes that operations teams adopt due to their focus on secure deployment and reliable service. To that efect DevOps applies the agile principle of iterative and incremental development methods and tools (e.g., Scrum, XP) (Beck et al., 2001). Most importantly, it extends the agile principle of working collaboratively rather than sequentially to a larger scope and with greater efect, including impacting speed of delivery (Bird, 2017). Whereas in agile the scope of collaboration is limited to those of business representatives such as Product Owners with those who design and prepare systems, with DevOps, collaboration is extended to production engineers, testers and release managers (A. Hemon et al., 2019b). With agile development, challenges regarding flows, organisation readiness, management and functional silos persist, notably between development and operations (Boehm & Turner, 2005; Dennehy & Conboy, 2017).

Clearly, without DevOps, agility remains in a paradigm with two separate functions. The studies and the development function follow values of creativity and speed, whereas the operations function pursues values of quality, stability and traceability. Agile development practices do not solve this problem of antagonistic logics if it is adopted only within the development function, i.e., without breaking silos between development and operations. In contrast, DevOps makes the IS function potentially smarter, as reflected by greater soft skills, competence and collaboration within teams (A. Hemon et al., 2019b) and by a DevOps learning efect which can also contribute to IS function transformation (Hemon & Rowe, 2019). The specificity of DevOps can be summarised through its goal: to improve fluidity in the software development pipeline, particularly by bridging the development and operations worlds. Metaphorically, breaking silos between two functional units means “opening” a valve on the pipeline to let the flow continue faster and with more features.

## 2.2. From four pillars of DevOps to a definition

Humble and Molesky (2011) highlighted four core values for DevOps: Culture, Automation, Measurement and Sharing. In their literature review on DevOps, Erich et al. (2014) precisely described these four pillars: culture is the extent to which an organisation practicing DevOps attempts to eliminate cultural barriers between the Dev and Ops functions; Automation applies to the software development and operational processes adopted by the project teams; Measurement refers to joint use of metrics by both Dev and Ops; Sharing refers to the extent that information is shared between Dev and Ops. These DevOps pillars are supported by principles mostly derived from Lean Thinking and Lean Management (Fitzgerald & Stol, 2017; Humble et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2016).

In our interpretation of the four values attached to DevOps, beyond information, sharing is the first pillar because it covers, at least partly, culture (i.e., shared practices and beliefs) and measurement and because it is a precondition for eficient automation.

First, culture and measurement can be partially subsumed in sharing. Building common ground is fundamental in teamwork (Bjørn & Ngwenyama, 2009) and we recognise that the existence of this common ground may not sufice for building and espousing a shared culture. However, we contend that being part of the same team allows us to share language and to develop a better understanding and more respect for what other team members are doing. This may not be the case when team members work with plan-driven methods and only interact occasionally (Bjørn & Ngwenyama, 2009), DevOps team members fundamentally share a lot of practices iteratively and permanently. Furthermore, we do not argue that in DevOps teams each member is entirely substitutable by another member – this would be impossible although some roles may rotate (A. Hemon et al., 2020). We claim that sharing information and observing others’ practices leads towards finding common ground and understanding values and measures.

Second, when suficient sharing is practiced, automation can be implemented and render work more eficient. Eficient automation is conditioned or facilitated by increasing the level of shared information and a common understanding between operations and development teams. Kim et al. (2016, p. 84) highlighted the principle of continuous learning across Dev and Ops teams, characterised by “a high-trust culture that enables all departments to work together efectively, where all work is transparently prioritized and there is suficient slack in the system to allow high-priority work to be completed quickly.” Barriers to knowledge sharing are an important issue in agile teams (Ghobadi & Mathiassen, 2016), yet sharing is essential for collaboration across diferent specialities like Dev and Ops (Riungu-Kalliosaari et al., 2016). DevOps is also conducive to extending the use of methods used in agile, such as Kanban dashboards, which facilitate transparent prioritisation (Hüttermann, 2012; Saltz & Sutherland, 2020).

Moreover, DevOps is based on a higher level of automation than agile methods and extends it to operations (Dörnenburg, 2018; Ebert et al., 2016; Virmani, 2015), i.e., automated continuous planning, automated tests, automated data collection, automated analysis, automated cloning, automated deployment, automated monitoring of alerts and alarms and an automated software supervision system. Automation increases the use of technology in order to follow a production process eficiently, i.e., with minimal human intervention. New tools, such as Jenkins, allow teams to automate the diferent stages of the continuous delivery pipeline for all branches and pull requests; the Docker platform enables distributed development and automated deployment with higher security through separate containers; and Kubernetes allows automation of the distribution and scheduling of several containers across a whole cluster.

Therefore, fundamentally DevOps rests on sharing and automation. Beyond our introductory proposition of a definition for DevOps teams, and lacking a definition for DevOps itself, we define DevOps as set of a principles for collaborative work implemented between the IS design and development function and the IS operations function, along with potentially other constituencies (stakeholders), which is founded on the sharing of culture, goals, measures, automation tools and automated processes towards continuous delivery of software. With DevOps, software development and operations teams must therefore collaborate, learn new skills and methods and master new tools. This higher level of automation, shared practices and common culture in DevOps potentially afects many elements like job satisfaction and risk perception.

## 2.3. How does DevOps difer from Agile?

Beyond the collaboration and iterativity principles, the third fundamental principle that supports DevOps is continuity throughout the end-to-end software development process. These core values allow for continuity, most importantly with automation and sharing. Several practices can be used to ensure this principle of continuity: automated provisioning, integrated configuration management, continuous integration, continuous delivery or deployment, infrastructure as a code, containerisation and continuous learning. However, DevOps practices are not limited to these practices. Continuous integration and continuous delivery or deployment are important practices that highlight the level of maturity or advances towards DevOps that we will come back to in the methodology section. More generally, all practices that support this principle of continuity and prevent bottlenecks are practices that potentially make DevOps possible. Fitzgerald and Stol (2017) listed various continuous activities related to business planning, development, operations, improvement and innovation.

This principle of continuity is limited in the Agile Manifesto and contradicted at a practical level. Although the Manifesto claims in its first principle that the “priority is to satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software,” the fourth principle states that “Business people and developers must work together daily throughout the project.” On the one hand, they encourage “continuous delivery” (over the end-to-end software development process). On the other hand, they implicitly limit the daily collaboration to the biz-dev functions. At the practical level, agile methods have been developed by and for developers and they are used mostly by biz-dev function (i.e., Scrum) or dev function (i.e., Extreme programming). In practice, agile methods do not consider this continuity regarding delivery or deployment. When a team follows the Scrum method, the team is able to deliver a new product or feature to their customer at the end of each sprint. Such an iteration can last one to three weeks before a release into production. The delivery is intermittent, sometimes sequential, and not continuous. DevOps allows product updates, and the ability to add new features within hours, sometimes minutes when tests and deployment are fully automated and there is a high level of sharing between Dev and Ops. Agile methods also consider the principle of continuous learning. However, scrum teams need to wait for the end of the sprint review to organise the sprint retrospective. Lessons are shared less frequently than in DevOps and with fewer people.

## 2.4. From Agile and DevOps Practices to Job Satisfaction/Dissatisfaction

## 2.4.1. Perceived Risks with Agile and DevOps Practices

Schmidt et al (2001) define a risk factor “as a condition that can present a serious threat to the successful completion of a software development project,” (p7) which can lead to software rework. Perceived risk is frequently associated with the acceptance and practice of a product, service, technology or method. Risks related to agile acceptance have been investigated and partially evaluated (Boehm & Turner, 2005). The literature focused on risk management (Albadarneh et al., 2015; Elbanna & Sarker, 2016; Kontio et al., 2004), or on risk linked to processes (Nyfjord & Kajko-Mattsson, 2007). However, Nerur et al. (2005) emphasised that caution is required when using agile methodology or incorporating it into existing practices. Without defining these concepts, some authors have specifically studied the challenges or barriers to the implementation and efectiveness of agile approaches. Conboy and Fitzgerald (2010) identified nine key people challenges related to the adoption of agile methods: developers’ fear of skilldeficiency exposure; broader skill sets for developers; increased social interaction; lack of business knowledge; understanding agile values and principles; lack of motivation; devolved decision-making; implementing agile-compliant performance evaluation; and recruiting challenges. Kalenda et al. (2018) also listed the main challenges faced when moving agile to a large-scale context: resistance to change; distributed environment; quality assurance issues; lack of commitment and teamwork; too much pressure and workload; lack of knowledge, coaching and training; and a requirement management hierarchy. Ghobadi and Mathiassen (2016) construe knowledge-sharing barriers as general software risks and classic mistakes in agile practices, i.e., insuficient planning or unrealistic estimations. Ghobadi and Mathiassen (2016) identified seven types of specific knowledge-sharing barriers in agile software teams. They argue that these barriers constitute three main areas of risk factors related to the team (1-team diversity; 2-team perceptions;

3-team capabilities), process (4-project communication; 5-project organisation) and context (6-project setting; 7-project technology). Because risks relate notably to the context (Ghobadi & Mathiassen, 2016), working condition improvements may reduce these risks. We will use this tripartite classification to structure risk factors in the findings section.

## 2.5. Agile and DevOps Practices as Enablers of Job Satisfaction/Dissatisfaction

Research has highlighted new ways of working and collaborating, investigated their efect on job satisfaction within agile teams, and compared agile and nonagile teams. Tripp et al. (2016) demonstrated the direct efects of agile practice use on job satisfaction. Through a quantitative study of agile and non-agile team members (mostly project managers and developers), using the job characteristics model (Hackman & Oldham, 1980), they found that the use of agile practices positively impacted job satisfaction among team members. Melnik and Maurer (2006) observed that twice as many agile employees are satisfied with their work than are non-agile employees. Acuña et al. (2009) reported that job satisfaction of agile team members is directly related to personality factors, e.g., agreeableness and conscientiousness.

Considering that DevOps extends agile values, principles and practices to the operations function, we could expect to find similar results in a DevOps context. Shropshire et al. (2017) identified two types of personalities: 1) supporting and enabling DevOps, and 2) stifling innovation and slowing DevOps practice. “DevOps puts new demands on the organisation. Some employees concentrate on the positives: improved software releases, increased uptime, improved collaboration, higher quality code, fewer errors, etc. Others are overwhelmed by its negative aspects: harder work, greater responsibility, blurred lines of authority, complex performance metrics, potential redundancies/layofs, etc.” (Shropshire et al., 2017, p. 1). These findings show how DevOps afects risk perception among its practitioners and produces fear and dissatisfaction that may prevent the use of DevOps.

As for any management method or practices, excessive application may cause risks and dissatisfaction. Although agile adoption generally creates job satisfaction, it could also induce dissatisfaction depending on the work conditions. Solinski and Petersen (2016) investigated the benefits and limits of agile practice usage, and found that agile can generate dissatisfaction within agile teams. Begel and Nagappan (2007) pinpointed perceived problems with agile as a source of dissatisfaction for many developers, i.e., scaling up projects, frequency of meetings and losing sight of the big picture.

Frequent deadlines and highly ritualised and sequenced agile methods increase employees’ stress levels (Petersen & Wohlin, 2009). Communication issues may also produce dissatisfaction. Agile could paradoxically be fatal for communication for agile teams potentially isolated from their environment (Karlström & Runeson, 2005), or for agile teams that cannot easily communicate externally (Pikkarainen et al., 2008). Melnik and Maurer (2006) identified reasons for dissatisfaction among employees working in agile, e.g., incompetent management and unclear company objectives.

Similarly, DevOps can also generate satisfaction and dissatisfaction. By enabling individuals practicing DevOps to work more quickly and save time, automation represents a potential source of satisfaction. Conversely, by increasing the quantity of features delivered and deployed, automation can also cause fatigue (Fortmann-Müller, 2018) and stress or dissatisfaction for DevOps employees. Breaking silos to increase sharing between Dev and Ops allows each team member to see beyond its traditional function. As noted by Nybom et al. (2016) in a firm where Dev and Ops mix responsibilities (i.e., by assigning both development and operations responsibilities to individuals in teams), DevOps may allow individuals to perceive more opportunities and related risks in a larger environment, but also increase the possibilities of making mistakes. Even in a firm where DevOps is practiced by mixing personnel (i.e., by increasing collaboration between Dev and Ops but keeping existing roles diferentiated), we may expect broader common understanding, cross-functional awareness and vision of opportunities, a possible source of job satisfaction, combined with a wider vision of risks and potential problems, which might induce stress and job dissatisfaction.

## 2.6. Orchestrating resources

Greater automation and sharing modify the resources used by the firm, i.e., technological, and human, and the way these resources are managed, acquired, developed and improved. Therefore, DevOps complicates software development more than Agile does because of the many changes required in resource management in a dynamic environment (Sirmon et al., 2007). Through automation and sharing, DevOps purportedly represents a significant challenge in terms of resource orchestration (Cui & Pan, 2015), both in terms of bundling processes (Sirmon et al., 2007) to form collaborative (Sirmon et al., 2011) and optimising capabilities (Sirmon et al., 2007) and concerning leveraging processes (Sirmon et al., 2007). As presented in the introduction, according to Helfat et al. (2009), orchestration is fundamental to achieving new combinations and co-alignments of resources and assets in a dynamic environment, like the software development industry. Resource orchestration could influence the perception of satisfaction among DevOps workers positively or negatively.

## 2.7. Theoretical lenses: Job design and Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory

Rather than positing hypotheses a priori, we follow an inductive theory-building approach (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007) in which our theorising is informed by an embedded case study (Yin, 2013). We retain Locke’s (1976, p. 1300) definition of job satisfaction: “a positive or pleasurable reaction resulting from the appraisal of one’s job, job achievement, or job experiences.”

To assess job satisfaction, following Tripp et al.’s (2016) studies of agile teams, we first selected job characteristics theory (Hackman & Oldham, 1980). This theory ofers organisations a framework to design work based on five core job characteristics (CJC), to generate individual satisfaction, global satisfaction and hence organisational performance. Hackman and Oldham (1980) explain these five CJC: 1) skill variety, defined as the extent to which the job requires the holder to use diferent skills and abilities and knowledge; 2) task identity, characterised as the extent to which a job enables the holder to achieve a whole identifiable outcome; 3) task significance, described as the extent to which a job influences and impacts the organisation and people’s lives; 4) autonomy, namely the extent to which a job gives the holders a feeling of freedom, allowing them to decide when and how to do their activities; and 5) feedback, which is the extent to which job holders receive clear information about their performance. These five CJC induce three critical psychological states in workers: 1) meaningfulness, the feeling of contributing, adding value, and being important; 2) responsibility, the degree to which the job holder feels accountable for the job done, and for outcomes; and 3) knowledge and understanding of the results by each job holder, which help them perform and improve their work. These three psychological states increase intrinsic work motivation, job satisfaction, quality of work, efectiveness and performance, while reducing absenteeism and turnover (Hackman & Oldham, 1976, 1980).

Given that the present study considers both satisfaction and dissatisfaction, Herzberg’s theory is quite pertinent. F. Herzberg’s (1964) two-factor theory stipulates that job satisfaction depends on two sets of factors. The first set, comprising extrinsic or “hygiene” factors, such as working conditions, correlate to dissatisfaction. The second set, namely intrinsic or motivation factors such as achievement, are linked to satisfaction.

Achievement, intrinsic interest in the work, responsibility and advancement would contribute greatly to job satisfaction whereas company policy and administrative practices, supervision, interpersonal relationships, work conditions and salary would not afect job satisfaction but may cause dissatisfaction. Thus, Herzberg believes satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not in opposites. These two dimensions have been partially investigated among IS employees. Some “hygiene” factors have been analysed, such as interpersonal relations among software development teams (Acuña et al. 2009), or ERP systems, which are seen as working conditions (Morris & Venkatesh, 2010).

Furthermore, job satisfaction can be directly related to risk. The level of exposition to risk or a compensation system linked to risk can influence job satisfaction (Cook et al., 1981). Mclain (1995) found a positive relationship between lower perceived risk and job satisfaction and workplace conditions. Similarly, individuals who perceived high levels of risk reported lower levels of job satisfaction (Nielsen et al., 2011). Ito et al. (2001) identified factors related to perceived risks afecting job satisfaction and staf’s decision to leave their current job. Sjöberg and Drottz-Sjöberg (1991) highlighted that job satisfaction was related to daily tangible events and concerns, including perceived job risks and stress level. Sullivan and Bhagat (1992) outlined the importance of organisational stress on job satisfaction and job performance. Consequently, the Human Resource Management literature posits a link between job satisfaction factors, work conditions and risk perception. Viewed as an extension of agile, DevOps can be both a source of job satisfaction and job dissatisfaction and risks and represents an environment in which work conditions play an important role (F. Herzberg, 1964).

## 3. METHODOLOGY

To answer our phenomenon-driven research questions, we employed an embedded case study data collection methodology that integrated interviews, observations and documentation (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Yin, 2013). We completed it with a semantic and inductive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) to build our empirical and theoretical propositions.

## 3.1. Design overview

We conducted this research in 2017 at Group X, a very large European firm in the services industry. The IT Staf is 15,000 people. They had been using agile methods, mostly scrum and XP, for more than 15 years and DevOps for 8 years. As one of the early adopters of DevOps practices, the company deployed the implementation of DevOps for a few years on pilot projects in two diferent ways. First, the firm underwent a gradual transition from a software development context working in agile to a DevOps context; second, they initiated a project directly following the DevOps approach. In January 2015, the firm launched a strategic plan to deploy DevOps by mixing personnel in teams constituted of Dev and Ops rather than mixing their responsibilities (Nybom et al., 2016) at the group level when appropriate. We conducted the research in two phases (Figure 1).

Figure 1. Case Study Research Design.

In Phase 1, we followed five steps to select job roles to interview in a sample of projects: 1) Scientific literature review to familiarise ourselves with knowledge about DevOps and related perceptions and formulate research questions; 2) Full immersion within four DevOps teams for 15 days by the first two authors to observe and interview them in order to compare elements identified from the literature with those present in practice, 3) Interviews by the first two authors with 12 strategists from Group X to incorporate their vision and to validate the sample. We selected five job profiles that evolve significantly with DevOps: two managerial roles, i.e., Product Owner (PO) and Department Manager (DM); two roles in operations, i.e., Production Engineer (PE), who performs tests as Ops (developers also perform tests in a DevOps mode) and Release Manager (RM), and one role in design (Architect, AR). We did not select developers for the following reasons. During our full immersion, we observed 60 developers and interviewed 8. Although developers’ work methods are certainly impacted by DevOps, these interviewees did not identify any specific or significant challenges with DevOps. Developers must consider additional constraints when coding in a DevOps context (e.g., automation, test for continuous integration) but coding activity remains central. Senior management also considered that this evolution towards DevOps was probably not as challenging for developers as it was for the other selected roles. 4) Preparation and validation of the interview guide for the second phase. After submitting the guide to strategists of Group X and to several researchers, we pretested it with five selected profiles and adjusted it, i.e., one question was simplified, and two questions were added (see Appendix A). 5) Three criteria were retained to select the sample: 5.1) the size of the project measured in terms of number of employees assigned to the project (Rolland et al., 2016); 5.2) the degree and nature of outsourcing on the project: fixed-price (FP) contracts could be problematic when working in agile mode, thus distinguishing FP from other outsourcing arrangements and insourcing, coded as “other” (see Table 1); 5.3) the level of maturity in terms of the agile-to-DevOps transition, described below.

The present study refines the criteria of the agile-to-DevOps maturity levels proposed by A. Hemon et al. (2019b). This maturity is at level 1 when teams utilise agile methods or develop a hybrid method (Fitzgerald et al., 2006). Agile teams expedite frequent releases because development becomes more iterative. However, DevOps does not yet exist because silos between Dev and Ops remain and this limits sharing. Common culture, automation of releases and common useful metrics are absent. Agile-to-DevOps maturity level 2 entails the use of Continuous Integration. When practicing continuous integration, the Ops function and the Dev function are aligned. Both functions perform unit and non-regression tests (Ståhl & Bosch, 2014), synchronised with code development. Continuous integration is a fail-fast checking practice that allows developers to merge their codes into a shared version control, using tools such as GitLab or Jenkins, and that allows the build automation and the code testing to become part of the version control. Developers can have feedback on the quality of their code very quickly because operations and quality assurance engineers have implemented automated code testing solutions like Selenium upstream. Continuous integration enables faster code merges with higher quality and less risk (Duvall et al., 2007). At Agile-to-DevOps maturity level 3 or Full DevOps, Continuous Delivery/Deployment means that integration tests, end-to-end tests, performance tests, and user acceptance tests are performed by the DevOps team based on automated processes and tools and are co-designed by the Development and Operations functions (Chen, 2017).

## 3.2. Data collection

We collected data over a period of eight months, from January to August 2017. Statistics, documents (e.g., three company-level HR agreements, three union documents on agile and DevOps, eight strategic-level presentations – HR, Organisational – Business), observations (pictures, 4 hand-drawn maps, field notes) and interviews performed in the first phase clarified the context of the 59 interviews in the second phase. This use of multiple sources of evidence in phase 1 corroborated the findings and strengthened the validity and credibility of the study (Yin, 2013). For instance, interviews done and data collected in phase 1 highlighted the challenge of work cadence in agile and DevOps. In phase 2, we confirmed through thematic analysis that the theme of working cadence was an important element of both risk and dissatisfaction. Phase 2 interviews were conducted by three interviewers, mostly by the first and the last authors. 59 interviews with DM, PO, AR, PE and RM, lasting 90 minutes on average, were recorded and transcribed.

## 3.3. Data Analysis

Our thematic analysis of the discourses followed the sixphase framework provided by Braun and Clarke (2006), which ofers an accessible approach for analysing qualitative data (see Appendix B). Thematic analysis is an appropriate method when the goal is to develop insights into patterns of meaning (themes) across data (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Researchers can thus go beyond a rich description of data and “see and make sense of collective or shared meanings and experiences. Identifying unique and idiosyncratic meanings and experiences found only within a single data item is not the focus of TA” (Braun & Clarke, 2012, page 57). Thematic Analysis primarily serves to identify common patterns in the way some topics are expressed, and, second, to situate these commonalities relative to the research questions. However Thematic Analysis can be used in various ways: inductive or a priori coding, semantic or latent (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Although we used systematically the same guiding questions (see Appendix A), those were not constructed to test a theory or a priori ideas. Thus, our thematic analysis was inductive. In addition, it was semantic rather than latent as shown in our paper that exploited the skills part (A. Hemon et al., 2019a).

After familiarising ourselves with the data, in the second step of the six-phase Braun and Clarke (2006), i.e., generating initial codes, we drew a cognitive map of each interview using Decision Explorer software from Banxia (see an example of an extract of an architect’s cognitive map in Appendix C). Three researchers agreed on the methodology of coding cognitive maps using the software and distributed the interviews to be coded. They then jointly ensured the homogeneity of the coding of interviews and the comparability of the cognitive maps. To improve reliability, each code was triangulated by at least two researchers, including the first and last authors, and codes were consistent and similar. During the third step, namely searching for themes, these maps also allowed us to highlight diferent factors and diferent themes for each individual, through the concepts mobilised throughout their discourse. For this study we did not use cognitive causal mapping completely (e.g., Reid et al. (2010)). We started with cognitive maps only to discover which factors were associated with each concept and which themes (e.g., collaboration and resource optimisation) emerged throughout the discourse. Because our interview guide consisted of sections separating the four interview questions and related concepts (i.e., [dis]satisfaction, risks, and work conditions, see Appendix A), interviewees did not systematically mention causal links between factors relating diferent concepts. In the fourth step (reviewing themes), we considered the themes individually and relative to the others. To clarify how diferences between agile and DevOps could reveal diferent team orchestration needs, we used the maps to interpret how factors interacted and interrelated between the diferent theoretical constructs when themes of collaboration and optimisation emerged in respondents discourse. For each theme, the corresponding subset of an interview was selected when at least two factors belonging to two diferent constructs surfaced in the respondent’s discourse. On a given theme, we then compared the aggregate discourse of the agile respondents with that of the DevOps respondents (at level 2 or 3) and discussed the themes (fifth step – Defining and naming themes). Thus, we did not systematically compare all the 12 projects individually, but we identified similarities and diferences between the group of four agile projects (level 1) and the group of eight DevOps projects (Level 2 and 3).

## 4. FINDINGS

Our qualitative method identified common and specific factors across projects in agile mode and DevOps mode. Results are systematically structured by Ghobadi and Mathiassen (2016) classification into three types of factors, related to the team, the process or the context. Team factors relate to team diversity (e.g., diferent backgrounds and languages, physical distance between team members), team perceptions (e.g., motivation), and team technical and social capabilities. Process factors concern project communication (between the client and the team) and project organisation (e.g., long split sprints, tight schedule). Contextual factors relate to project technology (e.g., lack of high-quality collaborative technologies) and project setting (e.g., cultural and budget issues or bureaucratic organisation).

Due to length limitations and given the scant mention of DevOps in the IS literature, for the common factors to DevOps and agile, and especially for DevOps-specific ones, we use only verbatim information from DevOps teams. For agile-specific factors, we present verbatim information from agile team members. We then compare how collaboration and resource optimisation are orchestrated in agile and DevOps teams.

## 4.9.2. Representation related to collaboration in agile

In the interviews with agile team members, the theme of collaboration appears diferently given certain specificities. 10 of the 19 respondents covered this theme by addressing at least two concepts in their discourse (see Table 8).

First, comparison of Tables 8 and 7 reveals more dissatisfaction factors and perceived risks than does the representation of individuals in DevOps (“Unfulfilled expectation of even closer collaboration” and “Need for collective endorsement of the team approach”). For these individuals, the opportunity ofered by agile methods of regularly teaming with other trades raised strong expectations. Some team members feel dissatisfaction and feel that their colleagues do not listen when these sharing opportunities are ineffective. This source of dissatisfaction also falls into perceived risks and dificulties (“Need for collective endorsement of the team approach”) because some believe that each team member must play the agile game to invest in the collective approach, collaborate suficiently, and maximally share information. These two specificities are identified in the discourses on agile practices; they are not found in the DevOps people’s discourses. Agile thus represents a first level of collaboration while DevOps represents a second, more intense and extended level of collaboration, often following a first experience in agile.

In contrast, suggestions for improving work conditions are much fewer than in the DevOps representations (see Table 7). These recommendations are not meant to enhance the team’s performance, as for the DevOps teams, but rather to ensure the first level of collaboration needed in agile. Thus, these work conditions aim to remove the sources of dissatisfaction and risks in the practice of agile methods, as F. Herzberg (1964) asserts, and do not seem directly related to satisfaction.

## 4.9.3. Representation related to DevOps project management resource optimisation

To a lesser extent, 14 DevOps team members identified several factors in the resource optimisation theme (cf. Table 9).

Table 9 shows the importance of dissatisfaction factors and perceived risks in the resource optimisation theme. These factors are related to the sequencing of the DevOps approach, creating constant time pressure, sources of dissatisfaction and even sufering. Sequencing in agile is limited to the development area, where developers use tools like Kanban board via Git. Contrary to DevOps projects, sequencing does not integrate operations activities. The DevOps continu ous flow-based principle (continuous integration, continuous deployment, continuous learning and experimentation) derives from cross-functional planning, cross-functional prioritisation and sequencing based on sharing and automation. These activities require greater risk anticipation and are very demanding. Findings indicate that people are sensitive to the quality of the final application delivered and regret the lack of time resulting from the shortening of deadlines imposed by the DevOps approach, hence reducing the depth of their work. Our interviewees afirmed tha sequencing is more complex, dificult and impactful in a DevOps context. A second source of time pressure dissatisfaction emerged in operators’ discourses (PE and RM). This dissatisfaction concerns the concomitant dificult management of projects and incidents, because the DevOps approach requires their strong involvement. This time pressure also generates risks, notably because of accelerating rates and the priority management imposed on the team members. Several respondents also mention the human risks of exhaustion, given that there is no recovery time between projects, training, or standby time. These three factors are supplemented by another risk (“Rushed project launches and post hoc corrections”) related to time allocated to the upstream phases of the project, and to the quality of the application put into production. This echoes the satisfaction factor, to which these individuals are sensitive. Suggestions for improving work conditions logically lead to better allocation of resources to projects (time and staf) to counter sources of dissatisfaction and related risks.

## 5. DISCUSSION

Our discussion relative to the contributions of this paper distinguishes between: (a) empirical propositions (Berlin, 1950) that stem from first order observations made by participations and comparisons and findings highlighted by us in the previous section, and (b) general propositions (Berlin, 1950) that articulate relationships between several higher level constructs into a theory of sharing and automation orchestration for job satisfaction. These general propositions and theory still need to be tested (Godfrey & Hill, 1995) and might be extended to other IS related phenomena.

## 5.1. Empirical Propositions

Several researchers underlined the scant literature on DevOps phenomena (Erich et al., 2014; Smeds et al., 2015). Hence, our research was motivated by the desire to fill this gap regarding DevOps practices, to determine if breaking the silos between Development and Operations afected job (dis)satisfaction. We made theoretical contributions based on three research questions. We capture these contributions through six empirical propositions related to satisfaction, dissatisfaction, risk, work conditions and orchestration, four of them being comparative and two of them (EP1 and EP2) specific to DevOps. Our first research question asked:

Q1) How do DevOps team members perceive job (dis)satisfaction and risks?

We established correspondence between the ten satisfaction factors (cf. Table 11) and the five core job characteristics (skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, feedback) of Job Design highlighted by Hackman and Oldham (1980).

Our findings difer from those of the study by Tripp et al. (2016) on job satisfaction within agile teams. They found that only three of the job characteristics (job autonomy, task identity, and task significance) contributed to job satisfaction. In contrast, our thematic analysis indicated that feedback and skill variety were also job satisfaction factors in both agile and DevOps teams. These findings are consistent with those of McHugh et al. (2011), which identified feedback and variety of work and skills as sources of job motivation in agile projects and with those of Morris and Venkatesh (2010) which identified as well feedback and skill variety as sources of job satisfaction when implementing a new ERP system. We went beyond these findings specifying which elements related to feedback and skill variety were sources of job satisfaction for the DevOps team.

These specificities can be explained by the conceptual foundation of DevOps, which aims to extend agile principles and values to the end-to-end process. In a DevOps environment, the collective ability to collaborate and group dynamics is fundamental, and the collaborative process must allow swift continuous improvement. The fact that DevOps transforms collaboration into integration is a source of job satisfaction. Hence, regarding Q1, we posit a first empirical proposition:

EP1: DevOps team members experience all core job characteristics leading to job satisfaction with more specific factors related to skill variety than do agile teams (see Table 6).

Furthermore, DevOps team have a wider vision of risks, as Nybom et al. (2016) noted. This may also explain why people perceive DevOps as riskier. However, our findings suggest that because of more numerous specific perceived risks, DevOps is not a panacea (see Table 6). In addition, functional problems can be hidden (see Table 4) or even created by automation. Hüttermann (2012) highlighted that faulty and/or hastened deployments of software exist even in a DevOps context and create technical debt. Whereas automation lets organisations face mounting pressure to deliver features to customers continuously, organisations still face the challenge of balancing the use of their resources and eforts between the development of new features and the management of technical debt.

Consequently, regarding Q1 we posit that:

EP2: With greater automation, DevOps teams do not quickly perceive risks related to certain functional and technical problems.

Finally, regarding work conditions, DevOps teams mentioned the need for member stability to allow collective routines and sense-making to develop within the team. They seem to be particularly sensitive to changes and to the team’s ability to cope with changes. They are aware that their performance level is highly dependent on the collective interactions of their team. Collective evaluation measures also stand out, along with the sharing of best practices, and highlight the importance of work conditions for sharing. This leads us to posit for Q1 that:

EP3: For DevOps team members, work conditions are critical for developing collective routines, sensemaking and sharing.

Our second research examined:

Q2) Do these perceptions difer between Agile and DevOps, and why?

Our findings show that some perceptions were idiosyncratic to DevOps and did not appear in agile (see Table 11). For instance, some feedback and skill variety factors are specific to DevOps. These factors are likely related to soft skills, notably flexibility, interpersonal skills and teamwork skills (A. Hemon et al., 2019b), which are also more important and diverse for DevOps teams than for agile teams. In particular, swift continuous improvement is required for decision making regarding ways to sequence and automate tasks. In DevOps teams, decision-making involves more constraints in a broader perimeter. DevOps may thus enable greater sequential variety, i.e., numbers of ways to sequence tasks (Pentland, 2003). However, some of these issues may require management approval and feedback which, if limited, hampers autonomy and creates dissatisfaction.

Feedback has also been identified as an agile practice source of job satisfaction by McHugh et al. (2011). They showed that feedback helped team members feel more comfortable and confident and build relationships within the team. Indeed, our findings go beyond those of McHugh et al. (2011), who asserted that feedback was limited among agile teams and that customer feedback was lacking. Lack of feedback from the hierarchy is another source of dissatisfaction (see Table 6). However, we found that the notion of feedback as a source of satisfaction comes from both the team members and the client.

Regarding dissatisfaction, in addition to the lack of feedback from the hierarchy, two other specific factors (see Table 6) hinder collaboration: operator overload and roles subject to external criticism. These three sources of dissatisfaction for DevOps teams are related to the project management context, namely overly tight coupling (see Table 3).

Regarding Q2 we posit that:

EP4: DevOps teams experience greater diversity of dissatisfaction sources than do agile teams due to frictions with their organisational environment and to specific needs in terms of coupling that do not exist for agile practices.

Unlike agile, DevOps risks are mostly related to the organisational context. For example, lack of coaching, planning constraints for new projects, and administrative red tape (see Table 6). This finding is fully congruent with Humble and Molesky (2011) paper on continuous delivery, which asserts that from a risk perspective, continuous delivery is more eficient; the main barrier is organisational.

Regarding Q2 we posit that:

EP5: DevOps teams’ wider perception of risks reflects their greater sensitivity than agile teams to contradictions in the organisational context that generate risks.

Finally, our third research question sought to understand:

Q3: How can these diferences between agile and DevOps reveal team orchestration needs?

We discovered that agile and DevOps reveal diferent team orchestration needs. We identified two themes important for team orchestration: collaboration (see Tables 7 and 8) and resource optimisation (see Tables 9 and 10). Both themes appear in agile and DevOps teams’ discourses. We found that orchestration is dynamic in an agile context, through collaboration and resource optimisation, but to a lesser extent than in a DevOps environment.

We found that orchestration in DevOps is more delicate and complex than in agile due first to the extension of agile challenges across the Dev and Ops silos, e.g., collaboration with more people with diferent interests, hence more trade-ofs; second, because of the influence of work conditions over the relationships between automation, sharing and risk management on one hand and job satisfaction on the other hand. For example, our findings point out that resource optimisation creates dissatisfaction among DevOps team members because they cannot deepen their work. Consequently, resource orchestration that only aims to optimise such resources could generate dissatisfaction in a DevOps context and would therefore be counterproductive (Hackman & Oldham, 1976, 1980).

Team orchestration requires collaboration and optimisation of the availability of adequate human resources but seems to difer between agile and DevOps teams. However, our findings go beyond Kim et al.’s (2016) assertions that Development and Operations teams need to ensure the availability of the right people and resources to support rollouts. Our paper reveals a higher variety of works improvement and fewer risks regarding collaboration (see Table 7), whereas resource optimisation induces dissatisfaction among DevOps team members (see Table 9) who cannot deepen their work (principle of Lean Thinking) (Fitzgerald & Stol, 2017). Consequently, based on these findings we posit for Q3 that:

EP6: Orchestration in a DevOps context requires a very high level of availability of resources, while orchestration in an agile environment faces the challenge of resource accessibility due to silos.

This richness of ideas regarding work condition improvements is fundamental for continuous integration and the continuous agenda (Fitzgerald & Stol, 2017; Ståhl & Bosch, 2014). Therefore, work condition improvements are more important in a DevOps context.

To summarise, our findings suggest that the DevOps approach ofers team members more sources of satisfaction, but also raises dissatisfaction factors and potentially more various risks, mostly due to organisational factors. Higher risk perception in DevOps leads to deep concern about resource optimisation. An important contribution of this paper is that it includes the need for work condition improvements, which is apparently more important in DevOps, and implies the desire for optimisation.

## 5.2. Theoretical elaboration and implications

Our findings show that in a DevOps context, team members are more focused on resource orchestration, particularly on collaborative and optimising capabilities, than individuals working only in agile. Such findings could be explained by the central roles of sharing and automation in DevOps teams. Their activation modifies software development processes more than with agile methods because DevOps calls for a continuous and eficient adaptation of more resources in a dynamic environment and over a larger perimeter. Inspired by Sirmon et al. (2007), Sirmon et al. (2011), we can say that DevOps, in comparison to agile, afects bundling processes by increasing the possibilities of combining the resources of the firm through greater automation and more sharing.

DevOps also impacts processes of coordination and optimisation by modifying these processes. DevOps transforms coordination processes into integration processes which are supported by automation and sharing. Such integration is continuous in DevOps and impacts team cohesion both positively and negatively. Our findings show that greater integration through greater collaboration on a larger perimeter was a source of job satisfaction. However, this higher integration is also a source of worry, for instance, concerning the need for team stability.

Furthermore, our findings indicate that the main challenge for orchestration in agile seems to be access to resources, while in DevOps access is not the central dificulty. Orchestration in a DevOps context must focus on the high level of availability or resources. In agile, orchestration of resources is fundamental and focuses on the capacity to ensure the availability of the right people and resources at the right moment to support the development process (Kim et al.’s, 2016). According to our findings, in DevOps, orchestration goes to a larger extent and is more sensitive to the environment.

Our findings lead us to propose the following theoretical model of Job Satisfaction when Agile teams move towards DevOps (Figure 2). From our six empirical propositions we formulate three theoretical propositions, which form the basis of a theoretical explanation of job satisfaction when agile teams move towards a DevOps context (i.e., the move from agile to continuous integration and then to continuous delivery and deployment) and constitute our more general theoretical contribution (see Figure 2). As a general principle, the more a team practices DevOps at a higher level, the more orchestration will have to consider and reflect on these propositions to be eficient.

The more advanced the automation implementation, the more the company applies the DevOps approach (Humble & Molesky, 2011) and the more project team members express diferent sources of satisfaction (see Table 2). However, some elements of the organisational context can hinder the functioning of the DevOps project management approach and limit satisfaction (see Table 3). Thus, by introducing work conditions likely to alleviate collaboration problems between team members, the DevOps project management approach will be less disturbed and will increase team members’ satisfaction (see Table 7). Hence our first theoretical proposition:

TP1: Under appropriate work conditions, higher process automation increases team members’ satisfaction when agile teams move towards DevOps.

The second important pillar of DevOps is sharing, an essential element for collaboration between Operations and Development (Riungu-Kalliosaari et al., 2016). Rich collaboration within the DevOps team thus encourages the emergence of group dynamics and continuous improvement. Such dynamics create additional sources of satisfaction for DevOps team members, unlike agile teams (see Table 2). Hackman and Oldham (1980) illustrates convincingly why DevOps teams can experience high satisfaction levels (see Table 11). However, this sharing can be hindered or facilitated by numerous work conditions to which DevOps teams are even more sensitive than agile teams. Notably, collective evaluation measures must be established and shared. Hence our second proposition:

TP2: Under appropriate work conditions, the more Operations and Development roles share within the project team, the higher team members’ satisfaction when agile teams move towards DevOps.

Without the ability to form a collaborative collective and dynamically adapt the speed and cadence at which DevOps teams work, DevOps will generate more risks and degrade satisfaction. Without the ability to optimise resources that can be shared and automated, eficiency and satisfaction will be impeded. The automation and sharing pillars of DevOps are closely interdependent, because sound automation is ensured by increasing the level of sharing between Operations and Development teams. While this DevOps approach provides more diverse sources of satisfaction, the emergence of specific risks can reduce team members satisfaction (see Table 4). Risk management appears crucial. Risks related to automation and to sharing barriers (Ghobadi & Mathiassen, 2016) must be mitigated. As we move closer to continuous delivery, automation and collaborative sharing become closely interdependent and resource availability needs to be managed to keep satisfaction high. Moreover, the sequencing of the DevOps approach induces constant time pressure that generates risks and sources of dissatisfaction (see Tables 3 and 4) that are especially related to reduced time allocated to some project phases. These new risks must therefore be taken into consideration so that they do not hinder DevOps team members’ satisfaction. Such consideration emphasises the need for a favourable organisational context and adequate working conditions (see Table 9). Hence our third proposition:

TP3: Orchestration of interactions between automation and sharing and management of related risks can influence job satisfaction when agile teams transition to DevOps.

Our second theoretical contribution is related to our theoretical lens. The orchestration of software development in contemporary digital ecosystems is deeply impacted by the evolution of its environment. New technologies, new methods and approaches, broader and more versatile human resources, new processes and changing boundaries influence software development. Consequently, as suggested by Tripp et al. (2016), we cannot simply analyse job satisfaction through Hackman and Oldham (1980) restricted lens. Thus, we decided to assess the relativity of our results and findings using a second framework, the Two-Factor theory (F. Herzberg, 1964; F. I. Herzberg, 1966). Integrating Herzberg’s theory led us to integrate environmental factors such as risk perceptions and work conditions. This theory also helped us refine our previous results. We discovered, for instance, tha individuals using DevOps could better develop a specific representation of resources optimisation than agile teams could. This comparison suggests that DevOps requires more complex sequencing, whereas individuals in agile are subject to fewer constraints and therefore perceive fewer risks and dissatisfaction factors. Using Herzberg’s framework, we also found that the DevOps approach is riskier. We showed that individuals’ dissatisfaction was linked to the risks related to non-optimisation of the resources supporting this DevOps approach. In addition, we demonstrated that work condition improvement can both reduce dissatisfaction and increase satisfaction. Consequently, our findings difer in part from F. Herzberg’s (1964), F. I. Herzberg (1966)) Two-Factor Theory and corroborate Maidani’s (1991) criticism and conclusions regarding Herzberg’s theory.

Finally, while Hackman and Oldham (1980) CJC framework convincingly illustrates why DevOps teams can experience high satisfaction levels, some aspects of the DevOps phenomena can be further explained by theorisation of work processes at the team level. For instance, autonomy experienced by individuals in DevOps and agile teams may be related to task variety and sequential variety (Pentland, 2003).

## 6. CONCLUSION

This paper investigated three questions. First, we examined how job (dis)satisfaction and risks were perceived by users of DevOps. We found that DevOps is associated with more satisfaction than dissatisfaction factors, although it is associated with diverse risks. Second, drawing on Hackman and Oldham’s CJC, we explained 1) that DevOps can increase job satisfaction compared with agile because agile teams expect closer collaboration with operations and because DevOps members display additional satisfaction factors related to group dynamics and collaborative process, and 2) that risks may be perceived as higher because DevOps grants practitioners a greater cross-functional view and hence the ability to detect risks beyond silos, but also because of greater automation and time pressure.

Third, we identified two important themes for team orchestration: collaboration and resource optimisation. Whereas risks related to resistance to collaboration are lower for DevOps team members, more risks appear relative to resource optimisation, and raise the need for more work condition improvements for DevOps team members. However, contrary to Herzberg’s predictions, these improvements mainly concern job satisfaction factors, and not solely suppressing dissatisfaction. Moreover, orchestration in a DevOps context needs very high availability of resources, whereas in an agile environment orchestration faces the challenge of accessing the right quantity of resources at the right time because of silos.

Further, we theorised that satisfaction is related to sharing and automation that both stimulate continuous learning and experimentation but also interact and require orchestration in appropriate work conditions. Dissatisfaction among teams relates to work conditions as well as to poor orchestration and insuficient risk management. This study sheds light on a contemporary challenge of orchestration applied to software development in a dynamic environment. We highlighted that the Agile Manifesto has been built on paradoxes, i.e., the “siloed” principle of continuity limited to a “biz-dev” relationship. We demonstrated how DevOps was diferent from agile. However, DevOps has its own paradox as for instance, its inheritance from Lean Management principles, particularly mapping the value stream and waste removal, and the need for resource orchestration that is not only focused on optimisation. Future research would have to determine precisely where the apex of resource optimisation is in a DevOps context before it afects DevOps job satisfaction, and potentially DevOps performance.

Matook et al. (2016) recently proposed that the selforganising characteristics of task autonomy, task and goal interdependencies and team diversity have a positive impact on exploitation in agile teams; whereas task and goal interdependencies do not have an impact on exploration. Considering DevOps as an extension of agile, DevOps focuses on automation and more sharing on a larger perimeter, hence with more task and goal interdependencies. Consequently, it would be worth investigating how the self-organising characteristics of task autonomy and task and goal interdependencies influence DevOps orchestration.

Whereas our theory building case-study is based on a rich sample of projects within a single large company, future research could now investigate these issues in a theory-testing approach or across a wider sample of organisations and consider change management strategies highlighted by Chen (2017). Another limit concerns the structure of the organisation we analysed, which included two distinctive hierarchical lines, i.e., Dev and Ops, with teams working remotely. Such conditions were favourable neither to agile nor to DevOps teams.

Comparisons we made with other firms showed that when there is a unified or centralised hierarchical line with a common dashboard, feedback from the hierarchy was much more important. Under such conditions, feedback characteristics (Hackman & Oldham, 1980), job satisfaction and performance would increase.

We can consequently interpret the studied case as revelatory: despite non-ideal work conditions, agile team members seek broader and closer collaboration, which DevOps can provide (A. Hemon et al., 2019b). Resource orchestration is all the more important as automation and sharing increase and interact strongly in DevOps teams.

## Appendix A – Interview Guide

The interview guide below comprises three sections. The first section is a set of questions regarding the project the interviewee is working on. Through these questions, we verified the compliance of the project with our three criteria, i.e., size, outsourcing and Agile-to-DevOps maturity. These questions also helped us better understand the context and interviewees’ perception of the projects they were working on. The second section focused on the interviewees. We used a list of questions to collect data about the interviewee: identification, age, gender, education level, external and internal professional background, seniority within the company, seniority in the position, experience with agile methods and DevOps, if any, role, and position along the software development pipeline.
