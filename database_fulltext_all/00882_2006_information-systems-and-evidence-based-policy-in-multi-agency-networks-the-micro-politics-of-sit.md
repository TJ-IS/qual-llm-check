# Information systems and evidence-based policy in multi-agency networks: The micro-politics of situated innovation

David G. Wastell <sup>\*</sup>

Nottingham University Business School, Jubilee Campus, Nottingham NG8 1BB, UK

Received 22 March 2005; accepted 8 November 2005 Available online 2 February 2006

## Abstract

The global prevalence of public sector reform, generically dubbed the New Public Management, ofers wide-ranging research challenges for the IS field. This paper addresses two important, though neglected, strands of the modernisation agenda, evidence-based policy and partnership work. The development of a Geographical Information System (known as MADE) to support multi-agency collaboration is described in the domain of crime reduction. The application of MADE to support policy work is contrasted in two local partnership contexts: the statutorily constituted Crime and Disorder Reduction Partnership, and an ad hoc forum instigated by the police to address the specific issue of licensing reform and its impact on alcohol-related violence. Although the GIS infrastructure is the same, the manifestation of EBP was markedly diferent. This is interpreted in terms of diferences in the situated micro-politics of the two settings. In the former, strong regulation by central government has ‘‘translated’’ MADE into a tool for generating performance targets and indicators. The second setting comes much closer to the true spirit of EBP, reflecting the greater autonomy of the network and the strong commitment both to evidence and partnership of its main player. In general, the findings demonstrate the importance of GIS to the policy process, particularly in the domain of multi-agency partnerships. There are a broad range of opportunities for IS research within the modernisation project. The need for ethnographic studies of change at a local level is highlighted, and for the adoption of a more critical stance than has been characteristic of mainstream eGovernment research to date.

## 1. Introduction

The UK public sector, like its equivalent across the world, is subject to much contemporary reform, generically dubbed the New Public Management (NPM). Although the content has shifted over time and there are international variations, there is arguably a discernible global pattern to the change agenda (Hood, 1991, 1995; Christensen and Laegreid, 2001; Schedler, 2003). In the UK, managerialism was the ‘‘dominant strand’’ up to the late 1980s (Rhodes, 1996), succeeded by an increasing emphasis on market competition, through contracting-out, quasi-markets and so on (Hood, 1995). Permeating NPM are several leitmotifs, generally synergistic though not without some cross tensions. There is an over-riding emphasis on performance and accountability, manifest in the pervasive use of numerical targets and performance indicators (Jacobs and Manzi, 2000). Other ‘‘doctrines’’ (Hood, 1995) include the clamour for greater discipline in resource use, the disaggregation of traditional bureaucracies and the shift towards inter-agency collaboration. IT/IS is integral to the reform programme (Heeks, 1999), giving rise to the burgeoning discourse on eGovernment within our field, marked by an eflorescence of dedicated conferences and special issues (e.g., Hackney et al., 2005).

Here, we address an important theme of the reform agenda, thus far largely unexplored within IS, namely the increasing emphasis on ‘‘evidence’’ as the basis for policy-making and governance. Typically such evidence denotes structured, quantitative data of the sort characteristically stored in information systems. For our discipline, the rise of evidencebased policy (EBP) is thus an important development. The paper will examine the nature and problematics of EBP in the context of multi-agency partnerships, a context of significance in its own right. Although central to the ‘‘modernisation’’ programme, scant attention has been paid to public sector partnership within our field, despite the prominent discourse on IS/IT as an enabler of new organisational forms in the business domain.

## 1.1. Evidence-based policy

Like other democratic states, many influences traditionally interweave in the production of public policy in the UK (Jones et al., 2001), including: ideological conviction, party-political wheeler-dealing, democratic debate in parliament (and other forums), the technocracy of the civil service and pressures exerted by institutional interests (e.g., trade unions). In the present era, ‘‘evidence’’ and ‘‘what works’’ pragmatism are in the ascendant (Davies et al., 2001; Mair, 2004). Hough (2004) argues that EBP embodies a ‘‘scientific rationalism’’ that resonates well with the rhetoric of modernisation. EBP is now prominent in most areas of UK social policy: healthcare (Niessen et al., 2000), social welfare (Walker, 2001) and crime management (Nutley and Davies, 2001); it was the central driver in a major national Crime Reduction Programme launched in 1999 that involved extensive collaboration with the criminological research community (MacGuire, 2004). Drawing on Davies et al. (2001), evidence is defined here as empirical data that have been garnered and analysed through a systematic process of enquiry and are available for independent scrutiny and verification. Although the notion of evidence does not preclude qualitative data, there is a natural tendency in EBP to privilege the sort of hard numerical data conventionally associated with information systems. Evaluation, as well as evidence, is also a core tenet of EBP’s scientific rationalism; policies will be adopted that show themselves to be efective, i.e., on the basis of ‘‘what works’’ rather than ideology, intuition or expediency (Davies et al., 2001; Mair, 2004).

Although prominent in the modernisation agenda, the difusion of evidence-based methods into the real-world of policy-making (and professional practice in general) has been desultory. Across the board, numerous studies document the trials and tribulations of the new pragmatism (Davies et al., 2001). Even in healthcare, where there is arguably a more ingrained research culture, evidence-based approaches still face major impediments to their adoption and resistance from practitioners (Niessen et al., 2000). Barriers commonly cited include the unintelligibility of statistical analysis, lack of critical skills to interrogate research evidence, threats to professional autonomy and autocratic management of change (Kajerno et al., 2000; Redfurn, 2003; Johnson and Grifiths, 2001). The partnership context studied here is particularly interesting terrain in which to examine the implemen tation of EBP. On the one hand, the inherent political diversity of multi-agency ventures necessarily complicates the policy process, arguably creating less fertile ground for EBP; alternatively, the greater need for coordination in such inter-organisational structures and the unclaimed political territory of the arena could provide more propitious conditions.

To the extent that EBP engenders policy objectives enshrined in numerical targets, there are clear afinities with another NPM doctrine, that of performance management, which is steadily colonising the UK public sector (Martin and Hartley, 2000; Boyne et al., 2002). Performance management has been fundamental to the reform programmes of successive administrations in the UK, of both political hues. It is encapsulated for New Labour in its Best Value regime (Martin and Hartley, 2000) whose aim is to demonstrate the value of public services to the citizen by following a ‘‘broad but rigorous performance management framework’’ (Jacobs and Manzi, 2000). The need to point to objective and quantifiable measures is now a key strategic priority for public managers, and performance indicators have proliferated through the public sector, a phenomenon which Power (1994) refers to as the ‘‘audit explosion’’. Public Service Agreement (PSA) targets form a key part of this governance regime (for a critical review, see James, 2004). Given their common reliance on hard, numerical data there is an obvious danger of EBP being subsumed by the managerialism of targets and indicators, debasing its scientific rationality into what Hough (2004) calls ‘‘scientific reductionism’’, an impoverished discourse dominated by headline statistics and political opportunism. This will be a theme to which we will return.

## 1.2. Partnership work in local crime policy

As noted, partnership work is of interest in its own right from an IS perspective. Part nership figures conspicuously in the modernisation rhetoric: no longer are public services seen as the monopolistic prerogative of traditional bureaucracies, but they are increasingly to be managed and delivered through networks of agencies: public, private and voluntary. In this centre-less, ‘‘hollowed-out’’ state, the primary role of government is to ‘‘steer’’ not to ‘‘row’’ (Osbourne and Gaebler, 1992), i.e., to make policy and manage performance, not to deliver services per se. Government (i.e., the exercise of central, sovereign power) has been superseded by the concept of ‘‘governance’’, defined as the management of largely self-organising ‘‘policy networks’’ (Rhodes, 1996, 2003). Disaggregation is impelled by a slew of forces, including neo-liberal dogma lionising the superior eficiency of markets and the private sector (Wastell et al., 2004); it is also possible to link the emergence of partnership working to concepts of late modernity and the risk society (Beck, 1992; Allen and Sprigings, 2001). In the UK, partnerships are ubiquitous. For 2001/2, Sullivan and Skelcher (2002) estimate the existence of around 5500 such bodies, formally constituted by central government, in the social policy domain alone, covering areas such as crime, health, education and social care (Stoker, 1999; Taylor, 2001; Balloch and Taylor, 2001). Fountain (2001) coins the term ‘‘virtual state’’ to refer to a polity that is organised increasingly in terms of networks whose operation depends on IS/IT. Information systems are vital in enabling the operation of such inter-organisational structures in the public sector, just as in the business domain; without sharing information to facilitate strategic decision-making and coordinated action, efective and coherent collaboration would seem to be practically infeasible (Pratchett, 1999).

Both EBP and partnership work are well established in crime reduction, the policy domain examined in this paper. In Europe, North America and Australasia, multi-agency collaboration is increasingly seen by politicians and policy-makers as integral to managing crime (Goris, 2001; Mair, 2004). In the UK, the incoming Labour government of 1997 quickly pushed forward its partnership agenda with the enactment of the Crime and Disorder Act (1998) which placed a statutory duty on local authorities, the police and other agencies to work together through Crime and Disorder Reduction Partnerships (also known as Community Safety Partnerships) to tackle crime and anti-social behaviour within each local government district. In Rhodes (2003) typology of policy networks, such partnerships qualify (in principle) as ‘‘intergovernmental networks’’, being characterised by extensive horizontal articulation across local agencies, with local government playing a lead role. The imperative to found crime policy on a firm base of objective evidence is integrally built into the modus operandi of Crime Reduction Partnerships. Legislation requires partnerships to carry out triennial ‘‘audits’’ of the distribution of crime and anti-social behaviour in their locales (MacGuire, 2002; Hough, 2004) to facilitate the development and implementation of shared strategies, the impact of which is to be monitored, again using evidence as the basis of the evaluation.

The idea of a hollowed-out, centre-less state (the so-called ‘‘Anglo-governance’’ school) is not without its critics. Marinetto (2003) argues that far from being weakened, the hand of central government is, if anything, stronger in the new institutional arrangements, though efected indirectly through the remote ‘‘gaze’’ of performance management. The tensions between central regulation and the need to respond to local needs present an demanding challenge for the efective management of public services (Newman, 2005), and there is evidence that tightened central control (emphasising short term, often politically expedient targets) stifles innovation and learning, as well as deterring partner engagement (Glendinning et al., 2002). Such imperatives would not seem propitious for evidence-based approaches which necessarily require longer horizons and a more reflective orientation to policy (Nutley and Homel, 2006). Being formally mandated and subject to central governance, it will be of particular interest to see how the EBP agenda plays out in Crime partnerships, compared to less regulated networks. To this end, the paper will contrast the fortunes of EBP in two distinct partnership settings: the original context of the

Crime Reduction Partnership, and an ad hoc network which has spontaneously sprung up to address a single acute policy issue, alcohol-related violence.

## 1.3. Aims and overview

Although information systems provide the enabling infrastructure for both evidencebased policy and multi-agency collaboration, when we turn to the eGovernment discourse in our field, it is almost completely silent on either subject. A content analysis, for example, of the proceedings of the last four European Conferences on eGovernment revealed that out of over 250 papers, not a single one explicitly addressed the intersection between IS and EBP, and only one paper dealt with multi-agency collaboration as its primary topic. Influential compilations such as Heeks (1999) also neglect both areas, and whilst Fountain’s (2001) seminal text does take networks as its primary topic, it is written from a public administration standpoint, not IS, and does not explicitly use the term eGovernment.

The general aim of the present paper is to extend the discourse on eGovernment within our field to address the issue of EBP in the context of partnership work. We will focus on the application of information systems to facilitate policy development in local crime reduction networks in the UK, drawing on the experience of an on-going project, the Mul ti-Agency Data Exchange (MADE). MADE was instigated to support a group of Crime Reduction Partnerships (in the county of Lancashire) in carrying out their crime audits, and to provide on-going support in planning, delivering and evaluating crime reduction interventions. We will draw on experiences from MADE in order to demonstrate the rel evance of IS to strategic decision-making in partnership work, illustrating the challenges for our field in this relatively neglected terrain. As we shall see, the adoption of EBP was far from smooth and untroubled, reflecting the general trends mentioned earlier The comparison of MADE’s utilisation for policy analysis, and the form taken by the emergent praxis of EBP in the two network types (i.e., mandated versus ad hoc), will be of central interest.

## 2. Methodology and context

A detailed description of the MADE project may be found elsewhere (Wastell et al., 2004) emphasising its user-led methodology and the bearing this had on the original project’s outcome. Here the focus is on the application of MADE to support strategic decision-making, and on the practical reality versus the rhetoric of EBP. The author has been extensively involved with the development and use of MADE since its inception in early 2001. He took a leading role in the production of the original software which involved considerable interaction with users, through workshops, site visits and training. Since then, he has been a member of the MADE Steering group, responsible for advising on its further development and undertaking bespoke policy applications. The two case studies reported here are drawn from his experience as a policy analyst in the latter role.

These commissions have involved considerable user contact: attending partnership meetings, one-on-one interviews, presentations of results and routine discussions. The cases represent ‘‘participant observer’’ accounts based on this direct involvement, using data drawn from a range of sources: documents (policy reports, meeting minutes), field notes and interviews, together with some ad hoc survey data. Where specific evidence is cited, the source is indicated in Table 1, although this summary naturally represents only a small sample of the empirical material on which the narratives are based.

MADE was developed in 2001 to support the crime auditing process in Lancashire, a large and relatively populous county (around 1.5 million people) in the north west of England, comprising 14 local authority (LA) districts. Each district has its own Crime Reduction Partnership, the structure being broadly similar across the county. There are typically two tiers: a high level policy/steering group (made up of senior managers) and a lower level operational group, with a wider membership of practitioner representatives. The two main partners are the Police and the LA; other key partners include: the Fire and Rescue service, the Probation Service and the Health Service. The main product delivered to the partnerships was the Ward Profiler, colloquially known as ‘‘The Interface’’. It comprised a set of aggregate statistics for all 300 ‘‘wards’’ in Lancashire, across the 14 districts. Wards form the basic geographical unit of UK local government; for each ward, around 120 indicators were produced, covering the following principal domains: socio-demographics, crime levels, ofender and victim profiles, other emergency service incidents (i.e., Fire and Ambulance). These indicators were embedded in an Excel spreadsheet, with tools provided to carry out simple analyses (e.g., ward histograms of crime rates). An illustrative screen dump of the main worksheet is shown in Fig. 1, depicting normalised burglary statistics for one large urban area, the city of Preston. As well as the Profiler, partnerships were provided with the Full MADE Database containing detailed, spatially coded data relating to individual crimes, ofenders, victims, fire and ambulance call-outs, etc. MADE is in efect a multi-agency geographical information system (GIS). This is critical: the presence of a common spatial key enabled both the calculation of geographical statistics according to any defined spatial unit as well as the integration of data from diferent agencies and sources to support sophisticated criminological analysis (see Fig. 2).

## 3. Findings

## 3.1. Case setting 1: policy-making in crime reduction partnerships

The primary purpose of MADE was to facilitate the 2001 crime audit. External evaluations were very positive; support from users has led to the project’s continuation, and MADE was used again to support the 2004 audit. Whilst generally positive, these earlier assessments do not reveal exactly how, and to what extent, MADE has influenced the policy process. Here we will focus more critically on its practical deployment to develop evidence-based policy. Much of the author’s involvement has been with Preston Crime Reduction Partnership, and this provides the first case setting. Preston is a major conurbation (pop. 130k) in the centre of the County. Its audit and strategy were produced by small team, led by the City’s community safety manager. The Audit was 32 pages long [DM1, Table 1], organised in a series of short chapters, each addressing a particular theme: youth crime, domestic burglary, etc. The centrepiece of each chapter was a statistical summary, produced with MADE, of crime prevalence relating to that theme followed by a ward level map, showing its distribution across the town. Strategies are typically less analytical documents than Audits, glossily produced for public consumption; Preston’s sets out a series of broad aims in each priority area, and specific targets are articulated (e.g., to reduce domestic burglaries by 22.5%).

The foregoing suggests that, despite MADE’s potential for sophisticated policy analysis, actual application in Preston has not moved beyond the crude mapping of crime statistics to set broad objectives and numerical targets. In this regard, Preston is typical. In a survey of all the 2001 audits in Lancashire, Wastell et al. (2004) found a preponderant reliance on the standard outputs provided by the ward profiler (e.g., simple histograms), with little or no additional analysis. The full MADE database had not been used at all, and datasets from non-police agencies had received scant use. The limited impact of MADE on policy work is corroborated by the following vignette. In early 2003, the author was commissioned by Preston’s community safety manager to provide in-depth analysis focusing on several key policy areas. A detailed report was written [DM2] which contained a series of detailed analyses. Fig. 2 provides an example. The Report was presented at the partnership’s Steering Group meeting in July 2003. Although politely received, little of substance has since transpired. When pressed recently, the manager tactfully replied that ‘‘The Group found the presentation interesting, and we’ve gone through the Report and kept the learning points in mind’’ [IV1].

Attendance at further meetings provides additional evidence of the marginal influence of MADE on policy debate. As noted, most partnerships have an operational group, which meets regularly to review progress against targets and to coordinate activities. Appendix A provides an example of the ‘‘crime discourse’’ at this level, illustrating the deliberations of a typical operational team. In the main, such ‘‘front-line’’ discourse involved the exchange of anecdotal information, lay ‘‘criminological’’ theorisation, informal updates and spontaneous negotiation of new initiatives. Such discussions were lively and clearly valuable, in terms of generating cohesion, shared understanding and social capital, but rather removed from the objective, rational-technical discourse of EBP!

The general picture in our first policy forum, the formal Crime Reduction Partnership, is of MADE playing a limited role in policy deliberations. Much of the local crime discourse is informal, parochial and anecdotal, with statistical evidence figuring as a marginal player. The predominant influence of MADE has been through the audit process, leading directly to the setting of narrow crime-based targets, with in-depth analysis conspicuous by its absence. The increasing pressure on partnerships (like all public sector bodies) to account for their performance in terms of targets and objectives is reflected in the comments of a senior member of one Regional Crime Reduction team [FN1]:

We’re about to start negotiating formal contracts around overall targets and individual ones. We’ll be held to account centrally, as well as us holding partnerships to account. If we’re given a higher target, we’ll pass it down, we’ll expect more . . . Now we’ve got clarity, we didn’t have that before. The bottom line is clear. . .

The conceptualisation of MADE as a component in a top-town performance management system was eloquently illustrated at a recent MADE user group meeting, convened to discuss the future development of the project. After over half an hour’s somewhat desultory discussion of possible new directions (including the need for more sophisticated analytic tools) suddenly the debate turned to the use of MADE to assist in the production of performance statistics. The following exchange [FN2] took place between two community safety representatives:

AS: It’s more of a performance management thing. .

DH: Yes, I’m thinking of the PSA targets, a statistical tool, maybe reporting twice a year or quarterly. . .

AS: it would be useful if partnerships could submit their targets, they all have diferent ones, and the targets were reported automatically, every partnerships performance indicators, we’ve got 12. . .

DH: that would be great, it would save us loads of work

Further enthusiastic discussion ensued, leading the author to ask whether the automatic generation of performance indicators should be the primary role for MADE, to which there was universal assent.

## 3.2. Case setting 2: the NightSafe Board

The Crime Reduction Partnership is not the only policy setting where MADE has been deployed. In June 2004, the author was commissioned by the NightSafe Board, another multi-agency forum, to undertake a review of the likely impact of imminent changes in licensing deregulation.<sup>2</sup> NightSafe had recently been brought into being by Lancashire Constabulary to coordinate alcohol policy across the county. The Police had major concerns about the possible adverse impact of the proposed legislative changes. A recent assessment by the Constabulary [DM3] had concluded that:

The impact of extending drinking hours has the potential to lead to even more antisocial behaviour, and whilst it is not possible to predict exactly what will happen when the Act is implemented, preparations must be implemented now in an endeavour to reduce the threat to resources, including both financial and welfare implications.

NightSafe comprises representatives from all the County’s Crime Reduction Partnerships, the health sector, the county council, and is chaired by a senior police oficer. The commitment to a ‘‘scientific’’, evidence-based approach, and the need to work in concert with partners is well attested in the opening remarks of the NightSafe’s Chair and a senior colleague at the Board’s inaugural meeting [FN3]:

Chair: NightSafe was originally conceived in Blackpool, as an intelligence-led, multiagency response, a structured approach, to attack the binge drinking culture. There’s a menu of tactical options but we need to be able to say what works. . . The Constabulary can’t tackle alcohol-related harm on its own, we need partners. . .

Senior police colleague: The Group is about sharing best practice. . . there’s lots of innovative work going on. We want to bring it under a ‘‘single badge’’ . . . We’re looking at spreading good practice, such as street drinking bans. . .

Two reports were produced by the author, the first being an international literature review of research on the relationships between policy interventions, alcohol availability and alcohol-related harm. The overall finding of the research review was to confirm the seemingly self-evident relationship between the supply of alcohol and levels of social harm. It was concluded [DM4] that:

The hoped for benefits of licensing reform hinge on the assumption that staggered closing times will attenuate ‘‘beat the clock drinking’’ reducing closing time ‘‘flash points’’. At best, there is scant evidence for this notion. The evidence tends if anything to say the opposite. The same may be said of deregulation in general: greater regulation, not less, would appear efective in curbing alcohol misuse and its attendant impact.

The second report was a local ‘‘epidemiological’’ analysis of patterns of alcohol-related violence in Lancashire, carried out using MADE. Temporal trends showed that the weekend increase in overall violence levels could be attributed in toto to the use of alcohol, and variations in local licensing policies also showed a clear link between closing times and violence levels (Fig. 3). The density of licensed premises also strongly predicted violence levels (Table 2), again in line with the research review.<sup>3</sup> The final highlight concerns the efectiveness of measures to curb drinking. In July 2003, a street drinking ban had been introduced in the centre of Preston. Analysis showed a 17% fall in serious violent crime after the ban, together with a 9% reduction in ambulance call-outs for vio lent assaults.

## 4. Discussion

As noted in Section 1, the scientific rationality of EBP has not enjoyed an easy passage in translation from rhetoric to practice. Indeed, as an example of these vicissitudes, we need look no further than the Government’s alcohol policy itself which flies com pletely in the face of the research evidence, all of which points in the direction of more rather than less regulation! The general obstacles that have impeded ‘‘progress’’ in the crime domain (Nutley and Davies, 2001) mirror a pattern of ‘‘resistance’’ encountered in other social policy areas (Davies et al., 2001). Cope (2004), for instance, found that ‘‘analytical products’’ were characteristically dismissed as ‘‘wall-paper’’ by police practi tioners, with a range of factors inhibiting their use, including: the privileging of direct experience over de-contextualised data, perceived threats to the autonomy of street oficers, a general lack of understanding of the value of analysis to support policing. EBP constitutes a novel form of policy-making, enabled by formal information systems. Although a common technology was involved in both policy contexts studied here, and there were similar potent injunctions to be evidence-based, the practice of EBP took a markedly diferent shape in the two networks. The two cases thus provide further illustration of the problematics of EBP, highlighting the contingencies of implementation and the putative influence of context, in particular the governance characteristics of the local network.

It is tempting to take a somewhat mundane view of the marginal impact of MADE in the first case setting, as merely a further manifestation of a generic pattern of resistance to EBP. An opportunistic survey at a recent MADE user group, for instance, found significant barriers to the use of statistical methods (Table 4) with many practitioners citing lack of expertise in statistical understanding as a major impediment, followed by problems in interpreting meaning. Indeed, the disinclination to use formal analysis echoes previous research within our discipline on Executive Information System (EIS). MADE provides a form of EIS, and there are obvious resonances between ‘‘resistance’’ to EBP and the problematics of EIS adoption (Setzekorn et al., 2002). The dificulty that our decisionmakers have in relating to formal data recalls Elam and Leidner’s (1995) argument that EIS is incompatible with the natural cognitive style of executives, which is intuitive and people oriented (rather than analytical/directive). The following quote from the Preston Community Safety Manager [IV1] provides an apt illustration of what seems to be a pervasive attitude:

Nutley and Davies (2001) draw on the Rogers’ (1995) difusion theory to address the problematics of EBP adoption. However, by reducing technological innovation to a simple, causal schema bearing on the binary decision to adopt or to reject, Rogers elides both the situated, socio-political nature of innovation and its temporal dynamics (McMaster et al., 1997), features that are emphasised in social constructionist accounts of technological change (e.g., Pinch and Byker, 1984; Barley, 1990). Here we turn to Actor-Network Theory (Latour, 1987) as our main theoretical tool for framing the relationships between technology and human agency (McMaster et al., 1997; Walsham, 1997). ANT sees innovation as a process not a decision, rejecting the crude dualistic separation of humans and machines in favour of their symmetrical partnership in ‘‘actor networks’’ (McMaster and Wastell, 2005). What is key is the alignment between the new technology (an artefact, or novel technique) and the extant network of organisational actors, their ingrained routines, cognitions and priorities for action. Where the ‘‘socio-technical fit’’ is weak, or the technology seen as a serious threat to established interests, there may be resistance, even outright rejection. Where assimilation occurs, the process is one of ‘‘translation’’ rather than adoption, an active, dialectical process endowing meaning to the innovation, modifying and bringing it into alignment with the prior order, whilst at the same time adjusting that order.

Green (2000) contends that bodies of evidence are not ‘‘neutral representations of reality, but are part of complex networks of technology and social relationships’’; the influence of evidence on policy is therefore situated, depending on the embedded agendas of individuals and agencies. It is too simplistic to interpret the present findings in terms of binary adoption decisions, of relative rejection of a technology in one setting and its enthusiastic adoption in another. EBP is an emergent actor-network, populated by human and technical elements, configured by external ‘‘structural’’ influences interweaving with endogenous forces. As a ‘‘technological actant’’, MADE has actually been assimilated in both partnership contexts, though its utilisation is markedly diferent, reflecting distinct patterns of translation and alignment in the two policy settings. The difering outcomes seem best understood in terms of the local ‘‘micro-politics’’, i.e., the internal priorities and agendas of partners and the influence of outside pressures. Partnerships are inherently complex and potentially conflictual, given the range of perspectives and agendas ‘‘around the table’’ (Green, 2000). As well as the local actors, central government, though not physically represented, is a powerful force, exercising surveillance and ‘‘remote control’’ through its technology of performance management. Crime Reduction Partnerships are ‘‘mandated’’ networks, constituted by statute: audits are a legal requirement, with policy objectives to be articulated in numerical performance targets. A top-level priority is to generate this audit and to provide the on-going performance statistics required by central government, operating though its regional proxies.

It is thus perfectly explicable that MADE is primarily perceived (i.e., translated) as a tool for performance management in this context, a handy mechanism for generating targets and indicators. Central regulation has again constrained innovation (Nutley and Homel, 2006), here relating to the enactment of EBP in these mandated networks. Scientific rationalism has given way to Hough’s crude reductionism (Hough, 2004), with the evi dence-base achieving limited penetration and enrichment of the local policy discourse. In general terms, this was the same pattern as manifested some years earlier in the national Crime Reduction Programme. Despite optimistic long-term plans, the Programme collapsed within 3 years, with the idealism of the original initiative running aground on the rocks of the short-term, managerialism which dominated the concerns of local crime partnerships (MacGuire, 2004; Hough, 2004). A strikingly diferent translation occurred in our second setting. The NightSafe Board, far from being centrally mandated, is an example of a truly autonomous network. It is not accountable in the same direct way to central government. It has developed according to local need, and is strongly driven by the Police, actuated by acute concerns over the growing problem of alcohol fuelled violence and fears over the exacerbating impact of liberalisation. The scientific rationality of EBP had clear appeal in this context, appearing to align perfectly with the underlying strategic imperative to take urgent, coordinated action, supported by evidential analysis and evaluation.

Although evidence was certainly more potent, it would be somewhat naı¨ve to see this as a simple triumph for EBP, as evincing its authentic spirit, with policy flowing directly from the research evidence. Green (2000) argues that practitioners operate in terms of a ‘‘naturalistic epistemology’’, in stark contrast to the positivism implicit in EBP. Creating a consensus for action, especially in a heterogeneous collective such as a partnership, involves the artful appeal to a range of sources of authority: professional expertise, local knowledge, common sense, personal anecdote (indeed, the very enactment of professional identity involves the display of such local, in-depth knowledge). Appeals to common sense are highly persuasive in most lay situations (Green, 2000), and the evidence of EBP certainly does not speak for itself in this domain. Indeed, its voice is inherently weak; research-style evidence is all too easily dismissed as ‘‘mere statistics’’ or ‘‘purely academic’’, however valid it might be in the scientific domain (Green, 2000; Cope, 2004). When judged against a naturalistic epistemology which privileges the individual and the concrete over the general and the abstract (Green, 2000), the rhetoric of EBP is weak, and strong advocacy is required if evidence is to have any influence on decision-making. Discourse in formal crime partnerships is manifestly dominated by personal knowledge, common sense experience and anecdote, and EBP played a relatively marginal role in this epistemological arena. In NightSafe, the advocacy of EBP was strong, being explicitly articulated by the Police who forcefully led the network. There was certainly a degree of openness to the rationality of EBP, and whilst the ‘‘body of evidence’’ did not drive policy, it had strong rhetorical power here, being invoked by the dominant political actor to add force to a policy agenda, albeit partially in place prior to the research.<sup>4</sup>

Having addressed our primary concern with EBP, we will round of with some brief comments on multi-agency partnership. Despite the burgeoning corpus of eGovernment research, little work in our discipline has been directed at inter-agency collaboration, even though the shift from a centralised, bureaucratic state to a de-centred, multi-level milieu of policy networks is one of the hallmarks of the modernisation agenda. The application of IT to enable new organisational forms hardly needs reiterating, and there is extensive research on the role of inter-organisational systems to facilitate partnership in diverse commercial domains (Allen et al., 2000; Wastell et al., 2004), although as yet meagre work on public sector collaboration. Wastell et al. (2004) provide a more detailed consideration of the role of IS in facilitating multi-agency cooperation, in particular highlighting the barriers to information sharing and the methodological lessons to be drawn from projects such as MADE. Certainly, information systems can promote more efective collaboration by supporting the formal administrative structures and decision-making processes of partnership work, building trust, mutual understanding and social capital (Thacher, 2004). Wastell et al. (2004) propose the term ‘‘Partnership Decision Support System’’ to refer a shared information system and suite of analysis tools enabling efective collaboration between partner agencies. A range of research issues present themselves around this concept, including the need for ethnographic studies to determine what sort of support policymakers and analysts need, and how it should be delivered.

## 5. Conclusions and future research

The overall aim of this paper has been to open up the discourse on eGovernment within IS, bringing into our remit more of the programme of institutional restructuring that is underway. Although the local, situated nature of the present work limits generalisation, nonetheless some general conclusions may be essayed. The rise of EBP gives information systems a new, pivotal role in the contemporary policy process, a prominence not enjoyed in more traditional policy-making regimes. The research highlights the practical potential of GIS to support strategic decision-making, especially in the context of multi-agency collaboration. GIS is particularly salient given the geographical way that the responsibilities of public bodies are demarcated, i.e., in terms of spatially defined jurisdictions. The presence of a common spatial key enables datasets from diferent agencies to be conjoined, as we saw, for example, in the evaluation of the street drinking curfew. Askew et al. (2005)

and Boulos (2004) provide useful parallel discussions of GIS policy applications in the environmental and health domains.

Despite this potential, we have seen a pattern of resistance to EBP. These vicissitudes have theoretical significance beyond the present substantive domain, relating to our general understanding of the nature of technological innovation and how it should be researched. The contrasting fortunes of EBP in the two settings clearly bring out the situated nature of innovation and the subtleties of human agency. Whilst difusionist models may give some crude theoretical purchase on salient features of technology and context, innovation cannot be reduced to such comforting, positivistic simplicities. We have seen that technological change is an active, dialectical process of translation, with local micro-politics decisively shaping the way that technologies are moulded as they are absorbed (or indeed rejected). Ethnographic studies, well exemplified by Green (2000), are clearly needed to bring out these nuances, embracing contextualist principles which do full justice to the complexity of causality and agency in organisational settings (Pettigrew, 1990).

The modernisation project as a whole, especially those elements encompassing EBP, performance management and policy networks, is manifestly fertile ground for IS research. There is a general tendency in our field to privilege the private sector over the public; the overwhelming majority of research papers in mainstream IS journals deal with the former. Yet, IT investments in the public sector are vast and the transformations sought are at least as challenging, if not more so given the complexity of most public sector organisations compared to their business counterparts. There is certainly much interesting work to be done, and we are needed too. There is a general lack of capacity within the public sector to carry forward the change agenda, evidenced by the extensive use of consultants by both central and local government, and collaborative research projects with academe have an important contribution to make in building this capacity (e.g., Buckeridge et al., 2002; Wastell et al., 2004).

Within this agenda, there is room for research in all methodological traditions and covering a wide spread of theoretical and practical concerns. A strength of IS research is its pluralism, including a long-standing critical tradition. EBP embodies something of a positivist orientation (Campbell, 2005; Hammersley, 2005), well worthy of critical interrogation. Quantification in general is deeply inscribed in the new managerialism, and research directly aimed at performance management, as well as EBP, is clearly called for, as information systems form an essential component of the new governance paradigm. The new managerialism raises many important questions relevant to our discipline. Performance management is not a neutral technical practice: it represents a system of surveillance (Jacobs and Manzi, 2000) and the canon of research on IS failure, going right back to the classic studies of Markus (1983) and Keen (1981), strongly suggests that information systems garnering such performance data will be resisted, overtly and covertly (e.g., through the massaging of data). The new paradigm is not uncontroversial, to put it mildly, and a critical stance will be required in guiding and informing our research.

## Appendix A. Crime discourse at the front-line

As noted in the main text, Crime Reduction Partnerships typically have a two-tier structure, with a high level policy/steering group and a lower level operational team. The author regularly attends the meetings of the operational group of Erewhon’s partnership (a pseudonym, referring to a mixed rural/urban area in the south part of the County). The meeting on December 23rd, 2003, is typical of the deliberations of this group, and is summarised here from field notes and minutes. 14 individuals were present, representing the Police (3 attendees, including a senior oficer), the Fire Service, the County Council (Youth and Community Service), the Borough Council (Parks Manager and Environmental Health), the local leisure centre and golf club, and two local housing associations. The meeting was chaired by the Community Safety Coordinator of Erewhon Borough Council (EBC).

The meeting began at 10 AM. Following apologies and ‘‘matters arising’’, there was a cursory report of November’s crime figures for the Borough as a whole, together with those of the previous November for comparison. The figures were classified by major crime categories (e.g., burglary, vehicle crime), but were not broken down by wards or subjected to any further analysis. Nothing exceptional was noted, and the meeting quickly moved on to spend considerable time reviewing funding opportunities for special initiatives and the progress of on-going bids. Thereafter a series of parochial issues were discussed: e.g., around vandalism to a local leisure centre, on-going anti-social behaviour orders (then a new government initiative to crack down on low level disorder), a spate of deliberate fires in one locality, a sustained problem of juvenile nuisance in another, a forthcoming educational initiative around alcohol misuse to be held in conjunction with the local alcohol trade.

The informal, almost anecdotal, character of these discussions is well captured in the following meeting minute:

7. Erewhon Leisure Centre – MG said that they were still having problems with the rear car park and they also had an abandoned vehicle that needed removing. One person had been spotted hanging around the corridors, he was monitored and appears to have stopped doing that. MG had received fire safety advice on the waste bins from SG, which had been useful. MG had contacted the ICT unit at EBC regarding the Smartwater property marking scheme. He had also spoken to DC about possible diversionary activities in January. ZB and LB had given advice on crime prevention, which had been useful and he had also been given the relevant information on drugs and the law. They are looking at the possibility of obtaining a mobile skate park for the centre’s use. He asked for more information on the Tower Project and possible links with Beyond Sport. ACTION: AR to contact AW for direct communication.

Much ‘‘lay’’ criminological opinion was exchanged in these discussions, e.g., that grafiti, although not seen as a significant crime in itself, was asserted to give a sense of neglect and decline that can usher in more serious forms of criminal damage (e.g., arson) and lawlessness. The meeting closed at 11:55. In nearly 2 h of discussion, less than 10 min had been taken up with ‘‘hard’’ quantitative evidence, either regarding performance against targets, the quantitative evaluation of past interventions, or anything approaching formal criminological enquiry!
