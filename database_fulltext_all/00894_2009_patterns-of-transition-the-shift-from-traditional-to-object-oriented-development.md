Abstract: While many of the changes that information systems professionals encounter are incremental requiring only modifications to an existing mind-set, others represent radical changes that require a fundamental shift in mind-set in order to fully benefit from the change. The goal of this study was to examine how software development concept thinking changes as individuals shift their mind-set to the object-oriented software development approach. The results indicate that the transition may not be as simple as a single “aha!” moment, but rather a series of cognitive shifts as constructs from the old mind-set are replaced by constructs in the new mind-set. In addition, this study identifies problems that may be encountered during the transition due to a lack of cognitive fit, confusion, and reverting to an inappropriate mind-set. The findings from this study may be used to decrease the learning curve and minimize the number of cognitive shifts experienced during the transition.

Key word s and ph rases: learning models, object-oriented approach, organizational change, personnel IS, personnel training, software development.

We are livin g in an in creasin gly in ovative world where not only are rapid incremental changes being made to the way we work, the way we live, and the way we play, but radical shifts are forcing changes to the way we think about work, life, and play. Within the field of information systems (IS), methods and techniques are soon obsolete and IS personnel must frequently transition to new roles, new technologies, and new approaches [50]. As such, IS personnel will need to retool or they will become obsolete themselves. Keeping up with these changes, and keeping people up to date with the latest technologies and methods, is a competitive necessity in today’s organizations.

Some technological changes facing IS professionals may be perceived as incremental (e.g., upgrading to new a version of an operating system), while others may be perceived as more radical (e.g., moving to enterprise resource planning [ERP]). With incremental change, the individual makes relatively small adjustments or refinements to his or her current practices or skills [51], in effect adding information to an existing knowledge base. For example, when an individual is introduced to a new version of an operating system currently in use, he or she is generally familiar with the basic concepts, functions, and structure and simply adds knowledge of a new function or menu. The new knowledge is integrated into his or her knowledge base for the existing operating system. On the other hand, radical change “goes beyond augmenting status quo” [51, p. 331] and requires a shift to fundamentally different practices. When faced with radical change, people who are experts in one way of thinking must transition to a significantly different way of performing their tasks or approaching problems. Not only do these changes require new knowledge integration, but often a change in mind-set<sup>1</sup> is needed to use the new knowledge effectively. For example, when moving to ERP systems, the individual must learn the new concepts associated with the ERP system (e.g., Advanced Business Application Programming [AB P]) and change the way he or she approaches process design and development to conform to the ERP. The “next new thing” is service-oriented architecture, a technological innovation that will require “deep and radical” organizational changes: “a process of adoption and adaptation that by definition cannot occur overnight” [56, pp. 25–26].

The problems associated with a shift in mind-set are especially acute in the area of software development. Historically, legions of programmers were required to develop information systems from requirements documents created by relatively few software engineers and even fewer systems analysts. Today, more and more high-level analysts and software engineers are needed while the need for programmers is expected to remain level at best or decline dramatically at worst [16, p. 11].

This move in personnel needs from low-level coding to high-level analysis has created a dual problem: large numbers of highly skilled programmers are being displaced while organizations lack the more abstract skills provided by well-trained analysts. Unfortunately, the move from programmer to analyst is not easy [21], and several researchers have documented the differences in thinking and skill sets for each job [76]. Losing these experienced programmers would have a significant impact on an organization as they have a vast amount of valuable business domain knowledge that will be lost if they are unable to make the move from programmer to analyst.

To maximize performance in their new role, developers need to change their thinking. The shift in focus of system development efforts from coding to analysis and integration means that a greater effort must be made to understand the fundamental cognitive differences across tasks. One way to increase the accuracy of analysts and the quality of their designs may be to increase cognitive fit. For example, Khatri et al. [37] found that cognitive fit (when the mental representation of the problem matches the mental representation of the task) leads to more accurate problem solving.

A concrete example of the move from a programming focus to an analysis focus can be seen in the transition from Traditional<sup>2</sup> to object-oriented (OO) software development [9]. In making this transition, individuals experienced in the Traditional software development approach have to shift to and embrace the more abstract world of OO development with its focus on reuse, flexibility, and ease of maintenance along with a more seamless integration of the analysis, design, and implementation development steps. Far from the waterfall model of development, OO development is generally concurrent, iterative, and evolutionary [83].

In addition to the focus transition (from programmer to analyst), Traditional and OO software development represent distinct types of approaches [33].<sup>3</sup> Developers who are experts in Traditional development must make a revolutionary change in mind-set as they transition to OO [9, 48]. There has been a considerable amount of research concerning the acquisition of expertise in general (e.g., [6, 68]), in software development (e.g., [41, 64]), and in the factors that influence the acquisition of software development expertise (e.g., [53]). However, little is known about the process and the problems encountered as expert Traditional developers transition to OO development techniques. The gap in our understanding of the transition itself leads to our research question: “How does software development thinking (cognition) with respect to concept understanding change as individuals transition from the Traditional to the OO software development approach?”<sup>4</sup>

In order to answer this research question, we used cognitive fit theory as our foundation. From this theory, we developed an instrument that maps an individual’s thinking to constructs in either the Traditional or the OO software development mind-set. This instrument can determine whether a developer, supposedly working within the OO mind-set, actually has the OO mind-set in place, or is still mentally operating under the Traditional mind-set. Note that the research instrument does not measure learning, but rather it measures the expert’s mind-set (i.e., approach, thinking) regarding software development concepts. The results of this study have implications for both theory and practice. For theory, it reveals how a software developer’s mind-set changes throughout the transition process and identifies problems that may be encountered due to a lack of cognitive fit leading to confusion and falling back on inappropriate mind-sets. For practice, this study provides insights for the development of interventions that may help ease these more radical transitions.

## Theory

Th e ad option of O and the realization of its advantages has been slow, many believe due to its long and difficult learning curve [3, 8, 63, 70]. While the individual transition involves both motivational (e.g., [58]) and cognitive components, this research is focused on the cognitive aspects of the transition. Novice programmers seem to have a great deal of difficulty understanding and applying OO concepts. This problem is especially acute for developers who are experts in Traditional development who are transitioning to the OO development approach [42, 48, 49]. Expert Traditional developers should have an easy transition as they have a deep understanding of the semantics of software development and a good understanding of the problem domain. Yet experts in the Traditional mind-set seem to have difficulty transitioning to OO development: “prior knowledge of a procedural and functional paradigm may interfere with the learning of UML [unified modeling language], which is based on an object-oriented paradigm” [65, p 47]. This apparent paradox is the motivation for the research.

## Cognitive Fit

Cognitive fit theory views problem-solving performance (efficiency and effectiveness) as a function of the relationship between the problem representation and the problemsolving task [78]. “Cognitive fit exists when the cognitive processes used to act on the problem representation and those used to complete the task match, resulting in superior problem solving performance” [2, p. 542]. In essence, when the type of information emphasized for the problem and the task match, the problem solver uses cognitive processes that emphasize that same type of information [15, 78].

Much of the early work on cognitive fit in the IS literature was focused on information presentation format (e.g., [71, 78]). For example, Umanath and Vessey [77] found support for cognitive fit theory in their study of MBA students’ performance via judgments based on varied display formats. Others have looked at adding antecedents to the theory and found that the technology used to represent the problem must fit the problem-solving task, and that both must match the individual’s problem-solving skills and processes in order to develop the appropriate mental representation needed to solve the problem [15, 79]. The result of this fit is increased problem-solving efficiency and effectiveness [78].

More recently researchers have looked at different outcomes of cognitive fit. For example, Hung [32] examined expert and novice professionals’ use of an executive support system. Novices rated their user information satisfaction score significantly higher only when there was cognitive fit with the task performed. In their study of IS professionals’ software comprehension in a maintenance context, Shaft and Vessey [60] found that cognitive fit moderated the relationship between comprehension and modification. Chandra and Krovi [18] looked at the impact of fit between external information and internal representation by users on information retrieval.

While many have suggested that the OO software development approach is more natural as it more closely mirrors the way humans think, the findings in the literature have been inconsistent. In a study of software developers, Agarwal et al. [3] compared the impact of cognitive fit on performance using process-oriented tools and tasks and OO tools and tasks. They found that better performance occurred with the processoriented tool/task match, and unclear results for the OO tool/task match. Sinha and Vessey [69], in their study of computer science students, found that performance effects of cognitive fit were present for LISP programmers but not for PASCAL programmers. Agarwal et al. [4] suggest that process-oriented representations are easier for semiexperienced modelers to comprehend than OO representations.

The amount of cognitive fit between a developer’s mind-set (the cognitive processes used on the problem representation) and the software development task (the cognitive processes used to complete the task) is related to the developer’s problemsolving performance [1, p. 140]. The mismatch between the problem representation and the problem-solving task requires that the problem solver transform his or her mental representation to match the task [37] or transform the task to match the mental representation. Kim and Lerch [38] found that developers experienced considerable cognitive difficulties when they needed to change their representations.

In practice, developers more often transform their task. Transforming the task is accomplished by falling back on what they know [17, 24]. In other words, developers with a Traditional mind-set approaching an OO task will experience low cognitive fit. Finding it difficult to change their mind-set, they may transform the OO task into a Traditional development task and use the tools and techniques that are familiar, yet inappropriate.

Little is known about the process as expert Traditional developers attempt to transition to OO expertise and how this tendency is resolved through experience and training. It may be a single monolithic “aha!” where the developer suddenly “gets” the OO mind-set (e.g., [49]), or it may be a more gradual process consisting of a series of transitions or even a smooth, continuous learning curve. Understanding the transition process can lead to better training programs, a smoother transition, and better problem solutions during the transition.

As a first step in the examination of the impact of cognitive fit on these transitions, this paper explores how software development thinking changes as a developer moves through the transition from the Traditional to the OO software development approach. We propose that at the start of the transition, the concepts from the existing mind-set (e.g., Traditional software development) predominate. As the transition progresses, concepts from the existing mind-set (Traditional development) are abandoned and concepts from the new mind-set (OO development) take their place. Later in the transition, the concepts from the new mind-set (OO development) should predominate.

## Method

Due to th e d ifficulty of followin g any one group of software developers for an extended period of time given IS job market volatility, a cross-sectional design was used to approximate a longitudinal study [11, 29]. An instrument for investigating the transition process should indicate which mind-set (the “old” or the “new”) is being activated as the individual tries to work in the new domain. Experts from each of the two approaches (Traditional and OO) were interviewed to gather relevant concepts that formed the basis of the instrument (Phase 1). The instrument was developed and validated (Phase 2), and then administered to a group of expert Traditional developers in the process of transitioning to OO development (Phase 3). These three phases are described below.

It is important to note that we are not attempting to capture expert behavior or knowledge in the research instrument but rather to tap into the developer’s mind-set or approach to software development. “Core differences exist in the mental model required to productively use process-oriented and object-oriented modeling technologies” [4, p. 84]. Retrospective, self-reported data are problematic for analyzing expert behavior [25]. However, it can be used for capturing broad thought processes and the mechanisms for expert performance. In this case, it was used for determining whether the developer was approaching software development with a Traditional or an OO mind-set.

## Phase 1: Knowledge Elicitation

The first phase of this research elicited knowledge from expert Traditional and OO developers to serve as “anchors” for both ends of the transition. Experts are often identified by self-proclamation or by other experts [81]. With self-proclamation being problematic, expert OO and Traditional developers were identified by other experts using a snowball technique [61] where one expert identifies another, and so on. These experts were asked to participate in a semistructured interview lasting from 30 to 90 minutes. They were asked questions such as

Think of a time when you have been given a requirements document and were asked to produce [an object-oriented/a traditional] solution. What was the first thing you did? How did you proceed from there?

The questions formed the theme of the interview, and follow-up questions with probes were used to elicit further details about the experts’ thought processes. The first round of interviews consisted of 35 OO experts and 20 Traditional experts (see demographics in Table 1). The interviews were tape-recorded and transcribed by one of the authors. Each transcript was examined to identify and extract declarative statements made by the expert. Declarative statements are made within a specific domain or topic and are reflective indicators of an underlying concept. The declarative statements expressed the fundamental concepts of software development as seen through the expert developers eyes [47]. Three expert OO developers and three expert Traditional developers (who were not included in the interviews) classified the declarative statements into conceptual groups. A “broad brush” assessment of interrater reliability between the classifiers was measured using the chi-square test. The results $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 1 . 0 7 1$ , degrees of freedom [df] = 5, p = n.s. [not significant]) indicate an acceptable level of agreement and that all developers were in general agreement about the classification categories.

The statements were analyzed to determine if the point of redundancy had been reached for both the OO and the Traditional experts. The point of redundancy [10] represents the point at which further data collection would not lead to the identification of additional concepts. The point of redundancy was reached at 20 expert OO interviews and at seven expert Traditional interviews. It is interesting to point out that the point of redundancy for the OO experts was approximately three times that of the Traditional experts. This may be an indication of the complexity of the OO approach or the pervasiveness of the Traditional approach.

A total of 1,265 evoked statements were identified and classified into 74 distinct concepts: 50 OO concepts and 24 Traditional concepts. The six experts revisited the concepts to identify those that are central to each software development approach. A  concept was considered to be an outlier and was eliminated if it was mentioned by only one respondent. Other concepts were eliminated if they referred to programming tools or languages rather than to the approach in general (e.g., “CRC [class/ responsibility/collaboration] cards”). Finally, concepts were eliminated if they referred to the benefits of the approach (e.g., change, extensibility, natural, and reuse). At the conclusion of the elimination process, 41 concepts remained: 25 OO concepts (with an average “mention rate” of 10.0 mentions per concept) and 16 Traditional concepts (with an average mention rate of 19.4).

## Phase 2: Instrument Development

Once the fundamental concepts were identified, the survey instrument was developed. As a starting point, the interviews were reexamined to extract representative statements for each of the 41 concepts identified in Phase 1. Five statements per concept were initially selected for inclusion in the instrument, for a total initial instrument length of

205 statements. The criteria for inclusion in the instrument were the statement’s content (was the statement definitional or merely commentary), accuracy, and parsimony. A pretest sort was used to address the internal and construct validity of the preliminary instrument. A convenience sample of five IS experts (three OO and two Traditional) participated in the pretest sort. The instrument was presented to each subject along with a separate list of the constructs. The facilitator had the subjects sort statements by construct [5]. After the card sort was completed, the subjects were debriefed and, based on the results of the sort, the statements were reworded or deleted as needed.

As we were interested in tapping into the individual’s mind-set, the instrument developed measured an individual’s agreement with an OO concept or with a Traditional concept. The subject was asked his or her level of agreement with a series of statements anchored in either the OO or the Traditional approach. The Likert scale ranged from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” with the addition of a “don’t know” option. This option was included as the instrument was anchored in the OO perspective [59]. The instrument included the statement: “Think about your most recent software development experience and how you approach object-oriented software development. Use that as your reference. Please indicate on a scale of 1–5 the extent that your approach to software development agrees with each statement.” Some sample questions are included in Table 2. Note that the statements are in the experts’ own words.

The instrument was pilot tested with 31 developers consisting of expert Traditional developers with no OO experience, expert OO developers, and expert Traditional developers who were at various points in the transition to OO. After completing the instrument, each respondent was debriefed to determine if the questions were confusing and if they related in a meaningful way to the intended concepts. The instrument was edited until consensus was reached. The final instrument consisted of 90 statements covering 17 OO concepts and 11 Traditional concepts.

As a final pilot test, the instrument was administered to eight OO experts and to six Traditional experts. Each group of experts was told to “think about your most recent (OO/Traditional) development project” to cue the respondent. The results of this test indicated that the instrument’s Traditional/OO anchors were indeed in place and were performing properly. The final OO and Traditional constructs and concepts are shown in Table 3.

Different participants were used than those participants in the previous phases of the project. Thirty-two organizations of various sizes and industries (e.g., telecommunications, manufacturing, aerospace, and financial services) provided access to their developers, and each participating organization provided a primary contact person to help secure management authorization and recruit participants.

## Data Analysis

A total of 177 respon ses were received . Duplicate, incomplete, and corrupted responses were eliminated. The instrument was to be given only to expert Traditional programmers and some verification of their expertise had to be made. While there is a correlation between experience and expertise, using years of experience alone as a universal surrogate for expertise is problematic [62]. However, a combination of objective (years of experience) and subjective (supervisor recommendation) can be used when assessing performance expertise [46, 75]. The cutoff point for determining expertise in Traditional programming was set at four years of professional programming experience [43], and responses with less than that were eliminated. A total of 131 valid responses remained for analysis.

Cluster analysis is used to identify groups of entities with common characteristics revealing associations and structure within a collection of data [35]. The objective of cluster analysis is to sort cases (people, things, events, etc.) into groups so that the degree of association is strong between members of the same cluster and weak between members of different clusters [34]. This study used a two-step clustering process [36] to draw upon the strengths (and diminish the weaknesses) of both the hierarchical and partitioning cluster analysis methods. Hierarchical clustering was used to determine the number of groups. The variables used in the cluster analysis were the individual’s responses to the OO and Traditional software development questions. The clustering variables were standardized items for each construct (e.g., OO Behavior, Linear Processing) through a squared euclidean distance measure of similarity. This method identified five distinct clusters, which are discussed later in the paper.

Once the number of clusters has been determined from the hierarchical cluster analysis, the second step, a k-means (partitioning) cluster analysis, can be performed. A k-means cluster analysis assigns cases to a fixed number of clusters [26]. The process begins with the creation of k clusters (in this case, k = five). Cases are assigned to these clusters based on their distance from the cluster center. Cases are reassigned to clusters iteratively until the reassignment of any case would make the clusters internally inconsistent. The resulting clusters should “exhibit high within-cluster homogeneity and high between-cluster heterogeneity” [44]. One method for validating the cluster results is to repeat the cluster analysis on a randomly selected subsample of the respondents. If the cluster assignments made within the subsample are consistent with those made using the entire sample, then the cluster results are not dependent on a particular subsample characteristic [30]. The split-half subsample clustering of randomly chosen cases resulted in the same classification scheme with 87 percent accuracy.

## Results

Th e participan ts in th e stud y were all Traditional software developers who were in the process of making the transition to OO. They all had some OO classes, all were currently assigned to OO development projects, and most had completed at least one OO project. They were asked to “think about your most recent software development experience and how you approach object-oriented software development” to cue the subject into his or her software development thinking. The instrument’s mix of OO and Traditional statements allowed us to determine the mix of OO and Traditional concepts that composed the subject’s software development mind-set.

The analysis revealed five distinct clusters, which we named Novice, Apprentice, Shifter, Journeyman, and Professional, and which are explained later in the text. The clusters were not statistically significantly different in terms of gender, age, or education. However, there were significant differences between the groups in their organizational tenure and job tenure. Interestingly, the Apprentices had the least amount of organizational tenure and the Novices had the most job tenure. The demographics for these clusters are shown in Table 4.

From Table 5, we can see that the clusters were not statistically significantly different in the number of years of Traditional experience, and marginally different in the number of Traditional projects (the Shifters were the standout with an average of almost 11 projects compared to over 20 for the other clusters). There were significant differences between the clusters on the number years of OO experience and the number of OO projects. A key demographic that distinguished Novices from the other clusters was the answer to the question: “Was your most recent software development project procedural (traditional, structured, task oriented) or object oriented?” All but one Novice were assigned to Traditional projects and thus were thinking Traditionally when answering the survey. All of the participants in the other clusters were currently assigned to OO projects.

The cluster centroids shown graphically in Figure 1 and numerically in Table 6 demonstrate a distinct progression (as described below), from individuals who strongly agree with the Traditional concepts and strongly disagree with the OO concepts to those who strongly disagree with the Traditional concepts and strongly agree with the OO concepts. Combined with the data in Table 5, we also see that there is a progression in experience as well, with the Novices having the least amount of OO experience (years and number of projects) and the Professionals having the most.

## Preshift: Novices and Apprentices

This group strongly agrees with the Traditional constructs and strongly disagrees with the OO constructs. The two clusters are not statistically significantly different on any of the Traditional constructs or on the Behavior and OO Development constructs. However, the Apprentices have a higher level of agreement (although they still disagree) with the OO Structure and the OO Modeling constructs (Figure 2). All but one of the Novices were assigned to Traditional projects and so should have been using the traditional mind-set. They had the least agreement with OO concepts and the most agreement with the traditional concepts. This cluster serves as an anchor point for the transitional clusters that follow.

As we can see from Figure 2 and the demographic data in Table 6, it appears that the concepts in the OO Structure construct are the first ones accepted by individuals transitioning to OO techniques, closely followed by those in OO Modeling. This makes sense looking at the concepts of the two constructs. OO Structure consists of the concepts Abstraction, Attribute, Class, Encapsulation, Inheritance, Instantiation, and Object. These concepts are fundamental to the OO mind-set [7] and are among the first taught in OO classes. The OO Modeling construct consists of the Identifying Objects and Object Model concepts. The concepts in the OO Structure and OO Modeling constructs are those needed for “programming in the small” (fairly smallscale tasks). That is, one could have a surface understanding of these concepts and still write passable OO methods or small programs. On the other hand, “programming in the large” (integrating many modules) requires an understanding of the Behavior construct (Collaboration, Message Passing, Relationships, and so on), and is not yet present in the Novices or the Apprentices.

## The Shifters

The Shifters lie between those who generally think Traditionally and those who generally think OO (see Figure 3). They still agree with the Traditional concepts and disagree with the OO concepts, but are moving. They seem to be giving up on the other Traditional concepts but have not yet adopted the other OO concepts. Being between the more-Traditional thinkers and the more-OO thinkers, the Shifters may be in a “tipping point” between the two approaches. The small number of individuals in this group may be due to the fact that individuals cannot keep OO and Traditional concepts cognitively together. This group deserves closer scrutiny in future studies.

## Postshift: Professionals and Journeymen

The two clusters are statistically significantly different $( p = 0 . 0 5 )$ on all of the Traditional constructs and the OO Structure and OO Modeling constructs. The Professionals are the furthest along in the transition to OO thinking as shown in Figure 4. As the Professionals thought about how they approach OO projects, they disagreed with statements within the three Traditional constructs—Linear Processing, Traditional Structure, and Functionality. They agreed with statements in three OO constructs—Behavior, OO Structure, and OO Modeling. However, they only slightly agreed with the construct of OO Development (including the concepts of OO Development, Layer, and Patterns).

The Journeymen’s responses for the OO constructs are similar to the Professionals’. They differ in their amount of agreement with the Traditional constructs, having the highest agreement on the Traditional construct of Functionality and having the highest disagreement on the Linear Processing construct. This makes sense when looking at the related concepts. The Linear Processing construct appears to be the most incompatible with OO, consisting of concepts related to the linear structure of Traditional programs. It is very difficult to view an OO program as a monolithic linear structure with its hierarchical structure of classes and its message-passing mind-set. On the other hand, it is still possible to view OO programs using the Functionality construct perspective, as it consists of statements related to the data processing part of the code/ data Traditional programming split.

## Transitions

Th is stud y sough t to examin e how software development thinking changes as individuals transition from the Traditional to the OO software development approach to better understand the long-term process. The increasing number of OO projects and years of OO experience for the clusters allows us to infer a sequence in the crosssectional survey of the expert Traditional developers transitioning to OO development [11, 67]. The results indicate that there is not a single “aha!” moment when an expert Traditional developer suddenly gives up the Traditional approach and adopts the OO approach. Instead, there seem to be multiple shifts as some OO thinking is adopted and some Traditional thinking is rejected in a series of shifts as shown in the panels in Figure 5.

The first shift (panel a) occurs when the Novices begin to think about OO Modeling and OO Structure as they transition to Apprentices. This is not surprising as, referring back to Table 3, the concepts contained in the OO Model and OO Structure constructs are among the first and the most basic concepts learned. The Novices generally “don’t know” what the OO concepts mean; the Apprentices know what the concepts mean but disagree with the concepts even when they attempt to use them. The Apprentice still holds strongly to the Traditional approach and beliefs of monolithic development and functional decomposition.

The data are consistent with generally accepted recommendations for teaching OO concepts (e.g., [55]). The OO concepts the individuals agree with are indeed the first ones taught. However, unlike true novices, who have no prior exposure to any software development techniques, the individuals in the Apprentice cluster strongly disagree with these OO concepts while strongly agreeing with concepts from their Traditional approach background. This result may indicate that proactive interference (where previous knowledge interferes with the acquisition of new knowledge) is affecting the individuals, and confirms results from prior research [49, 63].

The second shift (panel b) is from Apprentice to Shifter. The Shifters have begun to give up their Traditional software development thinking (e.g., Linear Programming). They no longer strongly agree with monolithic development and functional decomposition but do not entirely disagree with these concepts either. The Shifters also disagree somewhat less with the OO concepts in the Behavior construct.

This step represents the beginning of skepticism of the individual with Traditional concepts. The OO concepts of the Behavior construct (Collaboration, Message Passing, Method, Polymorphism, and Relationship) are directly antagonistic with the Traditional concepts of Linear Processing (Linear Flow, Linear Form, Linear Program, Linear Structure, and Monolithic) and Traditional Structure (Interaction, Functions, Functional Decomposition, Subroutine). For example, message passing and methods are incompatible with the concepts of function and subroutine. Again, we believe the transition to OO is being interfered with by the existing Traditional software development mind-set.

In the third transition (panel c), OO concepts are accepted as the Shifter moves to Journeyman. The OO constructs of Behavior, OO Model, and OO Structure gain acceptance. The concepts in the OO Development construct (OO Development, Layers, and Patterns) are still disagreed with (although less so). As the developers think about how objects, modules, and systems interact, they understand OO Development and again increase their knowledge of modeling these concepts. The final shift (panel d) shows the shift from Journeyman to Professional in which the Professional strongly agrees with the OO concepts and disagrees with Traditional concepts. At this point, it appears that the developers have stopped thinking in the Traditional approach and now think in the OO approach.

## Discussion

Th e results of th is stud y ind icate that as experts shift to new mind-sets, the transition may not be as simple as a single “aha!” moment, but rather a series of shifts as key constructs of the old mind-set are eventually replaced by constructs of the new mind-set. As seen in Figure 5, the shift in mind-set is in reality a series of shifts toward cognitive fit with the OO approach. From the data in Table 6, we can see two particularly significant points of transition. The first is the move from Shifter to Journeyman. Interestingly, the developers still slightly agree with the Traditional concepts, but also agree with the OO concepts in the Behavior, OO Model, and OO Structure constructs. The Journeymen may be developing OO projects that are a hybrid system of Traditional and OO concepts [23, 54]. When there is a lack of cognitive fit, developers can still create solutions; it is just a more difficult mental process because they have to manage different types of information and convert between the information types. This could contribute to the “long learning curve” experience documented in previous research (e.g., [3]).

Experts are able to rely on prior expertise when they encounter an unfamiliar problem. Without a strong understanding of (or agreement with) the new concepts, they fall back and use their existing knowledge. In this context, developers unable to make sense of the OO concepts fall back on (they strongly agree with) their more familiar

Traditional concepts. This is consistent with prior research which indicates that when there is a mismatch (lack of cognitive fit), the individual may fall back on what he or she knows, or limit the variety of the choice set [24]. As the developers gain OO experience, they rely less on their Traditional mind-set and eventually strongly disagree with Traditional concepts as they begin to “think OO.”

The second significant point in the transition is from Journeyman to Professional. In this case, the developers let go of the Traditional mind-set and fully utilize the OO mind-set. In this case, the mental representation of the task (OO development) and the mental representation of the problem (OO approach) match, leading to cognitive fit. Prior research (e.g., [3]) has found that cognitive fit leads to more effective problem solving, and thus, we contend it may lead to more effective software development.

When developers have a good cognitive fit with the domain (the representation of the problem) and the tools (the representation of the task), they use the mental processes that emphasize the same type of information to create their mental representation. Reducing the cognitive misfit associated with constructing a representation can facilitate understanding. By accessing the same mental processes to create their mental representation, the cognitive misfit of the developer is reduced, thus potentially increasing understanding and positively influencing performance. As a result, training may be altered to increase cognitive fit and lower the cognitive misfit of the developers. For example, the old OO training technique of CRC (class/responsibility/ collaborator) cards [14], or even more abstract techniques, can be used to prime the OO mind-set before starting the modeling process.

One way to increase cognitive fit may be to more quickly move the problem representation closer to the problem-solving task. In essence, we may be able to shorten the learning curve by increasing cognitive fit. This may be accomplished by providing individuals transitioning to the OO approach with examples of problem-solving tasks and expert OO problem representations. This may help the transitioner internalize an appropriate OO problem representation. In addition, training could be established to incorporate paired programming where an individual experiencing difficulty with the transition is paired with an OO expert.<sup>5</sup> Giving transitioners a role model who can explain his or her OO thinking may be the key to changing the transitioners mind-set.

OO training is currently focused on “programming in the small.” Most universities start with the OO Structure (e.g., Abstraction, Class, and Object) and OO Modeling concepts. Worse, many teach OO as simply an extension of Traditional programming (such as teaching C concepts and then adding the C++ OO concepts at the end of the semester). “It all starts at the beginning: OO programming learned naturally, not procedurally” [82, p. 129]. A more effective route that may avoid much of the difficulty would be to concentrate on the “programming in the large” aspects of software development, broadening their instruction in the Behavior and OO Development concepts. In addition, training programs might modify their instructional design to emphasize OO Modeling aspects, and then move to programming in the small.

This research has significant implications for managers, as IS personnel are continuously required to make shifts in their mind-set. Within the IS field, technologies, methods, and techniques are soon obsolete. As such, IS personnel will need to retool or become obsolete themselves. The methods used in this study have the potential to be relevant for other shifts. For example, the possibility exists that part of the blame for many of the widely documented ERP and customer relationship management (CRM) installation failures lies in IS personnel who did not understand how to think within the mind-set of the new system, despite their existing expertise and experience [31].

While a client’s existing knowledge may positively influence the successful implementation of an ERP [39], there is evidence that existing knowledge can also negatively affect the success of the ERP [52]. The client must unlearn what they already know while trying to acquire the complex new knowledge [57]. This research can provide an understanding of how existing knowledge interferes during this transition. Identifying the areas of difficulty for the individuals in transition may minimize the impact of these difficulties through retraining efforts.

This research also contributes to the body of more general research on expertise.<sup>6</sup> As an individual gains expertise, he or she creates larger chunks of knowledge and more integrated cognition (e.g., [19, 20, 28]). For example, chess novices do not have enough experience to recognize patterns so they see each piece on a chessboard independently, whereas experts see the chessboard as a whole unit or a specific pattern. This increased chunking can be seen in our study through the progression of OO thinking discussed earlier. As the developers move through the transition, they add pieces to their OO thinking, thus creating larger OO chunks. The first chunk consisted of the OO Structure and OO Modeling constructs, and later the Behavior construct was added, and finally the OO Development construct.

In addition, experts represent a problem at a deeper level, whereas novices represent a problem at a very superficial level [28, 45]. As we found in our study, one could have a surface understanding of the OO concepts and still write passable OO methods or small programs. In contrast, to develop large systems one needs an understanding of the Behavior construct (e.g., Collaboration, Message Passing). Once this construct is added to the developer’s OO cognition (i.e., mental model), he or she may develop large systems.

Contrary to previous literature on expertise (e.g., [25, 72, 73]) that found no relationship between performance and years of experience, we found a strong correlation between the two. While on the surface it appears that we have replicated previous research on expertise showing that more OO project experience leads to more acceptance and more “expert thinking” in applying OO techniques, in actuality, we have found the reverse. The data were clustered using expertise (the OO thinking items), not experience. The fact that the clusters have a positive correlation with OO experience is an interesting finding. This may be explained by the finding that in a complex domain, once an acceptable level of performance is attained, there are hardly any benefits from additional general experience. To improve performance, it is necessary to work on improving specific aspects of the activity [27, 66]. Thus, from this study, it appears that the “specific aspects” necessary to move performance from a basic functional level toward expertise are the OO constructs of Behavior and Development. Of course, this finding will need to be confirmed in future research.

## Limitations and Future Research

The first limitation deals with external validity. Future research should explore the external validity of this study because the ability to generalize these findings to all software developers and organizations may be limited due to self-selection bias, a nonrandom sample, and the use of an online/e-mail data collection method. This study was a cross-sectional study designed to approximate a longitudinal field study. Advances to research would be made and knowledge would be gained from taking a longitudinal approach to examining the cognitive learning process over time for specific individuals.

Another limitation of this study was the selection of cluster analysis as the method of statistical analysis. A problem faced by users of cluster analysis is that cluster analysis always produces clusters, and the fact that a solution seems reasonable is no guarantee that the results would be reproducible [26]. This is a primarily exploratory method and thus generalizations should be made with care. One limitation of cluster analysis is that different clustering methods can generate different solutions to the same data set. This risk was minimized by the use of multiple clustering methods. With multiple clustering methods, the data were analyzed and clustered from two perspectives (Wards hierarchical and k-means).

A final limitation involved the nature of the software development projects worked on by the developers. It is possible that some of the OO systems being developed were actually more appropriate to the Traditional approach, which could be a source of confusion for the inexperienced developers. However, the developers perceived that they were working on OO projects (via self-reported data regarding the project type). When we combine the developer perceptions with the number of different organizations and the number of different systems involved, we have confidence that our results are appropriate.

It is interesting to note that not all software developers will successfully make the transition. While the data in this group indicate that with time and experience, the OO mind-set becomes more solid, many may drop out, switch careers, or refocus their efforts on other endeavors before they make the full transition. The Journeymen had over three years of experience and worked in almost three OO projects, and yet they still have not fully transitioned to the OO mind-set. Each transition point in Figure 5 represents a shift in the way that the expert Traditional developer thinks about software development, and at each transition, the developer may fail to make that shift. At some point, the transition process may simply become “too hard” and the developer moves on to other things. Previous research has concentrated on those who have successfully gained OO expertise to attempt to learn how to help others make the transition. Little is known about those who drop out of the studies [49]. These developers have considerable domain knowledge, and their loss represents a significant loss to their organization. This is a very rich field for future research.

This study investigated the individual cognitive processes involved as people who are experts in one way of thinking transition into a completely different way of performing tasks or approaching problems. In the field of IS, such cognitive transitions can been seen following the shifts from mainframe to client-server to Web services, from relational databases to object-oriented databases and beyond, and from data management to information management to knowledge management, and so on. Other fields are similarly affected by these transitions. In education, the traditional classroom is being replaced by distributed learning environments, telemedicine is transforming health care, and continuous online auditing is transforming the accounting industry. In all of these examples, individuals who are expert in one way of thinking are forced by the new innovation to confront the unfamiliar. The foundation laid in this paper may be applied to these other transitions in future research.

We would be remiss if we did not address the boundaries of this study. We have deliberately focused on the individual and more specifically on the cognitions of the individual during a transition. What we have to remember is that the individual is part of a system, and this system can be thought of as a diamond consisting of people, tasks, technologies, and organizational structures in which every element of organizational life affects the others [40].<sup>7</sup> Change does not occur in isolation, and when changes are made to one or two of the facets (e.g., people and technology) problems can occur. Inevitably, the change will not impact just one facet of the diamond, but ripple through the organization. For example, if a new technology is introduced, it will not only impact the people, but at a minimum, the task and perhaps ultimately the group structure may change. Take, for example, the case of the introduction of the OO software development approach. This change will immediately affect the technology, people, and task facets. While not immediate, we would assert that the structure of the IS development group may also change as expertise and power may be shifted to adapt to the change in tasks.

Even within the people facet, there are multiple subfacets that might have differing effects on the success or failure of the transition. For example, while this research has focused on the cognitive facet, the motivational facet of the individual should not be ignored. Several studies have identified the positive influence motivation can have on learning (e.g., [13, 80]). Some might argue that the motivational aspect has an even larger influence on the success or failure of the transition than the cognitive aspect. Future research should take a more holistic picture of change and the change management process associated with the introduction of a new software development approach.

## Conclusion

Th e goal of th is stud y was to examin e how software development concept thinking changes as individuals transition from the Traditional to the OO software development approach. The results indicate that there are five stages that these developers pass through during the transition, which we named Novice, Apprentice, Shifter, Journeyman, and Professional. These findings shed light on how cognitive fit theory affects the transition process. Increasing cognitive fit may ease the transition to the new mind-set.

The increasing need to shift the focus of system development efforts from coding to analysis and integration, and the concurrent shift of personnel from the Traditional development approach to the OO approach means that a greater effort must be made to understand the fundamental cognitive differences across tasks and mind-sets. This research has provided one of the first steps in this process by developing an understanding of the cognitive changes that occur during the transition. This understanding may lead to better training methods and an easier transition for IS personnel.
