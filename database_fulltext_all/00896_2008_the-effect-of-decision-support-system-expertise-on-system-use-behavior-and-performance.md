# The effect of decision support system expertise on system use behavior and performance

Zoonky Lee <sup>a</sup>, Christian Wagner <sup>b</sup>, Ho Kyoung Shin <sup>a,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Graduate School of Information, Yonsei University, Seoul, Republic of Korea

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 20 April 2007 Received in revised form 9 December 2007 Accepted 19 April 2008 Available online 25 June 2008

Keywords: Decision support systems Systems usage Decision-making expertise Restrictiveness Focused planning behavior Efficient planning

## A B S T R A C T

We performed an empirical investigation into the effect of users’ decision support system (DSS) expertise on their problem-solving strategies. The results indicated that individuals who had only recently learned to use the DSS were confused or restricted by the set of functions provided by the system and did not plan well for their use of the DSS. Those who had previous knowledge of the system exhibited more focused and efficient problem-solving behavior. Our findings suggested that problem-solving strategies depended significantly on the user’s level of system expertise.

## 1. Introduction

We are still trying to understand how a decision support system (DSS) helps users improve their decision-making [16,22]. Previous studies have reported that some DSS usage leads to performance improvement, while other usage resulted in little or no effect, or sometimes worse performance [1]. Studies have shown that DSS use does not always improve decision quality, but it can help users develop a better understanding of the decision problem [12,23]. Thus, it is important to understand when and how a DSS helps users make better decisions [8]. We therefore explored the effect of the level of a person’s expertise in the use of a DSS on consistent interpretation of its results.

The effect of expertise in the use of a DSS on decision-making strategy and resulting quality has been ignored: most laboratory experiments have tested a DSS only once (possibly with a few pretests), comparing a control group with a treatment group [11]. This lack of studies on the long-term use of DSS has been attributed to the cost of long-term investigations [9]. Ignoring system expertise can bias research results in two ways, there may be: (i) a lack of expertise among subjects; and (ii) high variance in their levels of expertise.

Thus, if subjects are still at a novice level and cannot utilize the complete functionality of the DSS, the study may not be meaningful. Equally, if subjects have different levels of system knowledge, their joint effectiveness will be difficult to assess. Therefore, we believed that it was important to study DSS use when the user expertise was beyond a mere level of being ‘‘comfortable’’ with it.

Kohli and Devaraji [10] observed that the use of DSS over an extended period of time lead to improved decision outcomes. Our investigation went beyond this and tried to answer three questions:

\- Do subjects need a longer learning period than the ‘‘warm up’’ typically granted them?

\- Will users behave differently using DSS and solving problems when they are more experienced with the systems? And how are the differences represented (e.g., by choice of functions, demands on the interface, decision strategy, etc.)?

\- Does a higher level of system expertise lead to better decisionmaking?

\- We focused on the developing system expertise through repetitive experiments. Further, our study employed a control group to detect the effect of system expertise. Lastly, we examined the effect of system expertise, by collected data from system-generated logs of performance (such as time taken) instead of using subjective measures.

## 2. Conceptual framework and hypotheses

## 2.1. System restrictiveness

DSS restrictiveness is ‘‘the degree to which and the manner in which a DSS limits its users’ decision-making processes to a subset of all possible processes [20]’’ Decision-makers are thus obviously restricted to using only the functions designed into the system, and these restrictions influence the users’ decision processes. In empirical studies, system restrictiveness was found to be related to how individuals use the system [14]. Nakatsu and Benbasat [15] found that less restrictive systems were more effective in problem-solving situations. A study by Dowling and Leech [3] in audit firms showed that differences in DSS restrictiveness led users to employ different decision aid functions with different DSS.

One important factor is that a function of a DSS may not be as important as the perception users have of it [18]. This suggests that the degree of restrictiveness is stronger for system novices, because they do not fully understand all functions of the system. Mackay and Elam’s [13] investigation of the interaction between a system and functional knowledge involved dividing the subject pool into four groups representing two levels each of functional area and spreadsheet expertise. A comparison between the groups demonstrated that lack of expertise with the DSS inhibited the application of functional area knowledge. Members of the functional-area-expert/spreadsheet-novice group (high-low group) were constrained by their limited ability to use the DSS and thus unable to execute integrative problem-solving strategies efficiently, exhibiting results very different from those of the highhigh group. We expected to support to this finding but focused on the different system use and problem-solving strategy characteristics of system experts versus novices.

## 2.2. Expertise differences

Experts often develop task-specific strategies that are highly accurate while subsequently requiring less information processing [7]. The expertise we are considering involves competence to perform a task efficiently and effectively. Therefore, they have to exhibit good planning and search behaviors and their performance must be high, as demonstrated by significant speed and accuracy in task completion, yet they are not geniuses but competent performers (measured through performance rather than selfassessment)

## 2.3. System use behavior

By combining the notion of system restrictiveness and expert/ novice differences, we proposed that lack of expertise with a DSS would stop problems-solvers from applying their functional knowledge lead them to use strategies different from those of experts (Fig. 1).

## 2.3.1. Experienced system restrictiveness

As DSS users gain expertise in system usage, they spend less time in considering different unfruitful functions and alternatives and find the system to be less constraining, which in turn leads to less perception restrictiveness. Therefore, as system users progress to higher levels of DSS expertise, they gain a better understanding of a system’s capabilities and feel less restricted. Thus we believed that, by gaining expertise, an individual could learn to operate a system better, even if this means working around poorly designed software. Once internalized, the underlying system inefficiency will appear less restrictive. We hypothesized:

H1. Decision makers with greater DSS expertise will experience less restrictiveness than novice users.

## 2.3.2. Planning

Planning is the process of preparing action resources; and thus interacts effectively with systems. Many problems can be solved only by planning long sequences of moves, and thus planning is a fundamental part of problem-solving [6]. Experts usually take a top-down, breadth-first approach while novices tend to use a bottom-up process that lacks planning. Therefore, while experts consider various high-level hypotheses, novices often rush into problem solving. There are two potential reasons for lack of planning. First, understanding and representing the problem is challenging, and thus working resources are not available for it. Second, novices fail to plan ahead because the actual utility of the extra effort is not clear. Novices often are often lost in the detail. Also the two broad categories of errors occur in DSS use: knowledge-based errors include assigning too much or too little importance to a specific decision, or incorrectly evaluating an alternative while skill-based errors including those made in touchtyping or in copying values from one list to another, are the primary type of error made by novices [17]. Arguing on the basis of system restrictiveness, we assumed that a low level of system expertise would restrict problem-solvers in applying functional knowledge, leading them to use strategies that were closer to those of novices. Despite their knowing how to plan, a lack of DSS knowledge limits their ability to execute decision-aiding functions in support of the plans. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H2. Decision makers with greater DSS expertise will exhibit better planning behavior in problem solving than novices.

## 2.3.3. Problem-solving efficiency

Experts store and recall information in large patterns of ‘‘chunks’’ of original data and consider holistic similarity to prior concrete experiences. Through this, experts identify problemsolving processes that are akin to those learned and apply the solution to new problems [2]. Additionally, a experience allows experts to build a representation faster, and their strategic knowledge allows them to apply effective strategies [5]. Therefore, experts use more focused and efficient problems-solving strategies [24]. Even if novices could correctly identify and understand the deep structure of the problem spaces, the system could restrict their ability to apply their learning to the current situation. This lack of deep understanding leads to more effort and more computation in real time. This results in decreased efficiency. Therefore, we hypothesized

H3. Decision makers with greater DSS expertise will exhibit more focused and efficient problem-solving behavior than novices.

## 2.3.4. Performance

Effective problem-solving performance requires adequate domain, system, and strategic knowledge in addition to adequate reasoning based on this expertise [19]. We expected that those with greater system expertise would be better able to employ their task knowledge through decision-aiding functions. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H4. Decision makers with greater DSS expertise will perform better than novices.

## 3. Research methodology

To test our hypotheses, we carried out an empirical study that compared the performance of two decision maker groups in a decision task during six sessions over the course of two weeks. One group was supported by a DSS throughout the experiment; the other group used the DSS only in the last session. The model underlying our research design is illustrated in Fig. 2.

## 3.1. Experimental task

The task chosen for the experiment was a competitive bidding task. To perform this, subjects were required to make several bids in the way that a manager would make repeated sealed bids in an auction, trying to win contracts of sufficient value to remain in business. Subjects used resources from a limited resource pool (between 1 and 9 resource units) to outbid a competitor (represented by a computer program with identical resources and interests), in order to obtain winnings. Each ‘‘game’’ consisted of up to nine rounds of bidding. The game was terminated when one player obtained more than half of the available winnings (at least 23 out of 45). During each round of bidding, competitors were told the size of the stakes for which they were bidding, and upon which they would issue a sealed bid. While its rules were simple, the task required significant strategy thinking in order to ‘‘outsmart’’ the competitor (See Appendix A for a detailed description). We chose this complex, dynamic task in order to capture the complexity of real-world decision problems and to make sure that, even after several sessions, the task would not become trivial. All subjects competed against the strategies incorporated by a relatively sophisticated computer program, some of which was only activated only after several iterations of the task.

## 3.2. Decision support system

We developed a DSS specifically for the experiment using Visual Basic<sup>TM</sup> Macro functions. It was intended to provide a high level of control over the process and contained task performance monitoring functions. It contained interface characteristics that one would expect in a contemporary DSS, including buttons and pull-down menus (see Appendix B: Initial Screen and Appendix C: Menu structure of the System). In addition, it incorporated characteristics that allowed us to associate system information value with search cost, thus forcing subjects to weigh the information benefit against the cost/effort required to obtain the information.

Fig. 2. Research design.

Building an effective DSS first required the identification of a comprehensive set of ‘‘playing’’ (decision-making) strategies for which support would be available. To this end, we recruited a pilot group of 36 individuals to complete the task and to develop or identify up to three strategies as part of the exercise. We then compared and classified the suggested strategies (many of which were identical or very similar), and finally synthesized a set of strategies that became the basis of our DSS. To do this, we followed the following procedures:

\- First, we organized strategies according to their information requirements.

\- Second, we defined a unit set of information requirements for each strategy.

\- Finally, each unit set of information requirements became a DSS aid function.

Furthermore, the ‘‘computer opponent’’ incorporated a subset of the strategies identified by the pilot group. Based on these procedures, the DSS supported the information requirements of virtually every user playing against the component.

We intentionally designed the DSS so that it distributed information incrementally to users. A unit of information was displayed only when the user requested it via a button, menu selection, or control key. Providing multiple units of information at once clearly would have been more helpful to users but would have reduced our ability to observe users’ problem-solving strategies. Furthermore, DSS users were supposed to incur a distinct ‘‘cost’’ for their use of each DSS function and the information it provided.

The DSS went through multiple iterative design rounds and we thoroughly tested it before starting our experiment. In its final form, the system contained 22 decision-aiding functions.

## 3.3. Experimental design

In order to examine this effect of increased DSS experience on problem-solving behavior and decision outcome, our subjects had to use a DSS repeatedly. We then had to separate DSS learning from problem-solving learning, because we expected that subjects would become better at making decisions and thus playing the game, as well as using the software. As already stated, we devised an experiment in which two groups completed the task, one with the benefit of the DSS (system group), and the other without it except in the last session (system-less group).

Twenty-one graduate business students, nineteen men and two women, participated voluntarily in the study. One subject withdrew during the experiment, and thus all analyses were based on 20 subjects. All were familiar with computers (7.5 years average experience) and used them frequently (16 h/week average). Prior to the start of testing, we randomly separated subjects into groups: twelve subjects were the system group, while eight were the system-less group. Each individual attended six sessions during a 2-week period (Table 1).

Each experimental session was a one-on-one meeting between a researcher and a subject. At the outset of the study we familiarized each subject with the game, first with playing cards (not using the DSS) and later, for the system-group, with the DSS. At the starting of each subsequent session, we described the task to the subjects. Each session consisted of three consecutive rounds of bidding (with up to nine bid decisions made per round), resulting in 18 total games played by each with up to 162 bid decisions made during the experiment per subject. All subjects played against the computer program.

In their first session with the DSS, we gave about 20 min of instruction to subjects in the system group and asked them to use the system until they felt comfortable with its functions. Subjects quickly felt comfortable with the system, and all reported that they were able to use the software without any difficulty after one or two rounds of the exercise. A manual was available throughout, but was rarely used. The software logged all keystrokes and button clicks.

The system-less group reported their bid suggestions using pencil and paper and bids were entered by a researcher who fed back the results, thereby ensuring the same level of problemsolving skill learning as the system group. At no time during the first five sessions were subjects in the system-less group able to observe the DSS screens or functions. Immediately prior to the sixth session, subjects in this group received instructions about the DSS functions and practiced with the DSS up to the same level as subjects in the system group had. Neither subject group had a time limit for playing, though a built-in clock monitored time consumption. Subjects took 10–30 min to complete a session.

As an incentive, we offered the subjects cash prizes of \$3 per session based on their number of wins, as well as an additional ‘‘grand prize’’ worth \$100 for the best overall performer.

## 3.4. Operationalization of concepts

## 3.4.1. System expertise

The first element of our evaluation concerned differences in DSS use expertise, based on learning experience. We determined the expertise level (and the differences between ‘‘novices’’ and ‘‘experts’’) by considering their number of use errors. This differentiation criterion was in line with previous research involving expert–novice difference. We chose error rates, because it separated system expertise from task expertise and, especially in early rounds, was not as difficult to measure as that involving people slowing down to think about their decisions. Prior to our experiment, we compiled a list of possible types of DSS use errors and had written software functions to capture them. We identified two different types: simple and procedural errors. The first refer to users’ mistakes unrelated to system knowledge. One example included being out-ofrange in making a bid. The second referred to errors in the use of the system. One example was out-of-sequence, indicating that the user tried to invoke a function that was invalid at that time. Procedural errors are not so much human mistakes as mismatches between reasonable human behavior and system design characteristics that users can only avoid by sufficient system knowledge or practice.

## 3.4.2. System restrictiveness

System restrictiveness is frequently measured through questions on user perception; but this is subjective. Consequently, we chose to measure this variable by counting the number of ‘‘abandoned functions.’’ Users typically experience system restrictiveness when they invoke a function and their interaction with the system do not match their expectations; a typical response is to stop using the function and initiate another. Therefore, the number of abandoned functions shows the number of times that users tried to use functions that did not do what they expected.

## 3.4.3. Planning behaviors

To capture differences between novice and expert in planning behavior, we investigated behavior that indicated a lack of planning and generated log functions to mark their occurrence. Specifically, we looked at activities closely related to decisionmaking strategy. An important manifestation of planning in the bidding task related to choice of resources. A subject could use between one and nine resources during each round of bidding; however, after a particular resource level had been used once, it became unavailable. Choosing the right resource at the right time, however, was the key to winning the game (as both opponents had exactly the same resources available to them). Yet without close monitoring and planning, players would sometimes attempt to select previously consumed resources. In order to overcome this mistake, subjects were expected to either remember or use a resource-monitoring-decision-aid function (Resource-remaining). Subjects who did not monitor or plan during the exercise were reminded by a system message.

## 3.4.4. Efficient and focused behaviors

System experts should execute only functions that matched their strategic decision-making processes and use functions that provided benefits. System novices were expected to be unable to do this, and this would result in different strategies or attempting to use different decision-aid functions, leading to more functions being explored. Therefore, the number of functions-explored was used as an inverse measure of focused behavior, similar to Todd and Benbasat’s ‘‘units of information examined’’ [21]. In addition, time to complete the game was used as a simple measure for efficient and focused behaviors, since efficient and focused behaviors would be measures of expertise and learning. Of course, short time-taken and few functions-explored could be the pattern of a lazy, non-expert decision maker.

## 3.4.5. Better decision quality

Measuring decision quality is relatively easy when there is a clear win, loss, or tie, as was the case in our experiment. We assessed decision quality as the number of wins in each session, recording a win as 1.0, a loss as 0.0, and a draw as 0.5.

Our analysis demonstrated that, after the third session, subjects apparently had acquired system expertise, as indicated by a large drop in errors. However, one of the two measures of learning, simple errors, did not reflect this system behavior in an exponential learning curve pattern. Error rates dropped by a factor of 3 for Sessions 5 and 6, but variance were high and errors momentarily soared against expectation (see Session 3 data in Table 2). Therefore, we paid attention to the measurement of procedural errors, reflecting not only simple mis-typing but also a lack of understanding of decision support functions and their input requirements. Fig. 3 shows the system learning curve as the number of procedural errors averaged over all subjects. The dashed line depicts the average error rate for the system-less group in Session 6 of the exercise. The shape of the graph suggested that system learning occurred primarily during the first three sessions and stabilized afterwards. In order to confirm that learning essentially stopped, we employed the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test.

A series of paired tests showed that learning was prominent until the end of the third session (z = 2.3, p < 0.05), while further learning from Sessions 3 through 6 was not significant. Because each subject had a different learning curve, we could also analyze individual learning. The majority of the learning occurred by the end of Session 3 or 4, with the earliest being after the first session and the latest being after Session 4. Thus, we concluded that all subjects reached the level of system expert after the fourth session.

We compared procedural error levels for both system and system-less groups (See Table 2). In comparing the two groups, we applied a Mann–Whitney U-test, since it is one of the most powerful nonparametric statistical tests: it uses most of the quantitative information inherent in the data [4]. While we could have computed t-statistics instead, the small sample size and the assumptions of normality were a concern.

In the final session (the sixth), the system-less group recorded an average of 3.13 errors. In contrast, the system group exhibited 1.08 (Session 4), 1.0 (Session 5), and 0.58 errors (Session 6); the difference in errors was significant (maximum $\mathrm { U } = 9 . 5 , p < 0 . 0 1$ in Session 4). The system group clearly went through a learning experience; members of that group started with an average error rate of 6.00. What we did not expect was the relatively small number of errors for the system-less group (mean = 3.13) compared to the average error rate of the system group during its first session.

## 4.2. System restrictiveness

On average, 4.9 instances of function abandoning were exhibited by system novices compared to 0.6 by system experts, a greater than eight-fold difference, which was highly significant $( U = 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Of the 12 subjects in the system group, seven abandoned a function once and five subjects did not abandon any. In contrast, system novices abandoned functions between three and eight times. Within the system group there was a learning behavior with averages dropping from 4.3 to 0.6 (an 88% drop in abandoned functions from Session 1 to 6).

## 4.3. Planning

Players sometimes accidentally try to select resources that had already been consumed. To avoid this mistake, subjects were expected to either remember their past resource allocations or use a resource-monitoring-decision-aid function. Subjects who did not monitor or plan during the exercise were reminded by a system message.

System novices exhibited an average of 2.0 instances of lack-ofplanning behavior while system experts exhibited 0.58 instances. The differences were again highly significant $\left( U = 1 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 \right)$ . There was also a noticeable learning pattern in the system group, with instances of lack-of-planning behavior dropping from 1.8 to 0.6 (a 66% drop, but with high variance).

## 4.4. Efficiency and focused behavior

The system group showed a continuous decrease in the number of functions-explored, throughout the experiment. The average number of functions-explored was 7 and 11 for system experts and novices, respectively. This difference $( U = 1 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ indicated that system experts developed stable strategies that could be executed by invoking a focused set of decision-aiding functions.

## 4.5. Performance (game win) differences

In order to analyze the performance difference by group and session, we conducted ANOVA for the number of game wins per session. The analysis (shown in Table 3) indicated a strong session effect $( F = 1 0 . 6 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , As subjects solved the problem more often, they tended to win more often. Yet there was no significant difference in performance between the groups. In addition, there was no interaction effect between DSS use and game expertise. Assuming the medium effect size and $\alpha = 0 . 0 5$ , the power exceeded 95% for the test of group effect, and 85% for the test of interaction effect. In the final session, where both groups used the DSS, there was no significant difference. Thus, only the session effect had a strong impact on performance.

System novices seemed to be restricted by the set of functions provided by the system and showed less planning for problem solving. The system experts’ focus on a smaller number of functions (i.e., number of functions-explored) suggested that they developed stable heuristics. System novices, in contrast, found it difficult to apply reliable heuristics and demonstrated inefficient problem-solving behavior, as represented by more functionsexplored and greater time to complete the task.

By observing the process of DSS learning for the system group, we also understood how the DSS helped users make decisions. Fig. 5 has a continuous drop in the number of functions-explored. This indicated that users successfully identified useful strategies after exploring different functions and, once they discovered useful ones, stopped searching for additional ones.

Although system experts used the system differently from novices, there was no difference in final performance. At the same time, as seen in Fig. 5, the frequency of functions used increased up to certain levels (an average of about 2.7) and then stabilized. Since every decision-aid function in the DSS was associated with some level of effort for executing the DSS function and for completing the associated information analysis process, the number of DSS functions-used became a relatively simple measure for the cost of solving the problem; this grew during early sessions and remained relatively constant during later sessions; DSS users increased their ‘‘effort investment’’ during earlier sessions up to a threshold level. This was consistent with a cost/benefit principle, which postulates that the decision maker’s objective is to both increase accuracy (or decision quality) and reduce effort. Our results indicated that the users were willing to incur increased costs up to the third or fourth session in order to achieve better quality, but once they mastered the task the incentive to incur further costs dropped, resulting in a subsequently constant frequency of function use. The system group began to win an average of 2.4 out of 3 games from the third session on, as compared to 1.4 and 1.6 games for the first and second sessions, respectively.

## 5.1. Implications

Our findings strongly suggested a difference in problemsolving behavior depending on the level of system expertise. We were able to identify specific shortcomings in system-use such as planning errors, experienced restrictiveness, and efficiency hindrances. Thus, we believed that our study contributes to DSS research by calling attention to system expertise and theoretical aspects of DSS in understanding system restrictiveness and DSS Impact.

## 5.1.1. DSS research methodology

Our treatment of the system group prior to its first session was typical of many one-time DSS studies. However, DSS use behavior and problem-solving strategy was different for decision makers with DSS expertise than for novices. Our results confirm that people need preparation time even for a relatively simple IS task. However, merely feeling comfortable with the system is not enough for establishing expertise.

## 5.1.2. Theoretical contributions to DSS research

We successfully used the concept of system restrictiveness in the context of system learning by proposing that it depended on the level of system expertise. DSS can restrict the decision-making process in three ways: limiting feature availability, constraining the order of operations, and limiting user control over parameters. In our experiment, the DSS restricted the decision-making processes of novices in two ways: by the operational sequence of functions, as suggested by the larger number of planning errors and in their ability to control the system, as suggested by their abandoning many functions.

Interestingly enough, system novices who had task experience appeared to have less difficulty in system use than those who had less task experience, as indicated by a lower average number of procedural errors. It is likely that task experts better understand how the DSS makes predictions, and therefore better anticipate DSS information and action requirements. On the other hand, the better performance of the system group over the system-less group prior to the acquisition of task knowledge suggested that DSS could contribute to decision guidance when proper decision-making experience was insufficient.

The empirical data showed that, depending on the system expertise, users employed available functions differently. Our observation of how subjects develop system expertise provided a way to understand how a DSS contributed to the decisionmaking process. Subjects used decision-aid functions related to available resources more intensively and in a more focused fashion as their expertise grew. This efficient use of system functions suggested that the problem-solving strategies were effort-saving.

## Appendix A. The game

## A.1. Game

The game is played between you and the computer. Initially you and the computer are given 9 different resources marked numbers 1 through 9. For each round a random order will be generated without replacement from 1 to 9. For each order (or round), you have to decide to make a bid using one of the remaining resources you have. Anyone who makes a higher bid will win the order and score the amount of the order in the round. If you and the computer bid with the same amount of resources, the score is pending and is carried over to the next round (e.g., if you bid 5 and the computer bids 5 for the order of 6 in round 4, and you bid 8 and the computer bids 3 for the order of 3 at the next round (round 6), you score 9). You might think that your resource is wasted if you lose a round because you cannot use the same resource more than twice. Each round is repeated 9 times until all orders from 1 through 9 are generated or either you or the computer scores 23, which is more than half of all orders. (1 + 2 + 3+  +9 = 45). In sum, the objective of the game is to score 23 making full use of your resources.
