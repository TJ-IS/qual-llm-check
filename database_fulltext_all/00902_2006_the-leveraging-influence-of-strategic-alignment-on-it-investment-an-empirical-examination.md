# The leveraging influence of strategic alignment on IT investment: An empirical examination

Terry Anthony Byrd <sup>a,1</sup>, Bruce R. Lewis <sup>b,\*</sup>, Robert W. Bryan <sup>c,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business, Department of Management, Auburn University, AL 36849, USA <sup>b</sup> Wayne Calloway School of Business and Accountancy, Wake Forest University, Winston-Salem, NC 27109-7285, USA

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information and Computer Sciences, Covenant College, 14049 Scenic Highway, Lookout Mountain, GA 30750, USA

Received 17 September 2003; received in revised form 12 December 2004; accepted 21 July 2005 Available online 7 October 2005

## Abstract

Businesses have invested enormous sums in information technology (IT). The challenge now is to optimize these investments. We empirically examined the influence of the alignment between IS strategy and business strategy (strategic alignment) on the payoff of IT investment. Many studies have been performed on the value of IT investment and strategic alignment separately, in the past, but here we combined them by investigating the moderating affect of strategic alignment on the relationship between IT investment and firm performance for a group of manufacturing firms. The results indicated that there is a synergistic coupling between strategic alignment and IT investment with firm performance. Firms that have aligned IT and business strategies can inves in additional IT resources with some assurance that they will be leveraged substantially. One of our main contributions was in the examination of four differing perspectives of strategic alignment and their relationship with the payoff of IT investment. <sup>#</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Strategic alignment; IT payoff; IS strategy; IT investment

## 1. Introduction

Some analysts have estimated that one-third to onehalf of all capital expenditures of some firms are in IT [7]. Despite these huge IT expenditures, however, many senior business managers have questioned IT’s contribution to their bottom line [24,40]. Barua and Mukhopadhyay [3] noted that IT value research ignored the synergistic effects of IT with other organizational factors, such as business strategies, business goals, mass customization, and time to market. The impact of IT is realized mainly in combination with other organizational factors. One of these [31] is the alignment of information systems strategy with business strategy (referred to as strategic alignment here). There is some evidence that strategic alignment has beneficial effects. Henderson and Venkatraman [19], Reich and Benbasat [37], Chan et al. [9] reported that the alignment between IS strategy and business strategy is critical for enhanced results in many types of organizations. Lederer and Mendelow [25] noted that if IT investment is intended to support the firm’s goals, objectives and activities, efficiently and effectively, coordination of IS and business strategy is essential.

According to Brynjolfsson and Hitt [6], research into the relationships between IT and other organizational factors and the resulting effects on performance is needed if our understanding of the value of IT is to advance. Lim et al. [28] presented a framework to examine IT payoffs. This posited that contextual factors moderated the relationship between IT investment and firm performance. Our study used that framework in investigating one such contextual factor, strategic alignment, and attempted to determine whether the effects of strategic alignment and IT investment together were above and beyond the effects that each had individually on the firm’s performance.

## 1.1. IT investment and performance

Cumulative results from the earlier studies that examined the relationship between IT investment and performance, along with economics-based studies investigating IT productivity, were plagued with ambiguities and inconsistencies. Strassman [41] failed to find any benefits returned from investments in IT from his study of IT expenditures in the 1980s. Weill [45] found that transactional IT investment exhibited a positive relationship with performance, that strategic IT investment had a negative relationship with performance, and that informational IT investment had no relationship with performance. Results from several other studies, including some economics-based studies (e.g., [30]), at this time showed little or no relationship (e.g., [36]).

Recent work on the value of IT has been much more encouraging. Barua and Mukhopadhyay noted that later studies examining the value of IT investment have followed two research streams, one using production economics and the other focusing on ‘process-oriented models. Research in both streams indicated that the skepticism about the value of IT from the early studies has been mitigated [2,32]. In production economics, for example, Brynjolfsson and Hitt [5] reported positive returns on IT investment in analyzing data from 380 large Fortune 1000 firms covering the 5-year period from 1987 to 1991. Examples of similar positive results in process-oriented studies include Davis, Dehning, and Stratopoulous [12], Love and Irani [29], and Lee [26].

However, there are questions about how IT investment might combine with other organizational factors in affecting overall performance. For example, IT can reduce the firm’s fixed overhead costs, or reduce the variable costs of designing, developing, and manufacturing a product [43]. Related issues, such as business process redesign and human capital, also influence the realization of IT value [11]. Business goals are another activity associated with IT investment [42]. Chan [8] suggested that the question of the value of IT needs to move away from what does IT investment provide? to why, where, when, how, and to whom do these investments provide value?

## 1.2. Strategic alignment and performance

The literature on the strategic alignment of IS strategy and business strategy suggests that strategic alignment has a direct positive effect on performance [4,15]. Studies by Palmer and Markus [34] and Sabherwal and Chan [39] found more inconsistent results with strategic alignment leading to better performance under certain contingencies. Although some questions remain, the cumulative evidence points to a positive linkage between strategic alignment and performance in most firms.

## 1.3. Strategic alignment and IT investment

It seems reasonable to conclude that the alignment between IS strategy and business strategy should leverage the affect of IT investment on firm performance. There are several reasons to believe that strategic alignment might lead to better results. Closer alignment should indicate a closer working relationship between IT and business managers, which should lead to the development of more effective systems, especially long-term strategic systems [38]. Similarly, the alignment of IS and business strategy should facilitate more rational investments in IT and reduce improvident spending [18]. Strategic alignment would almost surely force an organization to take a long-term view of its IT investment. This should contribute to the development of more valuable enterprise-wide and interorganizational IT systems [17].

Through strategic alignment, IT and business managers are more likely to work in harmony and improve their strategic actions [33]. The alignment of strategies should increase involvement of business managers in IT activities. Top management involvement is an important factor in IT success [21,22]. Armstrong and Sambamurthy [1] showed that the inclusion of senior IT managers in top management teams and their informal interactions enhanced IT managers’ business knowledge. IT managers that are knowledgeable about business strategy and processes are better able to utilize their IT investment.

With the business and IT aligned, companies should be better able to create innovative IT applications to change business models and introduce new competitive approaches. Such IT applications are often disruptive and move industries from old established positions. Innovators of such transformative IT applications have been shown to produce positive, abnormal performance returns [14].

## 1.4. Perspectives of strategic alignment

In some past studies, strategic alignment has been associated with the process of planning the business and IS strategy, while in others, it has been used to explore the fit between realized business and IS strategies. Moreover, even within these, there are additional distinctions. In the process view, studies have looked at coordination and integration of the process of attaining strategic alignment. In the realized view, studies have considered whether the business and IT strategy match and if the two strategies have a moderating or leveraging effect on each other. It is clear that business and IS executives are concerned with coordinating or integrating the processes of developing business and IS plans.

There are three ways for the processes of developing business and IS plans to be coordinated or integrated. The business plan can be developed before, simultaneously with, or after the IS plan. The third, option, is seldom adopted today. In many organizations, especially in small and medium size manufacturing plants, the CIO does not even report to the CEO and is not part of the executive team.

With the first option, the business plan is created first. Then the IS plan may be coordinated with the business plan. In this study, we term this type a process coordination alignment of IS and business strategy. Fiedler et al. [16], argued that, as an unplanned endeavor can be counterproductive and costly, an organization should direct its investment in IT by coordinating IS strategy with overall corporate strategy. They proposed measuring coordination via a four-item instrument examining awareness, specificity, participation, and close interaction between planners, raising the following measures for a survey instrument:

1. Our IT planners are aware of the firm’s objectives, business strategies and long-term plans.

2. Our firm’s business plans provide clear directions for IT planning.

3. Our IT managers participate in the strategic business planning exercises.

4. Our IT and business planners interact closely in the formulation of the IT plan.

With this, people can be the link between the two processes in participating (choice 3) and closely interacting (choice 4). Although there may be some limitations with this because IT might not be fully exploited, past research suggests that this approach might influence the relationship between IT investment and firm performance. This leads to our first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. Process coordination alignment of business strategy and IS strategy will positively influence the relationship between IT investment and firm performance.

The second option is to develop the business and IS plan simultaneously; then the contents of the one plan can mirror the other. This type of strategic alignment process is termed integration alignment here. This is probably the most difficult option to execute, because participants are business and IS managers. King and Teo approached strategic alignment from this perspective and developed a four-stage model to measure this integration. Their scale assessed the outcome as either administrative integration at one end and full integration of the two processes at the other end. These approaches were described as:

1. Administrative integration, in which there is little significant effort to support business strategy using IT.

2. Sequential integration, in which IS strategy is derived from business strategy.

3. Reciprocal integration, in which business and IS strategies are interdependent, and influence each other.

4. Full integration, in which the two strategies are inseparable, and are developed together.

Although communication between business and IS managers may be a problem with full integration of the planning process in developing a business and IS plan at the same time, evidence seems to indicate that the benefits could override the problems. Therefore, our second hypothesis is:

Hypothesis 2. Process integration alignment of business strategy and IS strategy will positively influence the relationship between IT investment and firm performance.

The outcome viewpoint focuses on realized strategies, i.e. those initiatives that are actually implemented, while the process viewpoint involves the IS and business planning activities. An example of the outcome form of strategic alignment may be obtained from the combination of Venkatraman’s STROBE (STRategic Orientation of Business Enterprises) scale [44] and the Chan et al.’s STROEPIS (STRategic Orientation of the Existing Portfolio of Information Systems) scale. STROBE measures reflected business strategy by quantifying eight characteristics of business strategy: aggressiveness, analysis, internal defensiveness, external defensiveness, futurity, proactiveness, riskiness, and innovativeness. Chan et al. developed a coordinated scale to indicate implemented IS strategy on the same dimensions, except this scale reveals IT support for the business characteristics. For example, STROEPIS assesses IT support for aggressiveness, IT support for analysis, etc. In the Chan et al. study, two methods of determining the alignment between IS and business strategy using the STROBE and STROEPIS scales were used. One employed matching or the distance between the corresponding dimensions of STROBE and STROEPIS and the other employed moderating or the leverage of IT support on business goals between the two scale’s dimensions. Although arrived at differently, both scales provide an assessment of the alignment of IS and business strategies as they are implemented.

Strategic alignment as matching compares absolute difference in the standardized scores of the two variables. The greater the distance between the indicators of business and IS strategy, the greater the misalignment between the strategy outcomes. Again, there are indications in the literature that this form of strategic alignment could affect the relationship between IT investment and firm performance. Thus, our third hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. Outcome matching alignment of business strategy and IS strategy will positively influence the relationship between IT investment and firm performance.

The fourth type of strategic alignment is moderating alignment. The effect of this type is that of a leveraging consequence. Instead of considering the difference between the indicators, they are multiplied together to provide a measure of the leveraging effect. Comparing this with matching, low scores on both business and IS strategy will yield basically the same alignment value as high scores on both business and IS strategy, high scores of the strategies in moderation will result in a high measure of alignment versus low scores of the strategies that will result in low measure of alignment. This measure does not reward low level of business and IS strategy even if they are matched or very close in value. The higher the levels of business strategy and IS strategy based on the indicators, the higher the measure for the moderation form of alignment. This measure has been used several times in the literature and this kind of strategic alignment could potentially have an influence on the relationship between IT and firm performance. Hence, our last hypothesis is:

Hypothesis 4. Outcome moderating alignment of IS strategy and business strategy will positively influence the relationship between IT investment and firm performance.

## 2. Methodology

To address the research hypotheses, a survey of companies in the southeastern U.S. that manufacture fabricated metal products (US SIC 344) was conducted. We chose this industry segment because it has been acknowledged, with related industries, as having advanced IT in manufacturing [13]. In particular, our focus on a specific industry segment in a restricted geographic area was selected so we could control the influence of exogenous variables. We realize that this choice reduces the generalizability of our conclusions. However, questions concerning the synergies of IT investment and other organizational variables will not be resolved in one study.

We examined Dun and Bradstreets’ Million Dollar Directory and Ward’s Business Directory of US Private and Public Companies to develop a potential pool of manufacturing firms in this SIC. Our search resulted in a pool of 672 plants in the southeastern United States, however, some of these were quite small. It is generally felt that larger organizations will have more experience with the use of IT than smaller ones [10]. Therefore, we only included plants in our sample that had at least 75 employees. Of the original 302 of these, duplicates and attrition (mergers, acquisitions, and cessations) reduced the final list to 275 plants, all privately held.

Our research methodology involved the administration of two questionnaires in each manufacturing plant: one for the plant manager and one for the IT director. Since the businesses were private companies, we felt that the plant manager would know the business strategy, while the IT director should know the plant’s IS strategy. By having two respondents from each plant and combining their responses, we should reduce bias in the data.

## 2.1. Measures

The four measures of strategic alignment previously described were used. The first used the Fiedler, Grover, and Teng’s four-item scale for assessing the coordination of the two planning processes. We asked both managers to respond by selecting the item that most accurately described the situation in their plant, and we averaged their responses. The second measure, from the King and Teo integration scale, assessed strategic alignment from the process perspective; it also contained four items. We again averaged the manager’s responses to compute this score.

Two other measures resulted from the combination of Venkatraman’s STROBE scale (implemented business strategy) and the Chan et al. STROEPIS scale (implemented IS strategy); this measured strategic alignment from the outcome viewpoint. We asked plant managers to respond to the STROBE items, while the plant IT directors responded to the STROEPIS scale. We used both the STROBE/STROEPIS matching (difference) scores and the moderating (product) scores as alignment measures. The matching calculation utilized the differences between corresponding factors in the two scales and moderating method used the products of corresponding factors. The results from each calculation were averaged across the scale factors to determine a final alignment score for each method. The individual items for these scales are shown in Appendix A.

The questionnaire to the plant managers also asked about plant performance (revenues and profits before taxes and bonuses for the past year) and the number of employees in the plant. We used the employee count to standardize revenues and profits by size, resulting in two different measures of firm performance: profit divided by the number of employees and revenue divided by the number of employees, with the assumption of constant return to scale from labor. On the questionnaire for the plant IT director, we included a request for the total IT expenditures for the plant (3-year average of annual expenses for personnel, hardware and software). We also standardized this number by dividing by the number of employees and used this measure for IT investment. The measures are summarized in Table 1.

## 2.3. Data collection

We sent out 275 questionnaires to plants that manufacture fabricated metal products, with at least 75 employees. We received 84 responses of usable pairs of questionnaires from the two managers in one plant: a response rate of 30.5%. The plant manager had been with the company from thirteen to 16 years, and with the specific business unit from 10 to 15 years. Typically, the plant IT director had been with the company and the business unit for 10–12 years.

## 2.4. Diagnostic checking

Inordertocheckforviolationsoftheassumptionsofthe regression models in our analyses, we performed several standard diagnosticprocedures. Thenormality oftheerror termsinthemodelswasassessedbyexamininghistograms of the residual distributions. Residual plots were employed to appraise the homoscedasticity (constancy of variance) of the error terms in the models. The presence ofautocorrelation(correlationamongpairs oferrorterms) in the models was investigated with Durbin–Watson statistics. Multicollinearity (correlation among the independent variables) was evaluated by computing the variance inflation factors (VIFs) for the terms in the models.Ingeneral, wefoundthatourregression equations exhibited acceptable compliance with the assumptions of the linear regression model but in the moderated regression models we did detect a low level of multicollinearity in the interaction term. This was not surprising, given that the interaction term in each of the moderated regression models was a direct function of the two main-effects terms. Even though the extent of the observed multicollinearity was not judged to be serious, we adopted a conservative approach and reran all of the moderated regressions using centered versions of our variables. As recommended by Kleinbaum et al. [23], we centered the variables two ways: by subtracting the variable mean from the individual data points, and by computing z-scores. The regression analyses from both methods produced results similar to our original analyses.

## 3. Results

The dimensions of STROBE and STROEPIS were tested for construct validity using confirmatory factor analysis and for reliability with Cronbach’s alpha. The results from these analyses, given in Appendix A, show that the parameters for all the dimensions were within the guidelines specified for confirmatory analysis with all the fit indicators above 0.90, adjusted chi-squares with values less than 5.0, and the RMSR statistics below

0.08. All of the reliabilities for the dimensions are near or greater than 0.70.

The business performance construct was measured in two ways: using the plant manager’s estimate of pretax profits for the past year per employee, and the plant manager’s estimate of revenue for the past year per employee. Consequently, two regression analyses were conducted to address each of the hypotheses. For each of the business performance measures, an equation was first computed for the relationship between IT investment and strategic alignment (the independent variables) against business performance (the dependent variable). This equation provided the baseline information for the relationship and is termed the base model. Then another analysis was performed using the moderation or interaction term (IT investment - strategic alignment) along with IT investment and strategic alignment as independent variables regressed against the performance measure, either revenues or profits.

The results of the base model showed a significant relationship between IT investment and firm performance in each of the two equations. The relationship with firm performance was typically very strong at a significance level less than 0.001. On the other hand, none of the strategic alignment measures were significantly related to firm performance in the base models. This finding was the opposite to that in most past research.

The results from the moderated regressions on these data using revenue per employee as the measure for business performance are reported in Table 3. In three of these four moderated regression equations, the coefficient of the cross-product term was statistically significant, confirming that an interaction effect was present. This was the case when IS and business strategy alignment was measured via the Fiedler, Grover, and Teng process coordination scale and with both of the STROBE/STROEPIS measures. The sign of the coefficient of the cross-product term in each of these three equations was positive. This indicated that the strategic alignment of IS and business strategy created additional positive effects on revenue per employee above and beyond the use of IT investment and strategic alignment individually. Further, the $R ^ { 2 }$ for each of these three moderated regressions (0.598, 0.715, 0.661) showed clear improvement over the $R ^ { 2 }$ of the corresponding base models (0.522, 0.510, 0.496), providing additional evidence of the value of strategic alignment on IT investment.

The results of the regressions when profit per employee was used as the measure for business performance are presented in Table 4. Again, in three of the four moderated regression equations, the coefficient of the cross-product term was statistically significant, confirming that moderation was present. And again, this was evidenced by the Fiedler, Grover, and Teng process coordination scale and with both of the STROBE/STROEPIS measures. Likewise, the sign of the coefficient of the cross-product term in each of these three equations was positive, indicating additional returns from the synergy between strategic alignment and IT investment. The $R ^ { 2 }$ for each of these three moderated regressions (0.777, 0.872, 0.854) again shows distinct improvement over the $R ^ { 2 }$ of the base models (0.618, 0.629, 0.644). Thus, the positive influence of strategic alignment on IT investment was present for both revenue and profit, with the process coordination measure and both of the outcome measures of strategic alignment. Therefore, hypotheses 1, 3, and 4 were supported; hypothesis 2 was not.

The sign for the IT investment factor in each of the moderated regression equations was negative and statistically significant. Moderated regression includes an interaction term between the main effect factors. In the presence of a significant interaction term, the coefficients of the main effect factors in the equation would not normally be interpreted individually. However, in our work, the implication of the negative sign of the IT investment factor can be seen when the strategic alignment factor is set to zero (when there is no alignment between a firm’s IS strategy and business strategy, an increase in IT investment may decrease firm performance). This perspective also confirms the importance of strategic alignment.

## 4. Discussion

Our results indicated that the concept of strategic alignment of business and IT strategy is rather robust. We investigated four different perspectives of strategic alignment, two focused on alignment in the planning process of business and IT (coordination and integration) and two focused on the realized or outcome alignment of business strategy and IT strategy (matching and moderating). We found that one of the process alignment factors (coordination) and both outcome alignment factors (matching and moderating) showed a leveraging effect on the relationship between IT investment and firm performance. The moderating effects of these versions of strategic alignment are fairly large, as indicated by the increases in $R ^ { 2 }$ from our base models to the moderated models.

According to our findings, the real value of strategic alignment is in leveraging the firm’s IT investment, at least in small and medium size manufacturing firms. The firm can increase revenues and profits without investing more in IT but by better aligning IT and business strategies. One way to achieve this outcome is through strategic alignment. In addition, firms that have aligned IT and business strategies can invest in additional IT resources with some assurance that they will be leveraged substantially by the alignment.

Our study also showed that the process integration conceptualization of strategic alignment was not necessarily useful; it did not exhibit a leveraging effect on the relationship between IT investment and firm performance. This may be fairly significant in at least two ways: not every view of strategic alignment yields positive results and there may still be a problem with trying to create and develop both plans in unison. In small and medium size firms, the gap between business and IT managers might be very pronounced. Since slack resources are likely to be scarce there, the managers simply may not have the time to learn enough about each other’s areas to be effective in developing joint plans.

## 4.1. Limitations

This study utilized a survey of firms in the 344 SIC in the southern United States. They produce such items as doors, windows, ladders, components for metal buildings, metal joists, and boilers. This classification is narrow and thus limits the generality of our results. Another limitation was that the data represented a snapshot in time. They do not suggest what might occur as the firms progressed from one stage of IT growth and development to another.

## 5. Conclusion

The purpose of this research was to seek empirical evidence regarding the utility of different conceptualizations of strategic alignment on the effectiveness of IT investment. Generally, we found that most views of this concept were positively related to firm performance. The alignment of IS strategy with business strategy has been touted as a critical element of IT management and it proved to be so in our study – not as a direct contributor to firm performance – but as a moderator between IT investment and firm performance.
