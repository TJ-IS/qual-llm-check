## A B S T R A C T

IT researchers have recently distinguished habits from prior behavior frequency. We expanded this research by examining habit’s antecedents and investigating the simultaneous effect of habit and prior IT use on continued IT use. We found that the research model was relatively robust over four specific use activities of one software application. Indeed, prior IT use, satisfaction, and importance significantly influenced IT habits. Also, while prior IT use predicted continued IT use in the combined data set, habit only predicted continued IT use for activities with higher habit levels. Practical implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Since the early 19th century, learning theorists have been experimenting with animals and humans to understand the formation of habits, which can be defined as learned actions that have become automatic responses to cues and are intended to obtain one or more goals. They are automatic because they occur without a person’s awareness [20]. While often used to describe a behavior, habit is not a behavior in itself; it is a mind-set that drives a person to perceive habit-related stimulus cues [12]. Thus, habitual behavior is the action resulting from the cue. For example, we can describe a person’s behavioral tendency to stop at Starbucks for coffee on his/her way to work as a habit and the actual behavior of stopping at Starbucks as the habitual behavior.

Despite attempts to understand habit forming activities, research on it has progressed slowly [21]. This is surprising as habits allow people to initiate and control many daily activities in an efficient and non-taxing manner [1,24]. However, researchers have historically represented habit as a result of prior behavior [22], though habits are more than frequently repeated behaviors, which do not always form habits. For example a surgeon’s decision to operate is not the result of a habit.

While habits have mostly been studied as non-business behaviors, such as travel modes and exercise [3], IT researchers have recently begun to explore habit, which may be due to the extent to which people use IT automatically because of learning [11]. Examining habit and prior IT use as distinct constructs, IT researchers have shown that prior use frequency influences habits and that habits influence continued IT use. Measuring habit separately from prior behavior frequency has also allowed researchers to explore habit’s antecedents and to determine its effect, in addition to the effect of prior behavior, on future behavior.

Our study objective was to examine habit’s antecedents and the effect of habit and prior IT use on continued IT use. We developed a research model using two theoretical foundations: learning theory and a post-adoption model described by Jasperson et al. [7]. We investigated four antecedents: prior IT use frequency, satisfaction, task complexity, and importance. While theory suggests all four factors are antecedents to habit, only prior IT use frequency and satisfaction had been studied previously in an IT context. Also, Limayem et al. examined habit’s effects on continued IT use without taking into account the effects of prior IT use. Our research model included these additional effects (see Fig. 1). We tested the research model with data pertaining to four different IT use activities in one software application.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Post-adoption IT models

While initial IT adoption or acceptance is known to be important researchers have argued that post-implementation or post-adoption IT use is also vital for long-term success. Efforts have mostly used intention-based models such as TAM to examine initial acceptance. Some researchers also have used intentionbased models to examine post-adoption behavior. Early work found that similar beliefs explained less variance in intention for experienced than inexperienced users, suggesting that prior experience was an important factor in explaining continued IT use. More recently, Bhattacherjee [4] developed and tested a postadoption model based on expectation–disconfirmation theory. This model depicted satisfaction, perceived usefulness, and confirmation of expectations as the important predictors of individuals’ intentions to continue using a technology; it explained 42% of the variance in continued IT use intention for online banking users, and has been validated for other technologies [5,18].

Other researchers have suggested that post-adoption IT use behavior may be driven by habit, in addition to intentions and prior use. For example, the post-adoption IT model developed by Jasperson et al. proposed that their behaviors can become habitual; this model included factors pertaining to individual cognition, thought processes, and behavior after a technology had been installed and made available. The individual factors were connected by two feedback loops: one represented reflective thought about early use behavior, while the other represented non-reflective thought that became active as individual behavior became habitual. However, this was not empirically tested.

While some empirical researchers found that with increased prior use, intentions had less influence on continued use behaviors [10], others have empirically tested post-adoption IT use models that explicitly included habit; e.g., Limayem et al. developed and tested a model that integrated habit-related theories into Bhattacherjee’s model.

While providing insight into habit’s role in IT continuance, Limayem et al.’s model does not include several potentially important habit antecedents. It also does not depict the influence of prior IT use on continued use. Our model included additional antecedents and the path between prior and continued IT use.

## 2.2. Learning theories

Various learning theory researchers have studied habit and its role in predicting behavior. This has led to research involving resistance to change, computer learning [23], e-learning [6], and experts’ strategy choices [17]. While specific learning theories differ somewhat, there is general consensus that a drive to satisfy a need leads to a response to a particular stimulus, and if the response is reinforced and/or repeated, a particular habit is learned. Thus early learning theories and post-adoption IT models depict habit as an integral part of human behavior.

## 3. Hypotheses development

## 3.1. Prior IT use implies habit

According to the post-adoption IT use model, use is a factor that changes prior IT use that is not habitual, into continued use that has become habitual. Prior IT use is an individual’s use of an IT application and its features. Over time, prior IT use can create habits—an automatic tendency to use the IT.

Because using a distinct habit construct is a recent phenomena there are only a few studies linking prior behavior to habit. Limayem et al. found that past WWW use influenced WWW use habit, and Verplanken and Orbell found that habit was stronger for high frequency past behavior than for low frequency for a variety of behaviors.

H1. Prior IT use will positively influence IT use habit.

## 3.2. Satisfaction implies habit

The post-adoption IT model also describes the role of technology sense-making in forming habit. Technology sensemaking is a cognitive process in which individuals confirm or ignore pre-use expectations by comparing them to post-use outcomes. This evaluation results in satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the process, which can cause individuals to continue or change their use patterns. When satisfaction causes individuals to continue using IT, there is likely to be an increase in habit formation.

Despite the overwhelming agreement that satisfactory experiences can form habits, empirical findings for the satisfaction–habit relationship are few. An exception is Limayem et al., who found that satisfaction positively influenced WWW use habits.

H2. Satisfaction will positively influence IT use habit.

## 3.3. Attention: importance and task complexity imply habit

The post-adoption IT model proposed that individuals attended to interventions, such as training courses or some other person’s use, and that this can cause them to begin or increase use of specific features.

Two important factors associated with attention in postadoption use contexts are importance (personal relevance. that is closely related to involvement, interest, salience, and goaldirected arousal capacity) and task complexity.

Researchers suggest that habitual behaviors are more important and personally relevant, more goal-directed [1], and more self-identifying than non-habitual behavior. This implies that a behavior’s importance will increase the likelihood that a habit will develop. In support of this implication, Novak et al. [15] showed that the correlation between importance and web site usage increased over time. Thus, it seemed reasonable that the importance of a task would increase habit.

## H3. Importance will positively influence IT use habit.

Task complexity (difficulty, ambiguity, and lack of clarity involved in the task) has a role in forming habits; it can influence one’s attention such that less is paid to more complex behavior [13]. In our study, we examine subjective task complexity, which can be influenced by objective task characteristics or an individual’s familiarity with the task, assessed cognitive resources, and availability of tools and time [14]. Complex tasks require increased cognitive load, which can reduce individuals’ ability to pay attention. Also, complexity can represent shifting or unstable environments where new information emerges during performance. Stable environments are important for developing habits because they allow the smooth execution of practiced responses.

Empirical researchers have found an association between habit and task complexity. For example, Wood et al. found that habitual behaviors reported by subjects were less complex than nonhabitual behaviors, and Verplanken found that significantly stronger habits formed in a less complex word-processing task than in a more complex word-processing task.

## H4. Task complexity will negatively influence IT use habit.

In summary we predicted that prior IT use, satisfaction, importance, and task complexity would contribute to habit development.

## 3.4. Continued IT use

IT use that occurs after initial acceptance represents postadoption behavior and results from multiple usage decisions. We examined how continued IT use is affected by both habit and prior IT use as proposed by the post-adoption IT model.

## 3.5. Prior IT use implies continued IT use

Most researchers have agreed that past behavior influences future behavior. Similarly, researchers have found that prior IT use predicts its continued use [8,9,19]. Despite its predictive power, some have suggested that the prior behavior–future behavior link merely demonstrates temporal stability (the same factors that determined behavior in the past continue to exert their influence in the present) or may indicate unmeasured variables or shared variance among behavior measures [2]. Despite these limitations, Ajzen recommended including prior behavior in behavioral models because it can improve predictions, especially when intentions are weak or unstable, expectations are inaccurate, or when people have not developed a clear plan of action. We therefore included prior and continued IT use relationship to assess residual effects.

## H5. Prior IT use will positively influence continued IT use.

## 3.6. Habit implies continued IT use

We also anticipated that habit would influence continued IT use. The post-adoption IT model proposed that habit would influence continued IT use more as behavior becomes less reflective. This suggested that the stronger an individual’s tendency to perform a behavior automatically from learned responses, the more frequently he or she would perform the behavior. In fact, continued IT use frequency may increase because habitual behaviors are cognitively easier and faster to process than behaviors that require more reflective processing. Saba et al. [16] found that habit significantly predicted future food consumption quantity, and Limayem and colleagues find that habit significantly predicted continued web site use.

Researchers have found that a habit-continued behavior link may be more complex than a simple direct relationship. For example, habit can have a moderating role on continued behavior with behavioral intention. This moderating relationship may arise because habit lessens the need to access one’s intentions. Thus, as habits grow stronger they have more influence on continued behavior, and when they are weaker they have less influence. Our study did not consider intention but we did predict that habit’s effect on future behavior would be weaker when habit was at a lower level than when it was at a higher level. Ajzen contended that strong attitudes and intentions were better predictors of future behavior than weak attitudes and intentions, because these reflected some ambivalence, indifference, or uncertainty. Similarly, we expected this would hold for habit perceptions. We therefore tested this by comparing the habit-continued use relationship among subject groups.

H6. Habit will positively influence continued IT use frequency.

H6a. The positive relationship between habit and continued IT use frequency will be weaker (i.e. lower path coefficient) for specific IT use activities that have lower mean levels of habit.

## 4. Research method

Our study examined students’ perceptions and use of a university internet application (UIA) at a large urban U.S. university. We collected data from four specific use activities that students perform using the UIA; these included changing contact information (activity 1), enrolling in a closed section (activity 2), paying tuition and fees (activity 3), and viewing financial aid information (activity 4). We chose these activities as they represented different use and perception levels as determined from a questionnaire administered in a previous semester. Stimulus cues for these activities included moving, registering for classes, and changing a class schedule. While some cues may be infrequent, leading to infrequent system use, habits can form even for low-frequency behavior. Also, while habitual behavior can be volitional or nonvolitional, the UIA is a voluntary system in which individuals have alternative ways to perform the activity; for example, students can change contact information by going to the appropriate department and filling out a form. We validated this by asking subjects two questions about alternatives and found that all agreed there were alternatives for all these activities.

## 4.1. Subjects and procedure

Subjects were undergraduate students attending a second-year business course. They were offered extra credit for participating in this study or alternative activities. Early in the semester the researchers sent email invitations to participate in our survey. Four hundred and nineteen subjects responded. The online questionnaire randomly assigned subjects to respond to questions addressing a specific use activity provided they had performed it at least once in the past. By semester end, 371 of the original 419 (89%) subject pool had responded to a second online questionnaire to report their UIA usage during the semester for their use activity.

## 4.2. Measures

The scales are shown in Appendix A. For most measures we adopted prior researchers’ questions. We also measured prior and continued IT use as the number of times subjects used the system. We used one open scale item and one closed scale item to measure prior IT use and continued IT use as recommended by Kim and Malhotra. To lessen outlier effects in the analysis, we used a ranking of the open scale items. Because they were measured using different scales, we also standardized both use measures for prior and continued IT use.

## 5. Results

Before performing any analyses of the data, we assessed multicollinearity and common method variance for the data set. We found that these were not problems in our data.

Table 1 presents the demographics and means of all variable for each activity. We assessed the prior and continued use means for reasonableness based on the functionality offered by the system. For example, the prior IT use frequency (open) mean for changing contact information is 3.15. This seems to be reasonable frequency level for mean prior use given this use activity can include changing physical addresses, email addresses, and phone numbers for temporary and permanent residences. For each variable we also ran an ANOVA to compare means by activity. The activity means were significantly different for all variables except satisfaction and gender. We then used PLS-Graph to analyze the measurement and structural models for the entire data set and for each activity. This was an appropriate technique for our analysis because it makes no assumptions about the population or scale of the measurement; it has a sample size requirement of 10 times the largest number of independent constructs affecting a dependent construct and the sample size for each activity fell within these requirements.

## 5.1. Measurement model analyses

To analyze the measurement model we first assessed convergent validity by examining item loadings, the internal consistency reliability (ICR) and average variance extracted (AVE). We found that the second and third habit items loaded at less than the 0.70 minimum in at least four of the data sets, thus we dropped those items. While the second item for continued IT use loaded at 0.62 for activity 3, we retained it in our analysis because it loaded above 0.70 in the other activities and we wanted to preserve a two-item scale for the dependent variable.

Table 2 presents the loadings for all remaining factors. The ICR’s ranged from 0.79 to 0.98 and the AVE’s ranged from 0.66 to 0.96; these values are above the required minimum of 0.70 and 0.50, respectively (see Table 3). Thus our results showed adequate convergent validity.

Next we assessed discriminant validity by examining the crossloadings and the square root of the AVE’s. To compute the crossloadings we used the PLS procedure described by Yi and Davis [25]. Cross-loadings in PLS-Graph can be up to twice the size of crossloadings from a principal components analysis. We found that the cross-loadings in each data set were similar to those reported in other research ventures and are lower than the item loadings. This indicated discriminant validity. We also found that the square root of the AVE was higher than correlations among constructs. This also demonstrated discriminant validity.

## 5.2. Structural model analysis

The structural model for the combined data set supported all but two hypotheses. Prior IT use, satisfaction and importance significantly influenced habit supporting H1–H3. H4 is not supported because task complexity does not significantly influence habit. Prior IT use significantly influenced continued IT use, however habit did not. Thus H5 was supported but H6 was not (Table 4).

Table 4 also presented the structural results for each activity. H6a was tested by statistically comparing the path coefficient from habit to continued IT use in activity 2 to the same path coefficient in the other four activities. We found that the path coefficient for activity 2 was significantly lower than the path coefficient for activity 1 $( t = 2 . 3 2 , ~ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , activity $3 \ ( t = 1 . 9 4 , \ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and activity 4 $( t = 1 . 9 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . In fact for activity 2, the path was not significant. Thus, activity 2 with significantly lower mean habit from the other activities also had a significantly lower (and nonsignificant) influence of habit on continued IT use than the other activities. This showed support for H6a. There was only one other difference among groups. The beta coefficient for prior IT use to continued IT use was significantly higher for activity 4 than for activity 3 (t = 2.14, p < 0.05).

## 6. Discussion

Our study investigates the antecedents and determinants of IT habit. We found support for four out of six hypotheses using our combined data set. Our research model explained between 48% and 59% of the variance in habit, which is larger than that explained in prior IT habit studies. While the variance is low for some use activities, the variance explained for the activity with the greatest prior use is comparable to other IT habit studies. This level of predictiveness is surprising considering that our research model did not include behavioral intention, which typically explains most of the variance in continued IT use.

We used measures that have been validated by previous researchers and that reflect habit as a tendency to behave in an automatic manner, rather than simply assessing prior use frequency. We also examined four antecedents to habit, two of which were new. We developed these antecedents by merging learning theory and post-adoption IT use models. Our research was the first to show both effects in the same model. Our study provided evidence that habit is a separate effect. Finally, we examined four specific use activities that vary in habit strength, task complexity, importance, and use frequency and showed that the model was fairly robust over the use activities.

## 6.1. Habit’s antecedents

The antecedents included prior IT use, satisfaction, importance, and task complexity. As predicted, prior IT use had a significant effect on habit for the combined data set. The relationship’s path coefficients were significant and similar across all use activities, except for activity 1 where the effect was non-significant; this was important because the activities ranged from very low to moderate use levels. This also demonstrated that subjects reported development of IT habit despite low prior use; subjects somewhat agreed that they have a tendency to perform the behavior automatically. These results contradict researchers who suggest behaviors must be frequently repeated to form habits.

We found that the most influential habit antecedent was satisfaction. However, habits may not always be positive; habits may make individuals less responsive to small changes in stimuli, so that they may not change even if the habitual behavior is not the most appropriate response. At some point in the post-adoption period, habitual use could cause dissatisfaction.

The other two habit antecedents we investigated were importance and task complexity. Importance had a small but significant effect on habit, and this effect was similar in magnitude across all four use activities. Despite our predictions, task complexity did not have a significant effect on habit for the combined data set or for any of the specific use activities. However, in a supplemental analysis we examined task complexity’s indirect effect on habit through satisfaction for the combined data set. From this we found that task complexity had a significant negative indirect effect on habit through satisfaction.

## 6.2. Habit and continued IT use

This study also contributes to habit research by analyzing the simultaneous impacts habit and prior IT use have on continued IT use. For the combined data set habit does not have a significant influence on continued IT use. However, examining these effects by activity we find that all activities except for activity 2 show a significant relationship between habit and continued IT use. Because activity 2 has the lowest habit level, habit perceptions may need to be at a certain level to influence or predict future behavior. By showing a specific use activity where habit no longer influences continued IT use, this finding complements other work that shows that habit’s influence on continued IT use decreases (and intention’s influence increases) as behavior becomes less automatic.

Our study emphasized the need to examine these issues further by including prior IT use, intentions, and habit as predictors of continued IT use in settings that vary in use frequency.

## 6.3. Practical implications

One implication for practitioners is to be aware that IT use can be perceived as habitual or automatic, and that such perceptions and resulting behaviors can have positive or negative effects. For example, for most use activities habit can increase continued IT use.

Another practical implication is that understanding habit’s antecedents can help managers identify and manipulate habit formation; if managers want to increase users’ habits because they become more efficient, our study suggests that they should increase these factors. Further, organizations can encourage additional IT use by encouraging users to try out different system features via user training and ongoing system improvements.

Finally, managers and system developers should be aware that associating habits with ‘‘computer use’’ in general is not as accurate as understanding how habit affects each specific use activity. Increasing satisfaction with a system will increase habit, but increasing prior IT use and/or importance will only increase habit for some activities.

## 7. Limitations and conclusion

This study had two obvious limitations.

First, we collected data from student subjects using a university system. Thus it is possible that the results will not apply well to other IT operations or other populations that differ in age, experience, or context. However, students constituted a natural population of UIA users and the UIA was important to their interaction with the university. Thus, subject’s involvement with the IT was not a critical limitation as might be the case with other research using student subjects.

Second, because we used self-reported use measures, it would be difficult to determine how well our findings generalize to objective use measures. University privacy policies made it impossible for us to log subjects’ UIA use.

In summary, our research adds to an emerging IT research area. Today’s computer users are knowledgeable and in some contexts have often become ‘‘addicted’’ to new technology. While it is not clear yet whether habitual use behavior will have long-term benefits, academics and practitioners should understand how IT habits form and how they affect continued IT use. Our study contributes by (1) integrating habit antecedents and determinants from learning theories and post-adoption IT models, (2) including the effects of both habit and prior IT use on continued IT use in the same model, and (3) testing the model using data from four different specific use activities. Our findings show that habit can be predicted by prior IT use, importance, and satisfaction. Also, in some use activities both habit and prior IT use affect continued IT use, whereas in other use activities just one or the other factor affects continued IT use.
