## Abstract:

Recently, firms start to offer samples of physical products online to reach wider audience. However, literature has mostly examined offline product sampling or online sampling of digital products that differ from physical products. To address the gaps, we build on the theoretical logic of uncertainty reduction and signaling perspective to consider physical product characteristics in developing our hypotheses, and employed a unique dataset from an e-commerce platform for hypothesis testing. Our analysis reveals that online sampling could increase the physical product sales, while popular brands enjoyed greater advantages in terms of increase in both immediate and lagged sales.

Keywords: Online sampling; Physical products; Electronic commerce; Difference-in-difference.

## 1 1. Introduction

Product sampling, which offers a free amount or a trial of a product for consumers, is an old but effective way implemented by firms to enter a new market, promote a new product, or increase existing product sales (Jain et al. 1995; Marks and Kamins 1988; McGuiness et al. 1995). It is designed to allow consumers to experience a product firsthand, thereby reducing uncertainty about its quality and the cost of trying, with the aim to induce subsequent purchase. The effectiveness of this marketing strategy is evidenced by the extent of sales increment it can stimulate, which could be as high as 84% (Lammers, 1991) to 500% (Lindstedt, 1999).

Recently, firms start to offer samples of physical products such as food, cosmetics, personal care, and electronics to consumers through e-commerce platforms, e.g., Pinchme.com in the U.S., www.trynbuyindia.com in India, and try.taobao.com and yhd.com in China. Compared to doing so offline, administering product sampling online may afford several advantages, including (1) the ability to accurately target intended consumers. E-commerce platforms such as try.taobao.com run product sampling campaigns for brands and invite their registered customers to request for the product sample. They may then select (on behalf of the brand concerned) to whom a sample be given based on customers' demographic information provided and/or their purchase history (e.g., male customers who frequently purchased personal care products may be selected to sample a free shaver). In the offline context, e.g., a supermarket, staff who distribute product samples may need to rely on observations and hunches to decide who should be given a sample; (2) the possibility for product information to be disseminated to a wider audience given that whoever visits the e-commerce website can see the sampling campaigns. This can increase the exposure of the product and

the brand concerned. In contrast, in the offline context, the administration of product sampling is usually visible only to people who happen to be around the location at the particular time. As noted in the literature, offline product sampling usually affords only one-time exposure (Marks and Kamin 1988); (3) the capability to comprehensively track consumer responses after a sampling campaign ended, such as whether and when they purchase the product. Although this may be possible in the offline context, e.g., with the use of membership card in a supermarket, whether a customer uses the card in every purchase to enable accurate tracking is beyond control.

Despite the above advantages, whether and in what ways online product sampling may lead to favorable outcomes are not well understood. Previous research in the online context has investigated the effects of sampling of digital products such as music and software (Hu et al. 2010; Peitza and Waelbroeck 2006; Cheng and Liu 2012; Wang and Zhang 2009; Zhou and Duan 2012). However, physical products differ from digital products in that they are less vulnerable to the duplication and piracy issues plaguing the latter, which could severely discount the value of offering samples (Wang and Zhang 2009). For instance, consumers may seek a pirated copy of a software after they sampled it and found it to be useful (rather than buying it). This is less of a concern for physical products that are consumed offline. Taken together, this may imply that findings obtained about the effects of product sampling, both in the offline and online contexts (with regard to digital products), may not be entirely applicable to online sampling involving physical products.

In this study, we collaborated with a leading e-commerce platform in China to collect a panel data of 122 online product sampling campaigns to investigate their effects on sales. In our empirical analyses, we considered the immediate and lagged sales effects of online product sampling, and their variations across popular and non-popular brands. A difference-in-difference (DID) method was used to identify the effects of online

sampling. The results indicate that administering online product sampling could increase overall sales by 41.6% compared with a control group (with similar products that did not offer sampling). Furthermore, we find that for popular brands, online product sampling has both immediate and lagged sales effects, whereas only a lagged sales effect was observed for non-popular brands. An additional finding is that the magnitude of sales growth is larger for popular brands than non-popular brands. Taken together, our results suggest that the dominant effect of online product sampling may not lie in offering a direct trial experience to induce purchase; rather, an advertising effect may be equally, if not more, important for this strategy.

## 2 2. Literature Review

In this section, we review the extant literature on product sampling in the offline followed by the online context.

## Product Sampling in the Offline Context

In the offline context, product sampling has mainly been administered in-store or distributed to consumers outside of store. Heiman et al. (2001) suggested that the setting of product sampling can lead to different sales effects. Specifically, in-store sampling has higher effects on immediate sales, whereas sampling by mail delivery may have long-term effects. Below we examine the corresponding literature separately.
In-store sampling has been the most popular promotional tactic for grocery products. Free samples are often offered to a small number of

# ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

consumers with minimum targeting (e.g., customers who happen to be in the store). Previous literature has shown that offering free product samples in-store could increase sales (Heilman et al. 2004; Amor and Guilbert 2009), while the effect may be fleeting (Lawson et al. 1990) and extend to non-sampled brands within the same category (Lammers 1991).

Compared to in-store sampling, consumers who participate in mail-delivery sampling have to experience a longer interval to receive the samples, and its impacts on product sales thus demonstrate different characteristics. Researchers attempted to examine whether mail-delivery sampling could increase sales over a long period of time. Bawa and Shoemaker (2004) found that free samples can be highly effective in raising sales for at least 12 months after the delivery of the free samples. This sheds light on the long-term effects of sampling because no previous literature has studied such effects beyond two weeks, except for Gedenk and Neslin (2000). A key reason of why the two approaches lead to different sales effects (immediate or delayed) is that sampling reduces product uncertainty (Jain et al. 1995; Marks and Kamins 1988) by offering direct trial experience (ability to feel, touch, and consume the product); thus, whence consumers receive, the product plays an important role.

Furthermore, to strengthen the effects of mail-delivery sampling, some studies have shown that it may be more effective to combine free sampling with other promotions such as coupons (Scott 1976; McGuiness et al. 1995). However, Scott and Yalch (1980) found that such incentives (e.g., coupons) may weaken the effects of sampling when consumers attributed their behaviors to the incentives. In addition, previous research has also considered how sampling and advertising can be implemented in combination for better marketing outcomes. This is considering that product sampling can provide prospective customers with direct product trial experience to reduce uncertainty (but limited in its extent of reach), whereas advertising has its strength in communicating marketing messages to a large audience. Specifically, previous research

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

has investigated how interactions between advertising and sampling may be influenced by the sequence with which they are administered (Smith 1993; Kempf and Laczniak 2001), the focus of advertising (Micu and Coulter 2007), and the nature of the products involved (Wright and Lynch 1995; Micu and Coulter 2007), such as experiential versus non-experiential and hedonic versus utilitarian product types. For instance, Smith (1993) found that when advertising was administered before sampling, it could mitigate the negative effects resulted from uncontended trial experience. There has also been research that compares between sampling and advertising, and shows that attitudes based on sampling are stronger, more confidently held, and serve as better predictors of consumer behaviors than advertising (Smith and Swinyard 1983, 1988; Marks and Kamins, 1988). The assumption of these studies is that product sampling (in particular, in the offline context) and advertising can serve only exclusive goals (providing direct trial experience vs. increasing exposure).

## Product Sampling in the Online Context

In the online context, the extant research has mostly focused on the effects of sampling involving digital products or information goods. For example, Hu et al. (2010) find that offering music samples communicate a strong product quality signal that could attract a higher number of interested shoppers, thus leading to higher sales. Zhou and Duan (2012) examine the impacts of free trial version of software programs on consumers' online word-of-mouth (WOM) activities and their purchase decisions. They find that free sampling not only directly leads to more sales but also enhances online WOM effect. Peitza and Waelbroeck (2006) develop an analytical model to examine the effectiveness of sampling in the context of digital music. They find that the negative effect of downloading digital music may be overcompensated by the positive effect of sampling. Wang and Zhang (2009) also show, through an analytical model, that when sampling for information goods can be controlled by the

# ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

provider, the monopolist can actually benefit from offering free sampling. Schlereth et al. (2013) find that administering sampling on online social networks and engaging the influential network members could be viable. In general, these studies show that offering digital product sampling can provide favorable effects (such as increasing sales), while also noting that such effects may be constrained by piracy issues to some extent given their ease of duplication and distribution.

Notwithstanding some interests in the literature on digital product sampling, the focus on offering sampling for physical products in the online context has been scant. We only managed to identify one such study, i.e., Hui and Everett (2003), who conducted a survey to examine consumers' satisfaction of and willingness to participate in online product sampling in New Zealand. Although this study offers some initial insights into consumers' perceptions of and attitudes toward the administration of physical product sampling online, whether and in what ways such a strategy is effective yet to be elucidated.

Below we discuss our research hypotheses on the effects of administering physical product sampling online. We aim to provide a deeper understanding of its effects by considering two aspects: (1) immediate versus delayed effects – previous research notes that product sampling could induce immediate purchase as well as purchase after some time (Bawa and Shoemaker 2004; Gedenk and Neslin 2000; Heiman et al. 2001); thus, it is important to consider both to comprehensively capture the possible effects of administering physical product sampling online; (2) popular brands versus non-popular brands $^{1}$ –although the direct product trial experience offered by product sampling should be particularly

favorable for non-popular brands by reducing consumer uncertainty in these brands (Jain et al. 1995; Marks and Kamins 1988), we argue that in the online context, popular brands may also benefit given the signaling effect (Hu et al. 2010) and large exposure afforded that makes a sampling campaign doubling up as an advertising stimulus.

## 3 3. Research Hypotheses

Overall, we expect the administration of physical product sampling online to have both an immediate and a lagged sales effects. In addition, both popular and non-popular brands may enjoy the lagged sales effect from sampling, but only popular brands are expected to enjoy an immediate sales effect.

The guiding theoretical logic for the hypotheses builds on two aspects: 1) how product sampling may help increase product sales, and 2) the nature of the product brands in terms of whether they are well-known by the consumers (popular vs. non-popular brands). On the basis of the literature review, two key mechanisms identified by determining which product sampling may cause increased sales are reducing product uncertainty and signaling product quality. On the basis of whether a brand is well known (i.e., popular) or not (i.e., non-popular), the product offered by a brand for sampling may enjoy increased sales through one or both these mechanisms (a summary is provided in Table 1 below). We expect the benefit of product sampling in reducing product uncertainty to lead to a delayed sales effect in our context, given that consumers need to wait for the physical product sample to be delivered to them for trial and evaluation (Heiman et al. 2001). In addition, we expect such a delayed sales impact to be applicable to both popular and non-popular brands.

Being a promotional tactic originally designed to provide firsthand trial experience of a product to prospective customers (Bettinger et al. 1979),

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

product sampling is usually deemed to be less beneficial for brands with larger market shares given there are fewer potential customers to convert (Bawa and Shoemaker 2004). However, we expect popular brands to also enjoy significant sales effect as with non-popular brands from online product sampling, in particular due to a WOM effect. Previous research notes that the after-trial WOM generated by satisfied consumers may extend the sales effect to other consumers (Heiman et al. 2001; Jain et al. 1995). Unlike offline sampling, in that its WOM work only within limited social contact boundaries (Bhatnagar and Ghose 2004), online sampling WOM could reach a wide audience over a large geographical spanning. The WOM with wide coverage may then stimulate not only first-time purchase (mainly for non-popular brands and less so for popular brands), but also “remind” existing customers to repurchase the product (for popular brands). Furthermore, some consumers, though familiar with the brand, are unfamiliar with the specific product it offers for sampling. WOM in this case can further reduce uncertainty for them and lead them to make the purchase. Thus, we expect both popular and non-popular brands to enjoy lagged sales effect from administering physical product sampling online.

H1: Both popular brands and non-popular brands will enjoy the delayed sales effect from administering physical product sampling online.

Apart from a lagged sales effect, we expect the administration of physical product sampling online to also have an immediate sales effect. In the online context, the administration of product sampling may enjoy a salient advertising effect given the wide information dissemination afforded that expands its reach. Anyone who visits a website where a product sampling campaign is administered would be able to see the campaign, and become aware of the product concerned. Previous research notes that offer product samples to consumers may help signal the product quality of

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

a brand per the signaling theory (Spence 1973; Wright and Lynch 1995), which may be amplified in the online context. Hu et al. (2010) demonstrate that providing a sampling option is a strong product quality signal that could attract a higher number of interested shoppers, as well as better sales than those without the sampling option. In this sense, administering product sampling online may induce immediate purchases. Yet, such an immediate sales effect may only be salient for popular products and not for non-popular products. For popular brands known by many consumers, the provision of their product for sampling online may act as a particularly efficacious signal to the consumers about the product's quality (Hu et al. 2010). This may induce consumers to make a purchase, even if they have not sampled or tried out the product concerned. Indeed, brand itself serves a salient signal of its product quality (Erdem and Swait 1998). Thus, the combination of the two (popular brands offering samples of its product online) may be particularly effective to make consumers willing to take up the risk and buy the product directly.

However, for non-popular brands, consumers may still perceive a greater risk of buying the product given that they are not familiar with these brands (i.e., a weak signal of product quality). For instance, relating to the employment market, prospective employer may not feel confident to hire a candidate from an unknown university even if he/she attempts to use the certificate conferred by the university as a signal of his/her capability. For consumers to gain confidence to buy a product from an unpopular brand, they are likely to still prefer trying out the product firsthand. Yet, the actual trial of the product only occurs after certain time lapse as it needs to be delivered to the consumers (Heiman et al. 2001). Therefore, the sales effect of administering physical product sampling online for non-popular brands is likely to be delayed rather than immediate. ## 4 4. Research Context and Data

In this study, we collected data of online physical product sampling campaigns from a leading e-commerce store in China to test our research hypotheses. The e-commerce store sells a variety of products, ranging from grocery and household products, clothing, electrical appliances, and toys to cosmetic and healthcare products. A product sampling section was set up in the e-commerce store in October, 2012, which allows brands to offer product samples to the store's customers. An online sampling campaign in the platform typically includes four steps: (1) the platform publishes information about the free sample offer that lasts for one week; (2) consumers are invited to apply for the samples by registering and submitting their information within this week; (3) the platform selects applicants and sends out the samples after the end of sampling period; and (4) the platform encourages the consumers to provide feedback after sampling the product.

In the product sampling section, each product for sampling has its own webpage (see Figure 1 as an example) that contains information including product details, its retail price, number of samples available, and the current number of applicants. On the sampling webpage, consumers are also able to buy the product directly by clicking on a purchase link.

We collected the sampling products' sales data to examine the effect of administering the campaigns. The dataset contains 122 products that ran sampling campaigns during June and July, 2013 on the platform and their sales data. The dataset also included sales of 83 non-sampling products as control group. As shown in Table 2, products in the control group were purposely selected to be similar with the sampling products for

## 6.1 Sample Bias

From Table 7, we find that the daily average sales in the control group was significantly higher than that in the treatment group before the sampling campaign, which indicates the presence of sample bias. To rule out issues due to potential sample bias, we conducted a propensity score matching to control this bias in the control and treatment groups. A matching analysis computes the effect of a treatment by matching each sampling product with a non-sampling product based on observable characteristics (Manchanda et al. 2011). Thus, we focus the difference in daily average sales before the sampling campaign between the treatment and control groups. ## 7 7. Discussion and Conclusion

As an established promotional tactic, product sampling has recently been applied in the online context that possesses distinct characteristics (e.g., wide information dissemination) from the offline context. It is necessary to examine how product sampling works in this new context, before merchants take findings in the offline context for granted in administering their sampling online. Furthermore, although extant studies have examined the sampling of digital products online, our understanding about the effects of doing so online for physical products, again possessing distinct characteristics from digital products (e.g., no duplication issue, need for offline distribution), is lacking.

Our findings show that contrary to the intuition that product sampling should help non-popular brands more in increasing their product sales given its strength in reducing product uncertainty (Bawa and Shoemaker 2004), doing so online could be more beneficial for popular brands (i.e.,

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

those that are known by and attract the eyeballs of many consumers). This is manifested in two aspects: (1) popular brands enjoy both an immediate and a delayed sales effect, whereas non-popular brands only enjoy a delayed sales effect and (2) when delayed sales effect is concerned, the increase in magnitude of sales is greater for popular brands than for non-popular brands. Together, this may suggest that administering physical product sampling online could offer salient advertising effect for popular brands, when their brand quality signals are enhanced by the Internet’s wide information dissemination capability. Below we discuss the implications of our study’s findings to both research and practice.

## 7.1 Research Implications

Our study contributes to research in three ways. First, this study extends the research on product sampling to the online context, in particular with respect to physical products. Bulk of the extant research has focused on the offline context (e.g., Bawa & Shoemaker 2004; Heiman et al. 2001; Lammers 1991), with some studies addressing the online context but focusing on the sampling of digital products (e.g., Hu et al. 2010; Cheng and Liu 2012; Zhou and Duan 2012). Thus, this study enriches our understanding of sampling to different context and to different kind of products.

Second, afforded by our longitudinal data analyzed using the DID method, we could comprehensively and reliably examine the effects of administering physical product sampling online on sales. Specifically, we reveal the dynamics in its sales effects by considering both its immediate and delayed components. Our findings show that administering physical product sampling online may offer both immediate and delayed sales effects. This is in contrast to the literature on offline sampling that focuses on either its immediate or lagged sales effects

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

depending on the mode of administration, i.e., in-store or through mail delivery, and that only lagged effects are salient when consumers do not receive a sample in-store (e.g., Bawa and Shoemaker 2004; Heiman et al. 2001; Lammers 1991).

Third, by delineating between popular and non-popular brands, we unveil the differential effects of administering physical product sampling online. In particular, although a lagged effect for non-popular brands is within expectation given the strength of sampling in reducing uncertainty (Jain et al. 1995; Marks and Kamins 1988), popular brands are found to enjoy both immediate and delayed sales effects through additional signaling effects (Hu et al. 2010). This underlies that administering sampling of physical products online could engender both product uncertainty reduction and signaling of product quality. The findings also imply that a different perspective is warranted for understanding the effects of online sampling when physical products are concerned.

Indeed, one may be tempted to follow the findings from the offline literature on mail delivery of product samples (Bawa and Shoemaker 2004; Gedenk and Neslin 2000) when considering the effects of administering sampling of physical products online, given that both involve a time lapsed in sending a product sample to the consumers. Yet, doing so is likely to preclude the consideration of the presence of immediate sales effects. With the wide information dissemination afforded by the online context, the effects of sampling could gear toward one that is more similar to advertising – exposing to a large audience, the signal that a brand is offering a high-quality product.

However, if we follow the findings from the online literature on sampling of digital products (Hu et al. 2010; Wang and Zhang 2009; Zhou and Duan 2012), we may be tempted to conclude that all physical products can enjoy the signaling benefits of obtaining immediate sales effect. Yet, we need to recognize that physical products still need to be delivered to the consumers, unlike digital products that can be instantly distributed

# ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

and sampled. Indeed, our findings suggest that only when a product sample is offered by a popular brand, immediate sales effect can be obtained even before the sample is sent out. In addition, the magnitude of sales increase is greater for popular brands than for non-popular brands.

## 7.2 Managerial Implications

Our findings provide useful insights to both e-commerce stores and brands contemplating to administer physical product sampling online. To e-commerce stores, our research may provide guidance on how they should charge popular brands and non-popular brands for leveraging their platform to administer sampling. Given the larger benefits enjoyed by popular brands, e-commerce stores may maximize profits by levying higher charges to these brands. To this end, our research also provides both a subjective (through human coders) and an objective way (through news search feature offered by search engines) to perform the categorization to guide the design of differential charging schemes.

To brands contemplating to administer physical product sampling online, our research may help them set the right expectation on when the sales effects may occur, and by what extent, which depends on their standing in the market. In addition, our study proposes a viable method for brands to quantify the dynamic effects of online sampling over time. Given the convenience of collecting data in the online context, brands can use our method to measure the performance of sampling campaign more accurately, and based on this, make better decisions to improve payback from their sampling campaigns.

## 7.3 Limitations

Our paper has several limitations that also provide opportunities for future research. First, our data were obtained from an e-commerce store in China, which could introduce issues related to culture. Future research may replicate our study in other country/cultural contexts to extend the

# ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

generalizability of our findings. Second, the observation period of our data could be extended to reveal long-term, dynamic effects of product sampling that may come from the spread of WOM. Third, we only consider the differentiation between popular and non-popular brands. Future research may consider other product aspects such as search vs. experience product types (Franke et al. 2004) and the functional categories of products, e.g., electronic appliances and cosmetics.

In summary, our research provides timely and valuable insights into this emerging online promotional tactic that is promising, yet knowledge about its administration and exploitation is lacking.
