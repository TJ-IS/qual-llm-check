Abstrac t: Effective knowledge management has been increasingly cited as critical for businesses to compete successfully. Knowledge acquisition/capture, the first step in knowledge management, continues to be a bottleneck and is exacerbated when experts are geographically distributed. Furthermore, knowledge from multiple experts is likely to generate inconsistent knowledge for a given problem domain. There is thus a compelling need to generate consensus by resolving inconsistencies and conflicts that may occur among experts during the process of knowledge acquisition. This process is more challenging when dealing with virtual teams of experts. This study addresses task-based or cognitive conflicts among experts. A key objective of this study is to examine the effectiveness of two cognitive techniques—the repertory grid (or RepGrid) and Delphi—in generating consensus among experts during the knowledge capture process. A field experiment with geographically distributed real-world network experts involving multiple rounds of interaction over an extended period of time was conducted. Findings from this research indicate that, in the short run, Delphi works better than the RepGrid in reducing conflict and generating consensus. However, the RepGrid technique appears to perform better in the long run. We find similar results for satisfaction with the process and outcome. Our findings also indicate that experts using the RepGrid technique elicited more knowledge as well as higher-quality knowledge than experts using the Delphi technique. To sum up, our study indicates that RepGrid is superior to Delphi, and therefore managers should seriously consider the use of RepGrid in capturing knowledge from multiple and distributed experts when dealing with complex real-world issues.

Key word s a nd phra ses: cognitive conflict, conflict resolution, consensus generation, Delphi technique, knowledge capture, knowledge management, repertory grid technique, virtual teams.

Firms a re inc rea singly using virtua l tea ms to accomplish complex tasks, staffing them with the best people for the job irrespective of location. Virtual teams engaged in nonroutine and routine tasks may unknowingly utilize different processes for capturing, storing, and disseminating knowledge. Experts in virtual teams may have different cultural backgrounds and experiences and employ different mental models. They would likely filter information through their personal mental models and employ different patterns and heuristics to address the problem at hand, giving rise to misunderstandings or distortions [69]. This, coupled with a lack of face-to-face contact, can result in poor coordination and reduced cohesion among members [82]. This leads to communication obstacles and conflicts during the process of knowledge capture [18, 123], eventually affecting the performance of teams and resulting in greater conflict than in collocated teams [57, 67]. Poorly managed or unmanaged conflict in virtual teams decreases the satisfaction of members, reduces their commitment to the organization, and results in ineffective teamwork [32, 57]. Conflict in virtual teams may require timely intervention of technology adaptation from team leaders so as to ensure that teams remain effective [122]. Understanding issues relating to conflict can help managers better manage virtual teams and increase their productivity.

There are many different types of conflict, including interpersonal, task-based, and process conflict [65, 99]. This study specifically deals with cognitive or task-based conflicts that arise due to differences of opinion about a task. We choose to focus on cognitive conflict because virtual teams seem to report more cognitive conflict than collocated teams [57]. Another key reason for studying cognitive conflict is the ambiguity regarding its impact. Studies indicate that group members experience a decrease in satisfaction and likability of other members with an increase in the perceived levels of task conflict [64, 78]. Other studies suggest that task-based conflict can encourage more effective group functioning [3, 66] by promoting open discussion and consideration of a wider set of alternatives [39]. Lack of face-to-face interaction among experts in virtual teams may result in a greater focus on the task and cause more task-based conflicts. While previous research [126] has examined task conflict in virtual teams involving a single expert, we extend this stream and examine task conflict using teams of multiple experts during the knowledge capture process.

One approach to generate consensus and resolve cognitive conflicts among experts in virtual teams during knowledge capture is to create a shared understanding through the use of mental models. Shared understanding helps to structure and minimize cognitive conflict, making it easier to attain consensus [117] and positively affect group performance [88]. This approach is appropriate for our study because shared understanding from mental models allows members to predict information and resource requirements when communication is limited [79]. It can lead to a better understanding of the views of other experts [41] and also help members coordinate their collaboration and adapt appropriately [20, 41, 118]. We try to create a shared understanding in this study using two cognition-based techniques—repertory grid (RepGrid) and Delphi.

A key objective of this study is to examine whether the shared understanding created using cognition techniques such as RepGrid and Delphi can help generate consensus by resolving cognitive conflicts that may arise among experts in virtual teams during the knowledge capture process (illustrated in Figure 1). This will have implications for both research and practice. Although RepGrid has been used by information systems (IS) researchers in the past [62], its use in a virtual team context and comparison with Delphi (a competing approach) in the context of knowledge capture is novel. The use of field experiments with real-world network experts also contributes to its uniqueness. While prior research has examined Delphi and RepGrid separately in the context of information requirements elicitation [52] and knowledge elicitation [74], a major contribution of the current study is the first application of both techniques to create shared understandings in the context of virtual teams. This assumes greater importance when virtual teams are geographically and temporally dispersed. Success of either or both techniques will enable members of virtual teams to generate a shared understanding using mental models that are necessary to successfully adapt to their dynamic environments [20]. This research serves as a “proof of concept” study that establishes the viability of using cognitive methodologies to tap into mental models of geographically and temporally distributed experts. Finally, this study also examines an important technical domain of information technology (IT) (telecommunications), deals with capturing design aspects for networks (an important aspect of IS design), and includes distributed knowledge capture techniques, all of which attest to its significance.

## Theoretical Background

## Virtual Teams and Conflict Management

For the purposes of this stud y, we refer to virtual teams as an ad hoc collection of individuals who are geographically, organizationally, or temporally dispersed, but work in concert to accomplish organizational tasks using information and communication technologies (ICT) [67, 96]. Prior research on virtual teams has examined various issues that have been broadly classified under three categories: input, processes (socioemotional and task), and output (see [97] for a detailed discussion). Issues that have been studied include knowledge sharing [50], trust [63, 92, 96], leadership effectiveness [69, 126], decision-making effectiveness [111], and conflict management [26, 57]. Resolving conflicts is important because of its direct implication for the performance of teams. Specifically, examining conflict resolution in virtual teams is important [97] because of the different characteristics of collocated and virtual teams (see Table 1). There have been calls that researchers should pay greater attention to examining the issue of conflict management during knowledge capture from experts in virtual teams [100] since knowledge management systems are generally developed for relatively unstructured domains, where consensus on standard practices has not yet emerged.

Conflict in groups primarily manifests in three forms: interpersonal, process related, and issue based [34, 65]. Interpersonal or affective or relationship conflict [34, 40, 65] refers to personal disagreements over issues not related to task and is characterized by emotions such as anger and frustration. This type of conflict is undesirable because it tends to draw attention away from the task and affects group functioning [34], resulting in the use of suboptimal strategies for conflict resolution [84]. Process conflict [65] refers to disagreement over a team’s methods and processes. Last, issue-based conflict [34], also known as substantive conflict or task conflict [40, 65] or cognitive conflict [3, 108], refers to the disagreements or conflicts among group members pertaining to the task being performed [65]. Cognitive conflict may be desirable because it brings out the differing views and issues relating to the task and helps the group develop better solutions [34], achieve higher postmeeting consensus [108], and preempt group-meeting hazards such as groupthink [84]. In spite of these advantages, studies recommend that cognitive conflict should be minimized [55], using the rationale that a lower level of cognitive conflict leads to better understanding and makes it easier to attain consensus [113]. For the purposes of this study, we define cognitive conflict in terms of disagreement among experts and measure it accordingly. Studies have noted that none of the above conceptualizations can be considered better than others [6]. Cognitive conflict among experts can occur because of different reasons. However, the “virtual team” context of our study leads us to posit that a primary reason is the different mental models of experts.

## Mental Models

Mental models are defined as cognitive representations of events and situations that include the characters in those situations and their interrelations [48]. Mental models that are shared with others in a team lead to the formation of a shared understanding. Shared mental models [71] recognize the existence of knowledge structures relating to a group’s task [118]. Therefore, “cognitive fit” between the mental model and the task being addressed becomes very important [114]. Shared understanding from mental models may be used to reason and solve problems [49], guide incoming information [54], and assist in or direct recall [132]. Several types of mental models are used by groups [72, 94, 118]. For the purposes of this study, we focus on the mental models related to the task at hand because shared understanding from the mental models of the task will lead to common interpretations regarding the processes and goals of a team, thereby reducing cognitive conflicts.

Since conflict among experts with different mental models primarily emerges because of cognition differences, it is reasonable to expect that cognition-based techniques could be effective in resolving such conflict. The RepGrid is one such technique, in which different mental models can be elicited [121]. Although researchers have used the RepGrid for varied purposes, such as to elicit knowledge [95] and to conduct cross-cultural research [62], there have been no studies examining its use in resolving conflicts among experts in virtual teams. Another cognition-based technique that we examine in this study is Delphi. IS research has made use of the Delphi technique in the past to resolve cognitive conflicts and form a consensus on identification of IS issues that are of importance to academics and practitioners [12, 90, 109]. Although there are different methods to capture knowledge, such as strategic assumption surfacing and testing [85] and the symmetrical linkage system [56], we do not employ them because they lack a suitable framework for structuring critical decision-related knowledge [98].

## Repertory Grid Technique

Based on the Kelly’s personal construct psychology (PCP) theory [70], RepGrid is a “cognitive mapping technique that attempts to describe how people think about the phenomena in their world” [121, p. 40]. RepGrid uses concepts such as elements, constructs, and links. Elements refer to the objects under investigation; constructs are qualities describing the elements that a participant would arrive at by comparing two or more elements and contrasting with a third element (“construing”); and links are ways in which elements and constructs are related [121]. These concepts are used to create a matrix, which is then used to collect data and form mental models of each participant (see the Appendix for a discussion of the methodology).

RepGrid has been employed in knowledge acquisition and to construct expert systems in medicine [44], bid evaluation [95], engineering assembly planning [104], and spatial analysis [28]. It is a core technique in many approaches to knowledge acquisition [7, 107] and has been incorporated into many knowledge acquisition tools, including ETS, AQUINAS, and DART [10]. In the IS domain, RepGrid has been employed during requirements elicitation [51, 61, 91, 121], stakeholder analysis [91], and business–IS alignment [120]. In addition to qualitative data to generate the constructs, we also utilize quantitative data in this study to help reduce conflict and achieve consensus.

The RepGrid technique has specific advantages in the context of the problem addressed in this study. First, it utilizes qualitative and quantitative data. This allows experts to identify relevant constructs as well as evaluate them when resolving conflict. Once assimilated, it is a structured process that is easy to apply and enables experts to think deeper about the problem. The biggest advantage of using the RepGrid technique is that a relatively small sample size of 15 to 25 experts is sufficient to generate constructs that capture the meaning relating to a given domain [121]. It does possess some disadvantages of note. The process is often novel and challenging for participants. The use of an indirect thinking approach can be frustrating to some. The compare and contrast methodology may not elicit all relevant constructs, particularly if the cases do not involve significant differentiation. Nonetheless, the generation of both qualitative and quantitative data permits the use of a number of analysis techniques to facilitate resolution of disagreements and conflicts during knowledge capture. The cognitive maps produced can reveal the differences in constructs between experts and can provide the platform upon which the overall group can collectively diagnose disagreements. These advantages make the RepGrid an appropriate technique to create consensus in our study.

## Delphi Technique

The Delphi technique was developed at the RAND Corporation in the 1950s to generate consensus forecasts based on expert opinion [42]. It is a group process that uses written media to solicit and aggregate the judgments of several experts using questionnaires. The approach is iterative, allowing participants to review the opinions and reasoning of others, and subsequently revise their initial thoughts based on peer feedback to enable convergence toward a common understanding (see the Appendix for a discussion of the methodology). Delphi participants remain anonymous to ensure that dominant personalities or the professional stature of individuals do not disproportionately influence the outcome. There is no consensus in the literature on the appropriate group size for the Delphi technique, and studies have used between 15 and 20 respondents [59, 115]. Because all the respondents in our study were network professionals from the IT industry and had a common knowledge base, we considered the group relatively homogeneous and decided to use the standard size of 20 respondents.

IS applications of the Delphi technique include eliciting and ranking key IS management issues [12, 90, 109], specifying the characteristics of IS resources most relevant to IT infrastructure flexibility [37], identifying the dimensions of IS performance [110], performing postmortem evaluation of IT projects [68], exploring the role and use of enterprise systems [30], identifying software project risks in an international context [112], and determining the scope and requirements of knowledge management systems [89]. The Delphi technique does not require that members meet one another face to face and has been used to structure group communication in many group support systems [124]. Our objective is to use these above-mentioned techniques (Delphi and RepGrid) to resolve conflicts during the process of knowledge capture and arrive at a consensus.

## Knowledge Management and Knowledge Acquisition

Dra wing on the work of Liebeskind [73], we define knowledge as information with validity. Knowledge management (KM) refers to the tools, technologies, practices, and incentives deployed by an organization to “know what it knows” and make this available to people who need to know it when they need to know it [77]. In general, KM is largely regarded as a process consisting of several steps, such as knowledge creation, storage and retrieval, transfer, and application [2]. While managing each aspect is important for the overall success of a KM program, knowledge storage stands out because it involves creation and acquisition/capture of knowledge [46].

Knowledge acquisition is an extremely important aspect of KM but remains a bottleneck in the development of knowledge management/expert systems [9, 14, 46]. Knowledge acquisition is “the process of extracting, structuring and organizing knowledge from several knowledge sources, usually human experts, so that the problem solving expertise can be captured and transformed into a computer readable form” [75, p. 2-1]. Knowledge acquisition is not only a critical part of the KM process but also an integral part of organizational learning [60].

Studies generally indicate that the success of knowledge acquisition depends on contextual factors, including human factors [35, 58]. Most studies using human factors in knowledge acquisition focus on the “explicit” traits of human experts such as the level of expertise and problem-solving style rather than on “implicit” factors such as mental models. Explicit factors are easier to capture and study. However, implicit factors such as mental models are harder to pin down and have received little attention [58]. Our study seeks to address this by examining the resolution of conflicts that may be caused by the different mental models of experts.

## Study Hypotheses

Studies have indicated that conflict is generally higher in diverse teams than in homogeneous teams, more so where members are geographically dispersed [21, 130]. However, such task-based conflict decreased with increased shared team identity in geographically dispersed teams [87]. This is plausible based on the tenet of social identity theory [119] that a person belonging to a group develops a shared understanding with other members of the group and later tries to categorize groups as in-groups and out-groups in order to compare his or her group with other groups and make it look favorable. Developing a shared understanding would not only involve personal identification with a group but would also entail a reduction in task conflicts. Drawing on this theory and past research, we posit that task-based conflict in virtual teams should similarly decrease when members experience an increase in shared team identity or group cohesion that reflects an individual member’s sense of belonging to and confidence in the group [21, 103], and we test for the differential effects of the two methodologies in this study. It has been suggested that RepGrid’s methodology enables easier and faster collection of the underlying constructs of members than techniques such as Delphi [98]. Therefore, members using the RepGrid can examine the mental models of other members more easily. Once a member learns the positions of fellow members, it will help examine biases in his or her thinking and develop accurate explanations and expectations about the task [41]. In addition, such feedback facilitates learning and leads to more synergy and cooperation among team members [103]. Therefore, we expect that there would be a better understanding of the other group members’ views, leading to an increase in shared understanding and more group cohesion when using RepGrid rather than the Delphi technique. We expect that the degree of consensus will vary with the technique used to resolve conflicts and hypothesize that an increased shared understanding will reduce task-based conflict to a larger extent in virtual teams using the RepGrid technique than in teams using the Delphi technique. Given that our study spans multiple rounds rather than a single investigation, we focus on change in consensus and propose that:

## Hypothesis 1: Change in consensus will be higher in the groups using the RepGrid technique than in groups using the Delphi technique.

It is also important to evaluate the effectiveness of the RepGrid and Delphi techniques in terms of knowledge capture. For the purposes of this study, we operationalized ef fectiveness in terms of the completeness of knowledge elicited. A superior elicitation technique will help virtual teams capture knowledge that is complete and usable. Stud ies in IS have reported ambiguous results on the effectiveness of different knowledge capture techniques, thereby creating a need for further examination. Using schema theory [4] as a basis, we propose that the construing nature (“compare and contrast”) of the RepGrid forces participants to engage in a deeper cognitive processing than Delphi participants. According to the theory, schemata are conceptual knowledge structures that are used to organize and retrieve information. The methodology of RepGrid requires that participants figure out similarities and differences between dif ferent objects, which forces them to refer to more schemata than Delphi participants, thereby generating more knowledge. The nature of RepGrid also makes it possible to share mental models of experts with each other more easily; we posit that the mental models so collected should lead to greater effectiveness of the RepGrid because they outline the knowledge structures pertaining to a given task. As argued in cognitive fit theory, once knowledge structures relating to a mental model are captured, they could lead to a better “cognitive fit” with the knowledge structures of a given task and increase overall effectiveness of outcomes [114]. In the context of this study, it may translate to better-quality knowledge as well. In addition, there is ambiguity about the effectiveness of the different knowledge capture techniques. Currently, there is a paucity of empirical studies in evaluating knowledge capture techniques, and the literature is largely dominated by anecdotal reports on the usefulness of various evaluation techniques [86]. The few studies that examined the effectiveness and efficiency of knowledge capture techniques such as protocol analysis [17], structured and un structured interviews [1], and cognitive interviews [86] have reported mixed results. Burton et al. [17] evaluated four techniques—protocol analysis, interviews, laddered grids, and card sorting—in terms of efficiency and effectiveness and reported that protocol analysis took more time and generated less information than an interview. Agarwal and Tanniru [1] compared structured and unstructured interviews and found no clear evidence that a structured interview improved recall of information. Findings from Moody et al. [86] seem to confirm that cognitive interviews are more effective and efficient than structured interviews. The mixed results underscore a need for more research. Therefore, we propose that:

Hypothesis 2: The knowledge captured will be greater in groups using the RepGrid technique than groups using the Delphi technique.

The management of task disagreement and conflict affects satisfaction [31]. This study found evidence of improved group satisfaction when task conflicts were managed “agreeably,” that is, accommodating and problem solving, which is consistent with other studies that suggest that task conflict when managed agreeably increases trust and vice versa [125]. Both the RepGrid and Delphi techniques allow members of a team to outline their concerns and issues. These techniques give each member of a virtual team the option of integrating and discussing other members’ opinions and issues so that problems get solved effectively. These two techniques could result in increasing experts’ satisfaction in terms of outcomes and process [102]. However, the difference in the complexity of methodologies leads us to posit a difference in satisfaction levels between the participants. The Delphi technique’s methodology is relatively less complex than RepGrid’s as it does not involve “construing.” Furthermore, the representation of results as lists in Delphi might result in easier comprehension than the cognitively taxing maps produced by RepGrid. Consequently, we expect that the effort involved in learning Delphi might be lower than that of RepGrid, and should therefore result in a higher positive net goal attainment. This is likely to result in experts feeling more satisfied with the process. According to the goal-setting theory [76], a higher positive net goal attainment should also result in Delphi experts being more satisfied with the outcome, that is, the elicited knowledge, because of the lower effort spent in reaching goals. Therefore, we posit the following:

Hypothesis 3a: Process satisfaction will be higher in groups using the Delphi technique than in groups using the RepGrid technique.

Hypothesis 3b: Satisfaction with elicited knowledge will be higher in groups using the Delphi technique than in groups using the RepGrid technique.

## Research Methodology

Our resea rc h exa mines c onf lic t in terms of outc omes and also in terms of process by analyzing it through multiple rounds. Unlike most prior research in IS employing student subjects in a laboratory setting, we conducted a field experiment using realworld experts. Field experiments allow the examination of a phenomenon in settings that are closer to the real world and increase the external validity of the research findings. After random assignment to teams, the subjects were asked to provide knowledge to solve a specific problem, and strive to arrive at a consensus by using the RepGrid or Delphi techniques.

## Variables

The independent variable in our study was a set of three cases/scenarios that required the use of the two techniques, Delphi and RepGrid, to resolve inherent conflicts. The three cases were modeled on the cognitive conflict tasks of McGrath’s task circumplex [81] and dealt with different issues associated with network design. The cases were chosen after extensive validation by a panel of four experienced network professionals to ensure that each case entailed use of considerable network knowledge and that multiple solutions were available. The task involved identification of factors that were relevant to network design, assessment of their relative importance, and application to the three specific cases. The cases covered different configurations of organization structure and location, different needs for network performance, and different network technologies. Case 1 described the issues that arose from a campus LAN (local area network). Case 2 centered on issues from using WAN (wide area network) and ATM (asynchronous transfer mode) technology. Case 3 presented issues relating to the migration of LAN.<sup>2</sup>

The dependent variables were group consensus, effectiveness of knowledge captured, and satisfaction. We define consensus as the degree of agreement generated among team members [13, 83] in the identification and rating of knowledge constructs. As indicated earlier, the conflicts in our study are conflicts of ideas and opinions that legitimately emerge among experts with different knowledge bases and deep-seated opinions about addressing a task/problem situation that genuinely can be addressed by more than one approach. These different views and opinions, sparked by the nature of the task and cognitive differences, generate a rich canopy of potential solutions, but without careful structuring and coordination they can lead to a smorgasbord of ideas, individual member’s beliefs of superiority of their own solutions, and potential disagreement.

Several different measures of consensus or agreement are available. However, they all proved to be problematic for the situation at hand. The preferred option would be to use variance as a measure of consensus. For the small population case (the group size was five in this study), this proves susceptible to large variations for individual outliers. Moreover, the knowledge capture process sought agreement on multiple constructs, which would generate a variance vector, making comparison across groups difficult. One possible solution would be to convert this to a distance from absolute consensus, but the theoretical basis for doing so is suspect, and the statistical properties of the resultant scalar item are not fully known. A measure of disagreement for groups using nominal choices was proposed by Whitworth and Felton [129]. A strict adaptation of this measure to the situation at hand would entail treating unequal ratings as disagreement. As a result, disagreement scores would be artificially inflated, since minor disagreements are weighted identically to major disagreements. Use of this approach would generate high disagreement scores spread over a narrow range. Changes in consensus from one round to the next would be harder to assess effectively. Another approach would be to compute a distance measure (city block, Euclidean, Mahalanobis, etc.) as an estimate of disagreement between experts. In many respects, this measure has meaningful statistical properties. However, it is dependent on the number of constructs over which consensus is sought, with larger numbers of constructs generating higher values for distance. Because we did not constrain the number of constructs that a group generated, distance becomes a problematic measure of consensus. Another measure of disagreement between knowledge elicited from multiple experts is presented by Rush and Wallace [106]. The measure is based on knowledge represented in influence diagrams, a representation technique that was not employed in this study and was thus discarded.

Accordingly, we developed a measure of consensus that would not be biased by a single outlier, would recognize the magnitude of difference between ratings, and would apply for groups of any size, using any number of constructs, and a rating scale of any size. Assuming we have n members in a group, with m constructs being evaluated, and ratings ranging from min R to max R theoretically, our measure of disagreement is given by

$$
D = \frac {1}{n (n - 1)} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{m} \left(\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {m} \frac {\left| R _ {i k} - R _ {j k} \right|}{\max R - \min R}\right),
$$

where $R _ { i k }$ represents the rating of expert i on construct k. The measure utilizes a pair-wise comparison, so as to reduce the effect of outliers, and is normalized for the range of maximum disagreement on any construct, as well as the number of constructs considered. We tested the measure out for a variety of conditions, and it was not susceptible to problems for individual outliers or biased based on the number of constructs employed and the rating scale used.<sup>3</sup>

The second dependent variable, effectiveness, was measured in terms of the extent of knowledge captured. Because no upper bound was imposed on the possible number of constructs to be considered for the problem, we utilized the actual number generated. There is a lack of consensus on what constitutes satisfaction, the third dependent variable [116], with some suggesting that it is with the process and others with the outcome [131]. We measured it in terms of satisfaction with the process as well as with the outcome.

We elected to use group size as a control variable. In line with other studies, we used a group size of five, which is considered an appropriate size as noted in the metaanalysis of the literature on groups [43]. Using an odd size also precludes deadlock or stalemate when seeking consensus. We also collected some qualitative data using interviews and transcripts with the objective of gaining a deeper understanding of the consensus process.

## Sample

A significant challenge in this study was acquiring a reasonable sample of real-world experts in telecommunications and networks to participate in the study. We engaged in the following initiatives:

•	 47 global IT firms (located in the United States, Europe, India, Australia, and the Middle East), 105 IT firms at the Dubai Internet City, and 20 other firms in the Middle East were contacted via phone and e-mail;

•	 messages requesting participation of experts were posted on networking-specific newsgroups such as the North American Network Operators’ Group (NANOG) and IS World; and

•	 around 45 IT firms were contacted at the Gulf Information Technology Exhibition (GITEX), one of the biggest IT exhibitions in the Middle East.

A total of 45 experts finally agreed to participate and registered for the study. Participation of experts was voluntary. In many instances, experts for our study were vetted, based on our requirements, by the human resources managers of the companies we approached. We then reconfirmed the details with the experts to ensure validity. In some other instances, information about the participants was gathered from the Internet and company Web sites to ensure that they had relevant knowledge about networking. These experts were informed that they would get an opportunity to learn two new techniques to create consensus when working in team-based situations as well as receive a copy of the findings of the study. This proved adequate motivation to the experts who agreed to participate in the study and did so at different time periods. In order to ensure a balanced design, we did not constitute teams until we had secured the cooperation of 10 experts. These experts were randomly formed into 2 teams of 5, and then assigned to either the RepGrid or the Delphi technique. The total of 45 includes the 5 experts we used for the pilot study that we performed prior to the main study. The team size proved sufficient for both techniques.

Subjects for both the pilot and the main studies were real-world network experts with a minimum experience of 12 months; over 64 percent had more than 5 years of experience. All the participants were well educated; 90 percent of them had a bachelor’s degree and 40 percent had master’s or doctoral degrees. Examples of job titles of subjects included chief information officer, network manager, and network executive. Nearly 60 percent belonged to medium- to large-scale enterprises with employee sizes larger than 2,000. All of the teams included experts who were located in different places, indicating both temporal and geographic dispersion. Team members in the main study were located in the United States, India, and the Middle East, ensuring some degree of heterogeneity in terms of organizational culture as well. All of the subjects were initially sent an introductory e-mail about the experiment along with a background statement. They filled out a consent form and a background questionnaire that were common to both techniques. The experts were trained on the relevant technique using self-paced Web-based tutorials before the study began. They interacted with the experimenter (one of the researchers who played the role of a knowledge engineer) using e-mail and a communication tool on the study’s Web site. The experts sent the data (constructs and ratings) to the experimenter, who then cleaned, analyzed, and consolidated these into meaningful information and posted the results on the study’s Web site. The experimenter did not filter out any information. The experiment spanned five rounds, with the following major tasks in each round.

## Round 1

The experts were asked to read in detail the three case scenarios dealing with network design issues and elicit constructs that would be used to solve the networking problem. In the RepGrid treatment, each subject was asked to elicit constructs using a triadic approach. Members of the Delphi teams were asked to elicit constructs for the problem in the form of statements. The constructs generated in this round were collected at the study’s Web site. The overall set of constructs was common across all three cases.

## Round 2

The (Delphi) statements or (RepGrid) constructs/factors elicited in round 1 by all the subjects in each group were collated and screened by two independent referees who were considered experts in the networking domain to remove duplicates, overlaps, synonyms, and other subsumed entries. In case of subsumed constructs, only the more general construct was retained. Care was taken to ensure that homonyms were effectively addressed. Following this scrubbing, the collated set of unique statements/ factors was presented to the participants of each technique, who rated them in terms of importance to the network design problem. No omissions were permitted, as this would skew the conflict measure. Ratings were collected on a seven-point scale (1 = “not important,” 7 = “very important”). For the RepGrid teams, each factor was characterized by bipolar extremes so as to reduce ambiguity and facilitate data collection. The assessments were analyzed through the RepGrid technique, thereby permitting clustering on two separate dimensions—factor and expert. This allowed visualization of the relationship between factors as well as among experts. For the Delphi technique, the average rating for each construct was used as a measure of overall agreement among the team. The data collected in this round served as the basis for assessing initial consensus among the experts.

## Round 3

The feedback on individual ratings and group consensus was presented to all of the experts on the study’s Web site. The RepGrid experts were able to view the RepGrid analysis (see Figure 2), permitting them to get a better interpretation of potential disagreement and conflict between experts while also explicating the relationship between factors. Similarly, the Delphi experts got a better understanding of where their team stood because they viewed not only their scores but also the average scores for each statement. The experts were given the opportunity to revise their assessments of importance of the factors in this round.

## Rounds 4 and 5

Rounds 4 and 5 narrowed the analysis to each of the three cases outlined earlier. In round 4, the experts were asked to rate the relative importance of the elicited factors to the specific context of each case dealing with its own unique network design problems.

## Reliability and Validity of Measures

Of the three dependent variables used in this study, satisfaction was the only construct measured with multiple indicator item scales. Although we used prevalidated scales to measure satisfaction [93], we chose to retest these using data from our study. A value of 0.7 and above of Cronbach’s alpha as a measure of reliability is deemed acceptable [105]. As shown in Table 3, Cronbach’s alpha was above this threshold for both satisfaction constructs, reconfirming the high reliability of the scales.

Validity of a construct can be established using multiple approaches, including content, construct, and criterion validity. Content validity was established through a fairly thorough sampling of extant literature and confirmation by a panel of experts before and during the pilot study. We measured construct validity using convergent validity and discriminant validity. Convergent validity that assesses the correlation between measures of the same concept [53] was established using factor analysis. In order to improve the interpretation of the results, we used an orthogonal (varimax) rotation. As seen in Table 3, factor loadings confirmed the satisfaction of the two constructs to be unidimensional. We then tested our scales for discriminant validity using a joint factor analysis. Discriminant validity tests whether a given scale is sufficiently different from other related scales [53]. Our joint factor analysis results confirmed discriminant validity of the satisfaction constructs through the extraction of a two-factor structure matching with the predefined indicator items loading onto their associated scales. Finally, we assessed criterion validity by correlating these two measures with their respective criterion variables. The results indicate a high correlation between the multi-item measures and their criterion indicators, reconfirming validity. These results provide evidence of satisfactory psychometric properties (reliability and validity) of the measures for satisfaction used in this study.

## Pilot Study

Because RepGrid is not a commonly used technique, we opted to conduct a pilot study using five real-world experts. The objective of the pilot study was to determine the feasibility of RepGrid as a technique for capturing consensual knowledge from multiple experts in a virtual context. Following the procedure outlined in detail earlier (for the five rounds), five network experts working as a team volunteered their time and used the RepGrid technique to resolve their disagreements and come to a consensus. The subjects used e-mails and online communication tools on the study’s Web site to interact with the experimenter, who served as a knowledge engineer and coordinator. The pilot study experts generated a total of 30 factors, after elimination of duplicates and subsumed factors. These 30 factors were used to assess their relevance to the network design problem in general, and were also specialized to the three individual cases, following the same sequence as outlined for the main study. Disagreement measures were computed for rounds 2 though 5. Figure 3 presents the detailed results of the pilot group study.

When rating the importance of factors in addressing the problem, disagreement decreased by 14.8 percent from rounds 2 to 3. The results were similar when the analysis was shifted to individual cases. Disagreement decreased by 2.9 percent for case 1, increased by 3.6 percent for case 2, and decreased by 14.6 percent for case 3. Results from the pilot study indicated that RepGrid was a viable method to capture knowledge, as well as to create consensus among experts in a virtual team. Therefore, we proceeded with the main study following the procedures outlined earlier.

This was done so that we could examine the relevancy of knowledge captured and make inferences on the quality of knowledge captured (more overlap = better quality). In addition, we used a qualitative approach and measured the perceived quality of factors elicited by a team using a two-phased approach. In phase 1, we used the consensus results to identify the frequency of occurrence of factors, that is, to see how many teams elicited a given factor. The rationale was that if a factor was indeed important, it would be elicited by all of the teams. As a corollary, if a factor was elicited by more teams than other factors, then that factor may indeed be important. Phase 2 of the approach was to use the results (number of occurrences) from phase 1 and capture perceived quality of knowledge in terms of completeness or comprehensiveness of knowledge. Comprehensiveness of knowledge was captured as a weighted total of the elicited factors, with the weights indicating the relative importance of factors. Factors with more occurrences (i.e., elicited by more teams) were given a higher weight to reflect their relative importance. For this study, factors elicited by 6 to 8 teams were given a weight of 3, followed by a weight of 2 for factors elicited by 3 to 5 teams. Last, factors elicited by 1 to 2 teams were given a weight of 1. We calculated a weighted score for each team considering the corresponding weights of the factors elicited by a given team. Our results indicate that the RepGrid teams had a much higher average weighted score (97.75) than the Delphi teams (76.75), indicating that the RepGrid teams captured more important factors than the Delphi teams. Thus, RepGrid helped to capture knowledge of better quality than Delphi, thereby indicating its effectiveness as a technique, supporting H2.

## Satisfaction with Captured Knowledge and with Process

Our final hypothesis (H3) posited that satisfaction with the process and the elicited knowledge would be higher among Delphi than RepGrid team experts because it is managed more “agreeably.” Satisfaction scores for process and outcome were collected at the end of rounds 2 through 5, and are depicted in Figure 5. The findings indicate that the Delphi teams were initially more satisfied than the RepGrid teams, with the process as well as the knowledge generated. However, their satisfaction levels dipped a little and then remained essentially flat (consistent) over the rest of the study. In contrast, the satisfaction levels for the RepGrid teams were low initially, on both process and knowledge generated, as they may have initially struggled with the technique. As they mastered the technique, satisfaction scores climbed considerably to the point that they exceeded those for the Delphi teams, especially for “process satisfaction.” Although the final satisfaction scores for knowledge were only marginally higher for the RepGrid vis-à-vis the Delphi teams, it is worth noting that the rate of change was far higher for the RepGrid teams than for the Delphi teams. We offer the following explanations for this. First, experts using the Delphi technique took less time to learn the technique and were more comfortable with the methodology than experts using the RepGrid. This might have resulted in high satisfaction scores for the Delphi teams early on. As the study progressed, the RepGrid experts were able to perform the task more easily, contributing to increased satisfaction. This is analogous to the impact of task–technology fit on outcomes [45] in the sense that the methodology mattered initially, but over time, appropriation becomes important. Appropriation, defined as the process of selection/adoption/integration of technology by groups into everyday activities, is relevant because it could lead to new social structures that may in turn affect outcomes such as satisfaction. Delphi teams made fewer appropriation changes and hence had consistent outcomes and satisfaction levels. On the contrary, the RepGrid teams engaged in greater appropriation changes and had higher outcomes, leading to higher satisfaction levels. Second, the Delphi experts elicited fewer factors than their RepGrid counterparts. This enabled them to rate the importance of the factors without much difficulty, leading to consistently high satisfaction levels. On the other hand, our qualitative analyses indicate that experts using the RepGrid method were initially biased in their ratings in the second round due to the large number of factors. Viewing the cognitive map would have exposed these biases, and provides an impetus for reducing individual biases in subsequent rounds. With greater understanding of the task/issues, the task of fitting the factors to the cases became more manageable for most experts, leading to increased satis faction scores with the process and the outcome.

To summarize, the RepGrid technique appears to be effective in the long run in reducing disagreement among experts in virtual teams during knowledge capture. Upon initial use, it resulted in increased disagreement. In contrast, the Delphi technique, upon initial use, reduced disagreement within its teams. Although the Delphi and RepGrid team experts reduced their disagreement with sustained use, the change in disagreement was much more pronounced for the RepGrid teams. More importantly, the raw scores of disagreement were lower for the RepGrid than for the Delphi teams. Thus, RepGrid appears to be a superior technique to reduce disagreement and create consensus in the long run. RepGrid also enabled capture of a more comprehensive set of knowledge vis-à-vis Delphi. As for satisfaction, the RepGrid teams showed an increasing trend in satisfaction with both the process and the team-generated/-elicited knowledge. In contrast, satisfaction for the Delphi teams remained essentially constant over the course of the study. Contrary to our expectations, the RepGrid technique seems to also be a superior technique to create and instill greater satisfaction with the process and satisfaction with the team-elicited knowledge.

## Discussion

## Change in Consensus Among Experts

The inc rea se in d isa greement sc ores for the RepGrid teams from round 2 to round 3 was counter to that posited. The initial rationale was that the large number of factors contributed to greater disagreement. However, this explanation was quickly discarded as the maximum disagreement score is independent of the number of factors considered. After examining the detailed responses, we were able to ascertain that much of the increase was attributable to changes in ratings by a couple of experts within a team. While some of the revised ratings moved toward consensus, others indicated greater disagreement. The reasons for this emerged after examining the transcripts from the debriefing of the experts. It transpired that the construing approach of the RepGrid technique adopted in round 1 had a spillover effect in round 2. The experts focused on the scenarios to generate constructs and subconsciously used this information when rating the constructs in round 2. In round 3, as the primacy effect of scenarios faded and the experts concentrated more on importance, the true nature of disagreement within the team emerged. Clearly, the construing approach of the RepGrid technique, which proved effective in eliciting factors, proved detrimental in evaluating them. However, the primacy effect should lead to less disagreement in subsequent rounds, where the focus is on individual cases. This was the case; the disagreement in the RepGrid teams dropped 22.32 percent, 22.06 percent, and 25.59 percent across all three scenarios, each of which examined various issues related to campus LAN (case 1), WAN/ATM (case 2), and migration of LAN (case 3), whereas the improvements for the Delphi teams were more modest at 15.05 percent, 17.77 percent, and 17.39 percent for the three scenarios, respectively.<sup>4</sup> Another issue that emerged was the unweighted nature of the disagreement measure. The experts felt that consensus was more important for critical factors and were more tolerant of variance in factors deemed less important. Because no a priori weights could be assigned to factors, and the process involved progressive elicitation of weights, we chose to retain the unweighted scores.

## Effectiveness of Knowledge Capture

Our study showed that the RepGrid teams elicited more and higher-quality knowledge than the Delphi teams. Two possible rival explanations were considered. The first is that RepGrid experts are more knowledgeable than Delphi experts—this appears unlikely because the experts were randomly assigned to each technique. Analyses of the Delphi and RepGrid teams did not reveal any skew toward education, experience, or domain expertise. A second explanation is that the construing methodology of the RepGrid technique is superior to the listing methodology of the Delphi technique. The “compare and contrast” methodology of RepGrid, while cognitively more taxing, fostered a deeper cognitive processing than the Delphi participants. This methodological rigor of RepGrid could have enabled the experts to elicit more factors than the experts using the Delphi method.

## Satisfaction with Captured Knowledge and with Process

The results from our study indicate that both process and outcome satisfaction scores of the RepGrid teams increased over time. We offer the following three plausible explanations. First, experience effects could have played a role in the RepGrid teams expressing greater satisfaction for both aspects of satisfaction. In the initial rounds, the simpler methodology underlying the Delphi technique appears to have resulted in an elicitation of knowledge and ratings without too much effort. A Delphi expert remarked that “learning this technique was easy . . . did not take too long.” In contrast, some RepGrid experts noted that RepGrid was “taking more time than planned for.” This could have initially led the Delphi experts to express higher satisfaction than their counterparts using the cognitively taxing RepGrid. However, over time and with greater use, the RepGrid experts gained more familiarity and experience with the methodology and their own interpretation, resulting in their increased satisfaction levels. As noted earlier, these results are similar to those of appropriation effects [45]: methodology matters initially, but over time, appropriation becomes important. It is likely that the Delphi teams made fewer appropriation changes and hence had consistent outcomes and satisfaction levels. On the contrary, the RepGrid teams could have engaged in greater appropriation changes and had higher outcomes, leading to higher satisfaction levels.

Second, using the theory of cognitive fit, the RepGrid experts are more likely to have experienced cognitive fit problems in the initial rounds. This would have resulted in lower satisfaction levels initially. However, as the RepGrid experts gained mastery over the technique, they were able to better assess the quality of extracted knowledge, leading to increased satisfaction ratings. Third, the experts using Delphi experienced little additional learning, which is manifest by stagnant satisfaction scores.

## Implications and Limitations

## Implications for Research

The c ontributions a nd implica tions of this stud y to IS research are several. The use of RepGrid along with a quantitative analysis of the conflicts that arise during the process is a unique contribution. Results from our study indicate that increased task conflict created in the early rounds of RepGrid actually leads to better outcomes once the group works through them. This has implications not only for the design of group support systems but also for the ways teams handle intellective task meetings. Also, although previous studies have attempted to describe and measure disagreement and therefore cognitive conflict among experts, they have not been able to evolve a measure for disagreement for rating of multiple constructs. Furthermore, this study examines an important technical domain of IT (telecommunications), dealing with the capture of design knowledge for networks, and employs distributed knowledge capture techniques—all of which attest to the significance of this study. To our knowledge, this study is the first in IS literature to examine the RepGrid and Delphi techniques in the context of virtual teams and knowledge capture. By using RepGrid in conjunction with Delphi, this study answers the call raised by Tan and Hunter, who suggested that researchers should use RepGrid “as a means of validating other techniques or as a ‘preliminary’ phase to further interpretive or positivist investigations” [121, p. 53]. The current research adds to the scant knowledge about effective techniques for reducing cognitive conflict in the area of knowledge capture. In addition, this study examines temporal trends through a multiround application of the methodologies, addressing issues raised by Cotteleer and Bendoly [25].

A key contribution of this study is the demonstration of the effectiveness of the RepGrid technique for knowledge capture and for creating a shared understanding in conditions involving distributed expertise. Our study also highlights the temporal aspects of this effect. RepGrid proved useful in terms of the amount and quality of knowledge elicited, the degree of consensus generated among experts, as well as the overall satisfaction the process engendered. The technique proved challenging at the outset, but once mastered, produced effective results. As indicated earlier, appropriation effects would have played a role. Moreover, the construing approach forced deeper introspection and resulted in the generation of a larger and richer set of knowledge. The use of multiple clustering dimensions allowed the experts to visualize the collective importance of individual factors while assessing their position vis-à-vis the rest of the team. This led to greater reduction of disagreement, and improved consensus within the team. It also led to increased satisfaction with progressive use of the technique. The technique is not without drawbacks. The experts found the process cognitively taxing, leading to longer durations for each round. In addition, there was some spillover effect from the construing approach that increased disagreement in the early rounds. However, over time, the experts were able to master the technique and achieve greater consensus. The clear advantage of using the technique was in the area of knowledge capture. In addition to generating a considerably larger and more unique set of factors than the Delphi teams, the RepGrid teams also generated a larger common knowledge base. This would be a concern in most knowledge capture tasks. It is, however, important to note that these factors form only the first step in the knowledge needed to address the task at hand. Utilizing the elicited knowledge for addressing the network design problem either in the form of design algorithms or heuristics is beyond the scope of the technique. Organizations seeking to use RepGrid should ensure that the experts are properly trained in using the methodology, as well as understand its limitations.

Finally, this study is also one of the few in the IS domain to examine real-world experts in a virtual team setting and to develop shared understanding, as recommended by Beranek et al. [8]. The use of real-world experts also makes it different from other studies that typically use student subjects, limiting generalizability of their findings. In addition, the use of student subjects will likely entail less cognitive conflict as they draw from similar knowledge bases.

## Implications for Practice

Knowledge capture from multiple experts is often acknowledged as a problem that frequently generates conflict among the experts. This study offers several implications to practitioners engaged in knowledge management. First, managers should recognize that a certain degree of cognitive conflict is necessary for better solutions. However, it is also necessary that cognitive conflict be controlled so that it does not paralyze the process. In that regard, our study suggests that disagreement and conflict can be reduced and consensus built during knowledge capture from multiple experts. Managers can use the techniques mentioned in our study to increase the effectiveness and productivity of teams. Also, since managers of a virtual team do not have face-to-face contact with all team members, they can apply the methodologies from our study to facilitate technology adaptation and increase their productivity. Second, our study implies that there are key differences between the two cognitive techniques—RepGrid and Delphi. The results indicate that although Delphi is easier to use and leads to consensus among experts in the short run, RepGrid appears to be more effective in the long run. Managers should carefully weigh these aspects before planning to invest in these approaches as it involves a significant commitment of time. Managers should also plan a suitable training program for their employees so as to encourage the use of alternative methods such as RepGrid. Third, our study also indicates that although the RepGrid teams had lower levels of satisfaction with the process and outcome than the Delphi teams in early stages, their satisfaction levels were higher than those of the Delphi teams in the long run. Higher levels of satisfaction reflect reduced resistance and greater acceptability of this methodology. Such increased satisfaction levels of team members in a virtual team are extremely important because it could be a predictor of repeated usage. Therefore, we believe that practicing managers should seriously consider the use of RepGrid in capturing knowledge from multiple and distributed experts when dealing with complex real-world issues [101].

## Limitations

A key limitation of our study is the small sample size. However, this should be tempered by the fact that we used real-world practicing experts as subjects, unlike other studies that have relied on student subjects. The fact that the multiround study spanned over one year for some teams illustrates the major commitment made by the participants. The richness of collected knowledge, coupled with the time spent by each expert, is testament to the thoroughness and attention devoted during the knowledge capture process. Moreover, using real-world experts is expected to generate a larger number of and greater diversity in the constructs, as compared to student subjects, whose networking knowledge is expected to be very homogeneous, reflecting instructional material. Further, the use of real-world experts also tended to exclude the introduction of spurious knowledge, that is, introduction of factors that are not relevant to the problem. This is more likely to be a concern for student subjects. The use of the RepGrid technique certainly generated many unique constructs. As a result, the level of conflict in the group was expected to be large to start with. The consensus-building techniques that we used needed to overcome a potentially large level of conflict, and the reductions seen in the RepGrid groups serve as indicators of its effectiveness. With student subjects, one could expect comparatively little cognitive conflict to start with, and conflict reduction would be hard to come by.

As with any study, we faced several threats to internal validity [19]. We took a number of steps to minimize their impact. Selection and selection-maturation interaction effects were avoided by randomly allocating subjects to the two methods. The study spanned over a year and involved repeated assessments from the experts, making it potentially prone to history, maturation, and testing threats. Because we were measuring perceived importance and perceived satisfaction, it is doubtful that repeated testing could have led to “improved” performance. Likewise, external events and subject maturation are unlikely to affect one treatment selectively. Because consensus was a derived measure, and the disagreement scores were not communicated to the experts, it is unlikely that the subjects engaged in any form of gaming to “improve” their performance. The instruments used in our study were reliable and valid, and were consistently administered via a Web interface, thereby avoiding instrumentation effects. Mortality effects were reduced to a large extent by discarding the observations pertaining to teams if any experts dropped out. Finally, an examination of raw data from various rounds provided no systematic evidence for regression effects. External validity may be an issue because there are fewer controls that can be exercised in a field experiment. However, threats to external validity due to people and place were minimized because our study used real-world experts during normal work hours in their work settings.

Because this study dealt with teams, groupthink and deindividualization could also be of concern [5] as team ratings were shared in successive rounds. The virtual nature of the teams tends to reduce groupthink. Anonymity of responses eliminates fear of recrimination, encouraging all experts to contribute without inhibition. In addition, it prevented the domination by an individual or a subgroup. Likewise, deindividualization proved not to be an issue since the experts provided their responses without feeling pressure to conform, as borne out by the results in some cases, where disagreement actually increased. Finally, multiround studies cannot monitor interactions between participants continuously [16]. We minimized this by ensuring anonymity of the experts during all rounds of interaction. Dependence on the facilitator could, however, be considered a limitation. It may also be noted that we did not specifically evaluate the effectiveness of training the participants received in the two techniques used in this study. Perceived ineffectiveness and lack of understanding (especially of the RepGrid technique) could have played a role in expression of lower satisfaction in the early rounds. Finally, the use of one researcher for interaction with the teams could represent a source of bias, although care was taken to ensure that the interaction style was consistently applied to all of the teams throughout the entire experiment.

## Future Research Directions

This stud y ra ises ma ny questions tha t wa rra nt f urther investiga tion. The effectiveness of the techniques needs to be examined for problems involving different structure and scope. This study addressed a design problem that is eminently suited to both techniques. Knowledge-based systems can also address problems of classification, consultation, and diagnosis, which may entail some rethinking of the application of the techniques. We examined a problem that is relatively rich in scope, as illustrated by the number of factors that were generated. Many knowledge-based systems tackle simpler problems, and it is unclear whether the effectiveness of the techniques will differ for simpler or considerably more complex problems. Also, we chose to measure consensus using unweighted scores; it is possible that this approach may have led to more bias and was less efficient than when using weighted scores. Two other areas of further research relate to the compilation and subsequent use of the knowledge elicited. In our study, the compilation was entirely manual, including the identification and elimination of duplicate and subsumed factors. This is a candidate for automation, based on text analysis using domain-based semantic knowledge. Our study generated a number of factors that are relevant in the design of network systems. Incorporating them into design algorithms/heuristics represents a logical progression in the knowledge management cycle. The findings from this study open up the possibility of using the two cognitive techniques in conjunction with each other; for example, hybrid systems in which RepGrid could be used to capture knowledge initially, following which Delphi could be used to create consensus. Future studies could examine and compare the effectiveness of hybrid methods with pure methods.

## Conclusion

The f ind ings f rom this stud y represent a step toward making the knowledge capture process more efficient and effective. Our study shows that RepGrid is a viable technique for reducing conflict that occurs among experts during the knowledge capture process. Findings also indicate that although experts initially felt that Delphi was an easier method to use and were more satisfied with it, RepGrid proved to be a superior technique over time. Our findings should benefit not only researchers and practitioners looking to alleviate the knowledge acquisition bottleneck but also managers looking to increase the effectiveness of capturing knowledge from multiple distributed experts dealing with complex real-world issues. Additional studies could investigate the impact of other methods and factors on the knowledge capture process, especially in the context of virtual teams.

Repertory Grid (RepGrid) Methodology

The RepGrid tec hnique helps elic it personal constructs/mental models of participants using three major components: elements, constructs, and links [38]. Elements are the objects of thought or attention within the domain of investigation; constructs are qualities describing the elements that a participant would arrive at by comparing and contrasting two or more elements. This approach is also known as construing and forces a participant to think deeply about issues. Constructs are formed from a participant’s interpretation of the elements, and are bipolar in nature. Links are ways in which elements and constructs are related. For example, in an election scenario, elements represent the candidates, constructs would be the issues, and links capture the rating of candidates on issues. These components are represented as a matrix (RepGrid) with elements as columns and constructs as rows. The technique allows the facilitator or the subjects to define the elements, identify constructs, and rate the element on each construct [29]. These components are elicited and collected in three stages:

•	 Stage 1: The facilitator (or the participants themselves) identifies the elements from the domain under investigation and presents them to the participants. For this study, the facilitator supplied the elements in the form of three case scenarios. Each subject is asked to elicit constructs using the dyadic or the triadic approach. Under the triadic approach, each subject is randomly given three elements (cases) and asked to identify specific constructs by looking for similarities among any two elements and contrasting them with the third (construing approach). Constructs so elicited will be treated as bipolar using contrasting poles. In this study, the three case scenarios were used to elicit constructs on the basis of similarities and differences. Experts could provide as few or as many constructs as they wished.

•	 Stage 2: Constructs are screened to remove homonyms, synonyms, and other entries. Participants are provided feedback and asked to link the elements to the constructs using rating or ranking. The data collected can be analyzed qualitatively or quantitatively using techniques such as cluster analysis.

•	 Stage 3: The data are analyzed and transformed into a cognitive map and presented to the participants. This map depicts the proximity of participants and constructs. Participants use this information to understand the mental models of other experts in their group.

## Delphi Methodology

The Delphi methodology is a group process that uses written media to solicit and aggregate the judgments of a number of individuals using questionnaires [12]. The approach is iterative, allowing participants to review the opinions and reasoning of others, and subsequently revise their initial thoughts based on peer feedback to enable diverse viewpoints to converge toward a common understanding. These objectives are achieved in the following stages:

•	 Stage 1: The facilitator presents the object of investigation to the participants, who then use it to individually elicit issues in the form of statements. These issues/statements are collated to develop an initial questionnaire, which is then distributed to the all the members in the team. In this study, members of teams using the Delphi technique were asked to read all three cases and elicit constructs for the problem in the form of statements. These statements were then collated by the facilitator and sent to all the members of a team.

•	 Stage 2: The team members send their responses to the initial questionnaire to the facilitator in the form of ratings or rankings.

•	 Stage 3: The facilitator summarizes the responses to the initial questionnaire, uses it to develop a new questionnaire, and sends the summaries and the new questionnaire to the members of the team.

•	 Stage 4: Team members then evaluate their earlier responses based on the feedback report and vote on the priority ideas given in the new questionnaire.

•	 Stage 5: The responses are sent to the facilitator. The process continues for additional rounds until there is a consensus.
