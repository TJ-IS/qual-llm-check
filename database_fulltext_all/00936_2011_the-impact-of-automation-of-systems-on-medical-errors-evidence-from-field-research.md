Although there are studies based on anecdotal evidence and self-reported data on how automation impacts medical errors, no systematic studies exist that are based on actual error rates from hospitals. Further, there is no systematic evidence on how incremental automation over time and across multiple wards impacts the rate of medical errors. The primary objective of our study is to fill this gap in the literature by empirically examining how the automation of core error prevention functions affects two types of medical errors. We draw on the medical informatics literature and principal-agency theory and use a unique panel data set of actual documented medical errors from two major hospitals to analyze the interplay between automation and medical errors. We hypothesize that the automation of the sensing function (recording and observing agent actions) will have the greatest impact on reducing error rates. We show that there are significant complementarities between quality management training imparted to hospital staff and the automation of control systems in reducing interpretative medical errors. We also offer insights to practitioners and theoreticians alike on how the automation of error prevention functions can be combined with training in quality management to yield better outcomes. Our results suggest an optimal implementation path for the automation of error prevention functions in hospitals.

## 1. Introduction

Ever since the publication of the 1999 report by the Institute of Medicine (IOM), To Err is Human, which reported that medical errors are the cause of as many as 98,000 deaths each year in the United States, medical errors<sup>1</sup> have received much public attention. A recent report by the IOM concludes that errors harm 1.5 million people, and the cost of errors to the country is at least \$3.5 billion every year (New York Times 2006). Concerned with the rate of medical errors, the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) has instituted a system (since August 2007) under which the CMS would hold hospitals accountable for failing to avert eight largely preventable medical errors and would not reimburse hospitals for expenses resulting from these errors (Wald and Kramer 2007).

A highlight of the IOM report is the finding that most of the medical errors were not due to incompetent people but to bad systems that include all the processes and methods used to carry out various functions (Leape 2009). Medical errors can usually be traced back to manual transmission of information across different functional units of the hospital (e.g., information from the preoperative unit to the operating room to the recovery room to regular wards), manual calculations of doses, and unmonitored clinical interventions (Payne et al. 2007). It is here that automation of information capture and transmission between agents and across the different functional units of the hospital can reduce the rate of medical errors because they enable the automation of the checks and procedures, thereby removing or reducing the “human touch.” Keeping this in mind, the Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organization (JCAHO) started an initiative to bring together compliance norms and guidelines, process levels, and information-technology based monitoring (Leape and Berwick 2005). For hospitals outside the United States, the Joint Commission International (JCI) recommends procedural norms and performs audits leading to certification. The Joint Commission’s procedural norms regulate and recommend several tasks that lead to patient safety. These tasks—broadly categorized into three core error-prevention functions—are as follows: (1) observe and record the actions of agents, (2) recommend context-specific procedural controls on their actions, and (3) undertake periodic managerial review of the extent to which agents (clinical and nonclinical) are in compliance with procedural norms. All three functions play a role in the reduction of medical errors. Several hospital information systems (HIS) vendors are building modules (Miscrosoft’s Amalga and Epic Systems’ EpicCare Inpatient are two such examples) to automate the information capture and flow involved in these three functions.

Given the potential advantages of automation, more hospitals in the United States and abroad are investing money in implementing such systems in their practice. However, there are various questions related to automation and hospital performance that remain uninvestigated. First, what is the impact of automation of the core (medical) errorprevention functions on medical errors? No study, to our knowledge, has empirically analyzed the relationship between automation and medical errors using actual hospital data. Consequently, the actual impact of automation on the rate of medical errors warrants a more thorough empirical examination. Secondly, no study has looked at the differential impact of automating these three functions on the incidence of medical errors in hospitals. Finally, there is no study that is based on actual data on medical errors that has examined the effect of quality training programs and their complementary effect on automation of error prevention functions.

The main objective of this study is to address the preceding issues by empirically investigating the impact of automation of error prevention functions in hospitals on the rate of medical errors in hospitals. We accomplish our objective by using a unique panel data set of incremental automation over time of the error prevention functions and actual rates of medical errors at several administrative units (such as wards) of two major, large multispecialty hospitals (from two different countries). These hospitals were preparing to be audited by the JCI so as to be accredited. An important highlight of our data set is that we have been able to capture information on continuous incremental growth in the automation of hospital functions and the impact of such automation on medical error rates. Following the taxonomy given in the medical literature, we categorize medical errors into two types (we discuss these two types in greater detail in §3) and collect data on how automation impacts each type of errors. We also have access to the level of quality training programs at each of the administrative units in the hospitals. We develop a set of hypotheses about the impact of such automation on medical error rates over a period of time, and we test our proposed empirical model.

Results from our study suggest that automation of the three core error prevention functions helps to reduce both kinds of medical errors. Furthermore, we find that the automation of some functions has a greater impact on reducing errors than others. We argue for and find a differential impact of certain types of automation on certain kinds of medical errors. We also find evidence of significant complementarities between automation (of certain functions) and the training of clinical and nonclinical workers in quality management. Our study offers important insights regarding the effects of automation in healthcare settings.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review the pertinent literature. In §3, we provide the background on the medical terminology of the different roles of error prevention functions and the different types of medical errors. In §4, we present our proposed research framework and develop our hypotheses. In §5, we present our data and insights from our field research. In §6, we present our proposed empirical model and discuss results. In §7, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our study, and finally, in §8, we conclude with implications for future research.

## 2. Literature Review

Driven by a finding in the 1999 IOM report that states that the problem of medical errors is not because of bad people, but because of a system that needs to be made safer (Leape and Berwick 2005), researchers in the field of IS and medical informatics have begun to address the role of automation of the error-prevention mechanism in preventing medical errors. Given that adverse drug events are the most common and preventable type of medical errors (Seger et al. 2007), most of the extant studies have focused on the effect of computerized physician order entry on adverse drug events. For a systematic review on this topic, we refer the reader to Wolfstadt et al. (2008). However, at a broader level, when it comes to automation and medical error-prevention mechanisms, JCI’s requirements span three categories of functions: observing and recording the actions of agents, procedural controls that recommend modes of behavior, and managerial review of compliance with norms (we discuss these in greater detail in the next section). In a seminal study, Ball (2003) discusses the roles of information systems that sense the actions of clinical agents and help enforce behavioral controls so that agents are in compliance with procedural norms and systems that track wardlevel medical errors. The study refers to functions of sensing and monitoring; however, it is not based on actual data of how automation impacts medical errors. In an earlier study, Bates (2000) also looks at two of the three core error-prevention functions, namely, sensing and monitoring. The technologies studied include computerized physician decision support, robots for filling prescriptions, bar coding, and computerized physician order entry (CPOE). Other studies (for example, Bates et al. 1999, 2003; Bates 2000) comment and speculate based on anecdotal evidence of how automation of the sensing and monitoring functions could impact medical errors.

The use of technologies to monitor the behavior of clinical agents and to build procedural controls into systems that will enforce compliance with procedural norms has also been explored in extant research. Kohli and Kettinger (2004) used a physicians’ profiling system to “monitor and benchmark physicians’ clinical practices and outcomes” so as to reduce clinical procedural costs and to adopt practices benchmarked to produce better outcomes. Another recent study by Piontek et al. (2010) has established the effect of computerized monitoring on cost and quality outcomes. Although the studies discussed above shed light on the possible outcomes of the different types of automation, none have comprehensively looked at the automation of all of the core functions on the different types of medical errors. Furthermore, given that social and technical elements of a health-care organization interact with each other, (Wears and Berg 2005), it is surprising that no study has explicitly studied how automation interacts with the behavior of clinical and nonclinical agents.

Another notable limitation of the extant studies that have looked at medical errors is that they are restricted to analysis at a single administrative unit (i.e., ward) (for example, Payne et al. 2007, Raschke et al. 1998) or based on qualitative self-reported data (Wachter et al. 2002). From an econometric point of view, analysis at a single ward level cannot rule out the impact of the ward-specific effects on the error rates. To sum, no study to date has systematically examined the effects of automation of all of the three core error-prevention functions on the two types of errors using actual data in which automation is done across many different administrative units (or wards) in more than one hospital. Leveraging a unique panel data in which automation of the three core functions was done across different types of wards from two major hospitals (from two different countries), we seek to examine the interplay between the automation of error prevention functions and the extent of quality management training on the two types of errors. This data is also unique for a second reason in that it is the only study to our knowledge that captures the gradual and incremental automation of error management functions in a hospital and its impact on medical errors. As we will discuss in the modeling section, the cross-sectional time-series data with timevarying automation allows us to account for various unobserved effects to correctly estimate the effects of automation on medical errors. Finally, we also furnish the results of the postautomation surveys of the usage of the systems at each of the administrative units and demonstrate evidence in support of our findings. We hope that our comprehensive analysis will offer hospital managers important implications regarding automation.

## 3. Types of Error-Prevention Functions and Medical Errors

In this section, we review the error-prevention procedures and policies that are prescribed and audited by the JCI as a part of their certification process in order to introduce the readers to the medical terminology of the three core error-prevention functions and the different types of medical errors.

3.1. Three Core Functions and Their Automation The JCI’s accreditation policies emphasize compliance with the JCAHO standards on patient safety and error prevention. The three core functions that the JCI’s standards refer to include the following: (1) observation and recording of actions of clinical and nonclinical agents, (2) devising procedural controls that specify what agents can and cannot do, and in specific contexts, and (3) periodic review of agents’ compliance with the procedural safeguards specified by JCI. It is easy to see why these functions are the basic elements of any regime of policies that aims to prevent errors and minimize risk in a hospital setting. It is necessary to first observe and record (either manually or through automated means) the actions of agents in order to analyze whether their actions were appropriate; it is necessary to specify a set of procedural controls that control the agent’s response to situations by specifying what the agent can and cannot do. Finally, it is necessary to review the extent to which agents complied with the errorprevention norms and policies. The JCI accreditation process emphasizes the different elements that constitute each of these three core functions and audits hospitals on how these functions are executed in each administrative unit or ward.

Based on the classification of functions (tracked by the JCI) outlined above, we use the terms sensing, control, and monitoring to refer to these three core functions of observation and recording, procedural control, and review, respectively. More specifically, those functions that are associated with observing and recording an agent’s actions, we refer to as sensing functions.<sup>2</sup> We refer to those functions that involve imposing a set of controls on the agents’ behavior as control functions and those functions that involve managerial review or checking the extent to which agents were in compliance with policy guidelines as monitoring functions. It is important to note that although our terminology is different from the one used by JCI (and JCAHO), the functions that we refer to are identical to those audited by the JCI, and the difference is only semantic in nature. We use the terms sensing, control, and monitoring (rather than observation and record, procedural control, and review, respectively) for two reasons: (i) these terms are simpler (shorter), and thus intuitively clearer, in what they describe and (ii) sensing, control, and monitoring are often used in operations, management, and control theory literatures.

To better illustrate the three different functions, in the following paragraphs we preset a relatively simple and frequently executed task (in hospitals) and explain how three different functions involved in this task can be done either manually or via automated means. Consider a diagnostic lab in a hospital where a lab technician tests samples of patients’ body fluids. The body fluid samples of patients are stored in thermostatic storage to prevent them from decomposing. Lab technicians remove the samples from thermostatic storage and perform tests and then return the samples to storage (until needed again). There are very specific guidelines for how long different kinds of body fluids can be kept out of thermostatic storage. First, let us consider the scenario when the functions involved in this procedure are being performed manually. In such a case, the time at which the body fluid sample was removed by the technician from storage is observed and recorded in a logbook (manual sensing). When the time deadline by which the sample needs to be returned to storage approaches, the lab technician is alerted by another agent (manual control) who may be another employee or a supervisor. If the technician is delayed in returning the sample (beyond the stipulated time), then the delay is also noted manually. The logbook is reviewed (every fortnight or so) by a manager who prepares aggregate profiles of compliance by technicians by looking into individual entries and identifying individual instances of noncompliance (manual monitoring).

Now, let us consider the same example, but in a scenario when the three functions are automated. In such a scenario, the lab technician is identified through a digital card swipe and the time at which the sample is removed from storage is captured and recorded (automated sensing). As the time to return the sample to storage approaches, the system alerts the technician through audible signals and through a digital display and prompts him (automated control) to return the samples to thermostatic storage. If the technician overrides the system and returns the sample after the stipulated time, this is also recorded by the system. MIS reports are generated with aggregate compliance profiles as well as with individual instances of noncompliance (automated monitoring) and sent to supervisors electronically. It is important to note that prior to the automation of these functions in the hospitals under study, the three functions were being performed via manual means in the various administrative units of the hospitals under study. The hospitals automated these functions gradually and incrementally over a period of several months (spanning about three years). This means that at any given period, some functions were being performed through manual means whereas others were automated. The automation of these core error-prevention functions, it was believed, would help to reduce the two major kinds of medical errors.<sup>3</sup> Against this backdrop, we now discuss the two major types of medical errors.

## 3.2. Types of Medical Errors: Procedural and Interpretative Errors

As part of the certification process, JCI prescribes several process-level safeguards for different kinds of wards and administrative units in hospitals. To earn the JCI’s accreditation, hospitals must undergo an on-site survey and/or review by the JCI at least every three years. During this review, the JCI looks into any deviations from procedural norms for each of the administrative units. Any deviation from the prescribed norms of patient safety is investigated. It is important to note that the determination of what is and what is not an error is made only after the deviation from a norm is investigated. As per the JCI’s standards, some deviations from procedural norms are always treated as medical errors irrespective of what the context and circumstances are. These are often referred to by some hospitals as “absolute (procedural) errors” that are not justifiable under any circumstances. Examples of these include not disinfecting private wards between use by different patients, the entry of general ward patients into postoperative wards, etc. When a deviation from a procedural norm is investigated, the determination is made first as to whether or not it falls under an absolute procedural error category. If the deviation is not an absolute procedural violation, then the circumstances and other contextual information are taken into account in order to determine whether or not the deviation is an error. If it is deemed to be an error, it is categorized as “nonabsolute procedural error” and is then documented and archived for possible audit by the JCI. To summarize, the sequence of steps leading up to the classification of errors is as follows: (i) investigations of all deviations from (JCI prescribed) procedural norms take place; (ii) those that are absolute procedural violations are categorized as such and documented; (iii) those that are not absolute procedural violations are further investigated by taking into account the relevant contextual and circumstantial factors and a determination is made as to whether or not these are errors. Where they are deemed as errors, they are categorized as nonabsolute procedural errors and documented in detail for audit by JCI.<sup>4</sup> The classification of the two types of errors is summarized in Figure 1.

In our study, for expositional ease we refer to the two error types—absolute procedural errors and nonabsolute procedural errors—as procedural errors and interpretative errors, respectively. The reason why we emphasize the two types of errors is that the automation of the different functions could have different effects on the two types of errors. From a theory perspective, given the subjective nature of decision making and the exercise of judgment that leads to the classification of deviations as interpretative errors (or not), studying how the automation of different functions can differentially affect the two types of medical errors is important.

## 4. Research Model and Hypotheses Development

In this section we develop our conceptual framework and investigate how the automation of sensing, control, and monitoring functions impacts the two kinds of errors discussed above. In developing our framework, we build on the findings from the field of medical informatics and also draw on extant research in the domains of principal agent theory in economics (Holmstrom 1999) and complementary perspective in economics (Milgrom and Roberts 1995).

## 4.1. The Effect of Automation on Procedural and Interpretative Errors

Automation of the three core quality management functions enables the observation and recording of actions of agents in specific contexts, prompting of agents (via automated means) to take specific actions, and the periodic review of the extent to which the agents complied with norms and procedures prescribed by the JCI. In the manual regime, agents could hope that their actions were either not observed or not reviewed by managers. However, with increasing automation, the possibilities of these deviations from procedural norms going unnoticed diminish. Therefore, we would expect that automation of the three core quality management functions will result in a decline in both procedural and interpretative error rates would decline. Thus, we hypothesize as follows.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A</sup> <sup>(H1A).</sup> Automation of sensing systems should result in lower procedural error rates.

4.2. Automation and Agent Behavior in Hospitals We explore how automating the core error-prevention functions impacts the behavior of clinical and nonclinical agents in hospitals through the prism of principal agency and develop research hypotheses accordingly. Large, multispecialty hospitals (the setting of our study) hire physicians and other clinicians as employees and as consultants. Extant research in economics posits that the relationship between an agent hired to execute tasks and the principal that hires him are extensively characterized as being prone to the problems of principal-agency (Holmstrom 1999, McLaughlin 1994). Kohli and Kettinger (2004) explicitly model the role of the hospital as the principal and the physicians as the agents. The extent of attention to detail and diligence that needs to be exercised by clinicians requires that they put in significant levels of effort. Researchers have shown that when agents are required to make levels of effort that they are not directly compensated for, the result is suboptimal effort on the part of agents, which often manifests itself as negligence and oversight, and it then becomes necessary to monitor their performance using manual or automated means (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991, Jensen and Meckling 1976). As in other professions, in medicine too, medical errors are the result of several factors, some of which arise from suboptimal effort(s) on the part of agents.

It is very common that physicians in major hospitals (like the ones that we study) are compensated through a multipart tariff that includes a fixed fee (salary) for a base volume of work and additional payments for work volumes in excess of the base volume. McPake and Normand (2008) observe that when physicians are compensated through a fixed fee, they often divert their efforts towards ends that are likely to bring them additional compensation, and when this is not possible they do not exert the optimum level of effort. For instance, for an orthopedic surgeon to earn more than a fixed fee, he needs to perform procedures in excess of the contractually specified volume of work. Therefore, his incentives are to perform more procedures. Spending additional time over process-level details in each procedure detracts from his ability to perform additional procedures. In such cases, he may skip some process-level details that he sees as unnecessary or of minimal value to him, or he may delegate some of these tasks to his surgical support team (especially in cases where manual means are employed to record the identity of the person that performs these tasks). Where the incentives are weak, such as when physicians are compensated on a fixed fee alone, extant research shows that doctors actually shirk work and work fewer hours (Newhouse 1973). Shirking on the part of physicians has been suggested by Stern and Trajtenberg (1998), who argue that from a physician’s point of view, providing the best treatment and adhering to best-practice guidelines involves significant learning, search, and thinking costs, and some physicians may exhibit behaviors that help them to minimize such costs. Such behaviors range from exhibiting “clinical inertia”—i.e., not following the correct medical guidelines (Philips et al. 2001) to treating all patients as though they are representative of an “average” patient (Stern and Trajtenberg 1998) and being persistent in their choice of drug (Janakiraman et al. 2008).

In the case of lab technicians and other nonclinical agents, the problem is just as serious. For instance, a ward attendant may not test a bedside hand-held device (for medicine administration) at a private ward before a patient is admitted if he thinks that his act of omission will go unnoticed. One of the oldest problems known in hospitals is that clinical agents transmit infections because they have not observed all procedural safety measures before entering postoperative wards. Building on the results from the studies cited above, we suggest that clinical agents can deviate from procedural norms, which in turn would lead to the motivation to cover up such deviations on their part and also to foster collusion between them. Agents may also resort to collusion to exert suboptimal levels of effort to shirk work. When error prevention functions are largely manual, it is possible for some agents to enter into collusive arrangements in order to subvert the checks and balances built into the system. As we argue in the paper, observing the deviation from norms is critical to changing such deviant behaviors. Once a deviation from a procedural norm is observed and recorded (sensed), it has to be investigated. In a manual setting, when it comes to observing and recording (sensing) these deviations, a clinical worker would carry out the checks and would record the time at which the checks were made. However, we argue that if a clinical worker is not fully supervised, he may not follow the guidelines and, more worrisome he or she can record these checks to suit his/her convenience. Even when these checks are counterchecked manually by another coworker, there is potential for collusion between the two (we refer the reader to Table A-1 of the online appendix, where we provide a real-life, canonical example from our field study of collusion between clinical workers and how it was prevented by automation). Automating the sensing function would alert all the agents that the process of sensing and recording agent actions is not prone to human inefficiencies and collusion. This is consistent with the observation made by contemporary social scientists that technology in the form of panoptic cameras enables the observation of human actions and thereby influences human behavior (Zuboff 1989). Indeed, in the post automation survey of managers presented in Table A-2, supervisors and heads of administrative units state clearly that the greatest impact of automation on agent behavior came from automating the sensing function.

Thus, automating the sensing functions mitigates the threat of moral hazard and opportunistic collusion between agents. Therefore, when compared to the other two functions—i.e., control and monitoring—sensing systems should have the greatest impact on reducing medical errors. Thus, we expect that the automation of the sensing system will have a bigger impact on procedural and interpretative error reduction relative to the other systems. This leads us to our next set of hypotheses.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> Automation of sensing systems will have a greater impact on procedural error rates (decline) than the automation of the other two kinds of systems.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> Automation of sensing systems will have a greater impact on interpretative error rates (decline) than the automation of the other two kinds of systems.

When considering the automation of the three systems, the role played by the automation of control systems in reducing interpretative errors offers some interesting insights. When managing interpretative errors, the two key functions are sensing and monitoring. Whereas the automation of sensing systems lowers the risk of both moral hazard and opportunistic behavior on the part of agents, the automation of monitoring systems helps the hospital management (i.e., the principal) exert greater control over the clinical and nonclinical agents. Control systems help impose behavioral checks on agents even while they permit overrides by agents. What determines whether or not these overrides would be correctly used by agents is the ability of the system to capture the context (i.e., sensing) of the overrides accurately, as well as the ability of supervisors to investigate the reasons for deviations (i.e., monitoring). We expect, therefore, that automation of procedural controls on agents’ actions (control systems) will contribute less to the reduction of medical errors when compared to the other two systems. Thus, we hypothesize as follows.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> Automation of control systems is significantly less important to interpretative error reduction than automation of sensing systems or monitoring systems.

4.3. Quality Management Training and Error Rates The hospitals in our study trained their staff in quality control procedures in order to meet and exceed the standards prescribed by the JCI. Employees trained in quality management procedures (we note that this training differs from the training given to agents in the use of technology) were given rigorous training on which procedural norms could never be deviated from and when and under what conditions some deviations from certain procedural norms were acceptable. Given this, we expect that training employees in quality management will lead to lower rates for both the types of errors. However, we argue for a differential impact across the two types of errors. When agents encounter some procedural norms that they may deviate from depending on the context, the accuracy of their judgment is greatly improved through training in quality management. Because the role of judgment and interpretation is less important in the case of procedural errors, training agents in quality management techniques will have a greater impact on reducing interpretative rather than procedural errors. Thus, see the following hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6</sup> <sup>(H6).</sup> Training employees in quality management will have a significantly greater impact on reducing interpretative errors than on reducing procedural errors.

## 4.4. Complementarities Between Automation and Quality Management Training

Complementarity theory suggests that two activities are complements if “doing more of one thing increases the returns to doing more of another” (Milgrom and Roberts 1995). From an organizational perspective, two resources are complementary if having one increases the returns of having another resource. Several studies in the IS literature have relied on this complementarity perspective to understand how the interaction between information technology and other organizational resources helps to improve organizational performance (e.g., Barua et al. 1996, Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1998, Bharadwaj et al. 2007). In one of the earlier studies, Barua et al. (1996) argued that information technology and business process reengineering are not isolated, but complementary, factors that can be coordinated for better performance. Bharadwaj et al. (2007) argued that IS capabilities will be complementary to the coordination process of an organization and demonstrated the complementary effects of IS capability with manufacturing, marketing, and supply chain processes on manufacturing performance.

We draw on this notion and argue that the automation of control systems and quality management training programs are complementary activities; when agents have more quality training, they can exercise a more accurate judgment on when to override procedural controls. Thus, procedural controls do not become too rigid and hamper the effective working of agents. Likewise, having greater automated systems increases the value of quality training. Under the automated control systems regime, when an agent deviates from a procedural norm, he is forced to override the system’s prompt. This override of the system’s prompt in turn makes the deviation a deliberate act of judgment that he knows will need to be justified. It thus forces the agent to justify his action before he deviates from a norm. By forcing the agent to make a deliberate choice to override a control system prompt, the system makes the agent refer to one or more principles learned from the quality management training. In this context, a related recent study by Devaraj and Kohli (2003)—which uses data on hospital performance and technology usage—shows that the benefits from IT are not driven by the investment in technology as much as the actual usage of the technology. In extending this to our context, we argue that greater automation of manual procedural controls would in turn increase the value of quality training. In other words, automation of control systems and training in quality management are complementary in reducing interpretative error rates. This leads us to our next hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>7</sup> <sup>(H7).</sup> There exist complementarities between the automation of control systems and quality management training on interpretative error rates.

It is worth noting that we do not see similar complementarities between training in quality management and the automation of the other two functions (i.e., sensing and monitoring). Automating sensing systems will make the process of observing and recording agent behavior more accurate, but it does not impact the better decision-making abilities of agents as a result of the training in quality management. Similarly, by automating the monitoring systems, it makes the managerial review of agents’ compliance more accurate and less time consuming (than having to cull information from manual logs). However, this automation does not impact on the decision-making abilities of agents, nor does it make their judgment more effective, which is the impact of training in quality management.

## 5. Methods

In this section we describe the data that we collected and discuss the operationalization of the variables that we use in our model.

## 5.1. Data Collection: The Setting

Data for this research was obtained from two large Asian hospitals that had undergone JCI certification. Both hospitals were the first to be accredited by JCI and are considered to be leaders in the use of HIS in their respective countries, and both hospitals are known for the stringent quality control measures that they adopted in all of their administrative units.<sup>5</sup> The two hospitals’ proprietary records of medical errors were audited by a research firm that specializes in quality control audit, research, and consulting services. The data collection had two aspects. The authors made six visits to the two hospitals over a two-year period and held numerous discussions with senior managers in both hospitals and conducted several interviews with IT managers, quality control managers, and the chief medical officers at the two hospitals. The examples used to illustrate the impact of automation on the behavior of agents have been abstracted from these interviews. The authors also met several times with the managers of the firm that collected the data from the hospitals and audited the hospitals’ practices (prior to JCI certification). As mentioned earlier, the actual data on these types of errors was provided by the research firm that audited the two hospitals. The firm collected time-series data on errors and the extent of automation of error prevention functions across multiple administrative units of the two hospitals spanning a period of 36 months. The two hospitals have a full range of tertiary health-care services and offer 24-hour inpatient and outpatient services. Both hospitals have several operating rooms equipped for cardiothoracic surgery; transplant surgery; orthopedic surgery; urology surgery; ophthalmology surgery; ear, nose, and throat surgeries; minimally invasive surgery; laser surgery; and general surgeries. Both hospitals treat a large number of domestic and international patients each year. More recently (between June 2009 and December 2009), the authors met with the research firm again to collect information about the postautomation survey of managers at the two hospitals.

The unit of data collection across the two hospitals is at a functionally autonomous administrative unit, also commonly referred to as a medical ward (additionally, some units were labs and in-house pharmacies). The research firm termed the wards as “administrative units” and assigned a numeric code to each. There are a total of 21 administrative units in our data set across the two hospitals (9 administrative units belong to Hospital I and 12 to Hospital II). In the subsection that follows, we discuss how each of the variables was operationalized and present the descriptive statistics.

## 6. Model Specification and Results

We have two dependent variables—namely, the rate of procedural errors and the rate of interpretative errors. We formulate the rates of each of the two errors (respectively) as functions of the various hypothesized variables and control variables as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Error } _ {i t} & = \beta_ {o i} + \beta_ {1} \text { Auto\_Sensing } _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \text { Auto\_Control } _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {3} \text { Auto\_Monitoring } _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} \text { TQM Training } _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {5} \text { TQM Training } _ {i t} * \text { Auto\_Control } _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {6} \text { Sensing Checks } _ {i t} + \beta_ {7} \text { Control Checks } _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {8} \text { Monitoring Checks } _ {i t} + \beta_ {9} \text { Work\_Volume } _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {1 0} \text { FTE\_Load } _ {i t} + \beta_ {1 1} \text { Procedure Change } _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {1 2} \text { Hospital } _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array} \tag {5}
$$

where Error is either the rate of procedural errors or interpretative errors of administrative unit i at time t.

Because our model formulation is at the individual hospital ward level and the inherent error rates can be different in different wards, we take a three-pronged approach to make sure that our analysis is robust. The first one is related to the unique nature of our data, in that the error-prevention functions were gradually automated over time. This enables us to study the impact of incremental automation on the two error rates. The advantage of using time-series data is that the difference in the nature of work at the administrative units persists across time periods, although what actually changes between each time period is the extent of automation in each of the wards. In other words, whereas the cardiology department and the pediatrics department feature very different kinds of work within any single time period, what changes between time periods is the extent of automation within those two departments. Secondly, as explained earlier, we have controlled for the difference in volume of work across the different time periods by normalizing the error rate. A unit of work in the osteopathic ward is a procedure done on an inpatient, whereas in a diagnostic lab it is a single test performed on a patient’s sample(s). The volume of work (as measured in number of units of work) is larger in the diagnostic lab than in the osteopathic ward. To account for this, we divided the actual number of errors (of both kinds) in an administrative unit by the number of units of work performed at that administrative unit. Finally, we recognize that over and above the control factors that we include in our model, there could be factors that are unobserved (by the researchers) that could lead to unobserved heterogeneity in the intrinsic rates of both the errors across the different administrative units and across the hospitals. These could lead to time-invariant administrative unit-specific and hospital-specific factors that could impact the error rate. We note that we account for both time-invariant hospital-level and unit-level factors that could impact the error rates.

Extant literature has addressed the issues of unobserved heterogeneity by either a fixed- or randomeffects formulation. In our model formulation, we account for unobserved factors across the two hospitals and across the various administrative units within the two hospitals. We account for the hospitallevel unobserved heterogeneity by the fixed-effect formulation (via the dummy variable “Hospital”), and the unit-level unobserved heterogeneity by following the approach of random coefficients in which we allow the intercept to vary across the units, i.e., $\beta _ { 0 i } \sim N ( \beta _ { 0 } , \sigma ^ { 2 } )$ . Because the administrative units from a hospital are drawn from a common population (i.e., hospital), a random effect is more appropriate (Hsiao 2003). Furthermore, in our case, if we treat both of these effects as fixed effects, we cannot recover estimates of both of these time-invariant explanatory variables. If we were to treat administrative units as fixed and if we were to estimate the model by the well-known differencing transformation, then the hospital effects would drop out. If we were to treat them as fixed, but estimate via maximum likelihood, then it will likely produce inconsistent estimation of not only the fixed terms, but also of the effects of main covariates that we are interested in (Hsiao 2003). It is for the above reasons that we adopt a fixed-effects formulation at the hospital level and the random-effects formulation at the administrative unit level. To sum, we recognize that the mean/intrinsic rates of errors could differ across the administrative units in a hospital and could vary across hospitals. We account for these via appropriate variable operationalization and econometric specification.

Because our model formulation involves interaction terms, we first mean-centered the variables in the interaction terms so as to reduce multicollinearity (Aiken and West 1991). With respect to the multicollinearity among the main-effect variables, we performed various diagnostic tests. The Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) in our model were all below 4.86, which suggests that multicollinearity is not an issue in our context (only VIF values greater than 10 are indicative of multicollinearity) (Marquardt 1970). Another diagnostic is the Condition Indices, which are calculated from the eigenvalues. The condition indices for all the variables are less than 7.95 (only indices greater than 15 would indicate a possible multicollineartity problem), which again suggest that multicollinearity is not an issue in our context. Furthermore, the Durbin-Watson test for potential autocorrelation revealed that there is no evidence of autocorrelation in our data. The final set of parameters is obtained by simulated maximum likelihood (Greene 2007). We estimated the models pertinent to procedural and interpretative errors separately.

Table 3 presents the results of the model estimation for both the procedural and interpretative errors. The overall fit of the proposed model for both the errors is significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , as determined by the likelihood ratio, which favors the proposed model over the null model (with none of the hypothesized variables). This result, along with the prior result that quality management training exerts no effect on the procedural error rates, provides evidence in support of H6.

## 7. Discussion

The objective of our paper is to examine how the automation of different key functions in a hospital affects the rates of two major types of medical errors. Drawing on the literature in medical informatics, principal-agency, and the complementarity perspective in economics, and leveraging a unique time-series data set that spans multiple wards and multiple time periods, we empirically examine the interplay between the automation of different systems, quality training programs, and medical error rates. In the following, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications of the study.

## 7.1. Theoretical Implications

7.1.1. Complementarity Between TQM Training and Automation. Recent research in IS relied on the complementarity perspective in economics (Milgrom 1995) to understand how the interaction between information technology and other organizational resources help improve organizational performance (e.g., Barua et al. 1996, Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1998, and Bharadwaj et al. 2007). In a similar vein, we demonstrate that the automation of core error-prevention functions is complementary to training clinical and nonclinical agents in quality management procedures. In other words, technology amplifies the positive effects of training in quality management (in reducing medical errors), and training in quality management in turn amplifies the error-reduction impact of automation. The implication is that the training in quality management enhances the value of automation and vice versa.

7.1.2. Complementarity and the Nature of Decision Making. Extant research on automation and its impact on knowledge work states that technology’s role in ensuring quality of output is most effective when enforcing rules of compliance in highly codifiable and routine work and that automation loses its effectiveness in the case of work that is not easily codifiable and/or requires the exercise of subjective decision making and exercise of judgment (see Aron et al. 2008, Cohendet and Steinmueller 2000, Kellogg and Chase 1995). Our findings run counter to this. We show that the complementarity between automation and training in quality management holds only in the case of reducing interpretative errors (that involve subjective decision making) and not in the case of reducing procedural errors (routine and codifiable work). We find that with greater training in quality management, workers’ judgment (about when to deviate from a procedural norm) improves, and this in turn enables them to use the technology more optimally. When workers lack sufficient training, automation of procedural controls is either too restrictive (where workers do not override the system when they should) or too ineffective (where workers override the system when they should not). Worker training amplifies the benefits of automation, whereas automation in turn releases workers from manually imposing and enforcing procedural controls on each others’ work. The two hospitals in our study conducted postautomation surveys (through the research firm) on a variety of consequences of automation (we furnish the relevant portion in Tables A-2 and A-3 in an online appendix).<sup>8</sup> In each administrative unit, the head of the unit and a manager that had supervisory duties in the unit provided their feedback on a variety of issues that had to do with automation. Items 11 and 12 in Table A-3 highlight the role of automation on work that involves subjective decision making.

7.1.3. Agency Effects and Automation. Extant research shows that medical errors can result from (i) environmental factors, which in spite of the best efforts made by agents, can have an impact on the quality of their work; and (ii) the principal-agency problem that arise due to clinical and nonclinical agents exerting suboptimal efforts. We ask the following question: on which of these two errors does automation have a greater impact? Agents will rarely, if ever, admit to making suboptimal efforts that resulted in an error, they usually point to factors outside of their control (environmental factors) as the main cause of error. However, our model allows us to provide an interesting indirect answer to this question. Recall that of the three core functions, sensing systems are about automating the observing and the recoding of specific agent actions and that sensing human agent behavior does not really have an impact on environmental factors. As an agent’s behavior is closely observed and recorded through automated means, the agent realizes that (i) collusion as a means of concealing suboptimal effort (or misdirected effort) is no longer possible and (ii) his actions leave an audit trail whereby he can be held accountable for his deviation from prescribed norms. We demonstrate that of the three core functions, automating the sensing function has the greatest impact on error reduction. In other words, automation has the greatest impact on the behavior of human agents in reducing medical errors. Based on this, we posit that automation does help in reducing the problems of agency. Given this, automating the sensing function has a direct impact on human agent behavior (although automation in general may have some impact on mitigating the impact of environmental factors).

## 7.2. Practical Implications

7.2.1. Automate Sensing Systems First. Recent articles in practitioner-oriented journals have pointed out that hospitals face severe budget constraints and the high costs of implementing HIS in hospitals is likely to exacerbate this, and as a result, hospitals are slow to invest in large HIS initiatives (Pauly 2009). Given this, our findings can help to guide managers on the optimal implementation path towards lowering medical errors. Our findings make it clear that when it comes to automating error-prevention functions, the highest priority must be given to automating sensing systems. By automating the process of observing and recording agent actions, considerable reduction in error rates can be achieved. Automation of the other two functions can be taken up after the automation of the sensing function. If automation is phased over a period of time due to resource constraints, then it is best that the automation of the sensing function be given priority. In Table A-2 (of the online appendix), we furnish the postautomation feedback from hospital managers in each of the wards. In each administrative unit, the head of the unit and a manager that had supervisory duties in the unit were both asked to rate the impact of automating each of the three functions on the behavior of the employees in the unit on a 5-point Likert scale (where a rating of 1 corresponded to “absolutely no impact” and a rating of 5 corresponded to “very high level of impact”). It is clear from the results presented in Table A-2 that the impact of automating the sensing function was the greatest. We also find that automation of the sensing function received the highest level of consensus from respondents (based on lowest coefficient of variation) when compared to the automation of the other two functions.

7.2.2. Training of Clinical Agents in Quality Management. Our findings on the complementarity between IT and TQM training shows that there are hidden benefits to the automation of manual functions that are often not captured in a cost-benefit analysis. Traditional cost-benefit analysis focuses on the direct benefits but ignores the complementarity effects of automation. As in the earlier case, the head of each administrative unit and a manager from each unit were polled on their view about the link between training and automation. It can be seen from the Table A-3 (items 3 and 4) that there is clear support for our finding that training in quality management enhances the benefits of automation, whereas there is weaker support for the finding that automation enhances the benefits of training.

7.2.3. Real Impact of FTE Work Loads. While anecdotal claims and qualitative reports of the impact of over work on medical errors seem to suggest that errors are partly a result of the increase in workloads faced by clinical and nonclinical staff (Singh et al. 2007, The New York Times 2008), our study shows that across multiple wards and at two different hospitals the relative impact of workload on medical error rates is statistically insignificant. In our context, FTE loads steadily increased over time. However, we do not find that such increases have led to higher error rates. The data actually points to a decline in error rates over time with increasing automation. We posit that the interaction between quality management training and automation (complementarities effect) can help to offset additional FTE loads and can keep the error rates in check. This is in concordance with the view of the hospital managers (Table A-3, item 2), who report that automation helped to offset the impact of increasing FTE workloads while driving down error rates.

7.2.4. Limited Value of Procedural Controls on Agent Behavior. Our findings show that in the case of interpretative errors, where agents are required to exercise subjective judgment and evaluate multiple factors, automation has little or no direct impact. However, when combined with training focused on making better judgments, the automation of control systems yields clear benefits. Furthermore, we also show that there are significant complementarity effects between the automation of control systems and the levels of quality management training imparted. The old adage that $^ { \prime \prime } i t$ is not technology but people that matter in producing quality” is not entirely true. Support for this can be seen from Table A-3 (items 5 and 6; see the online appendix). Hospital managers hold that the automation of procedural controls (on agents’ actions) does not help to lower interpretative errors. However, when we combine the responses of managers in items 4 and $^ { 6 , }$ there is support for our finding that when training is combined with automation of procedural controls, it helps to lower interpretative error rates. Automation has implications for doctors as well—especially those that are not hospital employees who bring their own teams and are compensated by the volume of procedures performed. Surgeons often bring in their own teams of clinicians (e.g., anesthesiologists, junior physicians), for whose performance they are responsible. Postautomation, they get near-real-time information on the performance of their teams and can then benchmark them against the best practices by peers in their domain of work. Furthermore, the automation of procedural controls also enables doctors that are not employees of the hospital to receive the same quality of clinical support from hospital employees as their physician counterparts employed by the hospitals receive.

## 8. Limitations

Although our study helps us to better understand the finer nuances of the effects of automation on medical error rates, it is not without its limitations. Other hospitals that adopt these quality control mechanisms may not experience the same level of benefits in terms of a reduction in medical errors. This is perhaps because other hospitals may not have the same aspects of organizational culture as the hospitals in our study. Indeed, the social and technical elements are deeply interdependent and interrelated, and for any organization to succeed, both of these elements have to be jointly optimized (Wears and Berg 2005). Two key aspects of organizational culture in the hospitals that we studied were particularly noteworthy in making the automation effective. The two hospitals in our study are characterized by a culture wherein the demonstration of technical competence—in the form of adoption and the use of IT—conferred prestige on clinical and nonclinical employees. Clinicians as well nonclinicians were willing to use information technology and, indeed, welcomed it. As we show in (item 7 of) Table A-3 (see the online appendix), the heads of the administrative units as well as the supervisor from each of the units express strong levels of satisfaction with the extent and speed of adoption of the automated measures in the administrative unit (the very low coefficient of variation in the responses indicates a strong consensus).

A second important aspect of the hospitals’ organizational culture has to do with their metrics-driven style of management. The two hospitals place a lot of reliance on measuring the nature and extent of medical errors as well as being in strict compliance with the recommendations made by the JCI on preventing medical errors. As can be seen from Table A-3 (items 8 and 9), the heads of the administrative units as well as the supervisors are in strong agreement with the claim that they are both metrics driven and comply strictly with the JCI’s recommendations. These hospitals emphasized JCI procedures and controls and were in strict compliance with them, even before automation began. Thus, automation did not involve a major change of managerial style—it merely made the existing procedures and practices considerably more effective. These two aspects of the hospitals’ organizational culture were particularly effective in amplifying the benefits of automation. Other hospitals that automate the core error-prevention functions may need to create a comparable level of managerial focus on measurement before they can see benefits of this scale. Other hospitals may not have the same focus on JCI’s recommendations (or JCAHO in the case of the United States) and may therefore not benefit to the same extent as the hospitals in our study. As a project manager at one of the hospitals told one of the authors “automation acted as a force multiplier; it greatly enhanced the benefits of the excellent managerial practices and disciplines that were in place in our hospital.” It is also important to note that sensing systems may be seen by doctors as being intrusive. In the hospitals in our research setting, there was a culture of manually observing and recording the clinicians’ (including doctors’) actions, which were then reviewed for compliance. Our discussions with hospital managers suggest that automating these manual provisions and creating the sensing systems were not seen by the doctors (in the hospitals) as a significant intrusion into their work. This may not be the case with hospitals in cultures where physicians may see automated observation of their actions as being intrusive and a restriction of their professional autonomy.

Research by Ash et al. (2007) points to the importance of managing the unintended consequences of technological implementation in hospitals. They suggest that these need to be understood so that they can be prevented or overcome. Sittig et al. (2008), in their analysis of consequences of implementing technologies in hospitals, recommend that clinicians must be deeply involved in the selection and implementation process. Both hospitals encouraged extensive user participation in the design of systems that would automate the error-prevention functions. Table A-3 (item 10) suggests that in both of the hospitals, clinicians participated extensively in the design of the automated systems. Other hospitals that use off-theshelf systems to automate error prevention may not quite capture the same benefit if their users do not participate sufficiently in the design of systems. We note that although the abovementioned contextual factors are limitations of our study, we hope that they do highlight the point that organizational culture and technology has to be mutually integrated in order to reap the benefits of technology.

## 9. Conclusions and Future Research

In conclusion, our study provides new insights into the role of error-prevention functions on two main types of medical errors, namely, procedural and interpretative errors. We believe that this research can be extended to explore a variety of policy issues such as subsidies for technology adoption by hospitals as well as the use of technology to monitor rather than control the activities of clinicians in hospitals. Future research can also look at how technology affects organizational dynamics, agents’ adherence with the various automation specifications, and also issues of power relationships between the various agents. In particular, it would be interesting to investigate if the automation of error-prevention functions has a greater impact on doctors vis-á-vis nonclinical agents.

One can also study whether automations modify doctors’ behavior in ways that may diminish the effectiveness of automation systems. This research can also serve as a basis for further study on how training in quality management can be combined with technological mechanisms to produce optimal quality outcomes. This research can also be extended to explore design issues for each of the three kinds of technologies that we discuss in the paper. We hope that our results will spawn further research in this important area.
