# EQUITY CROWDFUNDING AND ACCESS TO CAPITAL FOR USER ENTREPRENEURS: EVIDENCE FROM A RANDOMIZED FIELD EXPERIMENT<sup>1</sup>

Sofia Bapna

Department of Information & Decision Sciences, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {sbapna@umn.edu}

Martin Ganco

Department of Management and Human Resources, Wisconsin School of Business, University of Wisconsin-Madison Madison, WI U.S.A. {ganco@wisc.edu}

We examine whether equity crowdfunding democratizes access to funding for nontraditional user entrepreneurs. User entrepreneurs start by creating a product to serve their own unmet needs with no expectations of monetary profit, then later decide to commercialize the product through entrepreneurship. In contrast, traditional (producer) entrepreneurs take a more profit-driven path to entrepreneurship and start by identifying an opportunity that has commercial potential. Through a randomized field experiment, we randomly reveal to some investors that a firm producing a product used by musicians is founded by a musician and conceal this founder-related information from other investors. Revealing the information suggests that the firm is a user innovator firm and concealing it suggests that the firm is a traditional producer firm. We find that investors are significantly more interested in the traditional producer firm. Through an additional field experiment, we identify that the bias against user entrepreneurs is statistical (based on a response to limited information) rather than taste based (based on an idiosyncratic dislike). Our findings suggest that user entrepreneurs can mitigate investor bias by displaying signals of quality such as those regarding firm growth and broad product appeal.

Keywords: Equity crowdfunding, randomized field experiment, entrepreneurship, user innovation, statistical discrimination

## Introduction

“While innovation is ubiquitous, the choice of which innovations become commercialized has historically been profoundly undemocratic” (Mollick & Robb, 2016, p. 72). This is because the opportunity to make investments in private ventures has traditionally been limited to a small group of experts—that is, venture capitalists and angel investors. It is hoped that recent legislation, along with advances in digital technologies, will facilitate the funding of ideas from nontraditional sources. The Jumpstart Our Business Startups (JOBS) Act Title III, which went into effect in the U.S. in May 2016, allows all individuals to purchase securities in private firms. This legislation is touted as a way to democratize access to capital for founders from traditionally disadvantaged groups because it increases the diversity of individuals who can make equity investments (Kalil & Rand, 2016; Mollick & Robb, 2016). In parallel with this legislation, advances in digital technologies have enabled the creation of equity crowdfunding platforms, providing the infrastructure that facilitates the process of funding between entrepreneurs and diverse providers of capital (Kim & Hann, 2019). While the JOBS Act Title III is intended to help nontraditional entrepreneurs by diversifying the investor pool, the digital context of equity crowdfunding should help such entrepreneurs by reducing spatiotemporal and network-related barriers to investor access. Such platforms allow capital seekers to simultaneously broadcast their pitches to many unaffiliated and geographically diverse investors.

In the crowdfunding literature, one question that information systems (IS) and entrepreneurship scholars have started to explore is whether crowdfunding benefits nontraditional and disadvantaged groups (Kim & Hann, 2019; Bapna & Ganco, 2021; Rhue & Clark, 2021; Johnson et al., 2018; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2017). Specifically, in the context of equity crowdfunding, prior research has identified some positive outcomes with respect to the democratization of access to capital. For example, investors in equity crowdfunding may view female entrepreneurs favorably (Bapna & Ganco, 2021; Johnson et al., 2018). In this study, we explore whether equity crowdfunding facilitates access to capital for another type of nontraditional capital seeker—the user entrepreneur—relative to the more traditional producer entrepreneur.

Two factors make user entrepreneurs nontraditional entrepreneurs—their path to entrepreneurship and their background. User entrepreneurs are individual users, i.e., consumers, who identify an unsolved problem or need through their own professional or personal experience, create a product or service to use it themselves, and later decide to commercialize the product by starting a company (Shah & Tripsas, 2007). User entrepreneurs represent an important source of innovations in many industries and their ability to commercialize products is vital to the emergence and growth of many market segments (Agarwal & Shah, 2014). Examples of user entrepreneurs in the consumer products segment include the parents, grandparents, and babysitters who introduced most of the innovations in the juvenile products industry such as strollers and car seats (Shah & Tripsas, 2007); a socialite housewife who invented an automatic dishwasher to keep her porcelain from chipping (Sobey, 2010); and sports enthusiasts (e.g., surfers and sailors), who were responsible for key innovations in skateboarding and windsurfing equipment (Shah, 2003). Importantly, user entrepreneurs’ path to entrepreneurship is atypical in that they first develop products for their own use without considering the products’ commercial potential. Furthermore, user entrepreneurs’ prior professional experience is typically outside the focal industry of the user innovation (Shah & Tripsas, 2007). In contrast to users, producer entrepreneurship represents a more traditional path to entrepreneurship and involves the creation of a company after the evaluation of the commercial potential of an opportunity (Shah & Tripsas, 2007). Producer firms create products with the explicit goal of commercialization and profit (Baldwin & von

Hippel, 2011; Gambardella et al., 2017), and founders of producer firms often have prior experience in the focal industry (Agarwal et al., 2004; Klepper & Sleeper, 2005).

Previous qualitative studies document that user entrepreneurs face significant difficulties when commercializing their innovations (Baldwin et al., 2006; Shah & Mody, 2014); important among the contributing factors are the barriers they face when accessing capital for commercialization and growth. Crowdfunding is suggested as a promising solution (Mollick & Robb, 2016) because it provides access to a large and diverse pool of investors, some of whom may be open to nontraditional user entrepreneurs. Along with the increasingly important role that user entrepreneurs play in the economy (Baldwin & von Hippel, 2011), examining the role of user entrepreneurs in the context of equity crowdfunding is thus highly salient.<sup>2</sup>

More specifically, the two research questions of this study are (1) whether equity crowdfunding democratizes access to funding for user entrepreneurs, and (2) whether discrimination against user entrepreneurs, if it exists in equity crowdfunding, is statistical discrimination.<sup>3</sup> In statistical discrimination, decision makers treat groups differently as a rational response to limited information rather than prejudice (Guzman & Kacperczyk, 2019). In contrast, in taste-based discrimination, the differences in the treatment of groups are a result of prejudice or idiosyncratic dislike for a particular group (Becker, 1957; Moser, 2012). Identifying whether a lower preference for user innovator firms, if it exists, is statistical or taste-based discrimination has implications for fundraising strategies. Specifically, in statistical discrimination, the differential treatment of groups can be mitigated with more reliable signals of performance (either positive or negative), and the negative perceptions of the stereotyped group can be mitigated by positive performance information (Guzman & Kacperczyk, 2019).

To answer our research questions, we implemented two concurrent randomized controlled field experiments in partnership with an equity crowdfunding platform. Our study involved one firm and we manipulated information that investors could see about that firm. In our main experiment, we randomly revealed to some investors that the firm is a user innovator firm and concealed this information from other investors, suggesting that the firm is a traditional producer firm. We found that the investors were significantly more interested in the producer firm. In the second experiment, we augmented the information about the producer and user innovator firm from the main experiment with information about the firm’s performance.<sup>4</sup> We found that with the performance information included, investor interest was similar across the two types of firms, suggesting that the discrimination against user entrepreneurs is statistical. We conducted manipulation tests to show that our experimental conditions were interpreted as intended. We also implemented a survey of the participants of the equity crowdfunding platform on which the experiment was conducted. The survey explores investors’ perceptions of firms founded by user innovators relative to those founded by producers and provides suggestive evidence regarding the perceived weaknesses of user entrepreneurs.

The study makes several contributions to the literature and has practical implications. First, we contribute to the emerging stream of crowdfunding literature that focuses on identifying whether crowdfunding democratizes access to capital to nontraditional entrepreneurs (Bapna & Ganco, 2021; Kim & Hann, 2019; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2017). We do so by showing that investors in equity crowdfunding favor producer firms and that the bias against user innovator firms is statistical. The latter finding has practical implications because prior research shows that in the case of statistical discrimination, the negative perceptions of the stereotyped group can be mitigated by providing positive signals of quality (e.g., Guzman & Kacperczyk, 2019).<sup>5</sup> Second, the study contributes to the user entrepreneurship literature (Smith & Shah, 2013; Gambardella et al., 2017; Baldwin et al., 2006) by being, to our knowledge, the first study to causally identify the fundraising performance of user innovator firms. Finally, the results of the experiments in conjunction with prior research and our supplementary surveys point to potential strategies that can be employed by user entrepreneurs and equity crowdfunding platforms to improve the fundraising performance of user innovator firms. Our findings point to the importance of including in the initial pitch, information regarding firm growth and broad product appeal, for example, to alleviate investors’ negative perceptions of user entrepreneurs.<sup>6</sup>

## Theory

## Investor Perceptions of Firms Founded by User Innovators vs. Firms Founded by Producers

Drawing on prior research, we discuss the differences in investor perceptions of user innovator firms relative to producer firms. We focus on broad categories of perceptions regarding market size and market growth, product novelty, and founder skills—that is, characteristics that were identified in prior research as being salient for explaining the differences between the two types of firms (von Hippel et al., 2011; Agarwal & Shah, 2014). These theoretical arguments guide our empirical examination.

Perceptions of market size and market growth: Prior studies have highlighted that user innovators are users or consumers who create solutions with the goal of satisfying their own unmet needs for a new or improved product because they are dissatisfied with existing commercial product offerings (Shah & Tripsas, 2007; Agarwal & Shah, 2014). User innovators usually develop products for their own use without the evaluation of a commercial opportunity. When user innovators begin to use the product in public, they may, often unintentionally, gather feedback from others that leads them to recognize some commercial potential of the product. As a result, they may decide to form a company to commercialize their innovation. Prior to entry, user entrepreneurs tend not to evaluate the extent to which the innovation meets the needs of a larger customer base and whether it will be broadly accepted by the marketplace (Shah & Tripsas, 2007). In contrast, traditional entrepreneurs or producers tend to create companies after evaluating the commercial potential of an opportunity. Producer firms start with an evaluation of users’ needs through market research, followed by the development of their own products through R&D (Gambardella et al., 2017), or they may observe the diffusion of user innovators’ designs to see which ones are likely to offer the basis for a profitable product (von Hippel, 2005). The entry by user entrepreneurs relative to firms by producers may thus be less calculated and, at least initially, less oriented toward market growth. Furthermore, user innovators tend to create more novel products relative to producer firms, and the market size and market growth for such products tend to be highly uncertain (von Hippel et al., 2011). These arguments suggest that relative to producer entrepreneurship, investors may associate user entrepreneurship with less desirable future market outcomes in terms of market size and market growth.

Perceptions of product novelty: Producer firms, especially in consumer goods, are reluctant to enter uncertain markets, as such markets inherently face the risk that they will stay small. As highlighted in prior work (von Hippel et al., 2011), the need to spread R&D and other innovation-related costs over many consumers to be profitable requires a market of a certain size to support entry. Entry decisions of producer firms are thus based on a different calculus relative to entry by user innovator firms. At the same time, the market for products with novel functionality tends to be uncertain (von Hippel et al., 2011). For example, when snowboards were first introduced, it was hard for anyone to envision whether the market was viable and estimate its size or customers’ willingness to pay for the products. Since producers tend to enter when they can see evidence of a profitable market, users often end up pioneering novel products (von Hippel et al., 2011). Furthermore, user innovation occurs while using a product or service; thus, user innovators’ tacit knowledge regarding the use, emerging user needs, and context of use leads to the identification of opportunities that may not be apparent to producers (Smith & Shah, 2013; Gambardella et al., 2017; van der Boor et al., 2014). For example, Sherman Poppen, a father of two, invented a snowboard as a toy for his children by bolting together two skis. Seeing how popular the invention became, he patented it, and it was later licensed by Brunswick (MacArthur, 2010). Finally, user innovators often come from outside the focal industry and may thus develop more novel innovations and identify opportunities that industry participants might miss (Shah & Tripsas, 2007). This is because individuals from outside a given field tend to frame problems differently, generate unique problem-solving approaches (Jeppesen & Lakhani, 2010; Agarwal et al., 2020), and their different prior knowledge provides opportunities to recognize novel combinations (Fleming & Sorenson, 2001). These arguments suggest that relative to producer entrepreneurship, investors may associate user entrepreneurship with positive perceptions of novelty and differentiation of the firm’s products.

Perceptions of founder’s skills: Given that user entrepreneurs are a heterogeneous group with a variety of backgrounds and career histories, only a small number of user entrepreneurs possess the business knowledge (e.g., of operational and organizational processes) and critical complementary assets (e.g., physical capital, brand equity, organizational knowledge) that are important for successful commercialization, profitability, and growth of a venture (Agarwal & Shah, 2014). Among the few who do, the knowledge may be from a different industry context than the one they are entering. When investors encounter a firm by a user innovator, such as a firm started by a musician or a surfer, investors may be reminded of the entrepreneur’s path to entrepreneurship, which may lead to negative perceptions about the founder’s ability to operate and grow the firm relative to a producer firm.

Based on the arguments above, when screening investment opportunities in similar firms by user innovators and by producers, in the absence of other concrete performance signals, it is likely that investors will have: (1) negative perceptions about the market size and market growth of user innovator firms relative to producer firms, (2) positive perceptions about the novelty and differentiation of products of user innovator firms relative to producer firms, and (3) negative perceptions of user innovator firm founders’ experience and the skills necessary to successfully operate and grow their businesses, relative to producer firms founders. If investors’ negative perceptions of user innovator firms outweigh their positive perceptions, then investors will favor producer firms relative to user innovator firms. We consider this an open empirical question and causally examine it in our analysis. By answering this question in the context of equity crowdfunding, we shed light on whether equity crowdfunding democratizes access to capital for nontraditional user entrepreneurs.

## Type of Discrimination

The arguments discussed above in the theory section suggest that if discrimination against user entrepreneurs exists in the context of equity-based crowdfunding, it is statistical discrimination. In statistical discrimination (Arrow, 1998; Neilson & Ying, 2016; Phelps, 1972), decision makers treat groups differently as a rational response to limited information. In other words, in situations with limited information about performance or quality, statistical discrimination exists when two parties of the same performance or quality are treated differently because of their ascriptive characteristics. In statistical discrimination, one group is stereotyped or treated differently due to lack of information rather than because of prejudice against the stereotyped group. When the differences in the treatment of groups stem from prejudice or idiosyncratic dislike for a particular group, the type of discrimination is taste based (Moser, 2012).

It is useful to know whether discrimination against user innovator firms, if it exists, is statistical or taste based. In statistical discrimination, the reliance on ascriptive characteristics [e.g., gender, race, type of entrepreneur (user or producer)] that results in the differential treatment of groups is likely to diminish when more concrete signals of performance or quality (either positive or negative) are available (Guzman & Kacperczyk, 2019; Botelho & Abraham, 2017). Moreover, an implication of statistical discrimination is that the negative perceptions of the stereotyped group can be mitigated by positive performance information. In the case of taste-based discrimination, providing a concrete signal of performance or quality is unlikely to alter decision makers’ perceptions. For example, in the context of venture capital decision-making for hightech entrepreneurship, Tinkler et al. (2015) found that nontechnical female entrepreneurs were evaluated as having less leadership ability than nontechnical male entrepreneurs; however, they also found that these gendered expectations were moderated when female entrepreneurs signaled technical backgrounds, suggesting statistical discrimination. If the gendered expectations are not moderated when female entrepreneurs signal technical backgrounds, this suggests taste-based discrimination.

The arguments above imply that if discrimination against user innovator firms relative to producer firms exists and a user innovator firm and producer firm of the same performance are treated similarly when a concrete signal of quality is provided, then the discrimination is statistical. If a concrete signal of performance does not attenuate the discrimination against user innovator firms, if it exists, this suggests that the discrimination is taste based.

## Data and Methods

## Institutional Details

Our study employs two concurrent randomized controlled field experiments executed on the equity crowdfunding platform “EquityPlatform” (name disguised).<sup>7</sup> Our experiments are similar in approach to those by Bernstein et al. (2017), Bapna (2017), and Bapna & Ganco (2021).

Individuals can use EquityPlatform to invest in companies in exchange for shares in that company. For every venture that fundraises through EquityPlatform, a campaign webpage is created on EquityPlatform’s website. The campaign webpage contains comprehensive fundraising-related information including venture and team details, as well as financing goals and terms. Each fundraising campaign is announced to EquityPlatform’s members, who are individuals who have signed up to receive campaign emails from EquityPlatform regarding investment opportunities.<sup>8</sup>

Members may sign up on the platform for a variety of reasons, from research to confirming their affiliation with a start-up that is fundraising to making investments (Bernstein et al., 2017). As with Bernstein et al. (2017), we focus on active members or active investors on EquityPlatform, i.e., those who have made at least one investment through the platform. Focusing on active investors allows us to study those individuals who are on the platform to make investments and receive returns. Importantly, active investors are the source of funds for founders and profits for the platform. Our study includes 636 active investors.<sup>9</sup> Appendix 1 provides further details regarding the study’s subjects.

For every firm that raises funds through EquityPlatform, members are sent a campaign email (by EquityPlatform) that contains the fundraising campaign pitch. Based on the information supplied in the pitch, investors decide if they are interested in knowing more about the venture. Investors can acquire additional information about the venture and the equity offer by clicking on the “View Offer” button in the email, which then takes them to the venture’s campaign webpage on EquityPlatform’s website. This initial screening stage is arguably the most important phase of the investment funnel because selecting ventures for evaluation is an essential step on the path to equity investment (Bapna, 2017; Bernstein et al., 2017).<sup>10</sup> The campaign webpage includes material that would typically be included in a company’s business plan. Investors consider this information and decide whether to make an equity investment in the venture through EquityPlatform.

In advance of launching a crowdfunding campaign for each venture, EquityPlatform presents a short description of the venture on the “Coming Soon” page of its website and gives individuals the opportunity to sign-up with an email address for early access to invest in the company. Those who sign up for early access are usually given one day to make an equity investment in the company before EquityPlatform opens the investment opportunity to the public. After the 24-hour early access period, an email pitch broadcasting the equity offer is sent to all members except those who already signed up for early access to invest in the company.

Harmony (name disguised) is a venture that raised equity capital through EquityPlatform. This venture was selected for the experiment for the following reasons: First, the founder of the company was a user innovator. Second, the venture was comparable to a broader list of firms participating in equitybased crowdfunding along several observable characteristics such as the number of founders, fundraising goal, and existence of nonfounder employees. In the section titled “External and Internal Validity,” we compare the firm in the experiment to other firms raising equity investments through crowdfunding. This analysis helps establish the representativeness of the firm in the experiment relative to a broader set of firms participating in equity-based crowdfunding and informs the generalizability of our findings.

Randomized Assignment of Independent Variables: User Innovator and Producer Firm

Investors on EquityPlatform were randomly selected to be included in this experiment. The investors in our study did not include individuals who signed up for early access to invest in Harmony or those who were associated (founder, employee, or board member) with either EquityPlatform or Harmony.

The subjects were randomly assigned to four groups: a producer firm group (control group), a user innovator firm group (treatment group), and two groups that were part of a second experiment (described in the section titled “Second Experiment”) that was executed concurrently with the main experiment. The main experiment tests whether investors discriminate against user innovator firms. The second experiment tests whether discrimination against user innovator firms, if it exists, is statistical discrimination.

The email pitch received by all subjects in the treatment and control groups was identical, with the exception that the emails sent to subjects in the treatment groups incorporated short additional text (described later in this section for the main experiment and in the section titled “Second Experiment” for the second experiment).<sup>11</sup> Everything else in the emails except the additional treatment text was kept constant and the emails were sent by EquityPlatform. Such exogenous random assignment of the treatments allows for causal inference (internal validity) of the effect of investor interest in firms founded by user innovators and rules out various alternative explanations because randomly assigning participants to the experimental conditions (treatments and control) “balances out potential nuisance variables that might affect outcomes” (Karahanna et al., 2018, p. iv). In addition, the experiment was conducted unobtrusively, the subjects were not aware that they were part of a study, so actor-observer bias was eliminated. Moreover, since the study was conducted in the subjects’ naturally occurring environment, it provides a high level of realism (Karahanna et al., 2018).

No other emails apart from the email pitch for Harmony were sent by EquityPlatform to the subjects in the study during the period of the experiment. The period of the experiment began when the email announcing Harmony’s equity offer was sent to the subjects and ended when the offer was no longer available for investment. Each subject was assigned to one and only one group; thus, each subject received no more than one email pitch about Harmony. The emails received by the subjects did not contain information regarding any other campaign apart from Harmony. The emails that the treatment and control groups received are described next.

Control email—Producer firm group (referred to as C): The text incorporated in the email sent to the control group was:

Harmony is a boutique manufacturer of world-leading premium <product>.<sup>12</sup>

Critically acclaimed with outstanding international reviews and enjoyed by an enviable list of world respected artists (such as members of U2, Guns N’ Roses, Faith No More, <three other artist’s names>).

Based on the text, readers are likely to assume typical (producer) entrepreneurship, since there is no indication to the contrary (i.e., the pitch does not mention a user innovation or user entrepreneurship).<sup>13</sup> Previous research (Shah & Tripsas, 2007) describes producer entrepreneurship as a “typical” path to entrepreneurship, wherein an individual or firm identifies an opportunity, evaluates its commercial potential (e.g., via market research or through observing the diffusion of user innovators’ products), and proceeds to develop a product with the anticipation of profiting from it by selling it to users (Gambardella et al., 2017).

Treatment 1 email—User innovator firm group (referred to as T1): The text incorporated in the email sent to the treatment group suggests a user innovator firm. In user entrepreneurship, an individual creates a product to satisfy their own needs and then commercializes the product through their own venture. For example, Josephine Cochrane, the inventor and entrepreneur who commercialized the automatic dishwasher, was frustrated with the chipping of her heirloom porcelain through handwashing and noted, “If nobody else is going to invent a dishwashing machine, I’ll do it myself” (USPTO, 2019).

Interest in investing: Following Bernstein et al. ## Estimation Approach

To determine the relationship between the type of firm (user innovator firm versus producer firm) and interest in investing, we used chi-square tests, which were used to examine if there is a relationship between two categorical variables. For robustness, we used t-tests and logistic regression models. Further, we employed two models to establish the association between interest in investing and actual equity investments. First, a logistic regression model was used when the dependent variable was the binary variable invested. Second, an OLS model was used when the dependent variable was amount invested. Because of the skewed nature of the data, we log-transformed amount invested before estimating the model.

## Second Experiment

The results of our main experiment indicate that investors are less interested in user innovator firms relative to producer firms. Our second experiment explores whether the identified discrimination against user entrepreneurs is statistical in nature. We do so by augmenting the information about the producer and user innovator firm (from the main experiment) with information about the firm’s performance. As we note in the section titled “Type of Discrimination,” in situations where there is limited information about performance or quality, statistical discrimination exists when two parties of the same performance or quality are treated differently on the basis of their ascriptive characteristics; the reliance on ascriptive characteristics (e.g., gender, race, type of entrepreneur [user or producer]) is likely to diminish when more concrete signals of quality or performance are available (Guzman & Kacperczyk, 2019; Botelho & Abraham, 2017). Thus, if we find that the differences in interest in investing across the two types of firms diminish when more concrete performance information is available, this suggests that the discrimination is statistical. If the type of discrimination is taste based—where decision makers have an idiosyncratic dislike or prejudice towards a particular group—the inclusion of the performance information should have little effect on the differences in interest in investing across the two types of firms.

The second experiment included two additional treatment conditions for the same venture as our main experiment and was executed at the same time as the main experiment. The two additional treatment conditions used in the second experiment are detailed below.<sup>17</sup> As with the main experiment, the email pitch received by the subjects in the treatment and control groups was identical, with the exception that the emails sent to subjects in the treatment group incorporated additional performance-related text.

## Response to User Innovator Firm with Positive Performance Information

Our second experiment shows that with negative performance information, interest in a producer firm is significantly reduced (Table 3, Test 2), while interest in the user innovator firm is not significantly reduced (Table 3, Test 5). Since we report the results of a field experiment with real investors, the performance signal used in the second experiment corresponded with the actual performance of the focal firm. This implies that our field experiment did not show that investors’ interest in a user innovator firm increases with positive performance information; it is conceivable that interest may not increase when user innovator information is augmented with positive performance information. If this is the case, our findings may not be consistent with statistical discrimination.

To alleviate this concern, we used three independent surveys conducted on AMT to explore investment-related responses to a user innovator firm with positive performance information, relative to a user innovator firm with no performance information (user innovator firm) and a user innovator firm with negative performance information (user innovator firm with negative perf info). Details can be found in Appendix 2, “Examining Responses to User Innovator Firms with Different Types of Performance Information.” We found that respondents were more likely to be interested in investing in a user innovator firm with positive performance information, relative to a user innovator firm without performance information or one with negative performance information. Also, consistent with Test 5 from Table 3 of Experiment 2, we did not observe a statistically significant difference in interest in investing in a user innovator firm with negative performance information relative to one where no performance information is provided. These results suggest that the type of discrimination against user entrepreneurs is indeed statistical as opposed to taste based and that the discrimination can be mitigated through the use of positive performance information.

## External and Internal Validity

The strength of randomized controlled experiments, such as the one in this study, is that they allow for internal validity (causal inference) by removing extraneous factors and the resulting endogeneity (Karahanna et al., 2018; Brewer, 1985). However, the external validity or generalizability of our study, i.e., the extent to which the results of our study can be generalized to other firms, depends on the representativeness of the firm in our study to the broader set of firms raising capital through equity crowdfunding.

Several factors mitigate concerns regarding the generalizability of our study. First, to evaluate the representativeness of the firm in this experiment, we compared it to a sample of 5,538 firms raising equity capital on AngelList, which is one of the top ten crowdfunding sites (Barnett, 2013). The comparison, shown in Table 7, indicates that the firm in this experiment is like this broader list of firms on a wide range of observable characteristics, e.g., the number of founders, fundraising goal, and existence of nonfounder employees. We found that in comparison to the broader AngelList sample, the pre-money valuation is slightly lower for the firm in our experiment but lies within one standard deviation of the broader sample mean. Overall, the differences between the AngelList sample and the firm in our study appear to be statistically and economically small, which helps mitigate concerns regarding the generalizability of the results of this study. Second, an email that is similar in format and style to the one used in the experiment is used to announce every crowdfunding campaign on EquityPlatform. This mitigates concerns that could arise if EquityPlatform were to only announce some campaigns and not others through their campaign emails.

A further concern regarding the generalizability of our study is whether the context of use for the user innovator in our study is different from the context of use for other user innovators. In our study, the user entrepreneur is a musician. Other examples of user entrepreneurs in the consumer products segment include sports enthusiasts (e.g., surfers and sailors) who were responsible for innovations in skateboarding and windsurfing equipment (Shah, 2003); parents, grandparents, and babysitters, who introduced most innovations in the juvenile products industry such as strollers and car seats (Shah and Tripsas, 2007); and a housewife who invented the first automatic dishwasher (Sobey, 2010). It is difficult to envision how investors may perceive competence across users of different types of products; however, it may be reasonable to assume that such perceptions are similar across consumer product categories. Investor perceptions of user entrepreneur competency may be different for professional users such as doctors creating medical devices or corporate users such as electronics firms innovating in the segment of their inputs (Adams et al., 2016). We therefore limit the generalizability of our study’s findings to user innovators of consumer products.

By design (and supported by our manipulation checks shown above), our study has strong internal validity, which is the extent to which the results observed in the study are a result of the treatment rather than some other extraneous factors that might affect the relationship between the cause and effect. While high internal validity, that is, the establishment of causality, is a strength of randomized field experiments such as the one in this study (Karahanna et al., 2018), a potential concern regarding the internal validity of the study is that rather than resulting from the treatment, the results could potentially be affected by prior knowledge about the firm, for example through communications between investors or through media coverage. This concern is mitigated because all subjects, including those with prior knowledge about the firm, are randomly distributed to the treatment and control groups.

## The Relationships between Interest in Investing and Investments and between Treatment and Investments

We provide additional analyses that examine active investors behavior through the investment funnel after they click on “View Offer.”

First, we investigate whether clicks on “View Offer” are associated with equity investments. The lack of an association between clicks on “View Offer” (i.e., interest in investing) and actual investments would be an indication that investors are investing without clicking on the “View Offer” button in the campaign emails. The analysis thus provides evidence that interest in investing is a meaningful variable and a predictor of actual investments. The correlation between interest in investing and investments helps to establish that the path to investing is primarily through the email pitch.

Second, for completeness, we investigate the effect of treatment on investments. Recall from the section titled “Institutional Details” that once an investor clicks on the “View Offer” button in the campaign email, they are taken to the campaign webpage on EquityPlatform’s website. This page contains information that would typically be included in a business plan and includes details about the team members, investment terms, firm performance, etc. The investors likely consider this information before making an investment, and thus the investment decision is no longer based solely on the randomized treatment (Bernstein et al., 2017). As we explain in more detail below, this means that the relationship between the treatment and interest in investing does not necessarily translate into a relationship between the treatment and investments.

## Association between Interest in Investing and Equity Investments.

We employed two dependent variables to establish that interest in investing is associated with actual equity investments. The first, invested, is a binary variable, which is set to 1 if a subject invested in harmony and is set to 0 otherwise. The second, amount invested, is a continuous variable, which is the amount invested by the subject in harmony, logged. The results of the logistic regression models, which are used when the dependent variable is the binary variable invested, are shown in Models 1 and 3 of Table 8. further, the results of the OLS models, which are used when the dependent variable is amount invested, are shown in Models 2 and 4 of Table 8.

For the two dependent variables, we consider two independent variables. The first is a binary independent variable, interest in investing (binary), which is set to 1 if a subject clicks on the “View Offer” button in the email pitch and is set to 0 otherwise (corresponding to Models 1 and 2 of Table 8). The second independent variable, interest in investing (log clicks), is the number of clicks on the “View Offer” button, logged (corresponding to Models 3 and 4 of Table 8).

The four models in Table 8, indicate a strong association between interest in investing and equity investments. For the logistic regression models (Models 1 and 3 in Table 8), we report the independent variable’s marginal effect. For Model 1, where the independent variable is interest in investing (binary), the average marginal effect (AME) indicates that on average, clicking on “View Offer” increases the probability of making an investment from 0.013 to 0.141, a change of 0.128 (p < 0.001) with a z-value of 3.63. The AME for Model 3, where the independent variable is interest in investing (log clicks), indicates that when holding other variables at their observed values, an increase of one standard deviation in the log of interest in investing—about 0.29—increases the probability of investing by 0.030 (p < 0.001). These analyses provide evidence that the path to investing is primarily through the email pitch.<sup>23</sup>

## Effect of Treatment on Investments

Table 9 shows the effect of treatment on investments (the outcome is the binary variable invested). We observed no statistical difference between the groups in terms of the actual investments.<sup>24</sup>

We would like to note that the statistically significant difference between the control and treatment groups in terms of interest in investing (the results of the main experiment in Table 2), the insignificant difference between the groups in terms of invested (Table 9, Test 1), and the significant correlation between interest in investing and invested/amount invested (Table 8) are all consistent. Specifically, there is no significant difference between the treatment (user innovator firm) and control (producer firm) groups with respect to invested because, after clicking on “View Offer” and seeing the campaign webpage, investors in the control group became “treated,” i.e., they saw information that the founder is a user innovator. Both control and treatment groups also saw more information about the focal firm, including performance-related information. Thus, conditional on arriving at the campaign webpage, the final decision to invest was based on the same content irrespective of whether the individual reached the campaign webpage through the user innovator firm email (treatment) or the producer firm email (control) groups.

A smaller number of investors arrived at the campaign webpage through the user innovator firm email relative to those arriving through the producer firm email (result of our main experiment). Investors arriving at the campaign webpage through the user innovator firm email saw negative performance information; this information is consistent with investors’ perceptions of user innovator firms, as we have theorized. Investors arriving through the producer firm email saw information that the founder is a user innovator and that the firm’s performance is negative; this information is not consistent with their expectations based on the content of the email pitch that they were exposed to. As a result, fewer investors from the control group (producer firm email) proceeded to invest (relative to the user innovator group). The difference between the treatment and the control groups with respect to investments is therefore small and not statistically significant. It is useful to emphasize that the actual investments depend not only on the content of the email pitch (treatment condition) but also on the content investors see on the campaign webpage. The information in the email pitch (treatment condition) determines whether the investor clicks on “View Offer” and arrives at the campaign webpage; information on the campaign webpage is what determines whether an investor invests.

It is also useful to note that, in general, there are benefits of increasing the click-through rate and inducing more investors to land on the campaign webpage (even though they do not apply to our specific firm because its performance is negative). For instance, consider investors who see the user innovator firm (treatment) email. The result of our first experiment shows that those who receive this email are less interested in investing (click-through rate is lower) than those who see the producer firm email. Those investors who see the user innovator firm email and do not click opt out of investing by merely seeing that the founder is a user innovator (without knowing the performance of the firm); these are individuals who will likely never invest based on just the information that they see in the email pitch. Investors who do not click on “View Offer” may never see positive performance information (if it is true for the firm) on the campaign webpage. Our second experiment establishes that the type of discrimination against user innovator firms is statistical, which implies that investors are more likely to click through to the campaign webpage of a user innovator firm and potentially invest if they are presented with positive performance information in the email pitch (if it exists for the focal firm) along with information that the founder is a user innovator.

## Follow-Up Survey of Platform Participants

In the section titled “Investor Perceptions of Firms by User Innovators Versus Firms by Producers,” our theoretical arguments, grounded in prior work, suggest that relative to perceptions about firms founded by producers, investors may form different perceptions about firms founded by user innovators with respect to product innovativeness, ability to gauge broader market needs and demand, and skills to operate and grow the company. To explore whether investors make these distinctions, which is informed by prior work, we surveyed members of EquityPlatform.

We asked members: If they were to consider two investment opportunities in comparable ventures founded by either a user innovator or a producer innovator, would they expect that: (1) “the user innovator’s venture has more novel and differentiated products than the producer innovator’s venture,” (2) “the user innovator is better able to gauge market need/demand for their products than the producer innovator,” and (3) “the user innovator has better experience and skills to run and grow the company than the producer innovator.” The questions were answered on 5-point “likely/unlikely” Likert items (5 = extremely likely, 4 = likely, 3 = neutral, 2 = unlikely, 1 = extremely unlikely). The responses to these questions are recorded in the following variables: more novel and differentiated, better gauge market need/demand, and better experience and skills to run and grow the venture.

Members of EquityPlatform were sent a request via an email from the platform to complete the web-based survey. The survey questions (described above) are shown in Exhibit A3 in Appendix 3. These survey questions were embedded in a broader survey sent by EquityPlatform that was used to better understand general investor preferences (e.g., industry and venture lifecycle stage). The survey did not mention or link to the harmony campaign in any way; thus, the survey captures investors’ perceptions of user innovator firms relative to producer firms more broadly and independent of the firm in the experiment.

The survey received 175 responses by active investors (response rate 27.5%). Figure 3 summarizes these responses.<sup>25</sup> In line with our theory, we find that: (1) 55% of investors thought that it was more likely (as compared to 19% who thought it was unlikely) for the user innovator’s venture to have more novel and differentiated products than the producer innovator’s venture (26% of responses were neutral or same), (2) 42% of investors thought that it was more unlikely (as compared to 26% who thought it was likely) that the user innovator is better able to gauge market need/demand for their products than the producer innovator (32% of responses were neutral or same), and (3) 29% of investors thought that it was more unlikely (as compared to 23% who thought it was likely) that the user innovator has better experience and skills to run and grow the company than the producer innovator (48% of responses were neutral or same). These results provide suggestive evidence that active investors perceive the strengths and weaknesses of user entrepreneurs discussed in the prior literature.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Crowdfunding is touted as democratizing access to capital for ideas from nontraditional sources (Kalil and Rand 2016) such as user entrepreneurs (Mollick & Robb, 2016). User entrepreneurs are nontraditional entrepreneurs because their path to entrepreneurship starts with creating a product to satisfy their own unmet needs rather than to profit from selling the product. Further, user entrepreneurs’ prior professional experiences are typically outside of the focal industry of their innovations (Shah & Tripsas, 2007).

Understanding user entrepreneurs’ fundraising performance is a relevant question. While it is often assumed that producer innovation is the dominant mode of innovation (Baldwin & von Hippel, 2011), consumers are a major and increasingly important source of innovation (Gambardella et al., 2017) and user innovators create products in all consumer product fields including clothing, dwellings, sports, and vehicles (Gambardella et al., 2017).

To theorize about investor response to user entrepreneurs, we drew from prior literature on user innovators and argued that investors may view user innovator firms as having strengths and weaknesses relative to more traditional producer firms. The positive perceptions relate to product novelty and the negative perceptions relate to the market size for the firm’s products and the founder’s ability to operate and grow the firm.

If investors’ negative perceptions of user entrepreneur firms outweigh their positive ones, producer firms will be favored relative to user innovator firms. We also argued that if discrimination against user innovator firms exists and is attenuated by a concrete signal of quality, then it suggests that the discrimination is statistical as opposed to taste based. Through two concurrent randomized field experiments implemented in partnership with an equity-crowdfunding platform, we found causal evidence that investors are less interested in investing in user innovator firms than in producer firms and that the discrimination against user innovator firms is statistical.

Our study makes several contributions to research. First, the study contributes to the long line of IS scholarship on crowdfunding. Specifically, we contribute to the emerging stream of crowdfunding literature that focuses on the democratization of access to capital (Kim & Hann, 2019; Bapna & Ganco, 2021; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2017; Greenberg & Mollick, 2017; Johnson et al., 2018). On the one hand, prior research has found that the reward-based and equity-based crowdfunding contexts are promising avenues for women to raise capital (Greenberg & Mollick, 2017; Bapna & Ganco, 2021; Johnson et al., 2018).<sup>26</sup> On the other hand, scholars have questioned the benefits of reward-based crowdfunding for entrepreneurs with low socioeconomic status (Kim & Hann, 2019) and for African American male entrepreneurs (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2017). Our study adds to this literature by showing that, in equity-based crowdfunding, investors favor traditional producer firms relative to user innovator firms. Although accessing capital through crowdfunding is promoted as an avenue for nontraditional user innovators to seek financing (Mollick & Robb, 2016), our results provide evidence that the challenges that user entrepreneurs may face in traditional settings persist in equity crowdfunding; however, our finding that the type of bias against user innovator firms in equity crowdfunding is statistical in nature has implications for strategies to improve fundraising performance.

Second, our study contributes to the user entrepreneurship literature (von Hippel, 2005; Smith & Shah, 2013; Alexy et al. 2020), particularly to the stream that focuses on the performance of user innovator firms. Qualitative studies document that user entrepreneurs face significant difficulties when commercializing their innovations (Baldwin et al., 2006; Shah & Mody, 2014) but systematic causal evidence that examines the performance of user innovator firms is lacking. Our study augments this literature by causally identifying user innovator firms’ fundraising performance in equity crowdfunding.

Our finding that the discrimination against user entrepreneurs in equity-based crowdfunding is statistical in nature implies that the differential treatment of producer vs. user innovator firms can be mitigated with more reliable signals of performance and that investors’ negative perceptions of user innovator firms can potentially be mitigated (cf. Guzman & Kacperczyk, 2019). Entrepreneurs have the choice of what information to include in their email pitches and how to organize and display the information in their campaign webpages, including what information to highlight. User innovator firms with performance that would be perceived as positive by equity investors may benefit by including this information in their email pitches and further highlighting it in their campaign webpage. Prior research (detailed in the theory section), our survey of platform participants, and our AMT survey point to the type of performance information that is likely to alleviate equity investors’ negative perceptions of user entrepreneurs. Specifically, investors associate user innovator firms with novel and unique products but have negative perceptions regarding firm growth, market potential, and founders’ abilities. Thus, user entrepreneurs may benefit from emphasizing (in their pitches and campaign webpages) information that is likely to alleviate these negative perceptions. For example, as we demonstrate in our AMT survey (detailed in the subsection “Response to User Innovator Firm with Positive Performance Information”), user entrepreneur firms could convey that their products appeal to a broad audience, rather than to a niche, exclusive, or small audience. Further, user innovator firms could highlight that they have achieved high growth and or that the firm is on the path to achieving such growth. Signaling growth implicitly signals founder abilities; in the absence of evidence of firm growth, the firm could show that the firm’s leadership team includes individuals who have experience in scaling a company and also indicate that the firm plans to scale its customers and revenue.

A strength of this study is the causal identification of investor interest in firms founded by user innovators. While randomized controlled experiments facilitate causal inference by eliminating extraneous factors and the resulting endogeneity (Brewer, 1985; Karahanna et al., 2018), a frequent limitation of such experiments is that they are based on a single firm (e.g., Bapna, 2017; Bapna & Ganco, 2021; Bapna & Funk, 2021). The comparison of the firm in this study to firms raising capital on AngelList reduces concerns about external validity (see the section titled “External and Internal Validity”), that is, concerns about the representativeness of the firm in this experiment to the broader set of ventures seeking capital through equity crowdfunding. It will be useful for future work, potentially in a laboratory setting, to examine investors’ responses to various possible combinations of founders for example producer firms, user innovator firms, user innovator firms with professional managers who commercialize the product, and producer firms with expert users who design and make the product. Further, since we only focused on one firm in a specific context, it will also be useful for future work to examine if investors’ responses depend on specific industry contexts or various firm-level contingencies. While our results are likely to generalize to similar types of products and firms, the positive perceptions associated with user entrepreneurship may outweigh the negative ones in some contexts and user entrepreneurship may be perceived as a positive signal of performance relative to producer entrepreneurship. Exploring the boundary conditions of our results is thus a fruitful avenue for future work. Another limitation of our study is that we are unable to identify investors’ precise motivations and their perceptions of the firm described in each of the experimental conditions. Large-scale surveys may better gauge the perceptions about user versus producer entrepreneurs than was possible in our post hoc survey that focused on specific dimensions identified in prior work. Finally, future work could examine the performance of user entrepreneurs in other crowdfunding contexts such as debt-based and reward-based crowdfunding. Our results may be driven by the fact that investors in equity crowdfunding are motivated by financia return, which occurs through dividend payments or an exit event such as an acquisition, and thus are looking for high potential ventures. As a result of the differences between equity-based, reward-based, and debt-based crowdfunding contexts with respect to the amount contributed, motivation to invest or contribute, the timing of the return, and the risk involved (Bapna & Ganco, 2021), we believe that our findings in the equity-based context may not be readily applicable to the reward-based or debt-based contexts. For example, in the reward-based context contributors may be willing to chip in \$25 to help a user entrepreneur, or in the debt-based context the founder’s credit rating and their likelihood to repay may be more important to the investor than whether the founder is a user or producer entrepreneur. In general, we hope our study will stimulate more work in this relevant research domain.

## Subjects in the Study

Members of EquityPlatform are individuals who have voluntarily signed up to receive campaign emails from EquityPlatform regarding investment opportunities. After excluding members who signed up for early access (see the section titled “Institutional Details” for details) to invest in Harmony and those who were associated (founder, employee, or board member) with EquityPlatform or Harmony, EquityPlatform had 4,385 members. Of these, 22% (960 individuals) were active and 78% (3,425 individuals) were inactive. Of the 4,385 members, 2,936 (about two-thirds of the members) were randomly selected for our study. Of these, 22% (636 individuals) were active (main analysis), and 78% (2,300 individuals) were inactive. The 1,449 members (randomly selected) who were not included in the study were kept aside for a message from the platform (regarding the Harmony campaign) that was not related to our study. Using t-tests (results available upon request) we confirmed that EquityPlatform members included in our study and EquityPlatform members not included in the study are statistically indistinguishable on observable attributes such as gender and prior investments.

## Examining Responses to User Innovator Firms with Different Types of Performance Information

Using three independent surveys on AMT, we examined investment-related responses to a user innovator firm with positive performance information, relative to a user innovator firm with no performance information (user innovator firm) and a user innovator firm with negativ performance information (user innovator firm with negative perf info). Each survey was sent to 100 respondents.

In the first survey, we provided the text sent to individuals who received the user innovator firm email (corresponding to the treatment email from the main experiment) and asked the respondents the following question:

Assume that you are an investor who has the funds to make an investment in a private company. Based on the information provided above, how likely is it that you will make an investment in Harmony?

Responses to the survey question were recorded in the variable make investment (very unlikely = 1, somewhat unlikely = 2, neutral = 3, somewhat likely = 4, and very likely = 5). The other two surveys asked the same question as the one mentioned above. In one of the surveys, we provided the text sent to the individuals who received the user innovator firm with negative perf info email (corresponding to treatment 3 from the second experiment). As we note earlier, the negative performance information indicates that the focal firm is in a niche market and that the business may be hard to scale. In the last survey (user innovator firm with positive perf info), we replaced the sentence with the negative performance information with the following sentence which suggests positive performance from an investor’s perspective.

The founders <names of two founders> have designed and commercialized over 15 <product> for use by a broad array of musicians ranging from professionals to armatures. Over the last 18 months the company has experienced impressive growth in several global markets.

The information about the products appealing to a broad array of musicians and the firm’s global growth over the last 18 months suggests that the firm is not in a niche market and is growing.

In the Appendix (section titled “Ensuring High-Quality Responses in the AMT Surveys”), we describe how we ensured that we received high-quality responses through AMT. Table A1 reports the responses to the surveys. Table A2 reports the results of Mann-Whitney (twosample Wilcoxon Rank-Sum) tests to compare the groups of respondents.<sup>27</sup> Test 1 in Table A2 indicates that the probability of an observation in the user innovator firm group having an outcome value for make investment that is higher than an observation in the user innovator firm with positive perf info email group is 56% (p-value < 0.1). Additionally, Test 2 in Table A2 indicates that the probability of an observation in the user innovator firm with positive perf info group having an outcome value for make investment that is higher than an observation in the user innovator firm with negative perf info group is also 56% (p-value<0.1). Finally, Test 3 in Table A2 indicates that there is not a statistically significant difference between the user innovator firm group and the user innovator firm with negative perf info group with respect to Make investment (p-value 0.51). The result of Test 3 is consistent with the result of Test 5 from Table 3. These tests indicate that respondents reported that they were more likely to make an investment in a user innovator firm with positive performance information, relative to a user innovator firm without performance information or one with negative performance information.

## Ensuring High-Quality Responses in the AMT Surveys

To ensure that we received high-quality responses to our AMT surveys, we limited participation in the surveys to AMT workers who met the following qualifications: they were located in the United States; their human intelligence task (HIT)<sup>28</sup> approval rate (%) for all requestors HITS was ≥ 98%; their number of HITS approved was greater than or equal to 50; and they had a U.S. bachelor’s degree. <sup>29</sup> Furthermore, in all the AMT surveys, we included a question to evaluate whether the respondents on AMT were carefully reading the prompts rather than randomly selecting answer choices. Subjects were asked to indicate the degree to which they agreed to the following statement: “This is a survey about a company that makes <product>.” Responses were recorded on a 3-point scale and included “Agree,” “Disagree,” and “Neutral.” The results reported in Tables 4, 5 and A2 exclude the responses from the individuals who failed the attention check.
