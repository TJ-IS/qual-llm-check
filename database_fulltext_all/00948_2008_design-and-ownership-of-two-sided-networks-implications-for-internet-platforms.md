# Design and Ownership of Two-Sided Networks: Implications for Internet Platforms

Yanis Bakos and Evan ge los Kats amakas

Yannis Bakos is an Associate Professor of Management at the Leonard N. Stern School of Business at New York University. He holds a Ph.D. in Management from the MIT Sloan School of Management. His research focuses on the economic and business implications of information technology, the Internet, and online media. Dr. Bakos pioneered research on how Internet-based electronic marketplaces will affect pricing and competition and on pricing strategies for information goods.

Evange los Katsamakas is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems at Fordham University School of Business in New York City. He holds an M.Sc. from the London School of Economics and a Ph.D. from the Stern School of Business, New York University. His research focuses on understanding, modeling, and analyzing the economic and business impact of the Internet, open source software, technology platforms, and disruptive innovation. Dr. Katsamakas has published in Management Science and other information system journals and he has presented at major information system conferences such as WISE, HICSS, and INFORMS.

Ab stract: Many Internet intermediaries operate two-sided networks, that is, they provide platforms to bring together two types of participants, or “sides,” such as buyers and sellers. This paper develops a model that characterizes the intermediary’s pricing in two-sided networks, the value created by these networks, and the allocation of that value across the two sides. It extends the two-sided networks literature by endogenizing the level of network effects as the result of relevant investments by the intermediary, which determine the design of the network. It shows that under certain assumptions about the available technologies, the design of the two-sided network is highly asymmetric independent of its ownership structure. The paper provides insight into design strategies for Internet platforms, and it discusses their welfare implications.

Key words and phrase s: intermediation, Internet platforms, network effects, two-sided markets, two-sided networks.

An incre asing numbe r of Inte rne t inte rme diarie s provide platforms that are twosided networks. These networks have two types of participants (“sides”), where each side derives positive externalities from the participation of members on the other side in the network. Examples include marketplaces such as eBay, which bring together buyers and sellers; search platforms such as Google, which connect advertisers and Web users; matching services that match members of different genders such as Yahoo personals; social networking platforms such as Facebook, which link networks of users with the providers of various services and applications; and music platforms such as iTunes, which link producers and consumers of digital media. Technology companies such as Microsoft and Intel have long succeeded with two-sided platform strategies for software applications [15, 21] and intermediaries are increasingly pursuing such strategies on the Internet. For instance, Salesforce.com, a software-as-a-service leader launched a marketplace and delivery infrastructure for on-demand applications in 2007 [29]. Facebook opened its social-networking platform to developers in April 2007; developers can now write applications and create business models based on adding new features and services to the platform [35].

A two-sided Internet platform embodies a design, which defines the architecture of the services offered and the infrastructure that facilitates the interaction between the participating sides, and a set of rules, such as pricing terms and the rights and obligations of the participants [17]. The emerging literature on two-sided networks [37, 41] extends the traditional network economics literature [14, 31], generally focusing on pricing considerations specific to two-sided networks. This paper is motivated by the observation that in addition to pricing considerations, the design of two-sided networks is a key strategic challenge for intermediaries as well. Network effects across sides result in part from corresponding investments by the intermediary, and thus intermediaries need to decide how much to invest in technologies that create value for each side of their network. For instance, a key challenge for search intermediaries such as Google is how much to invest in technologies that create value for advertisers, and how much to invest in technologies that create value for Web users. Similarly, Facebook’s investment in the design of its platform increases the magnitude of network effects its users and its application providers realize from each other. These observations motivate our modeling of endogenous network effects and their strategic implications, offering insights into appropriate design strategies for Internet platform providers.

In this paper, we analyze design strategies for two-sided networks in a model with sides that are heterogeneous in terms of their benefits from the network. In particular, network benefits for each side are endogenous, and they are determined by the investment strategy of the network intermediary. Because Internet networks and platforms employ a variety of ownership types, especially in the business-to-business (B2B) context [30, 36], we examine different network ownership regimes such as independent ownership, ownership by each of the two sides, and a spin-off case. By analyzing the relationship between ownership and design of two-sided networks, the paper extends existing literature on network ownership [46].

Real-world two-sided networks often demonstrate several types of asymmetry. For instance, one side may enjoy higher network effects from the participation of the other side: advertisers on search engines benefit more from the presence of Web users than vice versa. The intermediary may set different participation prices to each side, receiving correspondingly different revenues [2, 37]. For instance, Web users use

Google’s search platform for free, while Google derives its revenue from advertisers. As discussed earlier, the intermediary may make different investments in creating network effects for each side. As a result of these asymmetries, one side of the network may receive a larger fraction of the surplus created by the network. We show that under certain assumptions about the available technology, the network design will be highly asymmetric as the intermediary will focus its investment and pricing on extracting surplus from one side, after making enough investment to ensure the participation of the other side. This result is not affected by the network’s ownership structure, and it offers an endogenous explanation for the prevalence of asymmetric pricing in two-sided networks.

## Theoretical Background

## Two-Sided Networks

Two-sided networks, including two-sided markets and two-sided platforms, are characterized by cross-side network externalities between two types of participants. Because both sides are needed for the network to succeed, intermediaries face a “chicken-and-egg” problem. An emerging literature on two-sided networks focuses on the appropriate pricing to coordinate the two sides, and thus induce participation and maximize the intermediary’s profit. Parker and Van Alstyne [37] study strategic complements and substitutes in the information goods context, and they identify conditions under which subsidization across the two sides is optimal—a side is subsidized when the price it faces is lower than the price it would face in an independent market. Their insight that the intermediary lowers the price for the side that contributes more network externality surplus to the other side also applies to our setting, and we derive more specific conditions for prices and corner solutions.

Rochet and Tirole [40, 41] study competition between card-payment platforms and characterize the structure of fees charged by the intermediary in monopoly and oligopoly settings; in addition, they consider joint undertakings between intermediaries, both for-profit and not-for-profit. They show that the existence of “marquee” buyers, or exclusive/captive buyers, tends to increase the seller price, because it makes the platform more attractive. Armstrong [2] analyzes examples of two-sided market competition and discusses the effects of “multi-homing” (i.e., simultaneous participation in more than one network) by one side. This will tend to increase prices for the multihoming side, and decrease prices for the single-homing side, because intermediaries compete harder to attract the “single-homing” side. Caillaud and Jullien [10, 11] consider networks with homogeneous participants on the two sides and intermediaries that provide matching services. They analyze competition among intermediaries based on both participation and matching fees and show that the best strategy to dominate the market and protect market share requires setting low participation fees, along with the maximal feasible transaction fees. Rochet and Tirole [42] provide a literature survey and a formal definition of two-sided markets. Hagiu [24] examines the effects of price commitment in platform settings where sellers arrive before buyers.

In two papers related to our work, Yoo et al. [45] analyze pricing of independent B2B marketplaces focusing on the impact of heterogeneous participant switching costs and Yoo et al. [46] evaluate welfare implications of different ownership structures and find that ownership by a network side increases participation and welfare. Our base case complements Yoo et al.’s former result by considering sides with heterogeneous network benefits and our analysis confirms their latter result in a different setting while deriving detailed conditions for the possible pricing outcomes, including corner solutions, and showing that in some cases, the socially optimal outcome can be achieved.

Our major contribution is extending the existing literature by modeling endogenous network effects as the result of intermediary investments. Specifically, we characterize the resulting investment and design choices by the intermediary, and show how they affect the resulting two-sided network, frequently promulgating the highly asymmetric outcomes seen in the real world. We also show that asymmetric network design is frequently optimal for the intermediary, a result that holds for all ownership structures that we examine.

## Electronic Intermediation

This paper is also related to the literature on electronic intermediaries and electronic markets. Bakos [4, 5] discusses the impact of electronic markets, including the reduction of search costs and network benefits. Lower buyer search costs in electronic marketplaces promote price competition among sellers [6]. Emerging roles for electronic intermediaries [3, 7] include aggregating and matching buyers and sellers, providing trust [38] and an institutional infrastructure, and providing market information. Wang and Seidmann [43] show that a supplier’s adoption of electronic data interchange (EDI) can generate positive externalities for the buyer and negative externalities for other suppliers. Riggins et al. [39] show that negative externalities in the supplier side may stall adoption of a buyer-initiated interorganizational system (IOS). Dai and Kauffman [12] analyze the choice of a single buyer between using an independent e-market and creating a private network, taking into account factors such as a negative externality in the supplier side and the value of strategic information sharing. Ghose et al. [22] study the impact of Internet referral infomediaries in a supply chain. They show that retailers can use infomediaries as screening devices to identify high-valuation consumers. Bhargava and Choudhary [9] show that an information intermediary with aggregation benefits should offer two levels of service quality. The low-quality level aids in increasing aggregation benefits. In our model, it is optimal for the intermediary to invest in network effects asymmetrically to maximize network benefits for one side. Weber and Zheng [44] show that a search intermediary should base rankings of advertisers on a weighted average of product performance and bid amount. We contribute to this literature by analyzing the intermediary’s investment in cross-side network effects.

## Information Systems Ownership

Another related stream of information systems research is concerned with ownership of IOS. Bakos and Nault [8] adopt the incomplete contracts framework [23, 27] to study how ownership affects investment incentives in electronic networks where many firms have to invest to create value. Han et al. study how IOS ownership affects information exploitation and technology adoption [25] and how the relative importance and the specificity of investments affect the optimal ownership structure [26]. Our approach differs from this literature by focusing on the investment and pricing decisions of the intermediary in a two-sided market. Thus, while we do not consider multiparty investments, we are able to derive detailed results about investments, pricing, and division of surplus under the different ownership structures.

## Base Model

We conside r an inte rme diate d ne twork with two types of participants (“sides”), which, without loss of generality, we call “buyers” and “sellers,” and a monopolist intermediary. We assume an exogenous mass $N _ { b } , N _ { s }$ of potential participants for the buyer and seller side.<sup>1</sup> The buyers and the sellers that participate in the network enjoy network externalities from the participants in the opposite side. This network externality can reflect increased demand or increased variety of service offerings, or the increased probability of finding a satisfactory match among the other side’s participants. Buyers and sellers are heterogeneous with respect to their respective valuation of the network effect.

Buyers and sellers face participation prices $p _ { b }$ and $p _ { s } ,$ which are set by the intermediary. They choose to participate or not to participate in the network based on their respective participation utilities $U _ { \Theta _ { b } } = \Theta _ { b } \alpha n _ { \scriptscriptstyle s } - p _ { \scriptscriptstyle b }$ and $U _ { \boldsymbol { \Theta } _ { s } } = \boldsymbol { \Theta } _ { s } \beta \boldsymbol { n } _ { b } - \boldsymbol { p } _ { s }$ , where $n _ { b } , n _ { s }$ is the measure of buyers and sellers that participate in the network. The parameters a and $\beta$ are positive real numbers and capture the strength of the cross-side network effects for each side of the network. As a increases, buyer-side network effects get stronger; that is, the utility a buyer realizes from a given number of sellers increases correspondingly. Similarly, as $\beta$ increases, seller-side network effects get stronger; that is, the utility a seller realizes from a given number of buyers increases as well.

The linear specification of network externalities that we employ is consistent with previous literature, such as Yoo et al. [45]. While Yoo et al. assume that participants are heterogeneous in their participation costs, we consider participants that are heterogeneous in their network benefits, with corresponding types $\theta _ { b }$ and $\theta _ { s }$ for the buyer and seller side, where $\Theta _ { \boldsymbol { b } } \sim U [ 0 , 1 ]$ and $\theta _ { s } \sim U [ 0 , 1 ]$ . A higher type for a buyer or a seller means that the corresponding buyer or seller realizes a higher value from a network of a given size. This assumption captures the fact that users of two-sided networks typically differ in their valuation of the provided services. In Appendix B, we discuss how our results would be affected by more general network externalities functions.

The above utility functions reflect that there is no participation value to a participant when the size of the other side of the network is zero, consistent with previous research [3, 7]. The qualitative nature of our results would not change if we were to relax this assumption.

It is often the case that participants in two-sided networks experience externalities from other participants in the same side of the network. For instance, users of a platform can generate positive externalities on other users by creating a community that can provide support, collaborate, share information, and attract the development of more applications for the platform, or sellers in a marketplace can create negative externalities for other sellers by competing for buyers [12, 43, 45]. The implications of positive and negative same-side externalities have been studied extensively [14, 31, 32], including in the specific context of two-sided networks [45]. For simplicity and expositional clarity, we follow Parket and Van Alstyne [37] and do not consider same-side externalities; introducing positive or negative same-side externalities in our setting would increase or decrease the platform value for the corresponding side without qualitatively changing the nature of our results, or providing any new insights beyond the existing literature.

In the rest of this section, we analyze equilibrium pricing by the network intermediary and participation in the two-sided network. In the next section, we allow network effects to be determined by the intermediary’s corresponding investments, thus endogenizing the design of the network and the corresponding network effects for each side. The timeline (Figure 1) is as follows: first, the intermediary makes design decisions (if applicable); second, the intermediary makes pricing decisions; and, finally, potential participants for the two sides make participation decisions simultaneously.

Figure 2 depicts the regions identified in the independent intermediary pricing lemma (L1). One implication of this lemma is that a pricing strategy ignoring the interaction between the two sides would lead to a suboptimal pricing strategy for the intermediary. In general, it is not optimal to set the same participation price for both sides of the network, except when the network is perfectly symmetric (i.e., when $N _ { b } = N _ { s }$ and ${ \mathfrak { Q } } = \{ \beta \}$ . Instead, intermediaries should evaluate the potential asymmetries between the two sides of their networks in looking for ways to optimize their pricing strategy [37]. When the cross-side network effect is significantly larger for one side of the network, then an independent intermediary would set a low or zero price for the side with the lower cross-side network effect and would generate its revenues primarily or exclusively from the other side of the network.

The lemma thus captures the fact that optimal pricing in two-sided networks is often asymmetric, by showing the conditions that lead to “corner solutions” in pricing where only one side of the network is charged a positive price. It has been established in the literature that two-sided network models can lead either to interior<sup>3</sup> pricing solutions or corner pricing solutions [11]. In our setting, the pricing outcome depends exclu sively on the relative strength of the cross-side network effects and is independent of the exogenous mass of potential buyers and sellers. This result is used in our analysis of intermediary investments in design in the next section, as it allows us to derive the pricing implications of the intermediary’s investment decisions.

Highly asymmetric pricing is common in Internet-based two-sided markets. For example, Google’s search platform and most of its other services are free to Web users, while advertisers pay for access to that audience. Online stores and individual sellers are charged listing and participation fees for access to Amazon.com’s e-commerce platform and eBay’s marketplace, while buyers do not pay such fees. Merchants pay for access to e-commerce payment platforms, which are free to consumers. Adobe offers its Reader for free to consumers while it charges content providers for Acrobat. As Anderson recently observed [1], due to such cross-subsidies and the zero marginal cost of information processing, one can argue that there is such a thing as a “free lunch” after all: “it could just mean that someone else is picking up the tab” [1, p. 140].

The buyer-owned platform trades off the increased surplus that buyers realize from increased seller participation (which is maximized when there is zero price charged to sellers and thus all sellers participate) against profit from the sellers (which is maximized at positive prices that restrict seller participation). Corresponding results would apply to a seller-owned network.

The participation prices we derive may result in an accounting loss. For instance, in the region with predominant buyer-side network effects (B1), the network obtains no revenue, as the owning side realizes value in the form of surplus derived from its use. While this maximizes the value generated by the network, it necessitates a mechanism to allocate costs to the owning side. Ideally, this allocation should be independent on the participants’ actual use of the network to avoid creating deadweight loss.

Internet intermediaries are often owned by participants representing only a subset of one side; in that case, the resulting equilibrium will be intermediate between what is derived in Lemmas 1 and 2, depending on the specific ownership situation. For instance, if only some of the buyers own the network, they would still charge to the sellers the prices indicated by the buyer-owned intermediary pricing lemma (L2), which may be zero. However, they would charge a positive price to other buyers as they would benefit from the increase in network revenue and would not internalize the resulting deadweight loss. Thus, a network collectively owned by one side, such as Covisint, a buyer-owned network collectively owned by most of the major auto firms in 1999–2004, and WorldWide Retail Exchange, a network collectively owned by major retailers, might not charge usage prices to either buyers or sellers. On the other hand, a network owned by a subset of one side, such as Amazon’s marketplace, a business-to-consumer (B2C) platform owned by a major seller, is likely to charge positive prices to other sellers while it is free for buyers.

In other words, ownership by an independent intermediary is always inferior in terms of total value created. The surplus of each side, and the participation of each side, is larger under both buyer-side or seller-side ownership compared to independent ownership. This happens because ownership by one of the network sides internalizes better the cross-side network effects. Proposition 1(a) is similar to Propositions 1 and 2 in Yoo et al. [46] and complements their analysis by showing that these results hold even in a setting with corner solutions.<sup>4</sup>

Proposition 1(b) shows that when the network asymmetry is large enough and the side with the larger network effect owns the platform, then the socially optimal outcome that maximizes the total surplus is achieved, with full participation of both sides. For instance, if the network is owned by the buyer side and buyers realize strong externalities from the participation of sellers $( \alpha > 2 \beta )$ , buyers will trade off the revenues from a positive price that restricts seller participation against the buyer-surplus from having access to more sellers. The buyers find that it is optimal for them not to restrict seller participation and as both buyers and sellers fully participate in the network, the social optimum is achieved. Because there is no deadweight loss, there is no need for regulation or other types of policy intervention to improve social welfare [19]. Corresponding results apply in the case where the network is owned by the seller side, and sellers realize strong network externalities from the participation of buyers.

Proposition 1(c) shows that when the network is buyer owned, the suppliers’ participation price can be higher than the suppliers’ participation price under independent ownership. When $4 / 5 \leq \alpha / \beta \leq 2$ , the higher participation of the owning side creates sufficiently higher network externalities on the nonowning side so that it enjoys higher participation rates and higher surplus even though it must pay a higher price to participate in the network. Again, corresponding results would apply to a network owned by the sellers.

## Design and Ownership

A key aspe ct of our analy sis of two-side d ne tworks derives from the observation that the design of an Internet platform is important. In real two-sided networks, network effects are not exogenous, and they are significantly affected by design choices and investments made by the intermediary, such as the quality of technology offered to each side, the services offered to each side, and the mechanism and the rules of interaction between the two sides. In this section, we incorporate the impact of design decisions by the intermediary by endogenizing the network effect parameters α and β as design parameters whose actual value is determined by the relevant investments made by the intermediary. To our knowledge, this is a novel approach in the networks literature, which typically assumes that network effects are exogenous.

Our analysis captures cases where the value of the investment on one side of the network is conditional on the participation of the other side, and where this value scales with increased participation of the other side. Examples would include investment on one side of the network that improves the probability or the quality of a match from the other side of the network, investment that increases the variety of relevant participants on the other side of the network, and investment that improves the use of offerings from participants on the other side of a platform. For instance, Google has invested heavily in improving the targeted audience delivered to advertisers; the value of that investment is conditional on participation by Web users on the search side of the Google platform and scales with increased use of this platform.

Design decisions by the intermediary constitute the first stage of the game (Figure 1). The intermediary first designs the network by determining the network effects parameters α and β , then decides the appropriate pricing, and then the two sides make their participation decisions. We are looking for a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium in network design and pricing.

We consider design technologies with diminishing returns to the level of investment. We begin by assuming the equivalent of quadratic costs of generating networks effects, and specifically that $\alpha ( x ) = r x ^ { 1 / 2 } , \beta ( y ) = r y ^ { 1 / 2 }$ , where $x , y$ are the investments required to create network effects $\mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \alpha } \mathbf { \beta } \mathrm { , }$ , and r is a scale coefficient. When choosing its investment in creating network effects for each side $( \alpha , \beta )$ , the intermediary anticipates the pricing equilibrium in the second stage as specified in the independent intermediary pricing lemma (L1) and Table 1. Comparing the potential profits from choosing a design in the regions with predominant buyer-side network effects (E1), comparable network effects for both sides (E2), and predominant seller-side network effects (E3), the intermediary finds that profits are maximized by avoiding E2 and designing the network in the regions with predominant buyer-side or seller-side network effects (E1 or E3). In these regions, the intermediary’s profits are maximized by investing in network externalities for only one of the two sides, as incremental investments in the other side bring no additional profits to the intermediary. For instance, for network designs in the region with predominant buyer-side network effects (E1), an incremental investment in $\beta$ increases seller surplus but does not bring any additional revenue to the intermediary, as it does not affect the equilibrium prices and participation in the second stage of the game. As a result, the intermediary finds that profits are maximized by the highly asymmetric network design.

The key implication of our analysis for intermediaries is that they should concentrate investment in cross-side network effects on one side of the network, subject to the need to make sufficient investment to attract the participation of the other side. For instance, a recent Harvard Business School case discussing the PayPal payment platform asks: “How should investments be allocated to promoting growth on the two sides of PayPal’s network, that is consumers versus merchants?” [16, p. 12], and notes that eBay (PayPal’s owner) emphasizes investment on the merchant side, as PayPal collects fees only from merchants. Similarly, our analysis suggests that Google should invest in search just enough to satisfy the participation constraints of its Web users, and concentrate the majority of its investment on tools such as analytics that create value for the advertiser side.

The asymmetric design of the independent network design lemma (L3) implies that pricing will be asymmetric, too. As we discussed earlier, this is true for many Internet intermediaries and platforms, such as Google, eBay, and Amazon’s marketplace, and our analysis provides a possible endogenous explanation for this observation. Another implication is that the intermediary’s investment skews the allocation of surplus toward one side, as in the case of buyer auctions in B2B commerce [30, 36]. As Kaplan and Sawhney point out, “When they favor sellers, biased e-hubs act as forward aggregators that amass supply and operate downstream in a supply chain or as forward auctioneers that host auctions for buyers. . . . Biased e-hubs that favor buyers act as either reverse aggregators or reverse auctioneers” [30, p. 102].

Another implication is that an independent intermediary would be indifferent between investing in the buyer side or the seller side of the network. If the two sides differ in the number of participants, the intermediary will either invest in creating cross-side network effects for the larger side, charging that side a relatively small participation price, or will invest in creating strong network effects for the smaller side, charging that side a relatively large participation price. The intermediary’s profit is identical in both cases, and the side that does not get an investment in network effects will get free participation in the network (i.e., it will face a zero participation price). The above analysis assumes identical design technologies for both sides; in Appendix B, we show that when this assumption does not hold, the intermediary concentrates its investment on the side with the most productive design technology in the sense that a given investment will produce the largest network effects.

The independent network design lemma (L3) assumes that both sides in the network have zero exogenous participation costs, and thus can be induced to participate with minimal investment by the intermediary if they get free access to the network. In consumer markets, exogenous participation costs are often low, such as the cost of using Google’s search platform. In B2B markets, however, exogenous participation costs may be substantial, such as the cost of linking the internal systems of a buyer or seller with the marketplace platform. Thus, in real two-sided networks, even with the network made available for free to one side, the intermediary may need to invest in providing a significant level of value for that side in order to ensure its participation. Such participation constraints would reduce the asymmetry of investment between the two sides of the network, and the same outcome can result from other factors, including investment spillovers (analyzed below), strongly convex design costs or inherent network effects (analyzed in Appendix B), or factors outside our model, such as an alternative network offered by a competing intermediary.

The network is designed in such a way that the resulting equilibrium is within the region with predominant buyer-side network effects (B1) of the buyer-owned intermediary pricing lemma (L2), as shown in Table 2. Participation prices are set to zero for both the buyers and the sellers, resulting in zero revenues from the network. The buyer side benefits from the network by enjoying the network effects from the participating sellers through its participation in the network. The investment by the buyer-side-owned intermediary and the resulting total surplus is higher than if the intermediary was independent. A corresponding symmetric analysis and lemma can be formulated for seller-owned networks.

The insight for buyer-owned intermediaries is to concentrate investment in cross-side network effects on the buyer side of the network, subject to the need to make sufficient investment to attract the participation of the seller side. Similar to the independent intermediary case, the asymmetry of the design would be reduced by seller participation costs or available alternatives, strongly convex design costs, inherent network effects, or investment spillovers.

The buyer-owned network design lemma (L4) may seem counterintuitive because the intermediary has no revenues and thus cannot recoup its operating costs. In our setting, marginal operating costs are zero, and positive fixed operating costs can be absorbed by the buyer side or funded through side payments to the intermediary without changing the resulting equilibrium. While it may be difficult to find real-world examples of such collective ownership, there are several Internet platforms similar to the outcome of this lemma. One example is the RosettaNet Web-based standards platform that enables B2B trading by specifying business process definitions and technical elements for interoperability. These standards are created by the RosettaNet consortium and shared freely (royalty free) among firms to maximize network benefits. Its operating costs are recouped from firms willing to become consortium members to influence the design of the RosettaNet standards.

Another example may be offered by private B2B supplier networks (e.g., the ones offered by Wal-Mart or Cisco Systems) in which participation prices are zero, the suppliers’ network benefits typically are just enough to induce participation, and the owner firm designs the network to maximize its buyer-side network benefits.

Buyer-side ownership of the network affects the design only quantitatively, not qualitatively, compared to independent ownership. In particular, the design under buyer-side ownership and the design under independent ownership are both highly asymmetric; only the magnitude of investment differs. Thus, the ownership structure matters only to a degree: affects total surplus, prices, and participation, but it still results in a highly asymmetric network.

## Buyer-Designed Network Spin-Off

We consider a variation of the previous setting where the buyer side designs the network incurring the corresponding design costs, but subsequently the network is spun off, and the intermediary sets prices as an independent firm to maximize network revenues and does not internalize the impact on the side that designed the network. The buyer-side-owned intermediary invests in the first stage to maximize anticipated revenues from pricing plus buyer surplus. An interpretation of that case is that buyers may retain their shareholdings in the intermediary as long as they cannot influence its prices.

Spinning off a network and letting it price independently is never optimal for the owner side within the stylized static setting of our model. However, spinning off the network might be a way for the owning side to induce higher trust in the network and higher participation of the other side; in the case of a network owned by a subset of firms in one side of the network, spinning off the network may alleviate any concerns about bias favoring the owning firms. Such actions may be necessary to sustain growth of the network or to compete against other networks. Participants in two-sided markets often resist networks owned and operated by the other side of the market, and firms often resist participating in networks owned by their competitors. As a result, intermediaries owned and sponsored by one side of the market are often spun off to operate as independent firms beyond the control of the sponsoring side, as was the case, for instance, with Sabre in the airline reservations market and Covisint in the automobile supplies market. Networks may be spun off when the value of such gains outweighs the corresponding reduction in profits. Furthermore, spin-offs may be the result of regulatory restrictions, which may either require a spin-off, or may give the spin-off significantly higher operating flexibility, thus increasing its value. Consistent with our analysis, such spin-offs typically take place after the fundamental design decisions for the network have been made.

## Discussion

Eise nmann e t al. [17] sugge st that platform provide rs must address three strategic challenges: get the pricing right, cope with winner-take-all competition, and avoid envelopment by an overlapping platform. For instance, platform providers should subsidize quality-sensitive and price-sensitive users, and secure exclusive participation by “marquee” users. They should evaluate whether their market is better served by a single intermediary, as in cases where multi-homing costs are high and network effects are strong. Platform providers should also be vigilant for the threat of platform envelopment by an adjacent platform due to convergence [18].

This paper adds a fourth strategic challenge to this list: platform providers must also address the strategic challenge of network design. Specifically, intermediaries are able to impact the level and asymmetry of network effects through their investments. The design of a two-sided network (as defined in this paper) determines the value of the network to potential participants in each side of the network, and thus constrains the intermediary’s feasible pricing strategies. Appropriate network design may be more important for the intermediary than correct pricing, as design involves a longer-term and less flexible decision.

Our analysis provides certain guidelines for the design of two-sided networks and suggests that an intermediary designing a two-sided network should concentrate its investment on one side subject to participation constraints for the other side. The intermediary should then set the corresponding optimal two-sided pricing strategy, which will typically derive the bulk of revenues from the side where the intermediary made the majority of its investment in creating network effects. Asymmetries in pricing and investment are driven by the trade-off the intermediary faces between maximizing revenues from one or both sides of the network. The “corner solutions” that we derive where all investment is concentrated on one side of the network result from simplifying modeling assumptions that are not likely to be fully representative of real-world settings, and we are not suggesting that we should frequently see realworld outcomes where one side of the network receives no value.

In most realistic scenarios, participants will need to receive a positive value to participate in the network; for instance, because they will need to offset positive participation costs or because they can pursue other options that offer them positive value. In these cases, the intermediary will need to invest enough in the “least favored” side to attract its participation, or participation may be induced by factors such as same-side externalities or spillovers from the intermediary’s investments in the other side of the network. However, under a broad range of reasonable assumptions about the economics of available network technologies, the intermediary has a strong incentive to focus its investment on one of the two sides of the network and provide the other side with just enough benefit that would ensure its participation, thus creating a highly asymmetric network. This result is robust under different sets of assumptions and provides an endogenous explanation for a key puzzle identified in the two-sided networks literature—namely, why two-sided networks are often asymmetric [2, 37].

This asymmetric outcome is caused by the fact that profit-maximizing behavior by the intermediary requires pricing above marginal cost, which in turn will restrict network participation by pricing out some potential participants. Our analysis shows that the most profitable strategy for the intermediary is to make the minimum investment required to ensure full participation of one side of the network, and then focus its investment on the side of the network that will generate the intermediary’s revenues; splitting investments more evenly and trying to generate revenues from both sides of the network is less profitable because it prices out more potential participants. In other words, in cases where most of the value is generated from cross-side externalities, intermediaries should invest in one side to maximize participation, and invest in the other side to maximize revenues. Several factors, however, may reduce this design asymmetry in the real world, including positive or heterogeneous participation costs, competition, threat of entry, same-side network effects, strong cross-side inherent network effects that do not depend on the intermediary’s investments, and cross-side spillover effects for the design technology.

An active policy debate on B2B marketplaces [34] is concerned with issues of “fairness” and “bias” arising from intermediaries’ ownership by one side of the market. For instance, a report from a 2000 Federal Trade Commission workshop on B2B marketplaces notes that “several panelists strongly endorsed this third-party model as being essential to providing a fair and neutral marketplace. . . . Other workshop participants rejected that premise insisting instead that any marketplace has the incentive to act fairly and neutrally” [19, p. 12, emphasis added]. Our analysis suggests that the above policy arguments are incomplete. A marketplace owned by a single side is indeed likely to be designed so that the majority of its benefits are captured by the owning side. However, an independent intermediary is not likely to lead to a “neutral” marketplace as, in general, the design of an independent intermediary is also asymmetric. For instance, FreeMarkets, an independent industrial procurement marketplace, followed a strategy to invest in creating marketplaces beneficial to big buyers, who also provide the bulk of its revenues. Thus, independent ownership of a two-sided network will affect the level of investment in each of the sides of the network, but in general, it will not lead to a symmetric or “unbiased” platform. Moreover, as Proposition 1(b) shows, when the asymmetry of the network effects is strong enough, ownership by the side with the strongest network effect leads to a socially optimal outcome, confirming in a different setting the result by Yoo et al. [46] that buyer- or seller-owned B2B marketplaces provide larger total surplus than independent marketplaces.

Our analysis offers a possible design-related explanation for the failure of many independent Internet intermediaries during the dot-com era, especially in the B2B context [13, 33, 36]. Many of these intermediaries tried to establish marketplaces with a “neutral” design [30] and often succumbed to competition from networks and platforms owned by either the buyer side or the seller side.

Independent intermediaries have a strong presence in many types of Internet networks and platforms, however, such as search, portals, and social networks, to name a few. One possible reason why side ownership is not predominant in these markets may be that coordination inefficiencies are degrading the superiority of side ownership compared to independent ownership. Our assumption that ownership by one side would maximize the total surplus of that side would not hold if the participating owners cannot coordinate to reach the optimal design and pricing decisions for that side. Hart and Moore [28] analyze similar decision-making aspects of cooperative governance compared to independent ownership, showing that in several settings, coordination inefficiencies will prevent cooperative governance from achieving its theoretical superiority.

## Concluding Remarks

Many Inte rne t inte rme diarie s ope rate two-side d ne tworks, providing platforms that bring together two or more sides of network participants. For instance, Google connects users and advertisers and Amazon links consumers and merchants. The success of these intermediaries depends on adopting appropriate pricing and design strategies. In this paper, we studied the intermediary’s investment in creating cross-side network effects for the two sides of a two-sided network and showed that the optimal design is often asymmetric. We also analyzed the relationship between intermediary ownership and design in such a network and showed that ownership by one of the sides is often optimal in terms of maximizing profits and total surplus, compared to ownership by an independent intermediary. Our analysis should provide Internet intermediaries with useful insights in their efforts to optimize their profitability and growth.

Future research needs to address the effect of competition among intermediaries on the design of two-sided networks. Furthermore, many open questions for empirical research remain, including the estimation of the inherent and the endogenous network effects [20], and the evaluation of the asymmetries in pricing, revenues, investment, and allocation of surplus for real-world networks. Pricing and revenue asymmetries are relatively easy to identify. On the other hand, asymmetries involving investment and allocation of surplus are more difficult to measure, and thus we do not know how frequently they occur. Investment asymmetries are particularly interesting from an empirical standpoint, as they can cause or exacerbate all the other types of asymmetries. Finally, future research needs to characterize the various network technologies available to Internet intermediaries, which is a prerequisite for a better understanding of design choices. This type of research would help us classify the various types of business models for Internet platforms and better explain their relative success and failure.

\left(R _ {3} + B S _ {3}\right) ^ {*} = \left(R _ {2} + B S _ {2}\right) ^ {*} = \frac {9 r ^ {2} N _ {b} ^ {2} N _ {s} ^ {2}}{3 2 0} <   \frac {9 r ^ {2} N _ {b} ^ {2} N _ {s} ^ {2}}{2 5 6} = \left(R _ {1} + B S _ {1}\right) ^ {*}
$$

and the equilibrium follows.

## Proof of Proposition 3 (Investment Spillover)
