## a b s t r a c t

A burn injury is a disastrous trauma and can have wide-ranging impacts on burn patients, their families, and society. Burn patients generally experience long hospital stays, and the accurate prediction of the length of those stays has strong implications for healthcare resource management and service delivery. In addition to prediction accuracy, the timing of length of hospital stay (LOS) predictions is also relevant, because LOS predictions during earlier clinical stages (e.g., admission) can provide an important component for service and resource planning as well as patient and family counseling, whereas LOS predictions at later clinical stages (e.g., post-treatment) can support resource utilization reviews and cost controls. This study evaluates the effectiveness of LOS predictions for burn patients during three different clinical stages: admission, acute, and post-treatment. In addition, we compare the prediction effectiveness of two arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence (AI)-based prediction techniques (i.e., model-tree-based regression and support vector machine regression), using linear regression analysis as our performance benchmark. On the basis of 1080 burn cases collected in Taiwan, the empirical evaluation suggests that the accuracy of LOS predictions at the acute stage does not improve compared with those during the admission stage, but LOS predictions at the post-treatment stage are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more accurate. Moreover, the AI-based prediction techniques, especially support vector machine regression, appear more effective than the regression technique for LOS predictions for burn patients across stages.

## 1. Introduction

The healthcare industry faces constant pressures to lower costs by improving the utilization of resources while maintaining and even enhancing the quality of service. Effective healthcare resource management is especially indispensable for addressing these seemingly contradictory pressures. Of particular importance are hospital beds, scarce and critical healthcare resources, whose effective management can bene<sup>fi</sup>t healthcare service planning, medical resource allocations, and clinical governance and audits [21,33]. Toward this end, predictions of an inpatient's length of stay (LOS) represents an essential issue that can be supported by statistical methods or proliferating arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence (AI)-based prediction techniques. Prior studies have paid great attention on LOS predictions in various specialty areas, ranging from general medical divisions (including general surgery [26], internal medicine [26], trauma [8], psychiatric [21,31], intensive care [55], and long-term care [57]) to speci<sup>fi</sup>c medical diseases/treatments (e.g., transurethral resection of the prostate [1], stroke [6], chronic obstructive pulmonary concerns [23], cardiac surgery [51], single lung transplantation [29], and burns [2,20,54]).

In particular, LOS prediction is crucial for diseases or injuries that necessitate long treatments or involve scarce patient care resource. A good case in point is burn injuries, a disastrous trauma that can have wide-ranging complications that make such cases dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to manage and treat [12]. According to the study by Herndon and Spies [18], nearly 2 million people suffer burns every year in the United States, and approximately 100,000 of them are moderate to severe, requiring hospitalization [18]. Burn patients generally require long LOS for treatment. As the annual health statistics released by Department of Health, Taiwan, reveal, the mean LOS for burn patients was 11.43 days in 2004, longer than the average across all hospital beds (i.e., 9.96 days) [11]. Previous epidemiological studies of burn injuries also show longer LOS for burn patients than for others. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, reported average LOS of burn patients includes 20.4, 18, 16.7, 15.5, and 14.9 days, according to Burton et al. [4], Chien et al. [7], Lari et al. [28], da Silva et al. [45], and Lv et al. [33], respectively. However, hospital resources for burn patients are scarce. For example, only 0.17 beds, which account for only 0.29% of all hospital beds, are available for burn patients per 10,000 persons in Taiwan [11]. To facilitate the effective planning and management of such scarce resources and provide better patient care, it is essential to develop an effective LOS prediction mechanism for burn patients.

Accuracy of LOS predictions, the focus of most prior studies, certainly is important and has strong implications for healthcare resource management and service delivery. For instance, analyses of deviations (i.e., between actual and predicted) in LOS can facilitate healthcare service and resource planning [21], quality assurance [26,38], resource utilization reviews [1,38,55], and cost controls [6,8,44]. However, the timing of LOS predictions also is relevant, because LOS predictions during earlier clinical stages (e.g., admission) can provide an important component for service and resource planning as well as patient and family counseling, whereas later predictions (e.g., post-treatment) can support resource utilization reviews and cost controls.

Prior studies of LOS predictions for burn patients mainly deal with predictions at the earliest clinical stage (i.e., admission) [2,20,33,43,54]. Common variables used for LOS prediction include demographics, existing medical conditions, and injury characteristics of burn patients. Because the adequacy of treatments generally affects the actual LOS of burn patients, the inclusion of such treatment variables should improve prediction accuracy (i.e., LOS predictions made during later clinical stages should be more accurate than those during the admission stage). Evidently, the timing and accuracy of LOS predictions are inevitable trade-offs. As mentioned, prior studies on LOS predictions for burn patients mainly focus on the admission stage. From a decision support perspective for healthcare resource management and service delivery, it is both desirable and essential to investigate differentials in prediction accuracy across various clinical stages. Moreover, prior studies primarily adopt a linear regression analysis to predict LOS for burn patients [2,20,33,43,54], whereas alternative prediction methods that use AI-based techniques have received little attention. From the perspective of both research and clinical practice, it is also important to investigate other promising techniques and compare their effectiveness with regard to LOS predictions for burn injuries.

In this study, we consider three different clinical stages— admission, acute, and post-treatment—and apply promising AIbased prediction techniques to construct prediction models for each clinical stage. In particular, we employ the model-tree-based regression (namely, M5) [40,53] and support vector machine (SVM) regression (or called support vector regression) [46,52] as alternative methods to develop LOS prediction models for burn patients and adopt linear regression analysis as our performance benchmark. We empirically evaluate the effectiveness of each prediction model using 1080 burn cases collected from a regional burn center in southern Taiwan from July 1997 to September 2002.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: in Section 2, we review literature related to burn patient care and management and prior studies on LOS predictions for burn patients. Section 3 details our LOS prediction models during three different clinical stages and highlights the particular AI-based prediction techniques (i.e., M5 and SVM regression) we employ to construct the target LOS prediction models. In Section 4, we describe our empirical evaluation, including data collection, evaluation procedure, and criteria, and then discuss important evaluation results. We conclude in Section 5 with a summary and discussion of some future research directions.

## 2. Literature review

In this section, we review literature related to burn patient care and management. We <sup>fi</sup>rst provide a summary of factors that may affect the LOS and mortality of burn patients, followed by a review of prior studies on LOS predictions for burn patients.

## 2.1. Factors affecting LOS and mortality of burn patients

Prior studies on burn patient care and management pay great attention on the factors that affect the LOS and mortality of burn patients. A review of these relevant studies suggests four categories of factors: patient demographics, existing medical conditions, burn injury characteristics, and treatment factors.

Common demographics relevant to the LOS or mortality of burn patients include age [2,4,12,15,19,20,25,32,35,42,43,47,54] and sex [2,4,12,19,20,32,35,43,54]. In addition, some studies consider the race of burn patients [35,54]. Existing medical conditions also may have profound effects on the LOS and mortality of burn patients. For instance, according to a study of 31,338 patients, burn patients with different preexisting medical conditions (e.g., cardiac arrhythmias, congestive heart failure, diabetes, and hypertension) have signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly longer LOS [49]. McCampbell et al.'s study of 191 patients shows that burn patients with diabetes have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher rate of infection and thus longer LOS [34]. Another study of 2477 acute burn admissions indicates that nearly 50% of burn patients with prior cardiac history manifest cardiac dysfunction (most frequently, arrhythmia), but only 6% of burn patients without a prior cardiac history incur cardiac dysfunction [14].

Burn injury characteristics represent a major category of factors affecting the LOS and mortality of burn patients and usually become the most important factors governing burn patient care. Commonly investigated burn injury characteristics include the extent of the burn (usually measured by total body surface area [TBSA]) [2,12,15,19,20,25,32,33,35,42,43,47,54], the presence or absence of an inhalation injury [2,12,19,20,32,35,43,47,54], etiology of burns (generally classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as naked <sup>fl</sup>ame, scald, explosive, chemical, electrical, vehicle-related, or other) [2,19,20,43,54], and the degree of burns [2,4,33,54]. Moreover, hematology and biochemistry lab values (e.g., serum osmolarity, platelet count, creatinine) [12,47], <sup>fi</sup>rst-aid treatment [10], place of burn injury [54], lapse time (i.e., interval between burn injury and admission to hospital) [12,54], occurrence of clothing ignition [2], and date of admission (month and season; burn injuries are more common and often more extensive during cold seasons) [4,12] effects also have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

Advances in the treatment of burn patients have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly decreased the mortality rate and improved outcomes for burn patients [15,43,47]. Treatment factors thus include clinical variables pertaining to the course of treatment given to a burn patient during hospitalization. Those common treatment factors relevant to the LOS and mortality of burn patients include the need for surgical operation [33,43], the interval between admission and the <sup>fi</sup>rst surgical operation [19], and the type [12,19] and number [12] of surgical operations. Escharotomy, wound excision, and skin grafting represent the three major types of surgical operations for burn patient care, and several empirical studies show that early wound excision and skin grafting generally result in a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly shorter LOS [3,5,48]. Other treatment factors include time to intravenous access [47], complications (e.g., development of sepsis or organ failure) [19,47], and ventilator dependency [47]. Moreover, the routine use of antibiotics for prophylaxis plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the control of burn wound infection, which in turn has an immense in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the LOS [30,37].

## 2.2. Prior studies on LOS predictions for burn patients

Prior studies on burn patient care and management mainly concentrate on predicting the mortality of burn patients [2,4,12,15,19,20,25,32,42,43,47,54]; only a few focus on LOS predictions [2,20,33,43,54]. Wong and Ngim [54] develop a statistical model for LOS prediction using data about 336 burn patients collected from an Asian National Burn Center in Singapore. As Table 1 shows, 12 independent variables appear in their prediction model: age, age<sup>2</sup>, sex, race, etiology of burns, place of injury, presence or absence of inhalation injury, TBSA, TBSA<sup>2</sup>, full thickness TBSA (i.e., a measure of the degree and depth of burns), full thickness TBSA<sup>2</sup>, and interval between injury and admission. They use linear regression analysis to construct their LOS prediction model, and the resultant regression model, with a predictive power of $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 5 7$ , suggests that only TBSA, full thickness TBSA, age, and the presence or absence of inhalation injury are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors.

The regression analysis developed by Ryan et al. On the basis of data about 1598 patients with acute burn injuries admitted to Massachusetts General Hospital and the Shriners Burns Institute in Boston, their results show that these variables account for 63% of the variance, and TBSA contributes most of the variance. A study by Attia et al. [2] employ 357 cases of burn patients admitted to an Egyptian burn center to construct a linear regression model for LOS prediction, using age, sex, agent and intent of burn injury, occurrence of clothing ignition, TBSA, degree of burns, depth of burns, and presence or absence of inhalation injury as independent variables. Their resultant prediction model suggests six signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors for LOS: TBSA, degree of burns, depth of burns, occurrence of clothing ignition, sex, and presence or absence of inhalation injury. This model explains 44.5% of the variability in the dependent variable (i.e., LOS).

Ho et al. [20] develop a linear regression model using 286 severely burned patients admitted to the burn unit of Prince of Wales Hospital in Hong Kong. Eight variables, namely, age, age groups, sex, mechanism of burns, caustic agent, presence or absence of inhalation injury, TBSA, and TBSA groups, along with the squared form of some variables (e.g., TBSA<sup>2</sup>), are used to construct their LOS prediction model. Among these variables, sex, presence or absence of inhalation injury, TBSA, and $\mathrm { T B S } A ^ { 2 }$ emerge as signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors of LOS $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 )$ . Finally, Lv et al. [33] also develop a linear regression model for LOS prediction for burn patients, using blood transfusion, depth of burn, TBSA, and pattern of treatment (i.e., conservative or surgical) as the independent variables. On the basis of 113 cases of paediatric scalds admitted to the burn center of Shanghai Hospital, their resultant regression model suggests all independent variables signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors and reaches a predictive power of $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 3 4 3$

As Table 1 shows, most of the independent variables employed by prior studies to predict the LOS of burn patients fall under the categories of either patient demographics or burn injury characteristics. In most cases, the values of these independent variables are available right after burn patients get admitted to hospitals and the extent and depth of their burn injury has been assessed clinically. Thus, because most of prior studies do not consider treatment variables, these studies refer only to LOS predictions at the admission stage for burn patients. To better support the various roles of LOS prediction in healthcare resource management and service delivery, it is essential to examine the effects of prediction timing on prediction accuracy of LOS for burn patients (i.e., investigate prediction accuracy during various clinical stages).

With respect to the prediction technique employed, all of the prior studies adopt a linear regression analysis. In general, linear regression may not have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fl</sup>exibility or robustness across different applications because it explicitly enforces linear relationships among independent variables and requires strict conformance to speci<sup>fi</sup>c and often rigid assumptions, such as the independent and identical distributions of residuals. In contrast, AI-based prediction techniques take the computational approach to capture important relationships (possibly nonlinear) between a set of independent variables and a dependent variable and generally do not involve strict statistical assumptions. Prior studies have shown that AI-based prediction techniques achieve better performance than linear regression analysis [9,22]. Hence, for decision support purposes, it would be desirable to empirically compare the effectiveness of alternative methods that use promising AI-based prediction techniques with that of linear regression analysis. Such comparative evaluations are particularly important for burn injuries that require long LOS and involve scarce patient care resources, because the analysis results might generate valuable insights into the relative advantages or constraints of various prediction methods and, at the same time, better support healthcare resource management and service delivery decisions pertaining to burn patient care.

## 3. LOS prediction models for burn patients

In this study, we propose LOS prediction models for three clinical stages, namely, admission, acute, and post-treatment. Moreover, we adopt M5 and SVM regression to construct LOS prediction models at each clinical stage. In the following subsections, we <sup>fi</sup>rst de<sup>fi</sup>ne the three clinical stages and describe, for each clinical stage, the independent variables we employ for LOS prediction. Subsequently, we highlight the particular AI-based prediction techniques we select to construct the LOS prediction models for burn patients, that is, M5 and SVM regression.

## 3.1. Clinical stages and independent variables for LOS prediction

We intend to evaluate three clinical stages, admission, acute, and post-treatment, as possible LOS prediction timing for burn patients. The admission stage refers to the point at which a burn patient is admitted into a burn care unit and the extent and depth of his or her burn injury gets assessed clinically, before any treatments are performed. Therefore, the independent variables for LOS prediction available in this stage consist only of patient demographics, existing medical conditions, and burn injury characteristics. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we include the sex and age of the burn patient as independent variables from the patient demographics category. For the existing medical condition category, on the basis of our literature review and discussions with several physicians working in the burn center from which we collect cases for our evaluation (described in the Data collection section), we consider whether the target burn patient has a history of cardiac disease, hypertension, diabetes, or respiratory tract disease. The independent variables associated with burn injury characteristics involve the extent of the burn (measured by TBSA), etiology of burns (naked <sup>fl</sup>ame, scald, explosive, chemical, electrical, vehicle-related, or other), inhalation injury (presence or absence), <sup>fi</sup>rst-aid before admission (i.e., minutes of <sup>fl</sup>ushing cool water before admission), degree of the burn wound, and sites of burn. Because burns may occur in multiple sites, we follow ICD-9-CM (International Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of Diseases, 9th Revision, Clinical Modi<sup>fi</sup>cation) [13] to encode the degree of the burn wound and sites of burn using the following seven variables: degree of burn of face, head, and neck (ICD-9-CM 941), degree of burn of trunk and chest wall (ICD-9-CM 942), degree of burn of upper limb except wrist and hand (ICD-9-CM 943), degree of burn of wrist and hand (ICD-9-CM 944), degree of burn of lower limb (ICD-9-CM 945), degree of burns of multiple speci<sup>fi</sup>ed sites (ICD-9-CM 946), and presence of burn of internal organs (ICD-9-CM 947). As a result, the admission stage employs 17 independent variables for LOS prediction.

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne the acute stage as encompassing the <sup>fi</sup>rst 72 hours after a burn patient is admitted into a burn care unit. Early excision of burns generally occurs during this stage, which signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improves survival rates and decreases the LOS of burn patients compared with traditional, conservative excision [17,36,50]. Hence, in addition to the 17 independent variables for the admission stage, we include two treatment variables pertaining to the acute stage: whether any surgical operation is undergone (including escharotomy, wound excision, and skin grafting) and the interval between admission and the <sup>fi</sup>rst surgical operation.

Finally, the post-treatment stage, the latest LOS prediction timing considered in this study, consists of the period after all surgical operations have been completed. We consider three additional treatment variables for LOS prediction at this stage: the number of escharotomy treatments, the number of wound excisions and skin grafts, and the total number of surgical operations performed. Consequently, for LOS predictions for burn patients during the posttreatment stage, we employ 22 independent variables. ## 3.2. Investigated prediction techniques

An AI-based prediction technique essentially constructs an automated prediction model that captures important relationships among a set of independent variables and a dependent variable, which has a numeric (continuous) value. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a selected prediction technique uses a set of training instances (each with known values for independent and dependent variables) to construct a prediction model. The resulting model is then used to predict the dependent variable of a new (unseen) instance on the basis of its input variable values. Commonly adopted AI-based prediction techniques include neural network, model-tree-based regression, and SVM regression. The neural network approach appears reasonably effective and robust across different applications but is computationally demanding and often provides poor interpretability for its prediction results, if any. In contrast, the use of model-treebased regression and SVM regression techniques demonstrates satisfactory effectiveness in a wide array of applications, including exchange rate prediction [24], prediction of adequacy of high-alert antibiotics regimens [22], and travel time prediction [56]. Accordingly we adopt a model-tree-based regression technique (namely, M5) [40,53] and SVM regression [46,52] to build computational models for LOS predictions for burn patients.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, M5, a model-tree-based regression technique, combines a conventional decision tree and linear regression functions [40]. A model tree resembles a decision tree structurally but has linear regression functions at its leaf nodes rather than the discrete (output) classes that are common to conventional decision-tree induction algorithms (e.g., ID3 [39], C4.5 [41]). Constructing a model tree involves tree building and pruning [40,53]. In the tree-building phase, a decision-tree induction algorithm is employed to expand the tree. Unlike classical decision-tree induction algorithms, which maximize the information gain (or gain ratio) at each intermediate node, M5 uses an attribute-selection criterion that minimizes the intra-subset variation of the dependent variable at each branch. This selection criterion measures the error of a target node using the dependent variable's standard deviation of the training instances that pertain to that node, then calculates the standard deviation reduction expected from splitting the target node on the basis of a chosen attribute (i.e., independent variable). As a result, M5 selects the attribute that yields the greatest standard deviation reduction and uses it to split the target node in the decision tree (i.e., building the decision tree). For each child node, the described attribute selection and tree-building process continues iteratively until the node contains only a few training instances or its training instances exhibit small variations.

In the tree-pruning phase, M5 uses an estimate of the expected error at each node T. The absolute difference (error) between the predicted and actual (known) value of the dependent variable is <sup>fi</sup>rst calculated for each training instance, then averaged across all training instances that reach T. The resulting average error likely underestimates the expected error for unseen instances. To compensate for these probable underestimations, the average error is multiplied by ${ \frac { t + \nu } { t - \nu } } ,$ where t is the number of training instances pertinent to $T ,$ and v

is the number of attributes used to represent the prediction model at T. Thus, M5 computes a linear regression model for each intermediate node in the unpruned model tree. Each resulting linear model can be simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed by removing selected attributes, which minimizes the estimated error calculated according to the multiplication factor. The removal of an attribute reduces the multiplication factor and therefore can offset the inevitable increase in the average error across all training instances. Typically, the removal of attributes proceeds by following a greedy strategy; that is, one after another until the compensated error estimate decreases. When a linear regression model has been constructed for each intermediate node, the tree can be pruned in reverse fashion, starting from the leaf nodes and terminating when the expected estimate of error decreases.

As an alternative, SVM uses a set of training instances to construct a regression function for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation or prediction purposes [52]. When SVM is applied to prediction tasks (involving a continuousvalued dependent variable rather than a nominal one), it is referred to as SVM regression or support vector regression [46,56]. Particularly, SVM regression maintains all the features of the maximal margin concept desired for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation purposes (i.e., SVM) while searching for a function that approximates the mapping between the input domain and the real-number domain. The difference between the actual and estimated output of a training instance denotes the residual of that instance, which in turn manifests the accuracy of the mapping. Fundamentally, SVM regression minimizes large residuals, shows tolerance toward small residuals, and determines an optimal balance on the basis of the ε-insensitive loss objective function.

In the ε-SVM regression, the objective is to <sup>fi</sup>nd a function f(x) that has, at most, ε deviation(s) from the actual output $y _ { i }$ for all training instances. It strives to minimize the loss function as well as the Euclidean norm of linear parameters $( | | w | | ^ { 2 } )$ , which resembles a convex optimization problem. A soft-margin concept can be operationalized by introducing two non-negative slack variables, ξ and $\boldsymbol { \xi } ^ { * }$ , which measure the deviation of training instances outside the ε- insensitive tube. Hence, SVM regression is formulated as the minimization of the following function:

$$
\frac {1}{2} \left(\left| \left| w ^ {2} \right| \right|\right) + C \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \left(\xi_ {i} + \xi_ {i} ^ {*}\right),
$$

subject to

$$
\begin{array}{l} y _ {i} - w ^ {t} x _ {i} - b \leq \varepsilon + \xi_ {i}, \\ - y _ {i} + w ^ {t} x _ {i} + b \leq \varepsilon + \xi_ {i} ^ {*}, \text { and } \\ \xi_ {i}, \xi_ {i} ^ {*} \geq 0 \end{array}
$$

This optimization problem can be solved as a quadratic programming problem. The constant CN0 determines the trade-off between the empirical error and the structural error (i.e., the error that occurs in the training instances versus the error in generalization). In addition, the $\frac { 1 } { 2 } \Big ( \left\| w \right\| ^ { 2 } \Big )$ term dictates the complexity of the regression function.

## 4. Empirical evaluation

In this section, we provide empirical evaluation results for the two AI-based prediction techniques (i.e., M5 and SVM regression) across the three clinical stages, using linear regression analysis (or regression for short) as the performance benchmark. In this study, we use Weka (www.cs.waikato.ac.nz/ml/weka/), an open source machine learning software, to construct LOS prediction models based on the linear regression analysis, $\mathsf { M } 5 , ^ { 2 }$ and SVM regression techniques. To avoid over<sup>fi</sup>tting each prediction technique to our evaluation data set, we simply adopt the parameter values suggested by Weka for each prediction technique without parameter tuning. In the following, we <sup>fi</sup>rst describe our data collection, then depict our empirical evaluation design (including the evaluation procedure and criteria), and <sup>fi</sup>nally discuss some important evaluation results.

## 4.1. Data collection

We collect a total of 1505 hospitalized burn cases from a regional burn center in southern Taiwan from July 1997 to September 2002. Because the purpose of this study is to predict LOS for burn patients, we exclude burn cases for which patients voluntarily discharged themselves from the studied burn center before the completion of their treatment, were transferred to another hospital, stayed more than 2 days in another hospital before being admitted to the studied burn center, or passed away during treatment from our evaluation data set. Furthermore, we rule out burn cases whose LOS is less than 7 days, because such cases typically involve minor burn injuries, whose resource planning and patient care management generally are less complicated than those associated with more severe burns. As a result, our data set consists of a total of 1080 burn cases, each of which provides values for the 22 independent variables that we de<sup>fi</sup>ne in the previous section, as well as their actual LOS.

Table 3 summarizes important descriptive statistics for these 1080 burn cases. The ages of burn patients range from 1 to 86 years, with an average of 30.7 and a standard deviation of 20.5. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, 77.78%, 16.02%, 3.61%, 2.13%, and 0.46% of burn patients suffer burns of TBSA of less than 20%, between 21–40%, between 41–60%, between 61–80%, and 81% or more, respectively. In terms of etiology, the most frequent cause is scald, which accounts for 54.4% of the cases. Moreover, 7.1% of burn patients suffer from inhalation injury. In addition, 99 (9.2%) patients suffer third-degree burns on multiple sites. Among all burn cases, 532 (49.3%) patients receive surgical operations during their hospital stays, 109 of them during the acute stage. For the 532 patients who undergo surgical operations, the average number of operations is 2.51, with a standard deviation of 2.46. The actual LOS of the collected burn cases ranges from 7 to 193 days, with an average of 22.85 and a standard deviation of 20.7 days.

## 4.2. Evaluation procedure and criteria

To expand the number of trials when we estimate the effectiveness of the LOS prediction techniques for each clinical stage, we adopt a tenfold cross-validation strategy. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we split the data set randomly into ten mutually exclusive subsets of equal size. We then designate each subset (i.e., 108 cases) as the testing data set while the others (i.e., 972 cases) serve as the training data set. To minimize potential biases that may result from the randomized folding process, we perform this tenfold cross-validation process three times and estimate the overall performance by averaging the performance estimates obtained from the 30 individual trials.

We measure the effectiveness of a LOS prediction technique according to the mean absolute error (MAE) and the mean relative error (MRE), de<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} M A E = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left| P r e d i c t e d L O S _ {i} - A c t u a l L O S _ {i} \right|}{n} \text {and} \\ M R E = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {\left| P r e d i c t e d L O S _ {i} - A c t u a l L O S _ {i} \right|}{A c t u a l L O S _ {i}}}{n} \end{array}
$$

where PredictedLOS is the predicted LOS for the ith testing instance, ActualLOS is the actual LOS of the ith testing instance, and n is the total number of testing instances.

Variable subset selection, also known as feature selection, refers to the selection of a subset of independent variables relevant to the target classi<sup>fi</sup>cation or prediction task and is important for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation/prediction ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and comprehensibility of resultant classi<sup>fi</sup>cation/prediction models [16,27]. Particularly, we employ the correlation-based feature selection (CFS) method [16] for feature selection purpose. CFS is based on the hypothesis that good variable subsets should contain independent variables highly correlated with the target dependent variable but uncorrelated with each other. Accordingly, CFS uses a heuristic function to measure the “goodness” of variable subsets by taking into account the usefulness of individual features for predicting the dependent variable along with the level of intercorrelation among them. As Table 6 illustrates, the CFS method selects 9 out of 17 independent variables for the admission stage, 10 out of 19 for the acute stage, and 9 out of 22 for the post-treatment stage. It is worth noting that three independent variables are considered important to all clinical stages. ## 4.4. Breakdown analysis

We further conduct a breakdown analysis of the effectiveness of different prediction techniques (using all independent variables pertaining to each clinical stage) according to the extent of burn (i.e., TBSA). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we segment the 1080 burn cases into <sup>fi</sup>ve TBSA groups: 1–20%, 21–40%, 41–60%, 61–80%, and 81–100%. We obtain several interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this analysis. First, as Table 8 (a) demonstrates, for each technique, the corresponding MAE increases as TBSA increases from 1–20% to 81–100%. This phenomenon occurs across all clinical stages. That is, all of the techniques investigated are more promising for predicting LOS for patients with minor or moderate burns than for patients with severe burns.

The higher MAE for patients with greater TBSAs likely re<sup>fl</sup>ects that the treatment of patients with minor or moderate burns is far less complicated and more controllable than that for patients with severe burns. As a result, the predictability of LOS for patients with lower TBSA should be higher than that for patients with greater TBSA. Second, as Table 8(a) and (b) show, SVM regression consistently outperforms the other two prediction techniques in the <sup>fi</sup>rst four TBSA groups (i.e., TBSA between 1% and 80%) across the three clinical stages. However, for the most severely burned group (i.e., TBSA 81– 100%), M5 is most effective during the admission and acute stages, whereas the regression technique is most accurate at the posttreatment stage. This <sup>fi</sup>nding may suggest that SVM regression has a tendency to train its prediction model toward larger groups (i.e., with more training instances) than M5 and the regression technique, because the most severely burned group contains only a very small percentage of the burn cases (i.e., 0.46%) in our data set.

We also perform a breakdown analysis of the effectiveness of different prediction techniques according to the burn cases' actual LOS. We divide the burn cases into <sup>fi</sup>ve groups according to their actual LOS: between 7–20 days, 21–34 days, 35–48 days, 49– 62 days, and 63–193 days. Over all clinical stages, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings remain similar to those from the TBSA breakdown analysis. First, as we show in Table 9(a), for each prediction technique, MAE correlates positively with actual LOS (i.e., the corresponding MAE increases as actual LOS increases from 7–20 to 63–193 days). This positive correlation may occur because patients with more severe burns generally require longer LOS, and as we have discussed, the predictability of LOS for patients with minor or moderate burns is more accurate than that for patients with severe burns. Second, as we show in Table 9(a) and (b), SVM regression generally outperforms the other two techniques in the <sup>fi</sup>rst three LOS groups (i.e., actual LOS 7–48 days), in terms of MAE and MRE, across all three clinical stages. In contrast, when burn cases require longer LOS (i.e., 49 days or longer), the regression technique results in the lowest prediction error for every clinical stage, as compared with the prediction error recorded for SVM regression or M5 at the corresponding clinical stage. Because the last two groups encompass a very small percentage of the burn cases (i.e., 3.51% and 4.26%, respectively), this <sup>fi</sup>nding again suggests that SVM regression tends to train its prediction model toward larger groups, whereas the regression technique is relatively less biased toward larger groups.

Our empirical evaluation results suggest several clinical and managerial implications for burn patient care and management. First, if the timeliness of service and resource planning and patient family counseling is critical to some burn centers or burn cases, the associated LOS predictions should be conducted at the admission stage rather than deferring to the end of the acute stage, because the accuracy of LOS predictions at the acute stage does not improve compared with that at the admission stage. Second, because the prediction effectiveness at the later stage (i.e., post-treatment) appears to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater than that at the earlier stages (i.e., admission and acute stages), LOS predictions for burn patients should better be employed for resource utilization reviews and cost controls. Such understanding of the limitation of LOS prediction at the earlier clinical stages is important to physicians and administrators of burn care units and suggests the service and resource planning made at an early stage of each burn case should constantly be reexamined as the course of treatment given to the burn patient progresses. Third, our empirical evaluation results highlight the importance of the three independent variables pertaining to the post-treatment stage (including the number of escharotomy, the number of wound excision and skin grafting, and the total number of surgical operations) for effective LOS prediction. Hence, if attending physicians can somehow estimate the possible number of different surgical operations required when a burn patient is admitted into a burn care unit, the effectiveness of LOS prediction for the burn patient at an earlier clinical stage (i.e., predicting on the basis of LOS prediction models developed for the post-treatment stage with estimates on the number of different surgical operations required) could be improved. This implies that, to better facilitate service and resource planning as well as patient and family counseling during the admission and acute stages, burn care units should attempt to develop appropriate heuristics for estimating the number of different surgical operations required for each burn case. Fourth, the breakdown analyses point out that no single prediction technique can be effective for predicting LOS for all burn cases. Our empirical results indicate the appropriateness of using SVM regression for LOS prediction for burn patients whose TBSA is less than 80% and that of using M5 for patients belonging to the most severely burned group. Moreover, our empirical results also suggest that, when developing prediction models to support other clinical decisions, it is essential and desirable to conduct similar breakdown analyses in order to assess conditions where different prediction techniques can best be applied.

Our study has several limitations that warrant additional research attention. First, we include burn cases collected from a single burn center in Taiwan and thus our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings are only applicable to this data set. Performing similar evaluations with cases from other burn centers will strengthen the validity and generalizability of the evaluation results reported in the current study. Second, due to data inavailability in the studied burn center, we do not include other variables possibly important to LOS predictions for burn patients (e.g., whether complications are developed, routine use of antibiotic for prophylaxis, and supply of sustenance). Thus, evaluations of different prediction techniques across various clinical stages using burn cases with additional independent variables represent an interesting and desirable future research. Third, as mentioned, SVM regression outperforms its counterparts in some scenarios (i.e., TBSA lower than 80% or LOS less than 48 days), whereas M5 and the regression technique are more effective in others. Therefore, the development of a hybrid prediction technique that involves multiple methods might further improve the prediction effectiveness reported herein. Fourth, the actual LOS of our burn cases resembles a long-tailed distribution. When using any prediction technique to train a prediction model, the resultant model will be biased toward the middle of the long-tailed distribution. In other words, the prediction accuracy in the tail of the distribution (e.g., burn cases requiring longer LOS in this study) is expected to be inferior to that in the middle. This problem is similar, but not identical, to the class imbalance problem for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Given a dichotomous classi<sup>fi</sup>cation task, if the size of training instances of one class (denoted the majority class) is much greater than that of another (denoted the minority class), the induced classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model will tend to classify unseen instances into the majority class. Several mechanisms have been proposed in the literature to address learning from training data sets that are class imbalanced. However, learning over long-tailed distributions has not been duly examined by prior research and, thus, represents an interesting and important future research direction. Last but not least, other research issues related to burn patient care deserve further attention. For example, analyses of the clinical pathway of burn cases could shed light on why some treatments seem more effective than others. In addition, including the pathway of treatments as independent variables may further improve the effectiveness of LOS predictions for burn patients.
