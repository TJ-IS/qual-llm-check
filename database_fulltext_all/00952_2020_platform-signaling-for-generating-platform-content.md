# PLATFORM SIGNALING FOR GENERATING PLATFORM CONTENT<sup>1</sup>

Philipp Hukal Copenhagen Business School, Howitzvej 60, 2000 Frederiksberg, DENMARK {ph.digi@cbs.dk}

Ola Henfridsson Miami Herbert Business School, University of Miami, 5250 University Drive, Coral Gables, FL 33146 U.S.A. {ohenfridsson@miami.edu}

Maha Shaikh Kings College London, Strand, London, WC2R 2LS UNITED KINGDOM {maha.shaikh@kcl.ac.uk}

Geoffrey Parker Thayer School of Engineering, Dartmouth College, 14 Engineering Drive, Hanover, NH 03755 U.S.A. {geoffrey.g.parker@dartmouth.edu}

The generation of platform content is essential for platform growth and competition. However, the overwhelming number of platform complementors makes it impossible for platform operators to engage in extensive communication with each complementor about which content contributions are desired. Therefore, platform operators need to find a way to signal strategic interests to platform complementors. In this paper, we employ a mixed-methods design using data from the geodata platform OpenStreetMap to develop and test two distinct types of platform signals as a means of implementing a platform operator’s strategy: (1) opportunity signals, which aim to stimulate activity in new areas of the platform, and (2) endorsement signals, which aim to increase activity in existing areas of the platform. In particular, we examine how platform signals influence the generation of platform content in terms of the volume and diversity of information on the platform. We contribute important insights to the platform governance literature by developing and empirically testing a signaling perspective on the generation of platform content and discussing its implications for guiding platform complementors in content creation.

Keywords: Platform governance, content generation, complementors, mixed methods, OpenStreetMap

## Introduction

The generation of platform content is imperative for the competitiveness of a platform (Boudreau 2012; Cennamo and

Santalo 2019; Eaton et al. 2015; Parker et al. 2017; Tiwana 2014). The ongoing supply of platform content in the form of data, applications, or media increases the platform’s value for end users. In many cases, such content is generated by platform complementors, that is, external actors providing input to complementary products or services (see Gawer and Cusamano 2013; Nalebuff and Brandenburger 1997).

However, the platform operator—the entity that governs transactions on a platform (Boudreau and Haigu 2009; Tiwana 2014; Wareham et al. 2014)—is faced with a problem. The sheer size of many platforms makes it impossible for the operator to engage in extensive communication with each platform complementor about its goals. Yet, some communication is necessary, as the platform operator typically has strategic interests in generating content in particular areas of the platform rather than in others. For instance, the platform operator might want to stimulate new platform activity, such as the development of new use cases (Förderer et al. 2018). Alternatively, the platform operator might have a strategic interest in increasing activity in existing areas of the platform (Ho and Rai 2017; Rietveld et al. 2019).

In view of the necessity of governing through arm’s length relationships (e.g., Boudreau 2012; Tiwana 2014), platform operators may use signals to indicate strategic interests to complementors (see Connelly et al. 2011). Signals can lend direction to platform complementors without engaging in oneto-one communication. While demand-side signaling aiming at increasing platform adoption has received scholarly attention (e.g., Evans and Schmalensee 2016), platform signaling for generating new content is important to anyone wishing to guide content generation when the sheer number of complementors makes arm’s length relationships necessary.

We adopt a mixed-methods design (Venkatesh et al. 2013) to analyze five years of data (May 2009 to December 2014) in the context of the OpenStreetMap platform. We examine the platform’s acceptance of metadata tags as signals of strategic interest for stimulating content generation. We identify and test the effects of two distinct types of platform signals that communicate strategic interest: (1) opportunity signals, which aim to stimulate activity in new areas of the platform, and (2) endorsement signals, which aim to increase activity in existing areas of the platform. We address the following research question: What is the effect of opportunity signals and endorsement signals on the generation of platform content?

We operationalize content generation as a combination of the increase in volume and diversity of information on the platform. We find that opportunity signals grow platform content in volume and diversity, while endorsement signals increase content in volume but not in diversity. We discuss these findings and theorize about the potential and limitations of using signals to guide platform complementors in generating platform content. Our research offers a contribution to the platform governance literature by developing and empirically testing a platform signaling approach to generating platform content.

## Theory Development

## Platform Content Generation

The generation of platform content can take different forms to fuel platform growth (Parker et al. 2017; Yoo et al. 2010). Platform operators might develop content in-house to jumpstart interaction on the platform (e.g., Huang et al. 2018), including through the replication of content which is common in the platform genre (see Cennamo and Santalo 2019). In the long run, however, platform operators have to rely on content generation by outside complementors through relationships that are typically arm’s length in nature (Boudreau 2012; Tiwana 2014). With such light-touch relationships, it is not surprising that redundancy is commonplace (Boudreau 2018; Boudreau and Jeppesen 2015) and that a fair share of the content generation is unanticipated by the platform operator (Tilson et al. 2010; Wareham et al. 2014; Yoo et al. 2010; Zittrain 2006).

We define platform content generation as the growth in volume and diversity of information on the platform. Three aspects of this definition merit elaboration. First, depending on the type of digital platform, the content of a platform can materialize as available applications (e.g., software for iOS and Android), digital media content (e.g., videos on YouTube), or provided information (e.g., articles on Wikipedia). Such content supports a platform’s core interaction and serves as the basis for all exchanges enabled by the operator (Parker et al. 2016). Second, the volume of content provided on a platform largely mirrors its growth. The volume of content, be it data, information, or applications, tends to grow with the number of touchpoints on the platform (Parker et al. 2016). Thus, the number of interactions involving the platform and its participants is fundamental to the value the platform offers and mirrors the scale at which it operates. Third, the diversity of platform content can also be seen as an indicator of platform growth (Boudreau 2012; Cennamo and Santalo 2019; Lyytinen et al. 2018). A growing volume and diversity of content increases the value of the platform as the platform’s technical and economic configuration provides numerous and different touchpoints for interaction (Eisenmann et al. 2011; Parker et al. 2016).

## Guiding Content Contributions

There is an emerging body of literature that focuses on the generation of content by platform complementors. For instance, Ghazawneh and Henfridsson (2013) and Eaton et al. (2015) explore the Apple iOS platform and its use of APIs to attract applications. In a similar vein, Cennamo and Santalo (2018) examine platform architecture and how to stimulate complementor contributions by limiting the technical complexity of the platform.

A central theme of this literature is the interplay between content generation and efforts to control the platform. On a general level, work in this space deals with platform governance in terms of managing the tension between control and flexibility (Eaton et al. 2015; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2013; Svahn et al. 2017; Tilson et al. 2010; Wareham et al. 2014). Such governance strategies aim to reconcile the interests of the platform operator with those of the complementors (Eaton et al. 2015; Parker et al. 2017; Wareham et al. 2014). On the one hand, the platform operator wishes to control the platform, not least in terms of its integrity (Eaton et al. 2015; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2013). On the other hand, the operator seeks to ensure that complementors have the flexibility to create derivative content that makes the platform valuable in the eyes of end users. Reconciling both dynamics is the objective of the platform operator’s strategy (e.g., Wareham et al. 2014).

While this tension between control and flexibility is reiterated in slightly different flavors in the literature, control is typically seen as a counterforce to content generation. For instance, Ghazawneh and Henfridsson view the task of securing platform boundaries as preventing “the development of applications that risk infringing the platform” (p. 177). Svahn et al. (2017) take a similar position, viewing control grounded in the legacy of the incumbent firm as a competing concern to that of flexibility. However, there is recognition that control by the operator and flexibility for complementors are intertwined (Eaton et al. 2015; Tilson et al. 2010). A crucial question for platform researchers is thus how content generation can be guided in a way that helps enact the operator’s platform strategy despite its limited means to control each individual complementor.

## Platform Signaling

The sheer number of platform complementors makes arm’s length relationships necessary (e.g., Eaton et al. 2015; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2013). At the same time, the platform operator is faced with the question of how to guide platform complementors without individual engagement. Reflecting an information asymmetry between the operator and complementors (Boudreau and Hagiu 2008; Ho and Rai 2017; Rietveld et al. 2019), platform complementors cannot be certain about the operator’s interests and the platform’s strategic direction. However, complementors are indispensable for the generation of voluminous and diverse platform content (Boudreau 2018;

Cennamo and Santalo 2019; Rietveld et al. 2019). This motivates platform operators to search for efficient ways to govern their platform and guide content generation by complementors while retaining arm’s length relationships.

One such way to govern platform activity is through signaling. The platform operator can signal intentions about the direction of the platform with the aim of guiding content generation by platform complementors. The platform operator is the sender of information, while platform complementors can be seen as receivers interpreting the communicated signals (see Connelly et al. 2011). For platform operators to successfully signal which interactions are desired and where, that is, for the signal to be effective, two aspects are crucial. First, information about the underlying quality intended to be signaled needs to be correctly identified by the operator. Since platform complementors cannot know for certain where and which contributions to the platform are desirable, the platform operator has to identify a feature, activity, or functionality of strategic interest and make it known. Second, the signal needs to be easily understood by the receiver. It is best that the effort required to decode the signal be limited to facilitate information acquisition on the receiver’s end (Connelly et al. 2011).

Two perspectives on platform signaling can be identified in the literature. First, demand-oriented signals (see Boudreau and Haigu 2009; Brynjolfsson and Kemerer 1996; Evans and Schmalensee 2016) focus on the installed base. Such signals communicate the platform operator’s vision for the future of the platform with the intention of increasing the number of platform users. Signals are then a means of communication for achieving the critical mass needed for network effects to kick in (Brynjolfsson and Kemerer 1996; Evans 2009; Ondrus et al. 2015). Such “signaling devices” (Evans and Schmalensee 2016) are potent ways of showcasing hard-to-observe qualities of a platform. For instance, supplying complementors with their average Yelp review rating for display in their businesses showcases the platform’s current and likely future adoption. New complementors can join and commit to the platform in confidence as demand-side signals promise use of their service by attracting end users (Chintakananda and McIntyre 2014; McIntyre and Srinivasan 2017).

A second—and often overlooked—perspective on signals is a supply-oriented perspective. Supply-oriented signals guide platform complementors to engage in content generation in a way that is desirable for the operator. Complementary supply in certain areas can increase the production of content such that it aligns with the strategic interest of platform operators. Ultimately, this results in content generation without excessive input control from the operator (e.g., Wareham et al.

2014; Zittrain 2006). In contrast to a demand-oriented perspective, a supply-oriented perspective seeks to stimulate the production function of a platform by relating input to output. Supply-side signals are rarely studied, but forms understood in the literature include supporting complementors through promotions on the platform (Rietveld et al. 2019) or giving special status to developers (Ho and Rai 2017). Popular application marketplaces, such as those for iOS or Android apps, regularly engage in such signaling by highlighting individual complementors, for example, by advertising the “App of the Month.” Such signals thus highlight that complementor activity of a certain kind is desirable. Alternatively, operators may signal the creation of a new use case on the platform (Förderer et al. 2018). SAP, for instance, regularly communicates “white spaces” during developer conferences to alert complementors that content generation in certain areas is desirable. Such signals explicate the operator’s strategic interest and justify complementors’ continued activity on the platform (Boudreau and Jeppesen 2015).

In this vein, signaling to the platform’s supply side can be understood as a way to govern activity on the platform (Rietveld et al. 2019; Tiwana 2014; Wareham et al. 2014). While past literature links signals indicating sustained use and adoption of a platform to network effects (e.g., Dranove and Gandal 2003), platform signals can also address the supply side of the platform by communicating with complementors. Platform operators are generally thought to regulate their marketplaces by means of deciding which transactions are enabled on the platform in the first place (Boudreau and Hagiu 2009). With a focus on platform complementors and the challenge of finding efficient ways to balance control and content generation, signaling the operator’s interests holds potential as a platform governance strategy. As such, signaling is a way for the platform operator to guide complementors by making goals and expectations known. The operator enacts its strategic interests while retaining arm’s length control over where and which content is generated on the platform. It is, therefore, important to understand the effect of different signal types on the generation of content on platforms.

## Research Design

We conducted a mixed-methods study (Venkatesh et al. 2013; Venkatesh et al. 2016) of the OpenStreetMap geodata platform. Specifically, we implemented a sequential design using a quantitative-dominant approach. The quantitative analysis therein was motivated and guided by qualitative insights and prior theoretical work (Creswell 2016; Venkatesh et al. 2016). We present our conceptualization in the form of hypotheses for readability purposes, even though the process by which constructs emerged was more iterative (Berends and Deuken 2019; Berente et al. 2019). Thus,

We first engage with the platform literature to hypothesize the existence and effect of two general signal types and the strategic interests behind them.

We then draw on an in-depth content analysis of discussion forum data to present evidence for each type of signal used by the operator of the OpenStreetMap platform.

We finally use two original datasets of tagged geodata from OpenStreetMap to test our hypotheses using quantitative analysis techniques.

In what follows, we present the hypotheses development first, then describe the empirical context and explain the data sources as well as techniques used in the analysis, before presenting the results.

## Hypotheses Development

## Opportunity Signals

For platform operators, defining the activity footprint for a platform is crucial. The growth and continued innovation of platforms depends on the interaction of supply- and demandside actors in high numbers and of different kinds (Anderson et al. 2014; Cennamo amd Santalo 2019; Ondrus et al. 2015). The platform literature proposes several pathways for influencing a platform’s activity profile, including opening particular areas of the platform to grant access to external parties (Boudreau 2010; Eisenmann et al. 2008; Parker and Van Alstyne 2018) or expanding the platform by offering complementary new products (Eisenmann et al. 2011; Förderer et al. 2018; Gawer and Henderson 2007).

Platform operators thus have a strategic interest in creating new opportunities to increase the number and kind of interactions on the platform. For instance, Boudreau (2010) describes strategies that allow new categories of complementors onto the platform as “opening up markets,” the consequences of which are an increase in the volume and diversity of products offered on the platform. Similarly, Eisenmann et al. (2011) demonstrate how expanding the product categories offered on platforms is a powerful competitive move to “envelop” and occupy new areas of activity or compete in rival platform markets. Indeed, creating new areas for platform complementors to interact with end users has been associated with positive effects on the activity of complementors (Huang et al. 2018).

All things being equal, platform operators strive for larger audiences of complementors as the competition between platforms increases (Parker et al. 2017). For instance, if complementors contribute applications to a software platform, it is imperative for the operator to continuously enroll additional developers to maintain growth and innovation. In this vein, signaling that the number of users on the platform is likely to grow assures complementors that the chosen platform is the right home for their services (Brynjolfsson and Kemerer 1996). The logic of cross-side network effects holds that users prefer platforms with many complementors, and as a result, complementors have clear incentives to join and remain committed to platforms with larger user bases (Boudreau and Jeppesen 2015; Parker et al. 2017). Platform operators are, therefore, strongly motivated to increase the installed base through interaction with many different complementors.

Signaling new opportunities to engage with the platform is often motivated by a desire to fuel further activity on both sides of the platform. The potential of such strategies can be illustrated by Google’s decision to offer its own Photo App on the Android platform after observing sufficient demand in similar services (Förderer et al. 2018). Förderer et al. show how this decision sustained the generation of new content (here, apps) on the platform after Google signaled the inclusion of the new use case category. This step increased demand in the respective application category as well as areas adjacent to the new use case. We, therefore, hypothesize that using signals to alert complementors to new opportunities on the platform positively impacts the generation of content in terms of volume.

## H1a: Signaling new opportunities to platform complementors stimulates the generation of platform content in terms of volume.

At the same time, creating new opportunities on the platform can aim at increasing the generation of different kinds of content. Therefore, it is crucial for operators to enable novel activity that is truly complementary to the existing activity profile. Hence, the signaling of such opportunities needs to be well considered and planned. Any new activity offered on the platform that effectively enters in competition with complementors risks deterring complementor inputs in the desired areas (Gawer and Henderson 2007; Li and Agarwal 2017; Wen and Zhu 2019). Such moves do not generally aim at stimulating complementor input but rather at increasing the surplus for the platform operator by carrying out profitable activity formerly provided by complementors (Zhu and Liu 2018).

An example of successful opportunity creation was Intel’s decision to disseminate and offer proprietary connector components (see Gawer and Henderson 2007). On the one hand, Intel effectively substituted complementor activity on the platform by engaging in an activity that used to be covered by complementors. On the other hand, this created an opportunity for complementors to engage in new activity on the platform in areas that then became adjacent to the activity subsumed by the operator (Gawer and Henderson 2007). Under such circumstances, signaling new opportunities has the potential to offset detrimental effects to complementor activity by enabling spillovers to other areas (Li and Agarwal 2017).

A signal that expresses the platform operator’s strategic interest in offering new activity alerts complementors to new opportunities on the platform. Ultimately, this influences the generation of novel kinds of content on the platform. Such a signal makes the future aspirations of the platform operator explicit. It communicates in which area of activity interaction is desired on the platform and that new combinations of con tent are to be expected through the interplay of activity in new and adjacent areas offered on the platform. We thus hypothesize that signaling new opportunities also increases the generation of platform content in terms of diversity.

H1b: Signaling new opportunities to platform complementors stimulates the generation of platform content in terms of diversity.

## Endorsement Signals

Platform operators also have strategic interests in generating content in existing areas of activity on the platform. Such interests are often enacted through deliberate interventions by the operator to stimulate specific kinds of interaction through endorsements (Boudreau 2017; Rietveld et al. 2019; Rietveld et al. 2018). Endorsement signals express support of third parties and can include accommodating third-party requests, certifying complementors, or promoting desirable third-party behavior (see Ho and Rai 2017; Rietveld et al. 2019). For instance, platform operators can decide to publicly sponsor complementors if their activity aligns with the platform operator’s strategy. Such endorsement of actors aims to prompt activity in a certain area by reaching out to actors beyond the boundary of the platform (Boudreau 2017; Rietveld et al. 2019). Endorsement signals thus aim to encourage complementors to engage in existing areas of activity by selectively supporting third-party actors.

If done right, endorsement signals can have effects that promote the retention and increase of the activity for which the operator’s support was made evident while also encouraging further complementor activity. A recently reported example of this dynamic involves the music streaming platform Spotify, which began to endorse up-and-coming music artists by featuring them on curated playlists (Aguiar and Waldfogel 2018). In this case, the platform operator selectively promoted platform interactions in a way that had substantial effects on the number of interactions facilitated between the affected complementors and users on the platform. Similarly, complementors on a microlending platform enjoyed performance benefits once they had earned a certification offered by the platform operator to align complementors with its strategy (Rietveld et al. 2018).

An endorsement signal is thus a potentially powerful lever for operators with a strategic interest in stimulating platform growth through the generation of content in existing areas. First and foremost, signals that communicate the operator’s willingness to accommodate complementors is a strong signal in its own right. Such signals assure complementors that their commitment to a platform is worthwhile in the long run (Wareham et al. 2014). Complementors who receive the endorsement furthermore enjoy prominent positioning, while other complementors might wish to replicate their success. Both dynamics should lead to more activity in the area affected by the operator’s intervention. This leads to our next hypothesis:

## H2a: Signaling endorsement of complementor activity stimulates the generation of platform content in terms of volume.

In the practice of platform governance, platform operators often face situations where complementary use cases are unanticipated. Operators are therefore often compelled to react to emerging engagements between users and complementors on the platform. This gives operators the chance to decide whether existing activity fits their platform strategy or not. For instance, as reported by Wareham et al. (2014), implementation methodologies predefined by a large enterprise software vendor were amended by complementors to facilitate specialized instantiations in local markets. The vendor acknowledged that complementors deviated from suggested practices and supported the idiosyncratic alteration of its procedures ex post (Wareham et al. 2014).

Additionally, platform operators have to manage a trade-off between stimulating activity on the platform while simultaneously avoiding overcrowding. Work on the issue of overcrowding stipulates that innovative activity on a platform is a curve-linear function of the number and diversity of complementors that exist on the platform simultaneously (e.g., Boudreau 2012; 2018). In this vein, a platform operator needs to manage a large number of concurrent inputs by complementors to stimulate the generation of not only more but different kinds of content. By indicating an interest in supporting specific activity on the platform, endorsement signals address such issues of complementor overcrowding by giving attention to areas where the generation of platform content aligns with the interest of the operator. This claim echoes Wareham et al.’s notion of managing desirable and undesirable variation on a platform.

Endorsement signals address the delicate balance required to manage a large number of interactions on a platform. Since endorsements can be used prioritize the diversity of interactions over the volume (see Rietveld et al. 2019), they can have substantial effects on complementor activity. Endorsement signals thus help to overcome the inherent information asymmetry on platforms and guide the generation of novel kinds of platform content. West (2003), for instance, views IBM’s adoption of Linux for its server business as a strategy that eventually led to innovative platform content contributed by external actors after the operator endorsed the complementor.

Thus, endorsement signals lend direction to unanticipated activity as well as manage large crowds of complementors on platforms by indicating forms of desirable variation on the platform. We therefore expect that signaling endorsement of a specific activity also benefits the diversity of platform content by guiding desirable variation in the generation of platform content:

H2b: Signaling endorsement of complementor activity stimulates the generation of platform content in terms of diversity.

## Content Analysis

## Empirical Context

We study OpenStreetMap, which offers freely accessible geospatial data on a global scale. Launched in 2004, it is now considered the world’s largest web-based geodata platform. Dubbed “the Wikipedia of maps” (Fox 2012), it has more than 4.5 million registered users, of which 1.0 million contribute content to the map each month (as of November 2017).<sup>2</sup> The provided data are popular with developers, with more than 350 external service providers, including Apple, Wikipedia, Foursquare, and Craigslist, using the platform’s geodata for their products and services.<sup>3</sup>

OpenStreetMap is representative of many digital platforms (such as YouTube or Wikipedia) that rely on various actors to generate and reuse content. On OpenStreetMap, platform users (individuals, organizations, and institutions) contribute content in the form of geodata by uploading GPS coordinates to the OpenStreetMap database. This plain geospatial information is subsequently annotated with semantic metadata, called tags. OpenStreetMap tags describe real-world objects by attaching free text labels to GPS coordinates in the form of key = value pairs. Tags are constructed such that the key term indicates the general category of an object, whereas the value term qualifies its attributes. For example, the key highway=, is a high-level term to denote objects representing streets and associated objects. The value component of tags helps qualify geodata objects by specific kind (e.g., highway=residential). OpenStreetMap tags are crucial for the growth of content on the platform. Mapping data needs to be complete and consistent for external apps to continue to benefit from interaction with the platform’s database. Accurate tags ensure that the database produces consistent output at scale. In addition, a database object with a high number of tags indicates a rich and detailed thematic description of an otherwise plain location. Rich descriptions enable multifaceted use of geospatial data objects in external applications. Consider the locationbased service application Foursquare as an example. To provide detailed information to its users, the app uses OpenStreetMap data and tags to present detailed listings of objects in the public domain. This enriches locations with semantic information such that a GPS coordinate denotes a building containing a restaurant offering a certain type cuisine, as well as the restaurant’s business hours.

Continuous growth of mapping data is hence essential for OpenStreetMap in the competition with giants such as Google Maps. In particular, the supply of new, accurate, and rich mapping data is important for OpenStreetMap’s ability to compete. Such data enable the generation of novel outputs on the platform, which, in turn, require increasingly granular and up-to-date content.

Generating such content cannot be done by the platform operator alone. To guide the generation of mapping content and ensure the usability of the database, core community members act as operators of the platform. These operators are community members who are heavily engaged with and devoted to the platform. While there is no direct control over which tags are added to the platform, platform operators encourage the use of certain agreed-upon tag combinations through a simple mechanism. From the entirety of tags available on the platform, the platform operator can decide to encourage the use of certain key=value pairs. To do so, operators of the platform vote on the acceptable usage of certain tags. We know from our work on this case that only a small, recurring fraction of registered users is involved in the tag acceptance process. The decisions made by this very small group guide the direction of the entire platform and influence how all other actors engage with it (Huber et al. 2017). The share of community members voting on tags is consistently smaller than 0.01% of all contributors (i.e., registered users who contribute data). Members who vote on tags do so repeatedly and continuously: Nearly every second vote (\~44%) is cast by a contributor who has voted on tags before, and 20% of contributors are involved in tag votes over the entire observational time frame (2009–2014). Members of this group also take on tasks typically associated with “core membership” (see Setia et al. 2012). For instance, community members who have voted on tags more than once are in the 95<sup>th</sup> percentile of active participants in the community mailing list on the topic of using and discussing tags over the same time period. We provide details in the appendix. This disproportionate influence renders their actions akin to those of a platform operator despite the community-based collaboration model on OpenStreetMap (Budhathoki and Haythornthwaite 2012).

Typically, tags are documented and presented on the OpenStreetMap wiki forum. This includes a voting process, after which a tag is assigned a status that reflects its acceptance for use. The OpenStreetMap wiki represents the main source of information for participants (Davidovic et al. 2016; Ramm et al. 2010). The acceptance of a tag, therefore, has project-wide consequences. As a central stimulus to all users, tag acceptances communicate to complementors otherwise unobservable qualities of the platform (see Ho and Rai 2017), such as its plans and future direction. We, therefore, operationalized such tag acceptances in the central wiki environment as a signal by the platform operator.

## Identifying Signal Types

We identified platform signals by collecting data from the OpenStreetMap wiki forum. Using purposive selection (Yin 2015), we sampled 10 general purpose tags. Our sampling aimed at both maximizing information richness and enabling generalization (Creswell 2016; Rapley 2015). With the objective of reflecting the volume and diversity of content generated on the platform, we sampled tags according to criterion similarity on usage (number of objects), variety (number of key=value tag pairs), and purpose (usefulness for a wide range of applications) (see Kelle 2015; Yin 2015). The selected tags are foundational to mapping in general and used widely: generic descriptions of geographical features are applicable across local contexts and independent of technologies used by any part of the community. Most importantly, the sampled tags are of high relevance for platform complementors. For example, OSM objects with highway tags are used in navigation applications drawing on descriptions such as kind, surface, and quality of streets. Other tags enable routing computations, point-of-interest maps, or public transport applications. We provide a detailed overview of the sampled tags and their usage in the appendix.

The OpenStreetMap discussion forum is central for the acceptance of tags. Tags are typically accepted through a voting process initiated by a platform community member. Here, tags are presented individually or in closely related groups using dedicated web pages in the wiki environment. Wiki pages outline the rationale of why a tag should be accepted, give examples, and reference how the tag aligns with the existing metadata scheme. This commonly results in a discussion among members operating the platform before commencing a vote on whether to accept the tag.

We analyzed the tag acceptance proposals and the operator votes for each sampled tag in our research database to identify signaling on the platform. We followed a grounded approach to engage in a dialogue between literature, empirical material, and an emergent understanding of the phenomenon (see Berente et al. 2019). This stage broadly followed three steps. First, we systematically documented all accepted tags in our sample (n = 276). Using the history function implemented in the wiki environment, we tracked the changes in past versions of tag proposal pages and tag presentations. We recorded the exact point in time when each tag was first accepted. From there, we traced the proposal discussions backward chronologically to obtain an understanding of the original rationale among operators that ultimately led to the acceptance of a tag. Second, we engaged in an open coding round (Castro et al. 2010) to distill and name categories of observations. To this end, we took note of general attributes identified as motivations of operators as to why a certain tag was accepted on the platform. We examined the tag proposal pages, as well as related discussions, and took note of keywords and excerpts that pointed to why the tag was considered for acceptance. This step resulted in a list of 20 terms broadly describing the motivation for each tag acceptance. Salient among them were, for instance, “establish new tag category,” “delineate alternatives,” “increase detail of the map,” “increase number of use cases,” and “support external app.”

Third, in the subsequent step, we honed in on the platform operator’s perspective and coded the observations into distinct categories. The goal of this step was to corroborate the observations of tag acceptance and relate them to the strategic interests of the platform operator as the central “dimension” in our initial theory development (Castro et al. 2010). To this end, we paid close attention to notions of growing content on the platform and interacting with external complementors as well as discussions about in which areas of activity the plat form should be engaged. We undertook multiple iterations so that we could converge on a reliable, minimal set of categories that were evident across our observations.

During the time frame of our study, 276 tags from our sample were accepted on the platform. While all tag acceptances share the intention of increasing the volume and diversity of content on the platform, we demonstrate that tags are accepted in a way that corresponds with specific strategic interests to guide content contributions from complementors. It is through the use of signals, in turn, that these strategies are enacted. In the following subsection, we highlight the identification of such strategic interests and their corresponding signals in the empirical context. We present selected quotes from the forum discussion for illustration. These quotes are selected to be representative of the evidence we found for signals and the strategic interests to which they relate.

## Opportunity Signals

The first salient type of signal is what we call an opportunity signal. We classified tag acceptances as opportunity signals when the acceptance of a tag was motivated by an interest in opening up the platform to new areas of activity. This was evident when tags were accepted to enable entirely new forms of describing database objects and thus required the generation of new content on the platform.

Two tag groups (building=, shop=) were examples of accepting a new category of tags to broaden the activity profile of the platform. In both groups, we identified tags whose acceptance communicated new opportunities on the platform: 44 tags in the building category and 80 tags in the shop category. With a total of 124 out of the 276 tags, these tag acceptances present the largest group in our sample.

First, as early as 2008, the platform operators considered establishing an initial generic category (building=yes) but dismissed the plans to add building descriptions with reference to the focus of OpenStreetMap as a general-purpose map. However, with increasing competition, the platform operators appreciated that building tags help generate new content and offered an opportunity for the platform. This decision effectively opened up a new category of metadata and was a foundation for a layer of describing database objects in the platform that had hitherto not been practiced. For instance, in 2014, during which most building tags were accepted, the decision was summarized as follows:

If we do not enter more details into the map (e.g., because we use too generic [tag] types), we will limit the possible use cases and reduce the information in the database. [OSM tag acceptance proposal on building tags, March 2014]

Second, the tags in the shop=category followed a similar pattern. Metadata descriptions detailing commercial operations were long disputed on the platform. However, with a growing level of detail on the map and rising pressure to ensure that the map would remain useful, the decision was made to add the shop=category of tags. One operator remarked in his vote to include shop tags:

I support a new shop=<shop category> tag.... It makes things easier than sorting through amenity= tags for shops. [OSM Wiki, October 2006]

Similarly, another operator stated:

I think the proposal of separating this tag from the amenity stuff is a good idea. I think it would be better to have a separate category for shops, shop= bakery, butcher, etc. Then, shops can be rendered with a generic icon and the system doesn’t have to know about every kind of shop. [OSM Wiki, December 2006]

## Endorsement Signals

The second signal type evident in our data was what we refer to as the endorsement signal. We classified tags as endorsement signals when the acceptance of a tag was motivated by selective support of third-party use cases. OpenStreetMap geodata are used by a variety of external applications. These range from specific mapping services (e.g., routing and navigation) to general purpose applications using geospatial information as part of their product (e.g., Foursquare). External application developers take an interest in the OpenStreetMap tagging scheme, especially in the tags accepted on the platform. Using accepted tags as part of a specialized application ensures continued smooth operation of the third-party’s offering.

Through our in-depth exploration of tag acceptance proposals, we identified 72 acceptances that accommodated third-party requests or supported the external use of specific tags in the future. Such types of support included endorsing the use of a tag in end-user applications, as well as ensuring technical interoperability with mapping software such as editors or rendering engines. The tags accepted in this category were motivated either directly by an external application and its usage of a specific tag or by operators who made strong cases that accepting a certain tag would generate content by means of supporting external use cases.

In one example, the proposal page for accepting the tag highway=priority states:

This [tag] will help a safety app or automated vehicle know that emerging side-road vehicles may create a hazard at that main-road junction. [Presentation of the tag in the OSM Wiki]

Other pages, for instance, the page on the tag highway= traffic\_calming, point to benefits for external apps:

Having just been routed by my SatNav down a road with half a dozen speed bumps even though it is marked on maps as a tertiary route, I was struck by the need to have this information in the map data even if it is not displayed, so that these ways can be avoided. [OSM Wiki, December 2006]

In another case, a third party developer argues:

A reasonably new feature for OpenTripPlanner [a third-party application] is the ability to read highway=elevator nodes. When these nodes are connected to ways with levels ... they should be interpreted correctly. [OSM Wiki page of the external application, April 2012]

The acceptances of the remaining tags were not distinctly attributable to either one of the signals or to an additional strategic interest. For these tags, the information content on the proposal pages was too limited. However, these tag acceptances are nevertheless valuable for our study. They share the objective of increasing the level of detail of displayed map content, as well as providing clarity for platform complementors. Acceptances that do not present a discernible interest therefore act as a reference point for the efficacy of the signal types identified. We, therefore, include these tags as additional controls in the analysis.

## Data

The confirmatory hypothesis testing drew on an OpenStreet-Map database excerpt of tagged geodata. We obtained the full history of all geodata objects ever created in Europe from May 2009 to December 2014. The time frame was chosen to reflect data model changes in April 2009, thus ensuring consistency in the data structure (Ramm et al. 2010). As of August 2019, 71 tags describe at least 0.05% of all objects globally. Of these, 16 tags fit our sampling criteria (general purpose; nontechnical; nonadministrative), and we selected the same 10 high-level, general purpose tags studied in the first stage of the analysis. In addition to the qualities described earlier, these tags are also representative of the platform as a whole: the tags represent \~10% of all data objects, with each tag describing > 1.0 m objects globally (> 0.05%) and hundreds of possible key=value combinations.

We used the database excerpt to generate two data sets, each focusing on important aspects of platform growth: volume and diversity of platform content. To test the effects of signals on the volume of content, we used information on the use of each tag. In particular, we counted the number of database objects associated with each tag per month between May 2009 and December 2014. To test the effect on diversity of content, we conducted a random selection of OpenStreetMap objects tagged with one of the sampled tags over the same period. This information was used to compute the number of tags on each GPS data object and thus to reflect the level of detail of the map. The data in both samples run for 67 months.

## Quantitative Analysis

For the quantitative analysis of the volume and diversity of platform content, we implemented a modeling approach that offers high flexibility and interpretability while being thoroughly grounded in the methodological convention. To analyze the effects of signals on the volume of platform content, we used a logistic regression to assess whether tags accepted in line with signals are likely to grow faster than the rest of the platform. For our investigation of the effects of signals on the diversity of platform content, we used a quasi-Poisson model to compare the effect of an acceptance on the level of detail each tag provides before and after it is accepted. In both cases, we drew on the generalized linear modeling framework to enable the analysis of nonnormal data structures through linear inferences while ensuring interpretability (Hilbe 2011; Hosmser et al. 2013). In the following section, we detail our modeling approach for each aspect of growing platform content before presenting the results.

## Signaling Effect on Diversity of Platform Content

To test whether signals also increase the diversity of platform content, we analyzed the number of metadata descriptions that occur together with tags before and after a signal is sent.

Our variable of interest consisted of count responses. Counts represent the recorded tags per object, that is, the number of metadata descriptions that appear on each GPS data point. Rooted in the empirical context, tag counts are discrete and nonnegative; while OSM objects with no tags are very rare in the entire OSM database, they are impossible in our sample since objects are included on the basis of having at least one tag $( \mathrm { e . g . , } h i g h w a y \mathrm { = } )$ . This results in a nonnormal distribution of all tag counts in the sample (see the appendix for details). In the absence of normality and the inadvisability of transforming count data, we conduct the analysis using a quasi-Poisson regression (Bolker 2008; Hilbe 2011, 2014; Winkelmann 2008).

Quasi-Poisson models have been demonstrated to be efficient and flexible alternatives to more complex modeling approaches (Ver Hoef and Boveng 2007), and the approach is particularly useful to our research design for three reasons. First, as is often the case with real life count data, our sample does not adhere to equipersion; that ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ the variance does not equal the mean, and overdispersion is consistently greater than 1 (Hilbe 2011, 2014). Quasi-Poisson models extend the Poisson distribution through dispersion parameter estimates that account for the extra variation relative to the Poisson (Hilbe 2011, 2014). Indeed, quasilikelihood tests (Bolker 2016; Ver Hoef and Boveng 2007) of our base models yield a > 30% increase in precision for the quasi-Poisson model compared to stricter Poisson models, indicating that a flexible treatment of dispersion is needed to describe the data (Hilbe 2014).

Second, initial exploratory analysis of our sample suggested the applicability of a quasi-Poisson approach, as we found a linear relationship between the sample mean and variance. This indicated quasimodel specifications as the preferable choice. Under linear mean-variance conditions in overdispersed count data, the quasi-Poisson approach ensures more accurate estimates compared to possible underestimation of the variance when assuming normality or overestimation when assuming the quadratic relationships inherent to negative binomial distributions (Hilbe 2011; Ver Hoef and Boveng

2007). A linear mean-variance relationship is a key indicator of the applicability of quasi-Poisson models for count data analysis. Following Ver Hoef and Boveng (2007), we used mean squared errors for this diagnostic step (see the appendix for details).

Finally, as a nonparametric technique, the approach does not require a probability distribution function. Instead, the covariate effect on mean variations is analyzed. This is adequate in our research design, as we are interested in the differences in the average number of metadata for each signal type and model the expected response in the group means (Hilbe 2011). Observations are i.i.d. and no repeated measures are taken.

In sum, the use of the quasi-Poisson approach offers a robust and interpretable method whose estimates account for overdispersed count responses with high flexibility (Hilbe 2014). With $\Upsilon _ { c o u n t } { sim } P o i s s o n ( a )$ , the model is thus denoted by

## Robustness Checks

We applied a range of robustness checks to ensure confidence in our results. Initially, controls for three major confounders were added to both models.

First, the 2012 change-over to an Open Database License affected the logic of how and which data objects on the platform are available for reuse. The change commenced in July 2010, after which every registered user was automatically signed up to a new license agreement. From September 2012 onward, the new license was in place, and data objects that were not certified for reuse were automatically redacted. As a major external shock to the platform, the license change influenced all data use and had potential implications for both predictors and outcomes in our models. We, therefore, reran the analyses in both models using the above dates as cutoffs for subset analyses of objects affected by different licenses. Using the full models, we achieved consistent results, indicating that the effects reported here are not affected by the license change.

Second, using the OpenStreetMap wiki as the main source for our classification of tags necessitates that we control for possible confounding due to de facto acceptance by the operators. While tags are typically endorsed by a majority vote among operators, some tags are endorsed in less transparent de facto decisions. That is, operators unilaterally decide to change the status of a tag such that it appears as if it has been collectively accepted, but no majority vote is held. We therefore crossreferenced such tags to control for measurement errors arising from tags that may well be accepted but have not undergone a voting process. We reran the analyses on these subsets using the full models and achieved consistent results. However, in these subsets, tags that were accepted but did not fall within the conceptualized signal types perform substantially better in the quasi-Poisson model.

Finally, we ran subset analyses on the tenure of tags, that is, the amount of time each tag was documented and discussed on the community wiki. A source for potential bias is that the effects on volume and diversity of outputs after acceptance of a tag are mere functions of the age of a tag. For instance, objects with older tags could accumulate additional tags over time, or conversely, objects with recently accepted tags could be richer in description assuming that tag acceptance fills an awaited need for users. We reran the analysis with different cutoffs of the tenure of tags and achieved consistent results. The effects are robust for the analysis of volume for cutoffs below/above the midpoint of the observational timeframe. For the analysis of diversity, the effects are robust for the time point after the signal.

In addition to controlling for the three potential confounders above, we implemented two post hoc tests to rule out that the explanatory variable “tag acceptance” is endogenous. Therefore, we applied a control function procedure following Rivers and Voung (1988) and Train (2009), as well as an instrumental variable approach (Wooldridge 2002). The results of both tests do not show signs of endogeneity and are reported in the appendix. Furthermore, we ran both models with standardized coefficients and included alternative control variables (such as number of key=value pairs or number of users over time). (NB: These variables are omitted from the main analysis due to multicollinearity with the control for the number of objects that AIC tests indicated as the preferred variable.) We obtained consistent results.

## Discussion

The generation of platform content by platform complementors is highly desirable for platform operators as it builds and maintains the value of their digital platform (Boudreau 2012; Cennamo and Santalo 2019; Eaton et al. 2015). Our study proposes that signals offer a powerful way for platform operators to guide content generation, as extensive communication with each complementor is often virtually impossible. Anecdotal evidence suggests that platform operators announce future plans (Parker et al. 2017), endorse preferred practices (Ho and Rai 2017; Rietveld et al. 2019), or create new use cases (Föderer et al. 2018). However, a systematic investigation of how platforms communicate with platform complementors in regards to generating platform content is absent in the platform literature. To this end, we derived and tested the effect of two specific signal types (signals of new opportunities and signals of endorsement of existing activity) on the generation of platform content in the context of the geodata platform OpenStreetMap. In what follows, we discuss the findings of our research.

## Opportunity Signals

Opportunity signals alert complementors to new opportunities on the platform. In our research, we find evidence that such signals are highly effective for generating both a greater volume and greater diversity of content on the platform. As opening up areas on the platform for interaction among participants such as users and complementors is vital for growing a platform (Boudreau 2010; Parker et al. 2017), signaling can help enact such strategies. Opportunity signals indicate the interest in extending the possibilities to engage with the platform by making design choices known (Baldwin and Clark 1997; Tiwana 2014) and articulating the operator’s plans (Parker et al. 2017). Opportunity signals need to be sufficiently clear to be able to steer input in a way that aligns with the platform operator’s strategy. A clear signal by the operator indicates the degree of authority (Budhathoki and Haythornthwaite 2012) needed to enact the strategic interest behind the signal.

In particular, the finding echoes the need for platform operators to identify desirable variation on the platform that needs to be communicated to complementors to guide input to certain areas and not others (Boudreau 2010; Wareham et al. 2014). This finding thereby indicates how signals can help create stability in some areas of the platform while enabling variation in others (see Baldwin and Woodard 2009). The acceptance of a tag implies stability to the extent that the accepted tag is no longer intended to change. At the same time, the acceptance of a tag points to areas where more variation is welcome: Tag acceptances invite all platform complementors to engage and innovate with derivative offerings by interacting with the accepted tag in a new area. Thus, the identification of undesirable variation induces desirable variation somewhere else (Wareham et al. 2014), making signals a potentially powerful lever for managing platform growth.

In this regard, our research underlines that signaling where and how interaction with the platform is needed and preferred increases platform content generation in volume and diversity. Opportunity signals advertise the prospect of growth in the installed base (e.g., Hagiu 2006). If platform complementors act on this prospect, this leads in turn to the generation of content. However, while this speaks to the platform governance literature (e.g., Eaton et al. 2015; Karhu et al. 2018), it must be emphasized that platform signaling is distinct from direct means of control, such as releasing application programming interfaces (see Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2013; Karhu et al. 2018). Rather than designing the structural arrangements that control whether and how content can be generated by complementors, operators can use signals to guide where such content generation is desirable. Signals thereby afford the operator the ability to enact its strategic interests by mobilizing complementors without engaging with them directly.

A potential hazard for opportunity signals and their enactment of opportunity creation is a scenario where a platform operator enters in direct competition with complementors (Wen and Zhu 201p; Zhu and Liu 2018). This calls for careful planning as signaling an interest in a new activity can send mixed messages to complementors. In such cases, the platform operator risks simultaneously undermining the efforts of its complementors when creating opportunities for new activity (Cennamo and Santalo 2019; Gawer and Henderson 2007; Li and Agarwal 2017). The true motive is opaque to complementors because the new opportunity could also involve a platform operator occupying a complementary activity for its own gain (e.g., Eisenmann et al. 2011).

In our study of OpenStreetMap, we find no evidence that new opportunities competed with existing complementors. In the absence of direct competition with complementors, the operator’s signal is likely to be interpreted as a clear, productive, and positive signal that the platform intends to grow and likely will offer future opportunities for complementors. Thus, the operator reaffirms what content is desirable on the platform and thereby supports momentum in complementor activity (Rietveld et al. 2019). This suggests that the strong positive effects in our analysis are driven by the careful identification of the strategic interest behind the signal and the clarity of the communication.

## Endorsement Signals

Endorsement signals intend to increase activity in existing areas of the platform. Such signals clarify the platform operator’s interest in having platform complementors involved in the generation of specific content. Ultimately, such signals suggest predictability in the platform’s approach to complementors and instill confidence that platform complementors can use platform resources in the long run (see Wareham et al. 2014). Endorsement signals thus demonstrate the ability and willingness of a platform operator to accommodate platform complementors.

Our results present a mixed picture of endorsement signals. Tags affected by endorsement signals grew in volume. In this regard, the signal provided direction for platform complementors to engage with the platform in a manner desired by the operator (see Ho and Rai 2017). However, endorsement signals did not lead to the generation of more diverse platform content. In other words, tags affected by this type of signal were not associated with more detailed descriptions of the database objects in our sample.

In what follows, we explore two possible explanations for this result: signal strength and signal consistency. First, the signal strength might have been too weak, especially in cases where the signals were intended to selectively support some platform complementor activity over other activities. Since signaling is an indirect means of control on a platform (see Connelly et al. 2011), certain signals might be lost in the noise because they are meaningful to some but not all platform complementors. In cases when a signal does not reach its intended receivers, its efficacy vanishes.

The inconclusive effect of endorsement signals points to the debate on overcrowding on platforms (Boudreau 2018). Signaling the use of one tag over another might well aim to prioritize a certain complementor’s use case over another. However, if relationships on the platform are already established, the platform operator might have limited means to effectively intervene and guide content generation toward someplace else without sacrificing core interactions (Parker et al. 2016).

This observation confirms that the direction that can be exercised in platform governance is limited (Eaton et al. 2015; Wareham et al. 2014). Consider that tags are resources on the platform boundary (Eaton et al. 2015; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2013). As such, any tag in use represents potential interactions between actors on the platform—irrespective of an endorsement by the platform operator. As boundary resources, tags are pliable and subject to ongoing negotiation among platform stakeholders (Eaton et al. 2015; Kallinikos et al. 2013). Complementors might contest the platform operator’s decision to accept one tag and not another, lobby for the inclusion of their favorite tag, or ignore platform signals altogether by continuing to use a preferred tag.

Another possible explanation of the ineffectiveness of endorsement signals in guiding the generation of diverse platform content is signal inconsistency (Connelly et al. 2011). A signal is ineffective “if the receiver is not looking for the signal or does not know what to look for” (Connelly et al. 2011). For instance, in the case of signals that communicate the ratification of unanticipated use cases, the desired effect has already happened: content has been added to the platform and spawned new complements. Signals of endorsement therefore risk appearing inconsistent if the desired outcome can exist both in the future and the past. In cases where the desired effect has already happened, a signal, no matter how well intended, is less useful.

## Platform Signaling

On a general level, platform signals address the information asymmetry (Ho and Rai 2017) between platform operators and platform complementors. They assist in enacting the strategic interest of the platform operator through an indirect means of control (Wareham et al. 2014) while the operator remains at arm’s length with complementors (Eaton et al. 2015). Platform operators make design choices explicit through signals motivated by an interest in guiding desirable variation on the platform. If a signal prioritizes one kind of platform interaction over another, complementors are influenced to adapt. In the long run, such signals increase the reliability and decrease the uncertainty of platform governance (see Tiwana 2014), leading to favorable conditions for content generation on the platform.

While our findings show that only opportunity signals are effective for both increasing the activity within an existing platform area and boosting the generation of diverse content, it does not necessarily mean that endorsement signals should be seen as generally less useful. In building and maintaining their core interactions (Parker et al. 2016), platform operators will face a trade-off between the diversity and the volume of content offered. In some cases, the activity profile changes to the extent that the platform operator’s strategy risks being undermined. Consider that a content platform such as Spotify was long focused on increasing the volume of similar content (major music artists). Only over time did Spotify start to branch out and increase the diversity of content (e.g., independent artists, podcast content). As a result, platform operators might prioritize endorsement signals over opportunity signals to strengthen activity in existing areas and retain their current activity profile instead of opening new directions. Ultimately, the decision to deploy either signal type might vary depending on the strategic interests of a platform operator at a given time.

Last, signals help platform operators make their design choices, strategic decisions, and future aspirations explicit and known to platform complementors. This insight challenges the conception of seemingly hands-off generativity in platform governance research (Eaton et al. 2015; Wareham et al. 2014). While generativity is fueled by “uncoordinated audiences” (Zittrain 2006, p. 68), our research shows that a platform operator can offer direction without directly intervening in the actual generation of content on the platform. Platform signals make otherwise unobservable qualities explicit and thereby serve as means of indirect control. This, in turn, allows platform operators to engage with and articulate the behavior they desire from complementors (see Rietveld et al. 2019).

Our research contributes to the platform governance literature and has significant implications.

## Implications

First, our study provides a detailed and empirically grounded account of content generation on digital platforms. Much of the prior work on platform content generation deals with the governance challenge of reconciling the tension between control and flexibility. Complementing these efforts, our study develops a perspective on platform signaling to guide the generation of content on a platform. Our conceptualization of signals as deliberate actions by the platform operator that communicate where and what input is expected from platform complementors helps to frame further research on platform governance. Signaling presents a potentially powerful lever with which platform operators can steer interaction across the platform. Hence, our findings underline the impact of purposeful actions to control the tensions that arise from diverse interactions on the platform and provide evidence of the ability to guide platform content generation through signaling. In this regard, our study speaks to how platform participant behavior can be steered toward desirable variation on digital platforms (see Ho and Rai 2017; Wareham et al. 2014) and fuel further growth in the platform. Exploring these dynamics further offers a fruitful line of inquiry for future platform research. For instance, we studied a single mechanism of signaling. The acceptance of a metadata tag is akin to the standardization of a resource at the platform boundary (e.g., Eaton et al. 2015; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2013; Karhu et al. 2018). However, signals can be manifested in a variety of actions by the platform operator. They can be explicit (Parker et al. 2017) or implicit (Förderer et al. 2018) and perhaps even unintended. In striving to understand how growth and innovation on a platform can be cultivated through such actions, a range of signals are likely relevant, and their investigation motivates future work.

Second, we offer a conceptualization of strategic interests pursued through platform signals. While prior platform governance work touches upon the relevance of intervention by platform operators, our research explains how opportunity and endorsement signals manifest strategic intent. Future research could expand on our study of the strategic interests of platform operators and how they can be expressed in signals. For instance, there is always the possibility that alternative strategic interests exist that we neither expected nor observed (see Kelle 2015). The presence of a group of tags in our data that was accepted without a discernible rationale suggests that unobserved interests exist. Therefore, reflection and potential elaboration are needed in further studies on signaling and the strategic interests of platform operators.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we study platform signals and their influence on the generation of platform content. Our research extends the existing platform governance literature with a detailed view of how the generation of content on digital platforms can be guided by platform signals. A platform operator can use signals to enact strategies and communicate where and which content contributions are desired on the platform. Given the appropriate identification and communication of desirable content qualities by platform operators, signals can guide the generation of content on the platform in terms of both volume and diversity. To increase the efficacy of signals, platform operators should pay attention to the strength and consistency of the communication of the signal needed to stimulate generation of the desired content, attribute, or feature. Executed correctly, signals can provide a powerful lever with which platform operators can guide the generation of content on digital platforms and thereby support platform growth.

## Additional Analysis: Core Community Members as Platform Operators

We conceptualize a core group of OpenStreetMap contributors who vote on tags as operators of the platform. This group represents <0.01% (= 0.00893%) of all registered users who contribute geo-data. Theoretically, all contributors are able to discuss and vote on tags. In practice, however, it is merely a very small, persistent, and influential group who engages in tag acceptance.

The core group (i.e., the platform operator)

(1) represents <0.01% of all OSM contributors (i.e., users who registered AND contribute geo-data).

(2) decides on multiple tags endorsements; nearly every second vote (\~44%) is cast by a contributor who voted on tags before.

(3) is persistently active; one in five contributors who vote on tags, continue to be involved in tag votes over the entire observational time frame (2009-2014).

(4) exhibits behavior typical to “core” members of a community; contributors who voted on more than one tag are in the 95<sup>th</sup> percentile of contributors to the community mailing list on the subject of tag usage.

In conclusion, there is significant evidence that there is a core group of contributors exerting influence over the platform, its direction, and usage. The group’s size is massively disproportional to the impact of their actions and decisions. We thus conclude that the core group acts as the platform operator and by accepting tags wields influence through a stimulus that affects the platform—and its future development—as a whole.

## Data and Details

We examined tag endorsement proposals (Wiki pages describing tags) including votes. In our sample, 99 tags were decided by vote. The remaining tags were unilaterally “accepted” by a member of the core group.

## Group Size

In our sample and across the observational timeframe, 226 contributors voted on more than one tag proposal (560 unique contributors in total). On average, the group of contributors voting on tags therefore represents <0.01% (=0.00893%) of all contributors of geo-data (in 2014 the tota was \~151,000). Over time, the share decreased from 0.0026% in 2009 to 0.00012% in 2014 due to the group of contributors growing over time, whereas the group of contributors voting on tags remained small.

In addition to group size, three aspects underline the role of this group as core to the community and as de facto operating the platform.

## Influence

In our sample, every tag that underwent voting by the small core group saw recurrent voters, that is, these members voted on more than one tag. As a result of this, an average of 44% of all votes on tag endorsements come from contributors who voted on other tag proposals, too. This range varies from 17% to 78% in a given year.

## Persistence

Comparing early and later votes on tag proposals reveals that 18% of contributors who vote are active in the early, as well as in the later phases of the platform. In particular, this means that just under 1 in 5 votes in the time from 2009-2014 came from a contributor that already voted on a tag in the time leading up to 2009. This represents a large share of influence as tag proposals are voted on by an average of 8 (SD = 3.4) contributors.

## “Core” Membership

Contributors that engage in the voting take on roles typically associated with behavior of “core members” of a community. A case in point, contributors who voted on tags more than once are in the 95<sup>th</sup> percentile of active participants in the community mailing list on the topic of using and discussing tags. This is based on a sample of 20,000 e-mail messages among users and measures contributor activity as the number of email messages sent to/responding to the mailing list.

In sum, it is clear that a small group of map contributors decides over a large share of the votes on tags, and does so persistently over time. We can thus conclude that a very small group of users, with a vested interest in the operation and output of the platform, disproportionally affects all other users.

Decisions taken to accept a tag are thus controlled by a group that is insignificant in numbers, yet whose actions have significant effect for the platform. In so doing the core group acts as a de facto operator deciding over direction and development of the platform.

As a result, there are very good reasons to speak of this core group of mappers as the platform operator.

## Post Hoc Checks for Endogeneity

We used a control function (CF) as well as an instrumental variable (IV) approach to address potential concerns of endogeneity in the reported models.

1. For the logistic regression we achieved consistent results addressing the endogeneity of the of the binary treatment (i.e., tag acceptance).

(a) first followed a two-stage control function approach formulated by Rivers and Voung (1988) and well documented by Train (2009, Chapter 13). We modeled the residuals of the potentially endogenous treatment (acceptance) as an additional predictor for the logistic regression and achieved consistent results and an insignificant effect for the endogenous portion as a predictor (p = 0.6462).

(b) The logistic model results are robust when modelled as a linear probability model (LPM) using an ordinary least square estimator. Using this specification, we modeled the control variable “Tags Documented” as an instrument variable (IV) in a two stage least squares approach (compare with Wooldridge 2002, p. 85). We assume that the share of all tags documented in a tag group does not directly influence the outcome (the coefficient of the control is not significant). However, we can expect that it affects the likelihood of the event “tag acceptance” to occur since a number of tags are accepted in batches as well as in succession within a given group (i.e. key=). We hence used a Wu-Hausman test to compare both models (LPM and IV) and can reject the alternative hypothesis that the instrument variable specification is inconsistent with the linear probability specification in a model with the full dataset (H : IV = LPM, df = 8500, p = 0.1331) as well as a data set with a reference group of 100 tags that are never accepted (H<sub>0</sub>: IV = LPM, df = 596, p = 0.561). The variable, tag acceptance, is hence not significantly endogenous.

2. For the count data regression, we used a control function approach similar to 1(a) and confirmed that the endogenous portion of the treatment (i.e., whether a tag is accepted or not) is an insignificant predictor in the full count model (p = 0.310) (compare with Wooldridge 2002, p. 663).
