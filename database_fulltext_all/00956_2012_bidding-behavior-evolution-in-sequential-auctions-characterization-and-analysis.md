# BIDDING BEHAVIOR EVOLUTION IN SEQUENTIAL AUCTIONS: CHARACTERIZATION AND ANALYSIS $^{1}$

Paulo B. Goes

Management Information Systems, Eller College of Management, University of Arizona, 1130 E. Helen Street, Tucson, AZ 85719 U.S.A. {pgoes@eller.arizona.edu}

Gilbert G. Karuga
School of Business, University of Kansas, 1300 Sunnyside Avenue,
Lawrence, KS 66049 U.S.A. {gkaruga@ku.edu}

Arvind K. Tripathi
Information Systems and Operations Management, University of Auckland Business School,
Auckland, NEW ZEALAND 1142 {a.tripathi@auckland.ac.nz}

Retailers are increasingly exploiting sequential online auctions as an effective and low cost distribution channel for disposing large quantities of inventory. In such auction environments, bidders have the opportunity of participating in many auctions to learn and choose the bidding strategy that best fits their preferences. Previous studies have mostly focused on identifying bidding strategies in single, isolated online auctions. Using a large data set collected from sequential online auctions, we first characterize bidding strategies in this interesting online environment and then develop an empirical model to explain bidders' adoption of different strategies. We also examine how bidders change their strategies over time. Our findings challenge the general belief that bidders employ their strategies regardless of experience or their specific demand. We find that bidders' demand, participation experience, and auction design parameters affect their choice of bidding strategies. Bidders with unit demand are likely to choose early bidding strategies, while those with multiple unit demand adopt late bidding strategies. Auction design parameters that affect bidders' perception of demand and supply trends affect bidders' choice of bidding strategies. As bidders gain experience within a sequence of auctions, they start choosing late bidding strategies. Our findings help auctioneers to design auction sequences that maximize their objectives.

Keywords: Sequential online auctions, bidding behavior, bidding strategies, auction design

## Introduction

Online sequential auctions are becoming an important distribution channel for retailers who intend to dispose of a large inventory of items. Dell and Sam's Club, for example, routinely conduct a series of auctions selling identical items through their own auction sites to reduce large inventories. These auctions often span several weeks. Other retailers use auction houses, such as eBay.com or Overstock.com, for a similar purpose. Bidders in such sequences have the opportunity to participate in multiple auctions, and as they do, they learn from the experience and fine tune their bidding behavior in order to maximize their payoff. This evolution in bidding behavior affects the nature of market demand in these auctions. Hence, understanding bidding behavior in this environment is of fundamental importance for auctioneers. Insights from bidding behavior are critical to the design of auction mechanisms, which directly affect the auctioneer's revenue, the appeal of this new marketing channel to consumers, and the efficiency of the goods—consumers allocation.

Sequential auctions of identical items add interesting aspects. Since a bidder can participate in several auctions, she can observe the outcomes, make inferences, learn, and adapt her behavior or participation strategy. It can be argued that the sequential auction environment induces heterogeneity among participating bidders. We confirmed this assertion from real-world auction data, where we observed the coexistence of new and repeat bidders with different levels of participation experience. Figure 1 shows a common instance of high percentage of repeat bidders together with first-time bidders in a sequence of close to 100 auctions conducted by a well-known retailer through its online auction platform.

We would expect that bidders, who have the opportunity to participate in many auctions for identical items, will change and adapt as they gain experience by participation. Therefore, we would expect bidding patterns that are specific to the sequential environment. In this research, we are interested in understanding what drives the adoption and adaptation of the bidding strategies from an economic perspective, a consumer behavior perspective, and an information systems perspective.

We employ a two-stage approach. In the first stage, we use an inductive, data-driven approach to characterize and identify bidding strategies in the sequential environment. The taxonomy developed in this stage contrasts the bidding strategies in uniform price sequential auctions with the ones previously observed in isolated Yankee auctions. The second stage is intended to explain why bidders choose different bidding strategies. We develop an explanatory model to gain insights on the drivers of bidders' choices and their changes over time. Our aim is to investigate the participation strategies adopted by repeat bidders (Figure 1) in sequential auction environments. Based on economic and consumer behavior theories, we hypothesize about the factors that influence these decisions.

This study adds to the body of literature on auctions in general and online auctions in particular. Our unique data set on sequential online auctions allows us to track individual bidders over time and helps us explain how bidders select and change their bidding strategies over time. We demonstrate bidders' heterogeneity in demand and participation experience and connect this heterogeneity to their choice of bidding strategies. Our contributions are at two levels: theory and practice. Online sequential auctions are considered consumer engagement platforms as well as digital marketplaces. We shed light on important aspects of these economic information systems and inform the economics literature of auctions and consumer behavior. The performance of the auction mechanisms in the online environment, and in particular sequential auctions, is governed by a plethora of factors beyond consumers' valuations. We have identified these factors and analyzed the process of bidding, critical in these engagement platforms. We analyze the processes and examine how the users approach them, and how they use and learn from the information set that defines the platform. Economists may use these findings to analyze the performance of the auction mechanisms and digital marketplaces. Marketing and consumer behavior scientists can leverage the results to better understand digital engagement platforms. The second level of our contributions is managerial. Sequential auctions have been and will be increasingly used in various online environments to engage customers in repeated interactions. While we study an environment that is prevalent with retailers (disposing of inventory), online giants such as Google and Yahoo use sequential auctions in repeated interactions of selling keywords for online search. In both such examples, it is crucial that the auctioneer understand what drives the behavior of repeat consumers, so that the appropriately designed selling mechanism is in place. The auctioneer continuously collects data on the bidders and their strategies. Using the collected data, our findings can guide auctioneers in the design and implementation of sequential auctions that best fit their needs.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In the following section, we present an inductive, data-driven model to identify bidding strategies in sequential online uniform price auctions. The details of our conceptual model and the theoretical developments that underpin our analyses of strategy adoption and adaptation are then presented. Next, we develop a bidder choice model to explain why bidders choose certain bidding strategies. We conclude our research by summarizing our findings and providing future research directions and implications for sequential auction design. We also elaborate on how our findings can be used by auctioneers to maximize their objectives.

## Identification of Bidding Strategies in Sequential Auctions

In the offline, traditional environment, Rothkopf and Harstad (1994) argue that, due to uncertainty in the number of future auctions and the mix of bidders, the theory of repeated games is not appropriate for investigating bidding strategies in sequential auctions. Further, in the environment of non-sequential, multi-unit auctions, determining equilibrium bidding strategies is computationally intractable (Nautz and Wolfsetter 1997). In a nonsequential environment of isolated online auctions, Bapna et al. (2004) follow a data-driven, inductive approach that confirms bidder heterogeneity in online auctions. They further develop a taxonomy of bidding strategies, which has been validated in a few studied of online auctions (Bapna et al. 2008; Slonova 2005). Here we advance that taxonomy to fit the sequential auction environment.

Bapna et al. (2004) define bidder participation in an auction as the tuple $[TOE, TOX, NOB]$ . Time of entry $(TOE)$ captures the time of the first bid placed in the auction, while time of exit $(TOX)$ clocks the time of the last bid by the bidder in the auction. NOB is the total number of bids placed by the bidder in the auction between TOE (time of entry) and TOX (time of exit). These variables together capture the degree of participation (behavior) of a bidder in an auction, and can also be associated with the participation strategy pursued by the bidder. Through a K-means cluster analysis on a large data set from Yankee auctions, the following general behavior profiles or strategies were identified:

\- Participator: a bidder with low TOE, high TOX, and high NOB. This bidder actively monitors the auction throughout its duration and bids actively.

\- Evaluator: a bidder with relatively low or medium TOE, which generally coincides with TOX. This bidder places close to one bid (NOB = 1). This is the profile of a bidder who knows how much she wants to pay and does not want to spend time actively participating throughout the auction.

\- Opportunist: also a bidder with NOB close to 1, but high TOE and high TOX. This bidder places her bid toward the end of the auction, looking for a bargain. This general strategy has also been identified in the eBay environment as sniping.

In the sequential auction environment, because a repeat bidder participates in many auctions of the sequence, we augment the definition of a bidder's participation in an auction by adding the variable TLA (time since last auction). With this modification, the bidding behavior of a bidder in an auction is captured by the tuple [TOE, TOX, NOB, TLA]. TLA is the time elapsed between the current auction and the previous auction in which this bidder participated. This elapsed time between successive participation is akin to the recency measure in the RFM (recency, frequency, and monetary value) analysis in catalog mailing (Bitran et.al. 1996), which we discuss next.

Literature on catalog mailing explains consumers' repeat buying behavior (Bitran and Mondschein 1996; Fader et al. 2005; Schmittlein et al. 1987) by classifying consumers based on the time since last purchase (recency), frequency of purchases over time (frequency), and monetary value of last purchase. This approach is commonly referred to as RFM (recency, frequency, and monetary value) classification. In these studies, recency and frequency capture the evolution in buying behavior over time. Since bidders are heterogeneous in their participation cost (Cheema et al. 2005), bidders with a higher participation cost are more likely to skip some auctions in a sequence than others.

## Data Collection

The data used in this study was collected from a well-known online auction house (retailer) where bidder registration is mandatory. This feature is important because it allows tracking an individual bidder over multiple auctions. The auction house conducts a sequence of identical item auctions to dispose of a large inventory. This data set allows us to observe evolution of bidding strategies as bidders participate in many auctions of a sequence.

Auctions at this auction house are open, ascending, multi-unit Dutch auctions. Multi-unit Dutch auctions are uniform price auctions, where winners pay the lowest winning bid (Vakrat and Seidmann 2000). We developed a software agent that periodically downloaded bidding activity for a period of 6 months from the auction house website and recorded it in a database. We observed that this auction house conducted many auctions (sequence) of identical items. Using their unique identifiers, we tracked bidders from the first to the last auction that they attended in these sequences. We used only those auction sequences that began and concluded within our data collection period. Our sample data comprises of 6,107 auctions for 910 different items. The data set is rich enough to provide valuable insights into the environment of sequential auctions and repeat bidders. We acknowledge that there are also some limitations in this data set. For example, the data set is not rich in terms of variability in lot size across different auctions, which may limit our ability to explore the effect of lot size on bidding behavior. ## Cluster Analysis and Results

Results of the cluster analysis are shown in Table 2 and Table 3. The variables TOE, NOB, and TOX measure intra-auction participation choices. The variable TLA measures inter-auction participation, and is unique to the sequential environment. TLA is the time (days) between the current auction and the most recent previous auction in which a bidder participated. Since auctions in our data set (all conducted by the same seller) do not vary much in auction duration, TLA measures the time gap between shopping, and also relates to the number of auctions that are skipped in between successive participation by the same bidder.

We used K-means clustering to identify the bidding strategies. This method divides the data set in several clusters and minimizes intracluster distance while maximizing intercluster distance. Since the number of clusters is not known a priori, we employed the concept of the dissimilarity ratio (Ray and Turi 1999) to identify the best number of identifiable clusters. We repeatedly cluster the data by changing the number of clusters and observe the resultant cluster dissimilarity ratio to select the clustering partition with the largest dissimilarity ratio. All of the variables used in clustering the data are found significant (Table 2). The significance of TLA underscores the fact that sequential auctions induce heterogeneity in participation. The results of the identified optimal clusters are shown in Table 3.

The seven clusters that are presented in Table 3 are well defined according to the dissimilarity ratio analysis and the statistical tests we performed. They displayed the best value of the dissimilarity ratio among all possibilities of cluster formation using the K-means algorithm. The formed clusters have a very interesting interpretation in the sequential auction environment and also relate to previous research that was done in the nonsequential environment. They define seven different bidding patterns which indicate how bidders approach auctions in the sequence and within each auction.

If we look at the mean values of NOB (number of bids submitted in the auction), with the exception of Participants (P-R), which contains 5.45 percent of all points, all clusters display values that are close to 1 bid per auction. In sequential auctions, bidders opt to increase their participation across the auctions, instead of within auctions.

The TLA values in Table 3 are also very informative. A high (low) value of TLA points to a longer (shorter) gap between successive participation and vice versa. We term strategies with low value of TLA as recurrent and others as intermittent bidding strategies. The clusters are generally separated along the values of TLA < 1 (recurrent) and TLA > 6 (intermittent).

The two variables that display greater separation across all strategies are those of TOE and TOX, which relate to the time the bidders start and end participation in each auction. For those bidders whose NOB values are close to 1 (the great majority), the values of TOE and TOX allow us to generally classify the bidders into early evaluators, middle evaluators, and opportunists (late bidding). Figures 2(a) and (b) plot the derived bidding strategies for recurrent and intermittent strategies, respectively, along the dimensions TOE and TOX. Note the number of points for which TOE = TOX, which depicts the high incidence of evaluators.

Early Evaluators (EE) enter and leave during the early part of an auction (low values of TOE and TOX) and submit one bid on average (NOB = 1). EE-R are recurrent early evaluators who have very little gap (TLA) between successive auctions in which they participate, whereas EE-I are intermittent early evaluators who have a large gap between the successive auctions in which they participate. In essence, EE-R and EE-I bidders are similar in how they behave within an auction and are different in how frequently they participate in sequential auctions. Middle Evaluators (ME) are similar to EE bidders, except they enter and leave in the middle of the auction. Opportunists (O) are similar to EE and ME bidders in terms of number of bids (NOB), except that they participate when the auction is about to close. We find these strategies in both recurrent and intermittent categories. Participants (P) differ from other categories in that they revise their bids more frequently in any given auction and stay active for a longer period of time. They are very clearly identified in Figure 2(a). We do not observe participator strategy in the intermittent category. Perhaps due to their low participation costs, these individuals keep participating in successive auctions, without skipping.

The vast majority of bidders adopt recurrent bidding strategies. In addition, 95 percent of the observed strategies are either evaluators or opportunists. In general, a bidder adopts a strategy to maximize her payoff in response to the auctioneer's choice of an auction mechanism. It is true that optimal bidding strategy and bidder's payoff varies with auction mechanisms. Interestingly, our results show that the overall bidding strategies within auctions are similar to the ones identified by Bapna et al. (2004), despite the fact that the underlying auction mechanisms are different (Yankee/discriminatory pricing in the case of Bapna et al. versus Dutch/uniform pricing in our case). These different incentive structures should, in principle, induce different participation strategies. Bidders are price-takers in Dutch (uniform price) auctions but not in Yankee (discriminatory price) auctions because, in Yankee auctions, their bids directly affect their payoffs. Even the distribution of bidders' adoption of these strategies (column 1, Table 3) is also similar to the one observed by Bapna et al. in Yankee auctions. This needs further investigation.

## Conceptual Model and Research Questions

Bidding behavior has been studied in sequential auctions (traditional environment, not online) and isolated, non-sequential online auctions. Previous studies on sequential auctions assumed a fixed set of homogenous bidders throughout the auction sequence (Engelbrecht-Wiggans and Menezes 2001; Menezes and Monteiro 1997), while our study investigates bidding behavior in sequential online auctions where bidders are heterogeneous in their demand (single or multi-unit) and experience (new and repeat bidders). We argue that bidders' heterogeneity (in demand and experience) expands their strategy space and we posit that this wider strategy space is explored in different ways by users of such economic information systems, as impacted by their demand requirements and their experience level in the sequence.

Earlier studies on bidding behavior in online auctions were largely focused on late bidding and concluded that late bidding is related to higher bidder experience and common-value auctions (Bajari and Hortacsu 2003; Roth and Ockenfels 2002; Wilcox 2000). Extending this line of research, Bapna et al. (2004) found that bidders in private value online auctions also use late bidding strategies along with a range of other bidding strategies. What is not known is why bidders choose certain strategies and if they change their choices over time. Our research contributes to this body of online auction literature.

We develop a conceptual model (Figure 3) to identify the causal antecedents of choice and evolution of bidding behavior in sequential online auctions. We investigate three sets of antecedents and their interactions: bidders' demand, experience in sequential online auctions, and the design parameters of the sequential auction environment. We build our conceptual model by first looking at the implications of demand heterogeneity, subsequently incorporating the impact of experience and learning (Kagel1998; Laslier et al. 2001), and finally sequential auction parameters.

In general, multi-unit auctions attract bidders with single-unit demand (SU hereafter) and bidders with multi-unit demand (MU hereafter). A sequence of identical item auctions conducted over a time horizon have similar characteristics; they also attract SU and MU bidders (Goes et al. 2010). MU bidders in sequential auctions are likely to be resellers (Vincent and Chanel 2007), while single-unit buyers are individuals looking for bargains. Owing to their demand structure and inherent preferences (individuals versus resellers), SU and MU bidders are different in how they learn and change their bids over time. Goes et al. (2010) have shown that MU bidders spread their demand over multiple auctions, exhibiting a modified form of the demand reduction phenomenon (Ausubel and Crampton 2002). Following these observations, we argue that heterogeneous demand bidders (SU and MU) have different motivations and thus follow different bidding strategies. SU bidders participate in many auctions to secure one win. MU bidders participate repeatedly to fulfill their multi-unit demand. We conjecture that bidders' demand affects how they choose and update their bidding strategies.

Sequential auctions provide repeated opportunities for bidders to fulfill their demands. The extended choice of auctions over time defines a rich learning environment for the bidders. Through repeated participation, they learn about other bidders and winning bid trends, and they gain experience. Because bidders start participating at different points in the sequence, we observe bidders of different experience levels in each auction in the sequence. This mix of new and repeat bidders brings another dimension of heterogeneity to the bidding population. Studies in consumer behavior have found that buyers' heterogeneity affects their choice of shopping strategies (Kamakura et al. 1996). For example, in sequential auctions, experienced bidders may postpone the buying decision in order to gain more information so as to reduce the risk of a bad purchase (Zhao and Kling 2004). Indeed, Jeitschko (1998) showed that in sequential auctions, bidders update their own bids by observing other bidders from previous auctions.

As bidders participate in more auctions, their information set becomes richer. As a result of the information gained from participation, bidders change their bidding strategies over time to maximize their utility, adapting their risk profile and participation cost structure. Owing to differences in demographic variables such as income, consumers have different search costs and rationally choose to engage in price searches to a greater or lesser extent based on these costs (Stigler 1961). We argue that in addition to demographics, the motivation underlying SU (individuals) and MU (resellers) bidders also affect the choice. We study three components of the sequential auction experience: participation experience, which is gained by mere participation in many auctions of the sequence, strategy experience, which relates to experience with a particular bidding strategy and is gained by using that strategy one or more times, and experience from winning an auction. We conjecture that these three forms of experience affect bidders' choice of bidding strategies.

It has been shown in the auction literature that auction mechanism design affects bidding behavior (Pardoe et al. 2006) and eventually seller's revenue. The seller can change various parameters of an auction mechanism such as number of units or lot size in each auction, auction duration, number of overlapping auctions, start price, or bid increment, to name a few. For example, it has been found that a lower starting bid attracts a higher number of bidders, resulting in higher revenue for the auctioneer. Similar results have been obtained for lower bidding increments.

Specific to the sequential auction environment is the fact that it infuses supply and demand uncertainties (Burguet and Sakovics 1997). As mentioned above, bidders have several choices (auctions) to attempt to fulfill their demand. We focus on the auction sequence parameters that affect supply estimates of the participating bidders and hence their bidding behavior. These are the number of overlapping auctions (Bapna et al. 2009) of the same item available on the website, and the lot size of each auction in the sequence. Price sensitive bidders looking for bargains can participate in many auctions and, in the process, obtain a better estimate of supply (possibility of future auctions of identical items) and demand (number of bidders) which in turn can influence their bidding strategy. For example, Easley and Wood (2004) found that bidders shade their bids with increasing market demand (number of bidders in an auction). Neugebauer and Pezanis-Christou (2003) have also argued that sequential auctions create supply uncertainties and bidder strategies are formulated on the basis of supply estimates. We argue that lot size in an auction and number of overlapping auctions affect the supply estimates of the item and hence affect bidding strategies in these auctions.

With this background we present our conceptual model in Figure 3.

## Understanding Bidders' Choice of Bidding Strategies

## Demand Heterogeneity

While both single and multi-unit demand bidders come to these auctions to find good deals, bidders with multi-unit demand are in general associated with resellers (Goes et al. 2010; Tu 2005; Vincent and Chanel 2007). SU bidders look for one good bargain whereas MU bidders desire multiple items to resell in secondary markets. Ausubel and Crampton (2002) have shown that multi-unit demand bidders use demand reduction as part of their bidding strategy to increase their surplus. For example, in a multi-unit auction, if a bidder desires two items, she will bid higher for the first item and lower her bid for the second item. While this observation was made in isolated online auctions, when there are sequential auctions of the same item, MU bidders will spread their demand over several auctions to better understand other bidders and market price trends to maximize their payoffs. This spreading of demand was called the modified demand reduction (Goes et al. 2010). Using arguments from economics and auction theory, we posit that the differentiated demand functions of SU and MU bidders lead to different bidding strategies.

The economics literature on auctions makes a distinction between private value and common value auctions (Vickrey 1961). In private value auctions, bidding is guided by bidders' own private valuation of the item for sale. In common value auctions, the value of the items is the same for all bidders, who do not have perfect information about that common value. Prior research is focused on auctions that are either private or common value auctions. However, it well accepted today that most auctions are not exclusively private or common value; rather, they have elements of both (e.g., Laffont 1997). We use an economic argument based on the common/private value paradigm to theorize how demand heterogeneity induces different late/early bidding behavior. Bajari and Hortacsu (2003) link late bidding to the common value of the items. By bidding late, bidders observe the common value component of the item and bid accordingly to maximize their surplus. Since MU bidders are generally resellers, we argue that their bidding behavior is influenced by the potential rent they can extract from the resale of these items in a secondary market. Late bidding allows MU bidders to observe others' bids and market trends. By doing so, they observe the formation of the common value and compare that against their reference selling price in secondary markets. Hence, we argue that multi-unit demand bidders are more likely to employ opportunistic strategies, bidding late to maximize their surplus and minimize the winners' curse.

On the other hand, SU bidders are not constrained by a reselling price and are willing to pay a premium for the value derived from their consumption, which relates to a private value component above the common value component. Therefore, we argue that bidding strategies of SU bidders are influenced by the private value paradigm. SU bidders are willing to pay a premium but not more than what is required for a win. Indeed, it has been shown that SU bidders begin with a low bid in their earlier auctions and increase later, as they learn by participating in other identical item auctions (Goes et al. 2010). In other words, SU bidders, who gradually increase their bids, are evaluating in every auction as if their revised bid could deliver a win. Following this evaluating approach, these SU bidders are more likely to adopt evaluator strategies.

As demonstrated by Bapna et al. (2004), a large segment of evaluators place their only bid early in the auction at a higher value than the current bid value that is necessary to win. Another reason that may influence such behavior is related to the reserve price effect. The reserve price is the price level below which the seller is not willing to sell the product. In many auction formats, the reserve price is revealed up front at the beginning of the auction. Many studies have shown that a higher revealed reserve price in auctions leads to fewer bidders and less bidding competition (e.g., Simonsohn and Ariely 2008). We argue that by placing a high bid early in an auction, a bidder can gain advantage similar to the reserve price effect. With the high bid, and consequently a higher winning level, the auction will tend to attract fewer bidders and less competition, thus increasing that bidder's likelihood of winning. A similar argument was used by Easley and Tenorio (2004) to explain jump bidding strategies in online auctions.

Formally, we hypothesize that

H1a: Single-unit (SU) demand bidders are more likely to adopt evaluator strategies over opportunistic strategies.

H1b: Multi-unit (MU) demands bidders are more likely to adopt opportunistic strategies over evaluator strategies.

## Strategy Experience

In this research, we ask if bidders learn and change their bidding strategies as they gain experience. We measure bidders' experience by participation experience, strategy experience, and winning experience. Participation experience relates to the duration that the bidder is connected to the whole auction sequence, regardless of which strategy she used in different auctions. Strategy experience measures the cumulative experience of using a particular bidding strategy and winning experience is gained by winning an auction. In the context of consumer choice among different purchase channels for the same product, Balasubramanian et al. (2005) differentiate between the purchase process utility and the purchased product utility. The purchase process utility is explained through a framework that contains both economic factors and behavioral factors. Here we make a parallel between purchase process utility in a multi-channel environment and bidding strategy utility in the sequential auction environment.

In the development of our hypotheses related to experience, we use the concept of behavioral learning from the field of behavioral economics, which combines the behavioral component of learning through experience and the economic construct of utility. Behavioral learning has been extensively used in the Marketing literature in the context of repeated purchases (Rothschild and Gadis 1981). It is an important concept in the field of repeated economic games (Ho 2009; Laslier et al. 2001). Of particular relevance to our argument related to strategy experience is the cumulative proportional reinforcement rule, a measure of the behavioral learning process presented in Laslier et al. (2001). From past participation, the player in repeated games assesses the utility associated with each utilized strategy and then uses the cumulative utility obtained in the past for each strategy to decide which strategy to choose in her next game.

We argue that when bidders use a bidding strategy a few times, they learn by gaining experience and insights on the advantages and disadvantages of that strategy, even if they have not yet won an auction. The percentage of time a bidder uses a bidding strategy enhances her experience on that strategy (strategy experience). The longer bidders use the same bidding strategy, the more strategy experience (SE) they have, which generates better assessment of their utility (satisfaction/dissatisfaction and comfort/discomfort) with the particular bidding strategy. We relate strategy experience to the cumulative proportional reinforcement rule of Laslier et al. This strategy experience guides their strategy choice in future auctions. Higher (lower) cumulative utility increases (decreases) the likelihood of continuation of the same strategy and vice versa. For example, higher experience with a participant strategy may imply the realization of high participation cost, thus affecting negatively the cumulative utility, which in turn may reduce their likelihood of choosing this strategy in the future. We posit that the choice of bidding strategies is determined by the bidders' strategy experiences from the previous auctions, and that the effect can be positive or negative.

The participation/monitoring costs associated with a bidding strategy are one of the main factors that form the basis of strategy utility. Here cost refers to time and effort needed to play a particular bidding strategy. Some strategies may have higher costs over other strategies. A participatory bidding strategy requires bidders to remain observant in an auction for a longer time compared to other bidding strategies. From the results of the previous section, we have seen that only 5.45 percent of the bidders employ a participator strategy (P-R) in sequential auctions. In nonsequential auctions, Bapna et al. (2004) detected a much higher percentage of bidders utilizing participatory strategies. We argue that bidders in sequential auctions consider the sequence, not necessarily an individual auction, as their framework for bidding, and the high monitoring efforts of a participatory strategy in each auction deter them from adhering to such a strategy.

The time and effort commitment for playing a participatory strategy is higher than other strategies. Higher cost with a strategy may reduce bidders' utility and net payoff and, hence, as bidders gain experience with participatory bidding strategy, they are more likely to move away from this strategy.

Formally, we hypothesize that

H2: A higher experience with participatory strategy will make bidders less likely to choose this strategy in future auctions.

## Participation Experience

The literature on online auctions suggests that participation experience induces heterogeneity among bidders in online auctions (Pinker et al. 2003). By having participated in many auctions, experienced bidders are likely to be more strategic compared to others. Roth and Ockenfels (2002) found that experienced bidders follow different bidding strategies compared than new bidders and are more likely to bid in the final moments of an auction (Wilcox 2000). Our research investigates the effect of participation experience on bidders' choice of bidding strategies in sequential auctions. Sequential auctions provide an opportunity to participate in multiple auctions of identical items to find better bargains. By observing/participating in multiple auctions, bidders gain valuable information pertaining to supply and demand, which is critical in estimating bidding trends and expected winning bids in later auctions. However, this information comes with a cost of participation, which is likely to affect the bidding behavior. Hann and Terwiesch (2003) examined the bidding behavior on a name-your-price service, where buyers can negotiate price in multiple rounds of bargaining, and found that the participation cost has significant effect on the bidding behavior. Engelbrecht-Wiggans and Menezes (2001) found that due to non-zero participation cost, after each auction, bidders determine whether to continue or drop out of the auction sequence.

Our argument here is microeconomic, one that explores the tradeoff between cost of participation and increased acquired market information. We believe that bidders who have been exposed to an auction sequence longer are more experienced and have more accurate priors about bidding trends and expected winning bids in future auctions than inexperienced bidders. As a result, these experienced bidders are likely to choose strategies that maximize their net expected payoffs while minimizing their participation costs. Therefore, we argue that bidders with higher participation experience are likely to skip a few auctions in the sequence, preferring intermittent bidding strategies. Formally we posit that

H3: Bidders are more likely to switch to intermittent bidding strategies as they gain experience by participating in many auctions.

## Winning Experience

Goes et al. (2010) find that MU bidders reduce their bids in subsequent auctions after winning. Wilcox (2000) found that winning has a significant effect in changing bidders' participatory behavior. Here we use the microeconomic argument from Marketing: the reference price. After winning, the winning bid consolidates the reference price for MU bidders and affects their bidding strategy in future auctions. A reference price is a price that consumers are assumed to form in their minds as a result of experience (Krishnamurthy et al. 1992). Prices above the reference price are perceived as losses and prices below the reference price are perceived as gains (Krishnamurthy et al. 1992). Hence, MU bidders are likely to continue to bid late in an auction when they see an opportunity of winning at a price lower than their reference price. Early bidding strategies do not provide this option because early bidding strategies require commitment from the bidders at the beginning of the auction without knowing the final clearing price, which changes from auction to auction.

Therefore, for MU bidders who tend to bid late in auctions, this late bidding effect is likely to be stronger after their first win. Formally, we hypothesize that

H4: MU bidders are more likely to bid late in an auction, after they have secured their first win.

## Sequential Auction Environment: Supply/Demand Factors

Auction mechanisms have significant effect on the choice of bidding strategies (Bapna et al. 2009; Pardoe et al. 2006). In this research, we investigate the effect of auction mechanism parameters on bidding behavior in sequential online auctions. In particular, we examine the effect of number of overlapping auctions and the lot size of each auction. These auction design choices increase the availability of information about the current supply and demand of the items being auctioned. Using microeconomic arguments we explore how the increased level of information affects bidders with different demand requirements and their approaches to the auctions in terms of their private value/common value perspectives. In the development of Hypothesis 1, we established that SU bidders tend to follow the private value paradigm, while MU bidders primarily approach these auctions through the common value lens.

## Effect of Auction Overlap

When auctions are sequential, bidders in the first auction do not see or know about upcoming auctions because the next auction begins after the first one is closed. However, in the case of overlapping auctions, the successive auctions overlap by varying degrees. A complete overlap results in simultaneous auctions. It is clear that bidders have more market information when auctioneers run overlapping auctions. While participating in one auction, bidders can observe the clearing price, the number of bidders, and the number of items sold in a previous auction (Anwar et al. 2006). Before the auction they are participating in closes, they are able to observe upcoming auctions for the identical items. Thus we argue that bidders learn more about market supply (number of auctions and items sold) and demand (number of bidders, winning bid) in the case of overlapping auctions. We argue that bidders (SU and MU) respond to changes in market supply and demand by adjusting their bidding behavior to maximize their surplus.

For an SU bidder, the existence of multiple overlapping auctions brings the potential for maximizing her surplus due to an increased supply of items. Earlier we hypothesized that SU bidders are likely to choose early bidding strategies. However, with the additional market information brought about by the existence of overlapping auctions, we posit that SU bidders will be more cautious with their choice of when they place their bids in the auction in which they choose to participate. They will wait for the information derived from the completion of an existing auction and/or the start of the next overlapping auction before they will bid in the chosen auction, leading to late bidding strategies. The additional market information will have the effect of moving the SU bidding paradigm from private value to common value. Also, if they are participating in multiple auctions simultaneously, they will not bid early in each auction to avoid inadvertently winning more than one item.

In overlapping auctions, winning bid and market competition information spills over from one auction to other overlapping auctions. Ascent of price or competition dynamics in these auctions may change after the closing of previous auctions and the beginning of the next overlapping auction. We argue that in such a dynamic environment, MU bidders, who were hypothesized to use late bidding strategies, are more likely to choose late bidding strategies to have better control on the final price they pay. Since the seller can start another overlapping auction at any time, bidding late gives MU bidders more information about the market demand and supply and expected winning bid in the current auction.

## Effect of Lot Size

The sequential/overlapping auctions allow auctioneers to run many auctions in a short duration. In other words, this auction design allows sellers to unload sizable inventory in a short period of time. In sequential auctions, auctioneers have another parameter to control the market supply: lot size or number of items sold in each auction. For example, for an inventory of 100 items, should the auctioneer run 100 auctions selling one item in each of them, 50 auctions with two items in each auction, or some other combination? There is a stream of research looking at how sellers can develop an optimal lot size policy for an auction sequence (Chen et al. 2011; Pinker et al. 2010; Tripathi et al. 2009).

It is easy to see that a large lot size will give the signal to the auction participants of an increased supply of items. A larger lot size also plays the role of compressing the prices down for the specific multi-unit uniform format that we study. For example, if the lot size is three and the top bids are \$140, \$120, and \$100 from three bidders, all of these bidders (winners) pay \$100 each, following the uniform pricing rule. In this case, a bidder can bid \$140 earlier in this auction but would still pay \$100 when the auction closes. An increase in the lot size in a uniform price auction gives bidders an opportunity to bid early and still realize a higher surplus (the difference between the price paid and the bidder's valuation). Therefore, we argue that SU bidders, who follow the private value paradigm and tend to bid higher than MU bidders (Goes et al. 2010), are likely to adopt early bidding strategies. Early bidding increases their chance of winning while still reaping a higher surplus owing to the multi-unit uniform price auction mechanism. We posit that a larger lot size will further strengthen the early bidding propensity of SU bidders hypothesized in H1a.

We have already stated that the motivation of MU bidders is different from that of SU bidders. The payoff of MU bidders is conditional on reselling the item in a secondary market for a profit. Being resellers, MU bidders are very sensitive to future demand. To MU bidders, a higher lot size indicates the auctioneer's desire to sell items quickly (in fewer auctions), either because of excess supply or in anticipation of dwindling future demand. Therefore, MU bidders are likely to continue with late bidding strategies to understand market value/prices and demand of these items.

Finally, we hypothesize the effects of overlapping auctions and the lot size as

H5a: As the number of overlapping auctions increases, SU bidders are more likely to adopt late bidding strategies.

H5b: As the number of overlapping auctions increases, MU bidders are more likely to bid late in an auction.

H5c: As the lot size in an auction increases, SU bidders are more likely to adopt early bidding strategies in uniform pricing sequential auctions.

H5d: As the lot size in an auction increases, MU bidders are more likely to bid late in an auction.

## Explanatory Model for Bidders' Choice of Bidding Strategies

## Measures

It follows from previous research and our conceptual model that the choice of bidding strategies in sequential online auctions is impacted by many internal and external variables. The exploratory clustering analysis (presented in the second section) identified the bidders' choice of strategies in each auction in which they participated. We now investigate the antecedents driving these choices. We then explain the variables we use in our model.

Since this is an ex post analysis, we were able to tag repeat bidders as SU (single-unit demand) and MU (multi-unit demand) based on their revealed demand. A bidder who participates in many auctions of an auction sequence (for an item) and drops out of the sequence after winning one item is categorized as an SU bidder. Bidders who continue participating in these auctions after winning, signaling their demand for multiple units, are categorized as MU bidders. We define a binary variable Demand, which is 1 for MU bidders and 0 for SU bidders.

We hypothesized that a bidder's experience with a bidding strategy determines whether or not she continues with that strategy. The variable $StrategyExperience$ measures how frequently each specific strategy has been used by a bidder prior to the current auction. For example, if a bidder uses the EE-R strategy in her fifth auction, we count the number of times she has previously used this strategy in all four previous auctions. If she used the EE-R strategy in two prior auctions, her $StrategyExperience$ value will be 50 percent (two out of four previous auctions). Alternatively, one could investigate the entire sequence of chosen strategies by the individual bidders. However, the size of our data set does not allow us to consider such an approach because the number of cases for each strategy sequence set becomes too small for meaningful statistical analysis. This needs further investigation in future studies with larger data sets.

Participation experience measures the length of time since an active bidder first participated in an auction sequence. The variable ParticipationExperience measures the number of days since the first auction for each bidder in each auction in which the bidder participates. We define the variable OverlappingAuctions as the count of the number of auctions that overlap with a particular auction and close after the current auction. In the case of pure sequential auctions, the number of overlapping auctions is 0. The variable LotSize captures the number of units offered in the auction in which a bidder is participating.

Our data set contains auction sequences for different products, which we control for in the analysis. We define the variable ProductCategory and categorize the products in two categories, Computers and Electronics and Non-Electronics. Apart from product category, there are a few other variables, such as seller's reputation and auction mechanism, that can affect bidding behavior in sequential online auctions, and could potentially be controlled for. We discuss them next.

Seller's reputation in online auctions is known to have a significant effect on bidding behavior. It has been shown (Ba and Pavlou 2002; Resnick et al. 2006) that negative feedback hurts the seller's reputation, eventually affecting bidders participation in the auctions conducted by that seller. Along with the seller's reputation, auction mechanisms are likely to affect the evolution of bidding behavior. For example, bidders are likely to bid lower in discriminatory auctions as compared to uniform price auctions where all of the bidders pay the same price. In our data set, we do not need to control for seller's reputation or auction mechanism because all of the auctions are conducted by one seller, a well-known retailer, and follow the same auction mechanism.

## Summary

Table 10 summarizes the findings of our hypotheses testing.

Further discussion of the interplay between participation experience and strategy experience is in order. We found support for hypothesis H3 from the values of the odds ratio for participation experience in the intermittent strategies presented in Table 6. However, a closer look at the odds ratios for strategy experience for intermittent strategies (Tables 6, 7, and 9) tells an intriguing story. It appears that after switching to intermittent strategies, bidders do not continue with them for long. It seems that as bidders participate in more auctions, they are likely to switch to intermittent strategies (H3 hypothesis supported), but the odds ratios of the strategy experience (less than one) suggest that intermittent strategies are transient strategies. Bidders who experience intermittent strategies do not continue with them and switch back to recurrent strategies. Lower values of odds ratios of the interaction term (SE × PE) support our argument. This cyclical phenomenon can partially be explained by the weekend effect (Kauffman and Wood 2005) and certainly deserves further analysis.

We also confirmed that strategy experience plays a significant role in the choice of bidding strategy, but it is interesting to see some transience when other effects are jointly considered. In the case of MU bidders, they favor late bidding strategies all along and continue using them throughout the sequence. However, SU bidders do not continue using the evaluator strategies they tend to start with. We find that SU bidders stick to late bidding strategies once they switch to those strategies (higher value of strategy experience).

These observations show that bidders do learn from their experience in sequential online auctions and adapt their choices of strategies to possibly maximize their surplus and chance of winning. In our data set, we did find that winning probabilities are higher for late bidding strategies. As mentioned earlier, Roth and Okenfels (2002) find that experienced bidders choose late bidding strategies. Our research adds more nuances to their finding by showing the evolution of bidding strategies with bidders' experience in sequential online auctions.

Although this data set is unique in that it allows us to track individual bidders over a period of the auction sequence to investigate evolution in bidding strategies, it also has some limitations. For example, the data set does not have bidders' demographic information and experience outside the auction site, which could affect some of our findings. However, these limitations are similar to other studies that use secondary data sets such as ours.

## Conclusions and Future Research Directions

To the best of our knowledge, results on learning and strategy adaptation in sequential auctions have not been previously reported in the literature. Rather, the general belief has been that the identified bidding strategies are common across all bidders regardless of experience or demand function.

In this paper, we first extend the characterization of bidding strategies of bidders in online auctions when they bid in long sequences of auctions of identical items. We extend the behavioral description of the strategic bidding space provided by Bapna et al. (2004) by incorporating a behavioral variable that captures inter-auction participation availed to bidders in sequential auctions. We identify a separation of bidding strategies by their participation persistence in the sequence. We refer to such bidders as recurrent bidders. A second general class of bidders is made up of intermittent bidders, who skip participating in some of the auctions in the sequence. Within these two groups of bidders, we observe taxonomy of strategies similar to Bapna et al. Our results confirm the stability of that taxonomy not only across different auction mechanisms but also in an environment where bidders participate repeatedly over time.

We examined three antecedents—demand heterogeneity, participation experience, and sequential auction environment—to explain bidders' choice of bidding strategies. We have clearly demonstrated that heterogeneity in demand affects bidding behavior. We also find that both strategy experience and participation experience have a significant effect on bidding behavior in sequential auctions.

Parameters of the auction mechanism that are related to the observed supply and demand of the items for sale play a role in steering bidders to one strategy over another. Auctioneers need to plan carefully the deployment of sequential/overlapping auctions so that the selling plan does not affect market demand in a significant way. An increase in the number of overlapping auctions drives SU bidders to choose late bidding strategies but an increase in lot size splits SU bidders into choosing evaluator or late bidding strategies. It is interesting to note that MU bidders appear to be fairly consistent in their choice of late bidding strategy.

Our results show that MU bidders begin with late bidding strategies and stick with that choice as they gain experience, while SU bidders begin with evaluator strategies but move on to late bidding strategies after gaining experience. We thus conjecture that bidders converge to bidding strategies associated with higher winning probabilities and higher surplus.

Along with the contribution to economics and consumer behavior literature, our findings have many practical managerial implications. Understanding focal market segments and consumers belonging to those segments helps sellers in the long run. Our study segments bidders based on their demand and experience, and investigates their choice of bidding strategies. Goes et al. (2010) have shown how bidders change their bids in sequential auctions over time. The findings from our study help complete the picture of evolution in bidding behavior: how bidders change their bid amount and bidding strategies over time in sequential online auctions. We next outline a framework for sequential auctions design by auctioneers, which is enabled by our research. We are currently working toward the implementation of the framework.

## Design of Sequential Auctions: A Framework from the Auctioneer's Perspective

The problem that the auctioneer needs to solve repeatedly can be summarized as follows:

Given the inventory of items to be disposed through a sequence of auctions and inventory holding costs, design the sequence that maximizes total revenues.

From historical data of sequences of similar products, and based on our findings, the auctioneer can develop predictive models to infer the general categories of bidders who will be present at the sequence: SU, MU, and non-repeat bidders. Our study also provides the categorization of bidding strategies. Goes et al. establish how the categories of bidders will display and update their willingness to pay (Bapna et al. 2003; Bapna et al. 2004) in successive auctions. Because of the complexity and the large choice set for all the parameters, empirically calibrated simulation (Bapna et al. 2003) needs to be used to examine the evolution of bidding values and strategies for various possible ways of designing the sequence of auctions. The specific bidding strategies and willingness to pay of various bidders' segments are simulated using our findings and empirical bidding data. The idea is to choose the sequence duration, the number of auctions, how they overlap, the lot sizes, etc., that maximize the revenues.

Once the sequence starts, further refinement of the sequence design can be implemented by closely monitoring bidding activity. This refinement can take place by fine tuning the auction parameters that better fit the existing bidder categories and their strategies. Real-time promotions (e.g., buy-it-now offers) are also a possibility that can be facilitated by a deeper understanding of the bidding population and their strategies.

Along with mechanism optimization, insights from our study can help design better bidding agents and learning tools for new bidders. Finally, for bidders, it is never too late to know with whom you are competing. Our findings help bidders learn others' type and their bidding behavior, which can help in selecting bidding strategies.
