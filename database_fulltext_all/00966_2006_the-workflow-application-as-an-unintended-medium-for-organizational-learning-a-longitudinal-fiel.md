# The workflow application as an unintended medium for organizational learning: A longitudinal field study

David Kang 米

Chapman University, The Argyros School of Business and Economics, One University Drive, Orange, CA 92866, United States

Received 6 October 2005; accepted 23 December 2005

## Abstract

Research on information technology (IT) and organizational learning has treated IT as either a subject of or an intentionally designed platform for organizational learning. In contrast, this study investigates the unintentional efects of IT use on organizational learning processes. The findings of this longitudinal field study suggest that an IT platform can serve as both reification and propagation media for experimentation with new work practices. The use of an IT platform can produce online profiles of new work practices and spread them within a user community immediately and simultaneously in a decontextualized fashion. As a consequence, the intensity of collective learning in the process of institutionalizing new work practices can be reduced. - 2006 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Longitudinal field study; Situated learning; Community of practice; Workflow; Organizational lear ning; Banking

## 1. Introduction

Research on information technology (IT) and organizational learning has consisted of two main streams of analyses (Robey, Boudreau, & Rose, 2000) that seem to assume implicitly that IT is a single-purpose technological platform. Whereas the first stream perceives IT as a technology platform for work and uses organizational learning concepts to explain IT implementation (e.g., Attewell, 1992; Martins & Kambil, 1999; Orlikowski, 1996; Robey & Boudreau, 1999), the second explores IT as a technology platform intentionally designed to support organizational learning (e.g., Boland, Tenkasi, & Te’eni, 1994; Goodman & Darr, 1998; Hine & Goul, 1998; Stein & Zwass, 1995). Out of these two main streams of research, however, some emerging empirical evidence and theoretical discourses (e.g., Ka¨ko¨la¨ & Koota, 1999; Kuutti & Virkkunen, 1995) suggest a third potential research stream: Might the use of an IT program designed as a technological platform for work unintentionally afect the associated organizational learning processes? This question is becoming increasingly relevant and important in light of recent widespread deployments of new IT applications, such as workflow and enterprise resource planning, as key business process platforms (e.g., Elmes, Strong, & Volkof, 2005; Newell, Huang, Galliers, & Pan, 2003; Robey, Ross, & Boudreau, 2002). Some of their distinctive characteristics seem to suggest that these new mission-critical IT applications have the potential to afect organizational learning processes.

This potential can be illustrated better with the concrete example of sales practices, in which context we can consider an organization’s sales practices at both individual and collective levels. In a traditional sales support IT environment, data about individual sales practices are scattered across a variety of public and private domain applications, and the customer records and sales transactions generated by a salesperson are likely stored in a database. These transaction records, however, report little about how the sales were made. The more revealing types of documents—such as forms, memos, customer correspondences, contracts, and meeting minutes—are generated by ofice applications and stored in private directories, so their dispersed, private storage essentially denies systematic public access to these documents. Suppose a shared workflow application, which enables salespeople to create and store documents online in a consolidated fashion, is deployed to support the sales function. As either an intended or unintended consequence, the publicly accessible nature of the new application makes the consolidated online documents available to others. In this sense, the new workflow application might efectively act as a propagation medium that transmits individual sales practices to other users quickly and collectively. As such, it has the potential to influence the collective learning process by which individual sales practices get assimilated into the collective sales practice.

If an IT application deployed as a work platform acts as a propagation medium, how does it afect the organizational learning process associated with the transformation in work practices that follows its deployment? Answering this research question is important for several reasons. First, examining organizational learning processes will yield knowledge about how and why an organizational learning outcome might be produced. Second, because IT is much more commonly deployed as a work rather than a learning platform, the research question pertains to an important, pervasive phenomenon. Third, research in this area will not only provide guidance for IT design and management but also contribute to management research on organizational learning.

To understand the efects of IT use on organizational learning processes, the process must be observed as it evolves in its natural setting. Therefore, I conducted a longitudinal field investigation to answer the research question. The following discussion addresses the conceptual underpinning of the study, methodology and research design, the results of the data analysis, and their theoretical implications for conceptualizing organizational learning processes.

## 2. The conceptual framework

Two comprehensive reviews of IT and organizational learning research reveal little on the subject of how IT use afects organizational learning processes. Robey et al. (2000) assert that IT and organizational learning researchers have been preoccupied with either the intentional use of organizational learning as an implementation strategy (IS) or the intentional use of IT as a learning platform. Similarly, Templeton, Lewis, and Snyder (2002) identify three ways to conceptualize the relationship between IT and organizational learning. First, IS can be an instrument to achieve superior single-loop learning. For example, Stein and Zwass (1995) propose that successful single-loop learning is facilitated by the existence of organizational memory performance standards, which often accompany the adoption of organizational memory information systems. Second, IS can stimulate double-loop learning. Stein and Vandenbosch (1996) suggest that the design of advanced IS can be turned into a unique organizational learning opportunity for strategic renewal. Third, IS can help organizational members learn how to learn. For example Alavi, Wheeler, and Valacich (1995) find that the use of desktop videoconferencing led to higher measures of critical thinking skill development, group commitment, and group attraction.

Many studies suggest that IT use can afect organizational performance as the result of organizational learning. For example, Orlikowski (1992) observes that the extensive use of CASE tools led to the unintended consequence of ‘‘trained incapacity’’ among consultants who attempted to perform systems consulting without CASE tools. Pentland (1995) finds that automation of an energy-auditing process led to the loss of knowledge about how to perform an audit. Elmes et al. (2005) discover that the use of an enterprise system resulted in greater visibility of information that empowered workers to work more eficiently and efectively. Kuutti and Virkkunen (1995) demonstrate that an IT application could both capture the common object of work and serve as organizational memory. Ka¨ko¨la¨ and Koota (1999) even propose the design of ‘‘dual information systems’’ that enable an organization to both enforce institutionalized work practices and assist in the creation of new computer-supported work routines. What these studies have not explored, however, is the efect of IT use on the dynamics of organizational learning processes.

The boundary object literature implies that IT may afect organizational learning processes. A boundary object is any object relevant to the practices of multiple communities but used or viewed diferently by each of them (Brown & Duguid, 1998). An IT application such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) can serve as a boundary object for diferent units within a large organization (Pawlowski & Robey, 2004) and thereby facilitate knowledge sharing among organizational units. From this perspective, an IT application can be regarded as a knowledge propagation medium. The question left unanswered, however, is how such a propagation medium might afect the dynamics of organizational learning processes. Furthermore, this study refers to knowledge that is both reified on and propagated through the same IT application.

Before contemplating the efects of IT use on organizational learning processes, it is necessary to define organizational learning and adopt an organizational learning process model. Templeton et al. (2002) define organizational learning as the set of actions—knowledge acquisition, information distribution, information interpretation, and organizational memory—in the organization that intentionally and unintentionally influences positive organizational change. This definition is adopted here without the restriction of ‘‘positive’’ organizational change. As noted by Huber (1991), learning does not always increase the learners’ efectiveness because entities can learn incorrectly or correctly learn that which is incorrect. In selecting a process model, the basic premise adopted here is that organizational learning is a multilevel phenomenon, a premise supported by a reasonable degree of consensus (Argyris & Scho¨n, 1996; Huber, 1991; Weick, 1991). Insight and innovative ideas occur to individual persons, not to organizations (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Simon, 1991); however, aggregated individual learning does not equal organizational learning. Ideas must be shared, and shared meanings (Argyris & Scho¨n, 1996; Huber, 1991) must be developed by groups through the mutual reciprocity of intersubjective typification (Burrell & Morgan, 1979) and shared interpreted enactment (Weick, 1979). At the organizational level, relationships must become structured, and some of the shared meanings must be institutionalized as organizational artifacts (Hedberg, 1981; Shrivastava, 1983).

The ‘‘4I’’ framework shown in Table 1 and advanced by Crossan, Lane, and White (1999) is just such a multilevel organizational learning process framework. It suggests that there are four subprocesses that bind the three entity levels together in an organizational learning process.

At the individual level, intuiting is the preconscious recognition of the pattern and/or possibilities inherent in a personal stream of experience (Weick, 1995, p. 25). The specific context of this study prompts me to use ‘‘local’’ rather than ‘‘individual’’ to represent the lowest level of learning entity. While appropriating a shared workflow application, though one person could learn alone, a collaborating partner often is involved in experimenting with a new work practice, and ‘‘local’’ better represents this bilateral nature of learning. Interpreting is the explaining, through words and/or actions, of an insight or idea to one’s self and others. In conceptualizing learning at this level, the 4I framework emphasizes the emergence of insights and ideas that may lead to action (Crossan et al., 1999, p. 524). In the context of this study, action is experimentation with a new work practice.

At the group level, integrating is the process of developing shared understanding among persons and taking coordinated action through mutual adjustments. Dialogue and joint action are crucial to the development of shared understanding. To define the group entity more specifically, I use the concept of a ‘‘community of practice’’ (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998; Wenger & Snyder, 2000) to represent a group. A community of practice consists of people who are informally bound by shared expertise and a passion for a joint enterprise or practice, such as engineers engaged in deep-water drilling or consultants who specialize in strategic marketing. As described subsequently, the group of bankers used in this study resemble a community of practice. Finally, at the organizational level, institutionalizing is the process of ensuring that routinized actions occur. Tasks are defined, actions specified, and organizational mechanisms put in place to ensure that certain actions occur. It is also the process of embedding learning obtained by persons and groups in the organization.

The use of IT can afect several subprocesses of organizational learning. At the level of local learning, a new IT application provides new tools and functionality that can stimu late creative local experimentations with new work practices. More important, an IT program is a reification medium. According to the concept of reification (Wenger, 1998), people produce work-related artifacts, such as rules, procedures, and forms, that coalesce some of the key characteristics of a work practice; similarly, a workflow application cap tures many types of work-related documents, along with the track records of their production. Furthermore, as discussed in Section 1, the IT reification medium is unique because it stores all types of documents in a consolidated fashion and therefore provides more com plete profiles of work practices.

In its role as a propagation medium, IT might also afect the integrating subprocess that should lead to shared understandings. The 4I framework identifies dialogue as the primary means of social interaction for this subprocess, a conceptualization clearly rooted in the sociohistorical perspective of organizational learning. For example, scholars who advocate the notion of a community of practice believe that shared understanding can be achieved only through narration and story-telling among members of the community (Brown & Duguid, 1991, 2000, 2001; Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998) and have observed that a hallmark of learning through social interaction is its situated nature. Brown and Duguid (1991) argue that abstractions detached from practice distort or obscure intricacies of that practice; thus, the practice itself cannot be understood, engendered, or enhanced without a clear understanding of those intricacies and the role they play. Following this argument, it is highly desirable for community members to consider a new work practice together with the specific context in which it was conceived when they integrate it into a collective work practice. When new work practices propagate through dialogue, their contextual informa tion is most likely conveyed as part of the thinking behind the new work practices. Com pared with the richness of social interaction, an IT such as a workflow application is a ‘‘lean’’ propagation medium that transmits only the reified part of a new work practice without rich contextual information. But though it lacks richness, the IT medium is capable of reaching a large audience quickly and simultaneously. These characteristics of IT as a propagation medium might introduce new constraints and opportunities for social interactions during the integrating subprocess.

Integrating and institutionalizing are closely related subprocesses (Crossan et al., 1999) because new routines, rules, and procedures of work cannot be institutionalized without collective learning. The efects of IT use on the integrating subprocess will also afect the institutionalizing subprocess. A critical issue of institutionalization is the perceived legitimacy of new work practices, which can come from explicit organizational sanctions or community members’ collective acceptance. In the latter case, the integration subprocess should precede the collective acceptance of new work practices. But hypothetically, the integration subprocess can be disrupted by the use of IT. For example, users might imi tate a new work practice propagated through IT before engaging in a collective dialogue.

If this happens, what is the source of perceived legitimacy associated with this new work practice? If many imitations happen, does the wide adoption of the new work practice necessarily legitimize it? If not, what happens next? These are especially critical issues during the period of active work practice transformation that follows the deployment of a mission-critical IT application.

To answer the research question about IT’s efect on organizational learning process, a combination of objective and interpretive evidence is needed. Objective evidence can demonstrate whether locally created new work practices are reified online and subsequently adopted by many other organizational members. However, to ascertain that these new work practices are propagated online and understand their implications, interpretive accounts of the organizational members’ views also are necessary.

## 3. Methodology

Case study is chosen as the methodology for this study because it is especially appropriate in new topic areas and often leads to novel, testable, and empirically valid conceptual frameworks (Eisenhardt, 1989). Using a critical case (Yin, 1984), I investigate how and why organizational learning processes might be unintentionally afected by IT use. Because no prior study has observed the unintended efects of IT use on organizational learning specifically, this study will have the revelatory advantage (Yin, 1984) of observing and analyzing a phenomenon previously inaccessible to scientific investigation.

Because organizational learning is a complex, fluid, and context-specific process, it needs to be examined closely and continuously in its natural habitat. This requirement points to the need to conduct a longitudinal field study. Empirical evidence suggests that experimentation with an innovative IT use is especially active during the few months immediately following the IT’s deployment (Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994). The role of IT as a propagation medium also might be most salient during this period, as local appropriations of the IT find their ways into the collective work practice. From these observations, I infer that the best time to study the unintentional efects of IT use on organizational learning is the several months following the deployment of an IT practice.

I choose observation as the primary means of data collection. As observed by Barley (1990, p. 224)

Participants may realize retrospectively that changes have occurred, but they are unlikely to remember how or why the changes arose. Thus, if researchers are to understand how a technology becomes embedded in a particular social order, they need to observe the actions and interactions that actually generate the order.

A good case study should collect multiple types of data, create a case study database, and maintain a chain of evidence (Yin, 1984). The design of the case study also needs to contain well-defined data collection and analysis procedures. The discussion of these detailed aspects of research design follows my description of the research site and the types of data collected; it is easier to explain them in a specific context.

There are two major concerns in choosing a research site. First, I need adequate site access to fulfill my data collection needs. Second, I need to observe an organizational learning process with suficient intensity. The research site should use a mission-critical IT that supports a business process central to a community of practice; otherwise, there might not be enough observed usage to support a rich analysis. The chosen research site was the use of an IT application that served as the commercial lending workflow platform for a large West Coast commercial bank (bank X).

## 4. Bank X and data collection

## 4.1. The commercial lending operation and the workflow application

Bank X has more than \$30 billion in assets and more than 3000 commercial lending cli ent companies. The workflow application was deployed as the primary technology platform for the commercial lending business process, as shown in Fig. 1. Three functional departments—the line, the staf, and the credit administration—within the commercial lending group jointly owned this business process. In processing a commercial loan package, a portfolio manager (PM) from the line unit and an account executive (AE) from the staf were jointly responsible for the first four steps shown in Fig. 1. The PMs generated new business leads, negotiated lending deals, and maintained borrower relationships. The AEs supported them by providing high-quality credit analysis. Whereas the PMs ‘‘owned’’ the clients, the AEs ‘‘owned’’ the credit documents. Depending on the size and complexity of a loan package, one to several staf analysts (SA) could be assigned to the responsible AE as assistants. Once a loan package was compiled, the AE and PM would jointly submit it a senior credit administrator (CA) from the credit administra tion department for approval.

The parties involved in this commercial lending business process work from geograph ically dispersed locations. The PMs were stationed in field ofices close to their clients, whereas the AEs, SAs, and CAs worked from three centralized staf department sites. Prior to the use of the new workflow application, their collaboration relied on an antiquated document management infrastructure consisting of filing cabinets, interofice mail, fax, express mail, and file servers. The deficiencies of this infrastructure were obvious: Paper document exchange could take days; version control did not exist; a PM could not read a document in preparation; no one could get access to credit documents when away from the home ofice; and documents belonging to the same loan package were stored in diferent places on diferent types of media. As a result, the turnaround time was long, which management perceived as a serious handicap in a highly competitive market.

The bank commissioned the new workflow application project to overhaul the technological infrastructure of document management to reduce the commercial lending cycle time. At the center of the new workflow application was a Notes database that organized all commercial banking credit documents by borrowers and loan packages. The new workflow application provided extensive functionalities for viewing, routing, and collaborative writing. For example, the application provided 16 diferent views that enabled its users to sort the loan packages dynamically by responsible person, owner ofice, status, and so forth. The bank decided to make all documents viewable by all PMs, AEs, and SAs. During the design phase, the AEs voiced strong concerns about giving CAs unrestricted document access because they feared that a CA could see a work-in-progress loan package and became biased against it on the basis of the preliminary analysis. The general management concurred with that opinion and restricted CAs’ access to only those loan packages that had been submitted for approval.

## 4.2. The profile of the staf community

I primarily investigated the staf members (AEs and SAs) because they were the main users of the new workflow applications. The PMs and CAs were casual users who mostly read and occasionally responded to the credit documents. Confirming descriptions in the situated learning literature, new work practices propagated within the staf community through extensive dialogue and story telling. This propagation of new work practices had a couple of interesting characteristics. First, the social interactions were usually driven by particular problems or interesting issues at the moment. Rarely did inventors of new work practices broadcast them without being asked or stimulated by others. On the receiving end, there were no means for a staf member to systematically scan all emerging new work practices before the use of the new workflow application. It was socially inappropriate and time consuming for a user to survey new work practices by asking all co-workers or checking their dispersedly stored credit documents. Second, stories were generally relayed from one small group to the next by their common participants. This relay took time and often did not reach every member of the community. Interofice social interactions were much weaker and infrequent. Although bankers sometimes called their coworkers in other ofices to ask pointed questions, and some switched or alternated ofices on diferent days of a week, beyond these limited contacts, there was no interofice social interaction.

## 4.3. Data and data collection procedure

Onsite data collection lasted for nine months during 1996 and 1997: three months before and six months after the general deployment of the new workflow application. It was terminated at that time because empirical evidence (Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994) suggests that learning is most active during the first few months following IT deployment. The data I collected and coded also indicated that the intensity of experimentation diminished after the first 20 weeks.

At the beginning of the fieldwork, the bank management introduced me to all the bankers at several meetings and described my engagement with the bank as a quid pro quo arrangement. The bankers were told that the quid was for me to learn IT use in the banking industry and the quo was my technical support service to the bank. Management also explained to the bankers that I was not a paid consultant and would not report to management. By the end of the month, I became a friend and a regular member of their lunch crowd. The bankers seemed unconscious of my presence, even in their most politically charged discussions of banking. During the first three months, I examined the bank’s traditional commercial lending practice through document study, interviews, observation, and meetings. During the six months after the deployment of the workflow application,

I served as the system support director in the bank’s Southern California headquarters to provide face-to-face technical support for 18 PMs, 24 AEs, 8 SAs, and 6 CAs who worked in the Los Angeles ofice, as well as telephone support for 10 AEs and 3 SAs who worked in the Orange County ofice. I also spent one day per week in the Orange County ofice to provide face-to-face technical support for that group.

Several types of data were collected. Among them, the most important was the online document base. When the data collection ended, this document base had stored more than 1000 commercial loan packages containing approximately 12,000 documents and served as an objective partial record of commercial lending work practices. Every online document had a log section that tracked its creator, creation time, and history of exchanges and deci sions. This log section signified aspects of work practices, including work delegation, divi sion of responsibilities, and document routing patterns.

The second type of data were the descriptive notes of the 596 help events I personally handled. A help event would happen when a user encountered some problem with the new workflow application. Many of these problems involved both technical and task-related organizational issues. My interaction with a banker during a help event was usually face-to-face, and my inputs remained strictly technical. Under some circumstances, I could write down parts of conversations that happened during a help event. All help events were documented in detail immediately after their conclusion. Other types of data were also collected to provide a contextual understanding of the learning activities. I maintained constant informal engagement with the bankers on occasions such as cofee breaks, lunches and after-hour chats. I also attended all user meetings. When new practice patterns emerging in the document base came to my attention through browsing or hearsay, I would informally interview all the bankers who espoused such practices to understand the sequence of events and their opinions.

## 4.4. Data analysis procedure

A three-step procedure, as shown in Fig. 2, was used for data coding and analysis.

The emerging work practices were identified by reading help event notes, following leads provided by the bankers, and coding documents kept in the workflow application. A common reason that users asked for help was that they encountered problems while using the application in ways that deviated from their training. These ‘‘deviated’’ IT appropriations often indicated the emergences of new work practices. Because the bankers knew my interest, they frequently dropped hints, such as ‘‘you may want to take a look at this,’’ either when I worked with them or when they passed by my cubicle. To follow up such leads, I would then scan the document base and write down what I found. In addition, I coded each credit document (approximately 6500) produced by the bank’s two Southern California staf ofices according to their document control sections and views. At the end of each document, there is a control section created and maintained by the system that registers all actions, such as creation, status change, and transmission, that have been performed on the document, as well as the users who carried out the actions. In addition to the user views discussed previously, the workflow application had more than 90 views built for various internal system auditing needs. One such view sorted all loan packages by creation time and was particularly useful for the purpose of tracking what I had scanned. The document control data precisely registered instances of work delegation, loan package composition, loan submission, and loan approval that deviated from ‘‘normal’’ practices.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. The workflow application as an experimental platform

Because experiments with new work practices generated what was reified, I must describe such experiments before considering their reification. The new workflow application, designed as the commercial lending work platform, provided new functionalities, such as a shared public document repository, electronic document routing, transfer of document editing rights, and transaction logging. The appropriation of these functions stimulated local experiments of new work practices at the weekly frequencies shown in Fig. 3. These activities first peaked around week 4, at which point the users had acquired suficient understanding of the new application to explore its adaptations. The next two peaks, around weeks 8 and 14, both correspond to peaks of commercial lending activities. Heavier workloads during these two peak times increased the variety of commercial lending cases and consequently provided richer stimuli for innovation.

Slightly more than half of the users’ experiments were conducted to improve individual productivities. For example, users explored new ways of formatting inserted ASCII texts, inserted multiple documents as attachments into fields on forms that were supposed to hold only one object, and used the system as a backup tool for noncredit documents. These technically oriented IT adaptations generally did not constitute organizational learning activities and are therefore not the focus of this analysis.

Space limitations preclude a full discussion of all the other local experiments that were significant for organizational learning, but before examining a few representative experi ments in-depth, I provide brief descriptions of a few more examples to enable a concrete, broader understanding of the experiments. One innovative appropriation of the new workflow application was to store discussions about documents as subdocuments by ‘‘respond ing’’ to the main credit document. This local experimentation started a new work practice by turning previously private informal discussions about a credit document into public record. This new work practice was later adopted by others and afected transparency in the work place and learning activities among peers. Another example was the changed way in which AEs requested help from administrative assistants. With the new system in place, quite a few AEs started to assign administrative assistants as editors of their documents, often before leaving work for the day so that they could work from home the following day and call the administrative assistants to give them document change instructions. Previously, this procedure would have been dificult because documents were stored privately. Furthermore, this new work practice later led to debate about the hiring standards and salary of administrative assistants. Some experiments clearly ofered collec tive benefits. For example, the users learned to copy and adapt entire loan packages from similar prior deals that they and others had created, which was not possible before the IT introduction because the privately stored credit documents were not accessible to people other than their authors. Because well-done loan packages were much more likely to be copied, this new work practice both saved time and improved the overall quality of loan documentation.

Not all local experiments with new work practices escalated to the group and organi zational level, but many, such as the following three cases, did. These experiments are all examples of ‘‘work-around’’ activity in exceptional situations (Strong & Miller, 1995). One such experiment was the sharing of document templates among diferent users. Similar to other examples, this case was initially documented as a help event. Please note that I could not document the entire conversation because the bankers involved needed my technical assistance from time to time.

One afternoon, Gale (AE) from a remote ofice called Anita (AE) to find out if she had a template for a certain type of customer correspondence letter, and Anita answered positively. Gale then asked Anita to ‘‘email attach’’ the template to her. But Anita would rather drop the template as an OLE object on a form in the new workflow application because she hated the bank’s text-based email interface. (This part was reconstructed based on Anita’s debriefing afterward. Anita called me into her cubicle at this point and put Gale on the speakerphone.)

Anita: ‘‘Gale, I want to try out the new gadget and someone’s here to help.’

Gale: ‘‘What’s that?’’

Anita: ‘‘I mean RACER [the nickname of the application]. I just created a blank form under your loan package, and I’m going to drop the template into the field where we usually store the credit write-up. Just a moment.’

(Anita asked me to help her drop in the template)

Anita: ‘‘Gale, click the refresh sign at the upper left corner and double click on the new form.’’

Gale: ‘‘Sounds good to me.’’

Anita and Gale soon ran into a problem. The application was designed to allow only the creation of specific loan packages using 27 standard types of forms. They could create a form within a real loan package for temporary document sharing, because it would be deleted later. But doing this on a permanent basis would ‘‘contaminate’’ the hosting loan packages. To solve this problem, Gale and Anita created a loan package with a fake borrowing company to store all their templates.

Sharing document templates was not a new problem, but the new workflow application made a new solution available for this old problem. By putting the document template in a public document repository, Anita and Gale could work on the same copy of the document template and automatically keep the updated copy after current and future changes without incurring additional communication costs. This learning happened as a local activity, in that Anita and Gale seemed preoccupied with the local need of template sharing rather than how it would afect the collective work practice.

The newly introduced capability of direct electronic document transfer stimulated some local experiments that made certain traditional organizational control mechanisms inefective. One such experiment was conducted to evade a document exchange restriction during the loan approval phase. In the manual world, an AE would pack all credit documents in a yellow folder and submit it to a senior administrative assistant, who would verify the completeness of the loan package and pass it to a CA. The CA would simply approve or reject the loan or, more often than not, ask for clarifications, modifications, or even further analysis. In the latter case, the CA would instruct the assistant to return the entire loan folder to the AE. The senior administrative assistants served as the unyielding gatekeepers in this process. The bankers referred to them as the ‘‘wall’’ and frequently used the colloquial ‘‘throw it over the wall’’ in place of ‘‘loan submission.’’ Although the new workflow application was designed to mimic this traditional loan approval practice by restricting document exchanges between AEs and CAs to entire loan packages, it was easier to beat the system-based control, as evidenced in the following experiment. The following description of the experiment is based on the two bankers’ debriefing afterward and the notes of my observation:

One day, Tom (CA) asked Kim (AE) to make a small change to just one document in a loan package that Kim had just electronically submitted for approval. Right before returning the entire package, it occurred to Tom that he really would like to send back just the single problematic document because he did not want to evoke an unwarranted feeling of rejection. Kim also liked the idea because she just needed the document that should be changed. Having used the new application for several weeks, both felt that the new technology platform was an excellent testing bed for this idea. They agreed that it might cut down commercial lending cycle time if AEs could get feedback during, rather than after, the credit analysis process so that some mistakes could be avoided early. Stimulated, they experimented with the idea of informally transmitting just a single document. A ‘‘backdoor’’ in the application was soon uncovered. It turned out that the single document exchange blocking mechanism could be evaded by copying the particular document as a ‘‘doc-link’’ (the Notes equivalent of a URL) and dropping it into a Notes mail message. The receiver of the Notes message could easily ‘‘sneak into’’ the loan package to work on the document without getting the whole loan package back. Tom and Kim were thrilled by this technical discovery that would enable them to interact with each other more casually during the loan approval process.

This new work practice invented by Tom and Kim efectively introduced AE–CA interaction into the loan approval process, a practice previously prohibited by the bank on the basis of its concern that CAs’ involvements in credit analysis would induce decision biases due to their premature commitments to loan packages.

In another local experiment, the new work practice violated a rule that was formally articulated in the bank’s credit policy manual but was not enforced by the system.

Alan (AE) was assigned five business credit card applications from five brothers and sisters, each of whom owned a small business. They were virtually the same, but a full-blown loan package had to be created for each because they were separate legal entities. He did the first package in 2 h. It was already 8:00 p.m., and the deadline was the next morning. Alan figured that he could do one more package, plus other associated overhead, before going home. (The preceding is based on Alan’s debriefing afterward.) Alan called Mary (SA), who was also working overtime on something else, and me into his cubicle.

Alan: ‘‘Mary, can you create three packages following my example?’

Mary: ‘‘Are you sure? I thought only AEs could create and submit loan packages.’

Alan: ‘‘I know. But look at these little packages. The bank doesn’t even earn enough on

them to cover my time. Plus, we have a deadline to meet.’’

Mary: ‘‘They are your packages. Your wish is my command.’’

In the previous world of paper documents, the administrative assistants of the staf department enforced the rule that only AEs could create and sign loan packages. But this human form of control was again absent in the world of electronic document management. Alan acted on the basis of his belief that the extraordinary situation he faced made the formal control ‘‘unreasonable.’

New IT functionality could also change the meanings of some old questions. Consider the following mundane request made by a PM to an AE: ‘‘Can you give me the document? I would like to make a few changes.’’ Prior to the use of the new workflow application, this request meant that the PM wanted to mark some suggested changes on a copy of a document. Both the PM and the AE understood this because the AE always kept the original document on his or her private drive or file server directory and did not release any ori ginal documents. But a document stored in the workflow application is the original copy controlled by the person with the right to edit it. The familiar question now implied changing the original document.

The same AE who received this request had a diferent decision to make, as shown in the following story:

Sharon (PM) asked Errol (AE) to transfer the edit right of a credit analysis document to her because she wanted to make some simple changes. Errol was hesitant to honor this request as usual because he worried that Sharon might make some unauthorized changes that he might not even detect in a 17-page document. But being a good-natured person, Errol found it dificult to reject Sharon’s request because that would imply distrust and consequently harm their collaborative relationship. Errol finally honored the transfer request and later found that his worry was not unwarranted. Sharon instituted many changes to make the analysis much more lenient on the potential borrowing company. Errol regretted that transfer of edit right while trying to recall his original analysis in the process of arguing with Sharon and undoing most of the changes.

Because the AEs were charged with the responsibility of fending of potentially bad loans, they oficially controlled the credit analysis document that was the centerpiece in a loan package. Errol’s decision could be seen as an experiment that extended edit right to a PM.

## 5.2. The workflow application as a reification medium

In all the preceding cases, the workflow application stored not only the documents but also their creators, transfer paths, attached exchanges of comments, and sequences of creation. Because of this consolidated document storage, the new workflow application acted as a reification medium that portrayed more comprehensive profiles of work practices than the old fragmented document storage infrastructure did. The reification happened as both an intentional and unintentional consequence of local experiments. In the case of sharing document templates online, Anita and Gale intentionally left the fake loan package and the document template online for future reference. In the other cases, the reification was the unintentional result of work. For example, the fact that Mary created the three loan packages and submitted them for approval was registered online as soon as she finished her work. The system automatically recorded Mary as the owner of the three packages, whether she wanted that attribution or not. But regardless of why reification happened, these ‘‘footprints’’ of local experimentation with new work practices in the pub lic document repository became visible to others.

## 5.3. The workflow application as a propagation medium

Because all the bankers, except the CAs, could view the credit documents online, the credit documents as reified footprints of new work practices were broadcast to the community immediately. This actual broadcasting efect, however, was not stated as a system design objective; rather, the bank’s stated objective was to create a workflow platform that would provide document management for intrateam collaborative work. That is, it was not billed as a learning platform for the entire staf community of practice. However, the variety of Notes views provided a convenient means to sort the credit documents according to diferent viewing needs. For example, a user could easily spot the miscellaneous loan package created by Anita and Gale by using the view ‘‘loan packages by type’’ or notice Mary’s name in the view ‘‘loan packages by owner.’’ Therefore, the new workflow application was a potential propagation medium, but this potential could only be fulfilled when users on the receiving end tuned in to it, for which there is strong evidence.

The users performed both problem-driven searches and general surveys of the documents stored in the workflow application. They frequently searched the application for previously compiled loan packages that were similar to those on which they were working and thereby find loan templates or answer specific questions. The four-digit standard industrial classification code stored in a field on every loan package cover sheet made this search easy. In addition, there were strong indications that general scanning was widely conducted. Many bankers frequently dropped me a hint, such as ‘‘you may want to take a look at this,’’ that was obviously based on their knowledge of what was online. Most bankers also told me that they regularly read CAs’ responses to loan packages to understand their ‘‘hot button’’ issues, which enabled them to preempt those issues before they submitted their own packages for approval. Sporadic critical events, such as the following example, also seemed to heighten their interest in scanning the system. One day, Jack (an AE team leader) waved me into his ofice and pointed at the informal exchanges between an AE and a PM documented online. They used very unflattering terms to describe the CA who rejected the loan package. Jack waved his fist and told me, ‘‘If anyone on my team does this, he will get this.’’ Another indirect but nevertheless strong indication of system scanning was that the users rated knowledge sharing as the most important benefit of the workflow application in a survey conducted by the bank three months into its use.

System scanning activities were often intermingled with dialogues and story telling. For example, after the document template sharing experiment, I observed that Anita’s neigh bors discussed it with her immediately because they overheard the experiment. However, there were also cases in which the emerging work practices propagated only through the workflow application, which are the focus of this analysis because the efects of IT use were much more pronounced in these cases. The adoption frequency patterns of such new work practices reveal two types of scenarios: A new work practice could be continuously practiced or abruptly abandoned after a period of practice. An example of each is presented next.

The events that followed Alan’s complete delegation of three loan packages to Mary was an example of the first type of scenario. The system records indicated the pattern of its adoption shown in Fig. 4, which uses the creation times of loan packages owned by SAs because they best approximate the delegation decision times.

The system record indicated that Alan was the inventor of the new work practice, and there were regularly three to six weekly occurrences of such delegations after Alan and Mary’s local experiment. During the follow-up interviews with the AEs who subsequently exercised that type of delegation, I learned that they all discovered the new delegation pattern online through searching or scanning activities. The following informal interview with Andrea (AE) was representative of what happened:

Researcher: ‘‘Andrea, I saw you delegated a loan package to Dimitri.’

Andrea: ‘‘Yeah. If Alan could do it, I figured that I could do it, too.’

Researcher: ‘‘Did you talk to Alan about it?’

Andrea: ‘‘No. Why should I? We never worked on the same loan.’’

Researcher: ‘‘Were you concerned about the conflict with the credit policy?’

Andrea: ‘‘A little bit. But wouldn’t the first person who did this ask for permission?’’

The SAs complained that they were assigned AEs’ work without corresponding adjustments in training, compensation, or career path. The staf management then systematically surveyed the online document base and was surprised by the scale of the practice and the complexity of some of the delegated loan packages. But at that time, it was expensive or even impossible to reverse the consequences of the practice because it was time consuming, and some of the loans had already been approved. Without the authority to compensate the SAs as they had requested, the staf management only asked the AEs to exercise extreme caution in delegating their responsibilities. The management made this recommendation as both a gesture to comfort the SAs and an attempt to control the ‘‘rampant’’ spread of the new work practice. The discussion among the staf management, AEs, and SAs continued but did not produce any tangible results until the end of the study. Meanwhile, the SAs were left frustrated.

A scenario of the second type followed the experiment of transferring document editing rights from an AE to a PM. The adoption pattern of this new work practice appears in Fig. 5.

Approximately two weeks after Errol transferred document edit rights to Sharon, other AEs started to do the same. In my follow-up interviews with them, the AEs indicated that they did not talk to Errol before their adoption of the practice. Similar to Errol, the majority hesitated when they received edit right transfer requests from PMs, but instead of talking to their peers, they searched online to check whether such transfers had been done before and then followed suit when they saw precedents. Errol’s bad experience with unauthorized changes apparently did not reach many of his peers in time to influence their decisions. The AEs realized the complications of the new work practice only after many of them encountered similar experiences and finally talked to one another. Fig. 5 indicates that they stopped the practice of transferring edit rights to PMs by week 11. An interesting phenomenon happened after Errol’s initial experiment. The AEs subsequently involved in similar situations reported that the PMs used the visibility of the pre vious online cases to pressure them. When PMs asked for document edit rights, they often referred to the prior edit right transfer cases registered online to imply that their requests were not abnormal, which made it more dificult for the AEs to reject the PMs’ requests. One AE’s comment indicated why they felt that way: ‘‘If others are doing it, I don’t want to be seen as a particularly nasty person.’’ In other words, the AEs found it harder to portray a rejection as a conventional response rather than a personal one. This use of prior cases registered online reflected an interesting role played by the workflow application in the institutionalization process that will be discussed subsequently.

## 6. Discussion

The workflow application was designed and used as a technological platform for work. Through its unintended consequences though, it acted as a propagation medium in some cases and consequently afected some subprocesses of organizational learning. Consider the observed efects on the integrating subprocess first. In some cases in which the IT application acted as a propagation medium, it transmitted new work practices without their contextual information or peers’ interpretations. This decontextualized transmission of new work practices short-circuited the usual community debates about the conditions in which a new work practice might be applicable. In the case of loan package delegation, for example, no one on the receiving end was aware that Alan was under intense time pressure and that he had created an example before delegating three similar loan packages to Mary. Without debate, the AEs who imitated Alan delegated some rather complex loan packages to SAs without creating examples, and none reported time pressure as a factor in their decision making.

In these cases, the decontextualized transmission of new work practices led to a distinc tive order of learning activities, as shown in Fig. 6.

In this observed pattern, imitation immediately follows the local invention of new work practices. Social interactions in the form of dialogue only occur when adoptions obviously lead to serious problems. This order of events would be unlikely to occur if social interaction were the primary propagation medium, because a user could not imitate a work practice without learning about it through social interaction. When new work practices propagate through social interaction, the conditions in which they were invented, justifications for their legitimacy, and assessments of their pros and cons generally are all transmitted as well.

New work practices are often locally invented as exceptional measures in response to specific situations, and the complexity and fluidity of organizational work must preclude the notion of their unconditional applicability. In all the cases discussed in this study, none of the innovative bankers intended to create standardized work practices; rather, they were solving problems in specific situations. Imitating them without considering their specific situations was like moving trees from one place to another without considering the soil in either location. The findings of this study clearly underline the cost of imitation without social interaction. Collective learning may be bypassed early on but cannot be eliminated. When problems emerge and mandate social interaction, community members must deal with the often adverse consequences that could have been avoided if social interaction had preceded the wide adoption of a new work practice. In the case of the loan package delegation, tensions between SAs and AEs intensified when more delegations occurred. Mary told me that she agreed to create the three loan packages as an exception, but when the delegations started to happen on a regular basis, the SAs sensed that the exceptions were turning into established practices without serious debate, negotiation, or compensation. At that point, they forced a dialogue with the staf management and the AEs, but it had already become very costly to resolve the accumulated problem because many loan packages could not be reversed. Had there been dialogue early on to establish a shared understanding about the delegation practice, the workload implications for the SAs may have surfaced previously.

At the organization level, the IT application became a source of legitimacy that influenced the institutionalizing process. Andrea’s last comment dropped a critical hint: ‘‘But wouldn’t the first person who did this ask for permission?’’ She apparently attempted to justify her action using the appearance of the new work practice in the workflow application. Probing further into the legitimacy of this justification, it is probably rooted in the fact that the new workflow application was the oficial system for the commercial lending business process.

One may rightly question the validity of extending the organizational sanction of the system to new work practices that were reified on it, but even if Andrea was stretching her argument, the excuse was efective. If management does not police the system diligently to root out illegitimate work practices, should a banker be blamed for imitating something that appeared online? The point here is that IT as a propagation medium is not neutral or innocent; it automatically and unintentionally wraps the work practices that it transmits in a cloak of legitimacy. This implied legitimacy could be used or misused by users when they make their own adoption decisions

In other cases, this source of legitimacy was used in diferent ways with diferent efects. A critical diferentiating factor seemed to be whether the powerful users had predispositions either for or against the new work practice. In the work delegation case, the AEs had more power due to their seniority over the SAs, and many AEs had expressed their desire to delegate long before Alan and Mary actually put it in action. The AEs had a predisposition that the SAs could do more independent work than they were assigned. Consequently, the AEs used the workflow application as a source of legitimacy in sustaining the work delegation practice. In the case of the edit right transfer, the AEs also had more power because they had oficial control over document edit rights, but their predisposition in this case was universally against transferring edit rights to the PMs. The PMs used the workflow application as a source of legitimacy in pushing the edit right transfer practice but ultimately failed to sustain the practice. Furthermore, the predispo sitions of the parties in power seem to play an important role in determining the outcomes. This possible relationship between power and the use of IT as a source of legitimacy might be an interesting subject for further research.

## 7. Theoretical and practical implications

The preceding discussion suggests that the use of IT as a technological work platform can have some unintended efects on organizational learning processes, as summarized in Table 2.

Each of the roles played by the IT and their efects on the organizational learning process has been discussed. But at the level of theoretical discourse, an IT platform also can be conceptualized as a unified medium that performs all these roles in an integrated fashion. After all, the same IT platform simultaneously acted as an experimentation platform, a reification medium, and a propagation medium. The work of this new unified medium may difer from that of traditional media combinations, such as fragmented private paper and computer storage, dialogues, and story telling. Traditionally, local learning had led to locally accessible reification, but with the use of IT, reified new work practices are globally visible. When dialogue and story telling are the only means of communication, only the inventors of the new work practices can select them for broadcasting, and no potentia receiver has the means to scan them systematically. As a new work practice is relayed from person to person on its slow journey to the community members, it carries with it an ongoing debate, in that it gathers responses from the people who have contemplated it. When an IT application is added to the media mixture, local new work practices reified online get broadcast to all users instantly in a decontextualized fashion. For the receivers, the IT medium enables them to perform systematic search and scan operations. In addition, it hints at organizational sanction that could be stretched or even misused to justify various predispositions. Because the introduction of this medium changes the makeup of the organizational learning space, it is sensible to treat IT use as a constituting factor of organizational learning.

Although this study documents some important considerations for developing new theoretical frameworks of organizational learning processes that incorporate IT use, many questions remain. For example, though some significant efects of IT use on organizational learning processes were identified, I did not examine their consequences on organizational efectiveness. In addition, though these efects were found to be unintended consequences during the active work practice transition that followed the IT deployment, it would be interesting to investigate whether they are temporary or long-term efects. For the purpose of intervention, further research should discover and test good practices to manage these efects intentionally. To provide guidance for IT design, researchers must systematically investigate which and how IT design aspects mediate the efects of IT use on organizational learning.

The findings of this study can help practitioners become aware of the potential efects of IT use on associated organizational learning processes. From the IT design perspective, the lesson is that a careful balance should be maintained between enforcing current rules and procedures and enabling innovative post-deployment appropriations. The message to managers responsible for IT implementation and use is to stay vigilant regarding the dynamic evolution of a work practice, not simply allow the efects of IT use on organizational learning processes to be unintended consequences. In addition to its roles as a working and learning platform, an IT might be used as a sensory instrument for managerial monitoring and control.

## 8. Conclusion

Organizational learning has long been studied as a phenomenon that evolves only in organizational and social spaces. Even in IS research, IS are generally viewed as objects or supporting platforms of organizational learning. This study, however, suggests that IT might change the makeup of organizational learning spaces. As more work practices are codified on and transmitted by IT platforms with unprecedented speed and scope, IT is rapidly growing out of its traditional roles as a tool and resource to become a constituting factor in organizational learning. Following the recent call (Orlikowski & Barley, 2001) for greater interaction between the disciplines of organization studies and IS, this study asks a question missing in current organizational learning and IS literature and makes an initial efort to answer it. A critical implication of the findings for additional research in both IT design and management is that IT should be treated as a constituting factor in formulating both working and learning strategies.
