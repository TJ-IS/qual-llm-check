# Predicting adequacy of vancomycin regimens: A learning-based classification approach to improving clinical decision making

Paul Jen-Hwa Hu <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Chih-Ping Wei <sup>b</sup>, Tsang-Hsiang Cheng <sup>c</sup>, Jian-Xun Chen

<sup>a</sup> Accounting and Information Systems, David Eccles School of Business, University of Utah, Salt Lake City, Utah 84112, USA

## Abstract

Clinicians' drug regimen decision making is critical, particularly when involving high-alert medications. In this study, we use decision-tree induction C4.5 and a backpropagation neural network to construct decision support systems for predicting the regimen adequacy of vancomycin, a glycopeptide antimicrobial antibiotic effective for Gram-positive bacterial infections. We comparatively evaluate the respective systems using a total of 987 clinical vancomycin cases collected from a major tertiary medical center in southern Taiwan. We supplement each system using Bagging and then examine the predictive power of the extended system. Overall, our evaluation results show the overall accuracy of the decision support system based on C4.5 or the neural network to be significantly higher than that of the benchmark one-compartment pharmacokinetic model. Use of Bagging can considerably improve the effectiveness of each system across different performance measures, particularly for cases of decision classes in which the base systems (i.e., without Bagging) perform modestly. Taken together, our evaluation results seem to favor the use of Bagging to enhance the performance of decision support systems constructed using decision-tree induction C4.5. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Decision support in medicine; Pharmacokinetic data mining; Regimen adequacy prediction; Management of clinical use of vancomycin; Decision tree induction; Artificial neural network; Bagging

## 1. Introduction

The rapid advancements in clinical medicine and pharmacology have expanded our knowledge of diseases and their therapeutic treatments. As a result, a fast-growing array of pharmaceutical drugs has become available for patient care, which inevitably increases clinical drug-related problems. Since 1995, the American Society Health System of Pharmacists has recognized inadequate (or erroneous) medications and adverse patient reactions as medical malpractices. Such problems not only affect the patient's health and wellbeing but also demand enormous resources for corrections. The cost of clinical drug-related problems in the United States alone amounted to \$76.6 billion in 1995 [12] and is expected to exceed \$177.4 billion annually in the new millennium [5]. Of particularly alarming salience are problems surrounding sub- or overtherapeutic doses of high-alert medications, which have been identified as major concerns in the efforts to prevent health care errors by the Joint Commission on the Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations [27].

Sub- and over-therapeutic dose problems essentially result from inadequate regimen decisions by clinicians<sup>1</sup> and, in many cases, can have profound adverse implications for clinical efficacy and safety. The clinician's regimen decision is particularly critical when it involves drugs of a narrow therapeutic index that pose a high toxicity/impairment risk to patients. A good case in point is vancomycin, a glycopeptide antimicrobial antibiotic effective for Gram-positive bacterial infections and commonly used for treating methicillinresistant staphylococcal infections. This antibiotic is also a popular choice among clinicians for curing patients diagnosed with staphylococcal aureus and methicillinresistant staphylococcal epidermidis [18,31].

Managing the clinical use of vancomycin is challenging because of its narrow therapeutic index and significant, lasting, adverse effects on patients. An insufficient dose yields limited therapeutic effects and could increase the bacteria's drug resistance, whereas an excessive dose produces harmful vancomycin deposits that can severely impair the patient's renal function and cause poisoning to kidneys and/or ears. Such adverse effects can become even greater clinical threats when patients already have abnormal renal functions<sup>2</sup> or are receiving other nephrotoxic drugs concurrently [20]. Clinicians usually depend on general clinical guidelines for prescribing vancomycin, most prevalently, the nomogram [19] or pharmacokinetic analysis [4,8,10,13,21,33] to estimate or assess vancomycin regimen adequacy. However, neither method is necessarily effective. In general, the nomogram provides clinicians with a simplistic baseline for (initial) regimen estimations but does not support their predictions of a regimen's adequacy with respect to a target (normal) range of peak or trough concentration.<sup>3</sup> In a nutshell, pharmacokinetic analysis uses a statistical model derived from the clinical pharmacokinetic data of a large patient population. Building a pharmacokinetic model is resource intensive and requires the adequate understanding and selection of different mathematical or statistical models to relate dosage, sampling time, and pharmacokinetic parameters to plasma drug levels; regression models to describe the relationships between patient characteristics and pharmacokinetic parameters; a population model for inter-patient variability; and a variance model for random residual variation in the data (intra-patient variability) [3]. A pharmacokinetic model uses a specific set of population pharmacokinetic data, and therefore, its clinical effectiveness or applicability is greatly affected by those characteristics. Several important problems have been identified as inherent to the use of population pharmacokinetic data, including repeated measures (e.g., a patient contributing to a series of clinical cases), imbalance (e.g., an unequal number of clinical cases per patient), and confounding (e.g., a patient receiving specific dosages on the basis of that patient's prior reactions) [29]. These problems, together with the stringent resource requirements, make it difficult to develop effective statistical analysis tools for predicting pharmacokinetic parameters.

In light of the relative limitations of the nomogram and pharmacokinetic analysis, the use of a decision support system to predict the adequacy of a vancomycin regimen is desirable. Such systems can enhance the efficacy of initial regimen estimations by the nomogram and, at the same time, complement the pharmacokinetic analysis. Supported by the decision support system, the clinician will be able to estimate an initial regimen using the nomogram and then verify its therapeutic adequacy with the system. Likewise, the clinician can use the system to predict the adequacy of a regimen adjustment recommended by the pharmacokinetic analysis results. In both cases, the clinician's regimen decision making can be improved. In this study, we consider predicting the adequacy of a vancomycin regimen to be a classification problem that can be effectively supported by the promising supervised learning techniques in artificial intelligence (AI). Specifically, we use decision-tree induction C4.5 and a backpropagation neural network to develop decision support systems for predicting vancomycin regimen adequacy. We empirically evaluate the effectiveness of each system using 987 clinical cases collected from a major tertiary medical center in southern Taiwan.<sup>4</sup> To improve the prediction accuracy, we extend each system using Bagging, an ensemble approach to enhance the effectiveness of the respective systems, and then empirically evaluate the resulting predictive power. Overall, our evaluation results suggest that the prediction accuracy of both systems is significantly higher than that of the benchmark one-compartment pharmacokinetic model, that the use of Bagging considerably improves the performance of each system, and that Bagging-induced prediction improvements appear to be most prominent and statistically significant in the cases with minority decision classes in which the respective base systems (without Bagging) perform modestly.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the relevant previous research and highlight our motivation. In Section 3, we describe our data (including input variables and outcome classes) and the particular supervised learning techniques selected for supporting the targeted classification analysis. Section 4 details our evaluation design and system developments, followed by key evaluation results and their implications in Section 5. We conclude in Section 6 with a summary and discussions of some future research directions.

## 2. Background overview and literature review

In this section, we provide an overview of vancomycin and its clinical use and then review relevant previous research to highlight our motivation.

## 2.1. Vancomycin and its clinical use

Proper use of vancomycin in patient care has been a challenge to clinicians. Because of its advantageous bactericidal effects, sustained serum concentrations, and limited bacterial resistance [18,31], vancomycin enjoys wide use and broad popularity among clinicians. In addition to its common uses–for methicillinresistant staphylococcal infections, staphylococcal aureus, and methicillin-resistant staphylococcal epidermidis [18,31]–it is also commonly used to treat patients who suffer from streptococcus phylogenies, streptococcus pneumoniae, enterococci, corynebateririum, and clostridium difficile, as well as for patients who are allergic to penicillin or cephalosporin. However, because of its narrow therapeutic index and potential for severe, lasting, adverse effects on patients (e.g., renal impairments and ear poisoning), vancomycin consistently has been identified as one of the top three adverse effect-producing pharmaceutical drugs in Taiwan between 1998 and 2003, according to the pharmacovigilance report issued by the National Reporting System of Adverse Drug Reactions (http://adr.doh.gov.tw).

To cope with the constricted therapeutic index and severe toxicity/impairments, many clinicians follow general clinical guidelines for prescribing vancomycin. One salient practice uses the nomogram [19] to estimate adequate (initial) vancomycin regimens on the basis of creatinine clearance $\operatorname { \left( C L _ { C R } \right) }$ , which is a function of the patient's age, gender, weight, and serum creatinine (SCR, a common renal function index). The nomogram is constructed from the clinical efficacy observed from a large patient population. In general, clinicians should target an average vancomycin concentration at the 15μg/ml level [19]. Alternatively, a clinician can use an initial regimen of 8mg/kg and then adjust the time interval between administrations according to the estimated $\mathrm { C L } _ { \mathrm { C R } }$ of the patient [14]. Clinical guidelines based on the nomogram are intuitive and easy to use and can provide clinicians with a reasonable baseline for (initial) vancomycin regimen estimations [17]. However, the nomogram is primitive and may not be sophisticated enough to meet the clinicians' needs in various patient care scenarios. As a result, clinicians often must supplement the nomogram with their experiences and patient condition assessments to adjust the regimen recommendations properly.

## 2.2. Related prior research and motivation

The use of pharmacokinetic analysis to support the clinical use of vancomycin also has been studied [8,9,10,15]. A pharmacokinetic model represents a mathematical scheme for depicting the underlying complex physiologic spaces or processes. Accurate pharmacokinetic modeling is crucial for estimating the elimination (discharge) rate of an administered drug. Both one- and two-compartment models have been proposed and tested clinically. In general, an administered drug initially is distributed into a central compartment before diffusing into the peripheral compartment. For drugs that equilibrate with the tissue compartment quickly, clinicians, for practical purposes, often choose a one-compartment model that involves only one volume term (i.e., volume of distribution). When a drug equilibrates slowly with the peripheral tissues, clinicians can use a two-compartment pharmacokinetic model to describe its distribution in the human body. Overall, pharmacokinetic analysis with a two-compartment model can considerably reduce biases and therefore is likely to improve the accuracy of its predicted peak and trough concentrations [10,11]. To derive reliable estimates for the pharmacokinetic parameters commonly required by a twocompartment model, additional concentration-in-serum values are needed to characterize the patient's serum concentration–time profile fully. For example, the use of a nonlinear least squares regression analysis of a two-compartment model requires a minimum of four concentrations in each clinical case (included in the population pharmacokinetic data set). In turn, such stringent requirements greatly reduce the practicality and clinical value of two-compartment models, which consequently are not commonly used clinically [9]. Previous research has also investigated a Bayesian approach for combining population and patientspecific information (such as serum-level data) to predict peak and trough concentrations. Evaluation results suggest the predictive power of Bayesian methods to be greater than that of conventional oneor two-compartment models [9,11]. Similar to twocompartment models however, the use of Bayesian methods requires considerably more serum samples from the patient, which seriously constrains their clinical applicability.

Clinically, pharmacokinetic analysis based on onecompartment models is more feasible and affordable; however, the prediction accuracy of peak and trough concentrations is limited [10,11]. Therefore, AI-based supervised learning techniques seem promising and may be applied to improve the prediction accuracy. A review of extant literature suggests the prevalence of neural networks in this function. Using population pharmacokinetic data, Chow et al. [3] show that neural networks are capable of capturing the important relationships between plasma drug levels and patient-related prognostic factors, as obtained from the routinely collected pharmacokinetic data. Tolle et al. [29] compare the effectiveness of a backpropagation neural network and NONMEM, a popular software application based on a one-compartment pharmacokinetic model, for predicting the serum concentration level of administered tobramycin in patients suffering from pediatric cystic fibrosis and hemotologic-oncologic disorders. Their evaluation results suggest that the neural network approach is cost effective and capable of providing clinicians and pharmacists with timely analysis and recommendations complementary to the regimen suggested by NONMEM. Whereas Chow et al. [3] and Tolle et al. [29] concentrate on predicting the patient's peak and trough concentration levels in serum samples, many clinicians consider a vancomycin regimen (prescribed or under assessment) either appropriate or inappropriate, according to the target normal range of peak and/or trough concentration. For clinicians, a classification approach for predicting the adequacy of a regimen (such as appropriate versus inappropriate) is more desirable than predicting peak and trough concentration levels. At the same time, from a prediction accuracy perspective, assessing the adequacy of a regimen as a single prediction task may be more reliable than performing and then combining separate predictions for the peak and trough concentrations, respectively.

In another related study, Yamamura et al. [34] develop a neural network to identify intensive-care patients likely to experience drug concentrations below targeted therapeutic levels. Their results show that the neural network outperforms their benchmark, a multivariate logistic regression analysis. However, this study, like other similar ones, investigates neural networks exclusively and thus ignores other promising supervised learning techniques, such as decision-tree induction. From a research perspective, it is important to identify alternative learning techniques and comparatively investigate their performance (e.g., prediction accuracy) and limitations.

In this study, we use decision-tree induction C4.5 and the backpropagation neural network to develop decision support systems, then evaluate their performance in predicting the adequacy of vancomycin regimens. For clinical purposes, it is desirable to evaluate the effectiveness of alternative systems using prevailing or promising AI-based learning techniques. Such comparative evaluations are particularly important for drugs with a narrow therapeutic index and significant toxicity/impairment risks to patients because the analysis results can better support the clinician's assessments of the regimen adequacy and, at the same time, generate valuable insights into the relative advantages or constraints of the respective learning techniques.

## 3. Data and automated classification techniques

In this section, we describe our data (including input variables and decision classes) and detail the specific supervised learning techniques used to classify the adequacy of vancomycin regimens.

## 3.1. Data

We collected 1274 clinical cases of vancomycin administrations from a tertiary medical center in southern Taiwan. Among them, 287 had incomplete data and therefore were removed from our study.<sup>5</sup> As a result, our data set comprises 987 vancomycin cases, all of which were clinically administered between January 2000 and July 2004. At the studied medical center, clinicians use the nomogram to estimate initial vancomycin regimens and often depend on a one-compartment pharmacokinetic model for making subsequent regimen adjustments. Each case includes the patient's gender, age, weight, and SCR, as well as the vancomycin regimen administered (i.e., daily dosage and time interval between administrations) and the resulting peak and trough concentrations detected by therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM). Using the recorded peak and trough concentrations, the attending clinician can assess the adequacy of a previously prescribed regimen and then adjust the regimen accordingly. These data represent the input attributes of the decision support systems to be developed and evaluated. Results of our interviews with multiple clinicians practicing at the studied medical center, as well as at another regional hospital, suggest that these input attributes are highly consistent with those commonly recoded by health care institutions in Taiwan or elsewhere.

Because of our classification approach, each clinical case is associated with a particular decision class. For regimen adequacy assessments, we dichotomously classified the peak or trough concentration produced by a regimen. Compliant with generally accepted practices [15,20,28], we selected a target peak concentration range of 20–40 mg/l and a target trough concentration of less than 10mg/l. For each vancomycin case, we compared the patient's peak and trough concentrations (revealed by TDM) with the target concentration range to determine the regimen's adequacy. Hence, we defined two decision outcomes: appropriate and inappropriate. The decision outcome of a case (i.e., a previously prescribed regimen) is appropriate when both its resulting peak and trough concentrations are within the respective target concentration ranges, and inappropriate otherwise. Judged by the selected target peak and trough concentration ranges, 167 (16.92%) of the cases used in the study were appropriate and the remaining 820 (83.08%) were inappropriate.

We also examined the adequacy of a vancomycin regimen using its predicted peak and trough concentrations separately. According to the selected target range, a peak concentration can be low (less than 20mg/l), ontarget (20–40mg/l), or high (greater than 40mg/l). Similarly, a trough concentration can be on-target (less than 10mg/l) or high (equal to or greater than 10mg/l). That is, we defined three decision classes in the peak concentration analysis (i.e., low, on-target, and high) and two decision classes in the trough concentration analysis (i.e., on-target and high). Analysis of the cases used in the study shows 123 cases (12.46%) had low peak concentrations, 589 (59.68%) were on-target, and the remaining 275 (27.86%) were high. According to our analysis, 282 (28.57%) cases had on-target trough concentrations and the remaining 705 (71.43%) were high.

## 3.2. Investigated classification techniques: C4.5, neural network, and bagging

Classification analysis is a process for building a systematic classification model that induces or establishes relationships between decision outcomes and input variables using an automated learning technique and a set of training instances. A training instance is defined by its attribute values, which jointly determine its outcome class. The resulting model then classifies (predicts) the particular (decision outcome) class to which a new (unseen) instance belongs on the basis of its input attribute values. Different techniques have been proposed, including decision-tree induction, backpropagation neural networks, nearest-neighbor classification, decision-rule induction, and Bayesian networks [32]. In this study, we chose the neural network, a salient supervised learning technique commonly used to build decision support systems for this and similar clinical decision-making tasks with advantageous robustness. We also investigated C4.5, a supervised learning technique that offers computational efficiency and advantageous interpretability. Both learning techniques support classification analysis and have been shown to be effective in similar classification applications. Thus, we developed decision support systems using C4.5 and a backpropagation neural network and evaluated their predictive power empirically.

The C4.5 system constructs a decision tree from a set of training instances. Similar to its predecessor ID3 [22], C4.5 follows a divide-and-conquer strategy for constructing decision trees and generally prefers simple trees over complex ones because they are more accurate classifiers of future (new) instances [23]. Given a set of training instances, C4.5 evaluates the information gain (or gain ratio) for each input attribute and selects the one that yields the greatest information gain to branch a target node of the tree. The root node is the initial target node before the decision tree construction process begins (i.e., an empty tree). As the construction proceeds, the decision tree grows by creating child nodes–one for each value of the selected branching attribute emanating from the target node–and then classifying the training instances into appropriate, newly created child nodes. For each child node, the branching attribute selection and tree-construction process continues recursively when the training instances associated with the node do not belong to the same class or the specified termination condition is not satisfied; otherwise, the node becomes a decision node whose class is assigned through the majority class of the training instances associated with the node. Similar to other decision-tree induction techniques, C4.5 is popular partially because of the uncomplicated tree construction process and interpretable and verifiable classification models induced.

Artificial neural networks also have been widely used in various classification analysis tasks. A neural network consists of a set of nodes and weighted links, in which a node corresponds to a neuron and a link represents a synapse whose strength is denoted by a particular weight. Nodes usually are grouped into linear arrays, each of which is known as a layer. Use of a multi-layer typology has prevailed, whereby a network consists of an input layer, an output layer, and one or more hidden layers. A neural network is fully connected because a node in one layer is connected with all the nodes in the adjacent layer in the forward direction.

The backpropagation algorithm constructs a feedforward and adjust-backward neural network, in which data activation proceeds in a forward direction and error adjustments propagate in the reverse direction [25]. For each training instance, the forward pass brings it to the network and activates it through the network until it reaches the output layer. In the backward pass, the output predicted by the network (through its forward pass) is compared with the documented, known outcome of the instance. Error estimates for the output nodes then are computed and used to derive error estimates for the hidden nodes. Subsequently, the weights of the links that connect to the output nodes are adjusted to reduce the error estimates in the output layer. Ultimately, error estimates propagate backward to the weights of the links emanating from the input nodes. In each epoch, the neural network exhausts all training instances following the described feed-forward and adjust-backward process. The training of a neural network continues iteratively until it reaches a specified maximal number of epochs or the network successfully converges with respect to the defined condition.

We also investigated the use of an ensemble approach to enhance the predictive power of the C4.5 and neural network systems. This approach creates a learning setting in which a finite set of classification models (hereafter, base classifiers) are constructed (i.e., trained) and then used together to arrive at an overall classification decision for a new (unseen) instance. Prevailing ensemble techniques include Bagging [1,2,24,35] and

Boosting [1,6,7,24,26]. Bagging uses bootstrap sampling to generate multiple training data sets from the original overall training data set and then uses each resulting training data set to construct a base classifier. In each iteration, Bagging randomly samples the original overall training data set with replacements to create a distinct data set that consists of the same number of training instances as appear in the original data set. The resulting data set can construct a classifier using a specific learning technique (e.g., C4.5 or the backpropagation neural network). This process terminates after reaching a specified number of iterations. To classify a new (unseen) instance, a majority-voting scheme integrates all constructed base classifiers.

With Boosting, we first construct an initial base classifier using a set of training instances and a specific learning technique. The base classifier then predicts all instances, which initially are assigned equal weights, in the training data set. When the base classifier's prediction of a training instance differs from its known decision class (outcome), the weight of this instance increases. A new training data set is then randomly selected from the resulting set of weighted instances. Because the instances incorrectly predicted by the initial base classifier have greater weights, they are more likely to be included in the new training data set. As a result, the learning of the next (base) classifier, to some extent, is forced to focus on these challenging instances, namely, those not correctly predicted by its predecessor (base) classifier. This process continues until it reaches a specified number of iterations or the defined termination condition is satisfied, thus creating a sequence of base classifiers. To classify a new (unseen) instance, a weighted-voting scheme generally is used to obtain an overall classification decision by combining the respective decisions made by the sequence of base classifiers. Typically, the weight of a base classifier depends on its effectiveness in correctly predicting all instances in the training data set.

Results from previous studies have shown that the classification accuracy achieved by Bagging or Boosting is significantly higher than that reached by any individual base classifiers [1,7,24,35]. The comparative effectiveness of Bagging and Boosting is inconsistent. In 20 of the 27 data sets investigated, the use of Boosting in conjunction with C4.5 significantly reduced classification errors and, in general, outperformed Bagging with C4.5 [24]. In contrast, a neural network has been reported to perform significantly better when supplemented by Bagging than by Boosting in 8 of 11 examined data sets [35]. Several empirical investigations have suggested that Bagging can significantly reduce the classification variances and that the classification effectiveness of Boosting may be unstable [24,35]. Therefore, we chose Bagging to enhance the performance of the investigated systems, primarily because of its classification stability and the empirical support for its ability to enhance prediction accuracy.

## 4. Classification system development and evaluation design

In this section, we describe the design of the respective prediction systems and then detail our evaluation design, including procedures and performance measures.

## 4.1. Design of classification systems for predicting vancomycin regimen adequacy

We used the Weka open-source machine learning software (www.cs.waikato.ac.nz/ml/weka/) to construct the decision support systems based on C4.5 and the backpropagation neural network. When constructing a classification system using C4.5, we adopted J48 in Weka and used the gain ratio as the attribute selection criterion. We selected a 0.25 confidence factor to prune the least reliable branches from the decision tree under development<sup>6</sup> to improve its effectiveness. Finally, we chose a minimum of two training instances for each leaf node to avoid overfitting. Fig. 1 illustrates a decision tree induced by C4.5 for the peak concentration analysis.

Our design of the input/output nodes of a backpropagation neural network for predicting vancomycin regimen adequacy is as follows: We used a binary variable to represent patient gender (i.e., male = 0, female = 1). Patient age, weight, SCR, and daily dosage were normalized linearly over the range between 0 and 1. We used a 1-of-N coding scheme to represent dosing interval; that is, one input node denotes each of the dosing intervals examined. Altogether, our neural network design has 13 input nodes. For the dichotomous analysis of regimen adequacy, we defined 2 output nodes: appropriate and inappropriate. We used 3 output nodes in the analysis of perk concentrations (i.e., low, on-target, and high) and 2 in the analysis of trough concentrations (i.e., on-target and high). Fig. 2 illustrates the backpropagation neural network for the peak concentration analysis, with the input and output nodes specified explicitly.

We developed Bagging extensions to C4.5 and the backpropagation neural network, hereafter referred to as Bagged C4.5 and Bagged NN, respectively. Bagged C4.5 continued using the same attribute selection criterion, the confidence factor for post-pruning, and the minimum number of training instances per leaf node. Similarly, Bagged NN employed the same network topology, learning rate, momentum factor, and maximum number of epochs experimentally selected in the previously conducted parameter tunings. Both Bagged C4.5 and Bagged NN require an additional parameter, namely, the number of iterations, which represents the number of base classifiers to be generated. We experimentally searched for an optimal number of iterations over the range between 2 and 100. Our experimental results suggested the classification effectiveness of Bagged C4.5 and Bagged NN improved noticeably when the number of iterations expanded from 2 to 10 and then leveled off thereafter, across the targeted analyses (i.e., regimen adequacy analysis, peak concentration analysis, and trough concentration analysis). Fig. 3 illustrates the overall accuracy of Bagged C4.5 and Bagged NN over the investigated range of iterations for the regimen adequacy analysis. As shown, the performance of both Bagged C4.5 and Bagged NN became fairly stable after 10 iterations. For computational efficiency, particularly in the case of Bagged NN, we chose 10 iterations for both systems; that is, we built 10 base classifiers for each system.

## 4.2. Evaluation design

We used an 80/20 training/testing strategy, thereby randomly dividing the overall cases into two subsets: training (80%) and testing (20%). We used the training cases to construct each classification system for predicting vancomycin regimen adequacy, and then we used the testing cases to evaluate its predictive power. To reduce plausible biases from a single randomization, we performed the randomization 30 times (each time using different training and testing subsets) and report each system's performance based on its average effectiveness across the 30 trials.

Specifically, we evaluated each system's prediction performance in terms of the overall accuracy and predictive value for each decision class specified in the evaluation. For dichotomous evaluations, we consider two decision classes $C _ { 1 }$ and $C _ { 2 }$ . We calculate the overall accuracy and predictive value for each decision class as follows:

$$
\text { Overall   accuracy } = \frac {\mathrm{TC} _ {1} + \mathrm{TC} _ {2}}{\mathrm{TC} _ {1} + \mathrm{FC} _ {1} + \mathrm{FC} _ {2} + \mathrm{TC} _ {2}},\tag{3}
$$

$$
\text { Predictive   value } (C _ {1}) = \frac {\mathrm{TC} _ {1}}{\mathrm{TC} _ {1} + \mathrm{FC} _ {1}}, \text { and }\tag{4}
$$

$$
\text { Predictive   value } (C _ {2}) = \frac {\mathrm{TC} _ {2}}{\mathrm{TC} _ {2} + \mathrm{FC} _ {2}}\tag{5}
$$

where $\mathrm { T C } _ { 1 }$ represents the number of cases correctly classified as in $C _ { 1 } , \ \mathrm { T C } _ { 2 }$ denotes the number of cases correctly classified as in $C _ { 2 } , \mathrm { F C _ { 1 } }$ is the number of cases incorrectly classified as in $C _ { 1 } ,$ and $\mathrm { F C } _ { 2 }$ indicates the number of cases incorrectly classified as in $C _ { 2 }$

Our evaluation criteria can be generalized for classification problems that involve n decision classes, such as peak concentrations being low, on-target, or high. For such generalized problems, we calculate the overall accuracy and predictive value for each decision class $C _ { i }$ as follows:

$$
\text { Overall   accuracy } = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathrm{TC} _ {i}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\mathrm{TC} _ {i} + \mathrm{FC} _ {i}\right)} \text { and }\tag{6}
$$

$$
\text { Predictive   value } (C _ {i}) = \frac {\mathrm{TC} _ {i}}{\mathrm{TC} _ {i} + \mathrm{FC} _ {i}}\tag{7}
$$

where n represents the number of decision classes, $\mathrm { T C } _ { i }$ denotes the number of cases correctly classified as in $C _ { i } ,$ and $\operatorname { F C } _ { i }$ indicates the number of cases incorrectly classified as in $C _ { i } .$

We included in our evaluations the one-compartment model (described in Section 2.2) to serve as a performance benchmark. For each testing case, we estimated its peak and trough concentrations using the benchmark one-compartment model, then compared those estimates with the specified target peak and/or trough concentration range to predict the adequacy of the regimen in this case.

## 5. Evaluation results and discussion

## 5.1. Evaluation results of regimen adequacy classification analysis

Table 4 summarizes the evaluation results of our regimen adequacy classification analysis. As shown, C4.5 exhibited an overall accuracy of 82.42%, significantly higher than that of the neural network (80.81%). The overall accuracy of both systems was greater than that of the one-compartment model (68.03%), and the difference was statistically significant at the 0.01 level. Both systems showed a modest predictive value for the appropriate decision class (approximately 42%), which was significantly higher than that by the one-compartment model (27.66%). The C4.5 system appeared to have a higher predictive value than the neural network system, but the difference was not significant statistically. At the same time, both systems showed a satisfactory predictive value for the inappropriate decision class. Specifically, the neural network system appeared to have a higher predictive value (87.89%) for the inappropriate decision class than C4.5 (86.60%), and the difference was statistically significant at the 0.01 level. For both systems, the predictive value varied considerably between cases of the appropriate versus the inappropriate decision class. This finding may be partially explained by the imbalanced distribution of the decision class in our data set (i.e., a dominance of cases having inappropriate decision outcomes), as well as by the narrow target range for peak and trough concentrations, due to the tight therapeutic index of vancomycin. Nevertheless, the predictive value observed in the cases with the appropriate decision class was satisfactory and encouraging, especially compared with the one-compartment model's effectiveness.

## 5.2. Evaluation results of peak concentration analysis

As summarized in Table 5, the overall accuracy of the C4.5 system was comparable to that of the neural network system (approximately 65%); both significantly outperformed the benchmark one-compartment model (44.7%). Both systems showed a comparable predictive value for cases of the on-target decision class (approximately 68%). The predictive value for the high decision class was comparable between systems (i.e., 62.4% for C4.5 and 60.3% for neural network), and the difference was not statistically significant. Both systems seemed least effective for cases of the low decision class (52–58%). The neural network system appeared somewhat more effective than the C4.5 system, and the difference was statistically significant at the 0.01 level. The evaluation results of the peak concentration analysis suggested that the neural network system exhibited better overall accuracy and showed high predictive values in the cases of a low or on-target peak concentration, whereas the C4.5 system seemed more effective for cases with high peak concentrations. The predictive value difference between the systems was statistically significant for cases with low peak concentrations but insignificant for others.

When incorporated with Bagging, both systems improved noticeably in overall accuracy: from 65.27% to 66.31% for C4.5 (significant at the 0.05 level) and from 65.67% to 67.39% for the neural network (significant at the 0.01 level). According to our comparative analysis, both the C4.5 and the neural network systems seemed to show comparable Bagging-induced improvements in overall accuracy and predictive value for cases with on-target peak concentrations. The C4.5 system seemed to benefit significantly more than the neural network systems in cases of low peak concentrations, whereas the neural network system showed a significantly greater gain than the C4.5 system in the cases of a high peak concentration. In effect, the predictive value of C4.5 for cases of high peak concentration decreased when the use of Bagging was incorporated. Overall, the results from the peak concentration analysis suggested that the use of Bagging generated considerable improvements for both systems across all performance measures. The Bagging-induced enhancements in the predictive value of C4.5 appeared to be the greatest in magnitude and the most (statistically) significant for the cases of the low decision class (i.e., the minority decision class in our data set), whereas such improvements reached the maximal (both magnitude and significance) point for the neural network system in the cases with high peak concentrations.

## 5.5. Discussion

Overall, our evaluation results suggest the following. First, the overall accuracy of our decision support systems is significantly higher than that of the benchmark one-compartment pharmacokinetic model. Second, the use of Bagging can significantly improve the performance of each system, as measured by the overall accuracy and predictive value for each decision class (e.g., low, on-target, or high). Third, as shown in our evaluation analyses for regimen adequacy and peak and trough concentration, the Bagging-induced effectiveness improvements gained by each system appear to be most prominent, if not statistically significant, for minority decision classes in which the base system performs modestly. In many cases, the use of Bagging does not sacrifice but instead improves the predictive effectiveness in the cases with majority decision classes. Fourth, the performance of each investigated system is fairly insensitive to the size of its training data set, thus making the selected learning techniques and their constructed decision support systems increasingly applicable in clinical settings in which large pharmacokinetic data sets are not readily available for system development (training).

Despite the difference in magnitudes and statistical significance, the effectiveness of the systems based on C4.5 and the neural network is largely comparable. However, the construction of a neural network is significantly more demanding computationally than that of a decision tree using C4.5. The differential computational requirements become a greater concern when incorporating the use of Bagging. In our case, a total of 10 base neural networks needed to be constructed for the Bagged NN. On the basis of the interpretability of the classification model induced, C4.5 appears more clinically appealing than the neural network. Taken together, our analysis results may favor the use of Bagging to enhance the performance of decision support systems constructed by decision-tree induction C4.5.

## 6. Conclusion

This study responds to the challenge of managing the clinical use of vancomycin by developing decision support systems based on promising learning-based classification techniques in AI. Specifically, we investigated decision-tree induction C4.5 and a backpropagation neural network, as well as their extensions using Bagging, for increasing predictive power. We evaluated the effectiveness of each system using a total of 987 clinical vancomycin cases collected from a major tertiary medical center in Taiwan. Our overall evaluation results are encouraging and suggest the clinical efficacy and value of decision support systems, with or without Bagging. According to our analysis, both systems predict the adequacy of vancomycin regimens more effectively than does the benchmark one-compartment pharmacokinetic model. The reported systems can complement existing practices for managing the clinical use of vancomycin, such as when clinicians primarily use the nomogram for initial regimen decision making and depend on a one-compartment pharmacokinetic model for regimen adjustments. For example, a clinician could use our systems to verify the adequacy of an initial regimen suggested by the nomogram or to assess the adequacy of a regimen adjustment recommended by pharmacokinetic analysis before administering it to the patient. Our analysis results suggest the potential of AI-based systems for supplementing, if not replacing, pharmacokinetic analysis; however, the potential replacement would require substantial large-scale clinical evaluations and clinicians' acceptance.

The current research can be extended in several directions. First, we considered all regimens as homogeneous in this study; further research should distinguish between, for example, initial versus followup regimens. With this differentiation, we can further scrutinize and compare the effectiveness of the reported systems in different regimen categories. Similarly, assessing the systems' performance by involving patients of different medical conditions is also important. Particularly important is conducting clinical evaluations with patients who have normal versus abnormal renal functions or kidney diseases, because of vancomycin's adverse effects on renal functions. Second, whereas we evaluated the systems using archival vancomycin cases (which captured the clinical practices at the studied medical center), our continued investigations target direct performance comparisons between the systems and pharmacokinetic analysis based on two-compartment models. Conducting similar evaluations involving clinicians and vancomycin cases from other healthcare institutions is also desirable and can enhance the systems' clinical efficacy or increase the validity of our evaluation results. Third, evaluations of the reported systems can be expanded. Of particular importance is predicting the likelihood that a regimen will produce adverse effects, which are of great concern in clinicians' use of vancomycin. In addition, our reported approach, including the automated construction of learningbased classification systems and evaluation designs, can and should be applied to predict proper dosages of other high-alert medications.
