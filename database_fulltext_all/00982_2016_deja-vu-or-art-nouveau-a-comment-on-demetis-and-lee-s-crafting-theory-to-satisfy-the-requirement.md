# Déjà Vu or Art Nouveau? A comment on Demetis and Lee's “Crafting theory to satisfy the requirements of systems science”

## a b s t r a c t

In this commentary, we reflect on the arguments made in Demetis and Lee's inspiring article. We first affirm key attributes of systems science as conditions for more rigorous theorizing in IS. While several of these attributes are already rmly embedded within theories used in IS research, others presented in the article deserve closer consideration. We also give reasons why general systems theory (GST) has been neglected as a unifying theory in social science: empirical difficulties (as addressed by Demetis and Lee), suspicions about the usefulness of grand theory, and GST's core assumptions about goals and boundaries. Given these concerns, we question the wisdom of the déjà vu grand theoretic aims of GST to establish requirements for IS theories. Rather, we urge IS scholars to pursue art nouveau by adjusting some of the more limiting constraints of systems science. We specifically advocate a more trans-systemic approach to theorizing that moves away from a “containerized” view of organizations and leverages our presumed deeper understanding of IS artifacts. Our way forward advocates not only adopting requirements from systems science, but reciprocally imposing requirements on systems science to engage with IS artifacts.

## 1. Introduction

Demetis and Lee take us each back in time to the beginning of our respective careers. The rst author enjoys re-acquaintance with material from mid-20th century, as well as exposure to the more recent contributions of Luhmann. The first author encountered the work on GST in graduate study (1970–1972), reading Boulding (1956), von Bertalanffy (1950), Miller (1965), Shannon and Weaver (1949), along with Emery and Trist (1965) and other organizational systems theorists (e.g. Kast & Rosenzweig, 1972; Katz & Kahn. 1966: Thompson. 1967). The second author encountered Luhmann, also during her graduate studies, much more recently (2015). Our collective assessment is that revisiting GST is both rewarding and daunting, insofar as valuable insights are often veiled by arcane jargon. In part, such difficulties are inherent in any attempts aimed at “general" theory applicable across many scientific disciplines. As evident in Demetis and Lee's article, confusion also stems from translations of Luhmann's abstract terms into English. From a purely pragmatic perspective, readers need to invest more effort to understand GST/LST than many other theories. However, such investments can produce rich rewards.

Among the rewards obtainable from our own efforts are the six requirements articulated by Demetis and Lee. At a minimum, a researcher wishing to inject new insights into theorizing about IT and organizations would be reminded, or discover first-hand, that organizations and information systems have common characteristics that are shared with many other types of systems.

Moreover, Luhmann's distinction among living, psychic, and social systems draws attention to differences that older versions of GST tend not to address. Also valuable are Demetis and Lee's discussions about the limitations of positivist, interpretive, and design epistemologies to address particular characteristics of systems. We are grateful to the authors for providing these important insights.

In a constructive academic spirit, we also wish to comment on aspects of Demetis and Lee's arguments with which we disagree. We frame these more critical comments by examining three reasons why GST lost its appeal to research disciplines from which IS drew theoretical insight. Because IS did not yet exist in anything but nascent form 60 years ago, many IS scholars may be unaware of the GST wave of excitement and its subsequent ebb of interest. To the extent that certain core systems concepts entered and stayed within the fields of management (including organization theory and organizational behavior), sociology (including institutional theory), and engineering, much IS theorizing reflects inherited concepts such as cybernetics, socio-technical systems, and whole-part relationships.

## 1.1. Why GST lost its appeal

Demetis and Lee clearly explain the empirical difficulties that led scholars to avoid GST in favor of more traditional theory. Institutional pressures may favor novelty in theorizing, but empirical work needs to conform to conventional practices that editors, reviewers and readers understand and can evaluate. Given the complexity of systems as conceived in GST, early studies of systems often went no further than simulation methods to verify or demonstrate the principles of systems theory (Forrester, 1968). While simulation is a valuable tool in many respects, the validity of simulation studies ultimately rests upon the validity of assumptions underlying its mathematical models. These assumptions often are not derived from direct empirical evidence, which empirical re searchers prefer.

Perhaps a more substantive reason for neglecting GST grew out of a general suspicion about the value of grand theory. Organization studies and IS have generally eschewed grand theory in favor of mid-range or grounded theorizing, which are deemed more practical and manageable. GST qualifies as grand theory with its aim to unify science and specify the nature of systems at different levels of analysis. This approach can easily lead scholars away from their interests in investigating practical problems of a particular type, such as those typically undertaken in IS research. This is especially true of interpretivists who usually employ qualitative research methods to generate detailed and nuanced analyses of complex local situations, with limited aspirations to generalize. The idea of grounded theory suggests that theories should be constructed from empirical observations rather thar specified a priori. GST would not qualify as a theory that is grounded in data but rather a proposal for general system properties and their dynamics.

Based on this argument, we find Demetis and Lee's claim that systems science could facilitate communication between academia and industry to be puzzling. Given the abstractness and complicatedness of both GST and Luhmann, they would seem to exist alongside of Giddens' structuration theory (Giddens, 1984) as examples of arcane academic language rather than appealing alternatives, as Demetis and Lee suggest. Checkland, 2000, acknowledged by Demetis and Lee as an exemplary application of systems science, succeeds more as a methodology for analyzing socio-technical systems and less as a theory upon which research can be based. We have no argument with the value of Checkland's work and its impact on practice, but it would seem unfair to claim that Demetis and Lee's GST and LST principles, which lack lay terminology, would be easily understood by IS practitioners.

GST has also been critiqued throughout its history for its core assumption that systems are goal-oriented and structured in an orderly fashion to accomplish those objectives. This critique was keenly developed in sociology where such assumptions were challenged as too conservative and unrealistic in the face of tumultuous social change (Gouldner, 1970). In sharp contrast to the traditional structural-functional view of society and institutions articulated by Parsons (Parsons, 1960), sociologists and institutional theorists looked for ways that theory could explain disorderly events of the times (sexual revolution, undeclared foreign wars, political scandals, civil rights activism). Since GST offered little insight into systems that were unregulated and chaotic, social theories moved in directions that dealt more realistically with the dialectic of power and conflict.

We therefore caution against any theorizing that simplistically approaches systems as being oriented to the achievement of singular goals and optimization. Demetis and Lee acknowledge this potential oversimplification in their case analysis by stating: “Hence, ‘goal seeking’ is a dynamic process that emerges out of the structural coupling of any system with its environment and does not always indicate convergence into a singular goal.” This observation needs to be turned into a more general principle in which systems are viewed as comprising multiple and often conflicting goals and a process of “internal negotiation.” Of course, this principle is already well incorporated into social theory, for example in Giddens' (1984) statement that members of society share “…feelings that they have some sort of common identity, however that might be expressed or revealed. Such feelings …do not presume a ‘value consensus’. Individuals may be aware of belonging to a definite collectivity without agreeing that this is necessarily right and proper.” (p.165).

Despite overall neglect of systems science, GST concepts crept into organization studies and IS and remain there. For examples, theoretical approaches drawing from socio-technical systems and sociomateriality (Leonardi, 2013), structuration theory (Orlikowski & Robey, 1991), and complexity theory (Braa, Hanseth, Heywood, Mohammed, & Shaw, 2007) all engage with social systems. Indeed, systems thinking is deeply imbedded in IS design and methodology, which in turn shapes the way we conceive of organizations. We might argue, therefore, that many of the key insights from general systems thinking have become part of the IS lexicon. While the links back to GST as a source for these ideas may be neglected, IS remains enriched due to its adoption and absorption of many of the tenets that Demetis and Lee offer.

In summary, GST did not simply fall out of favor out of mindless neglect. Systems science is already deeply imbued in IS thinking and research. However, its original formulations demand methodological innovation, which is risky. GST also offers an orderly, grand-theoretic view of the world that contradicts abundant evidence of intractable social problems. Because applied sciences, such as IS and organization studies, are usually undertaken with the hope of mitigating problems in the real world, mid-range and local theorizing is more often chosen than grand theory. This does not mean that the principles of systems science as articulated by Demetis and Lee should be discredited or ignored. Rather, their potential contributions should be placed alongside more grounded approaches to research and theory.

## 1.2. The way ahead

While there are understandable reasons for avoiding engagement with GST in IS theorizing, we believe, along with Demetis and Lee, that there is much more to be gained by re-engaging with systems science. We can draw a lesson about the use of grand theory from IS experience with structuration theory (Giddens, 1984). Approaching structuration theory in the 1980s generated frustration since the theory generally resists testing through traditional positivist methods. However, Giddens' work has stimulated a prolific record of research in the IS field (Jones & Karsten, 2008) even though structuration concepts are not easily understood. We believe that systems science can serve the interests of IS and organizational researchers in a similar way. Despite the cognitive burden, those wishing to re-engage with systems science can apply basic concepts and generate newer insights that match the issues faced in IS research. Our suggestion for ways forward with these requirements for IS theory is to clarify the ways in which IS artifacts influence the way that environments impinge on internal autopoietic processes.

Luhmann conceives social systems as autopoietic communication systems in which the relation between the system and the environment plays a key role (Seidl & Becker, 2006). There is no input-process-output relation between the system and the environment. The environment only “irritates" the system by triggering the (internal) autopoietic process. Operative closure (Luhmann, 1997) is said to be a key element of systems' interactional openness. The environment which is composed of other social systems is said to be structurally coupled to the social system considered, allowing reactions to the irritations. Autopoiesis (re)produces the system, through the synthetic process of communication (utterance - information-understanding) (Luhmann, 1986). The breakdown of communication threatens the continuity of autopoiesis and thus, the existence of the social system

We find the emphasis on operative closure to be a major limitation that reduces the potential contribution of systems science to IS. The coupling, both loose and tight, between the system and the environment is the focus of the idea of bracketing in organization science (Hackman, 2003). Scholars in both management (e.g. Johns, 2006) and IS (e.g. Hong, Chan, Thong, Chasalow, & Dhillon, 2013) have proposed the integration of environmental interactions in their analyses. Without a clearer explanation of environmental interaction, systems science reproduces old thinking about organizations as “containers” in which IS systems are de veloped and used (Winter, Berente, Howison, & Butler, 2014). While both GST and LST clearly delineate systems from their environments, and focus on the relationship between open systems and their environments, they hold fast to a traditional view of organizations (systems) that can be challenged. With the advent of technical platforms that transcend organizations, that belong to no one in particular, and are created and used by a diffuse set of actors, thinking about IS and organizations needs to be revised. GST poses that systems at all levels have the same characteristics (a multi-level theory) (Staw, Sandelands. & Dutton. 1981). and it reinforces the idea that each level is distinct and self-contained. Neither GST nor Luhmann focus on relationships across levels (cross-level theory) (Rousseau, 1985). Winter et al. (2014) give us a new option by challenging the container view of organizations and offering an unbounded view of IS and its capacity to redefine what organizations are.

One conceptual approach that avoids the bounded container problem is Galison's (1997) notion of trading zone, which has been used in IS-related research to understand technology platforms (Boland, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2007; Kellogg, Orlikowski, & Yates, 2006). Trading zones imply more than identifying the roles and functions of boundary spanners, but draw attention to the fluid and shifting spaces and activities within and across communities involved in exchange. Trading zones also operate with minimal structuring using gateways connecting loosely coupled parts so that interactions across disparate trading entities and local systems can be accommodated. These insights can be drawn from an approach to system that focuses on the interactional exchanges among systems and develops their implications for autopoietic selforganization.

Notably, these less bounded views of systems arise mainly through recognition of technological advances. Since communications, now largely mediated by technology, are the motor driving the autopoietic process, the opportunity to theorize technology's role in communication offers potential for IS theorists to contribute to systems science. By drawing upon the knowledge of IS technology and access to data sources mediated by technology, IS researchers can help to ground the abstractions of Luhmann and GST with data. For example, the preponderance of social media networks could generate insights into the origin, growth, persistence and maintenance of social systems that are not self-contained. Such a contribution may change the character of systems science to be less “grand” in scope but also more useful to those seeking guidance on specific IS research questions. Our way forward thus would adyocate not only adopting requirements from systems science. but reciprocally imposing requirements on systems science to engage with technical artifacts. Such reciprocity suggests an alternative title for Demetis and Lee, perhaps for their next essay: “Crafting Systems Science to Meet the Requirements of IS Theory”.

## 1.3. Conclusion: Déjà Vu or Art Nouveau?

Our use of familiar French-language phrases is not merely a pretense. In architecture, art nouveau was the first 20th century style to stop looking backward in history for ideas, instead drawing inspiration from contemporary times.<sup>1</sup> Hopefully, interest in older systems theory, while looking backward, can also serve as a springboard for fresh ideas that engage directly with contemporary technologies and society. Focusing on IT artifacts and their intertwined relationships with social systems would actually address one of Luhmann's more prescient thoughts, that “society is becoming more and more dependent on technologically determined structural coupling with its environment” (Luhmann, 1997, p.180). Based on this insight, it is curious that Luhmann stated that autopoietic systems do not communicate with each other and that he did not create a fourth type of autopoietic system, namely the “technical system”. Such an inclusion might have helped to advance Luhmann's more radical agenda of societal critique.<sup>2</sup>

Understanding systems science is a daunting exercise and a challenge to empirical work, but it can offer a thrilling perspective for the IS field if we engage it seriously. Weber (2003) reminds us that the identity of the IS field is ultimately defined by the nature of problems we want to solve. A less bounded approach to theorizing about systems would lead us toward solutions of problems related to the wider, and perhaps more important, set of relationships that transcend individual organizations and information systems. Demetis and Lee have opened the floor to discuss how we may draw rigorously from the past. Our hope is that IS scholars can make systems science more thrilling and more relevant to IS and organizational researchers of the future.
