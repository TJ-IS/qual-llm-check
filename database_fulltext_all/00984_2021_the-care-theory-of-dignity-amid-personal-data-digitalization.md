# THE CARE THEORY OF DIGNITY AMID PERSONAL DATA DIGITALIZATION<sup>1</sup>

Dorothy E. Leidner Hankamer School of Business, Baylor University, Waco, TX 76798 U.S.A. {Dorothy\_Leidner@baylor.edu}

Olgerta Tona Swedish Center for Digital Innovation, Department of Applied IT, University of Gothenburg, 41756 Gothenburg, SWEDEN {olgerta.tona@ait.gu.se}

With the rapidly evolving permeation of digital technologies into everyday human life, we are witnessing an era of personal data digitalization. Personal data digitalization refers to the sociotechnical encounters associated with the digitization of personal data for use in digital technologies. Personal data digitalization is being applied to central attributes of human life—health, cognition, and emotion—with the purported aim of helping individuals live longer, healthier lives endowed with the requisite cognition and emotion for responding to life situations and other people in a manner that enables human flourishing. A concern taking hold in manifold fields ranging from IT, bioethics, and law, to philosophy and religion is that as personal data digitalization permeates ever more areas of human existence, humans risk becoming artifacts of technology production. This concern brings to center stage the very notion of what it means to be human, a notion encapsulated in the term human dignity, which broadly refers to the recognition that human beings possess intrinsic value and, as such, are endowed with certain rights and should be treated with respect. In this paper, we identify, describe, and transform what we know about personal data digitalization into a higher order theoretical structure around the concept of human dignity. The result of our analysis is the CARE (claims, affronts response, equilibrium) theory of dignity amid personal data digitalization, a theory that explains the relationship of personal data digitalization to human dignity. Building upon the CARE theory as a foundation, researchers in a variety of IS research streams could develop mid-range theories for empirical testing or could use the CARE theory as an overarching lens for interpreting emerging IS phenomena. Practitioners and government agencies can also use the CARE theory to understand the opportunities and risks of personal data digitalization and to develop policies and systems that respect the dignity of employees and citizens

Keywords: Personal data, dignity, digitalization, inductive top-down theorizing, dignity disequilibrium

## Introduction

Digital technologies have become so ubiquitous in everyday life that the social, physical, and biological lives of individuals around the world are increasingly subject to tracking, analysis, and decision-making by numerous agents, human and technology alike (Constantiou and Kallinikos 2015; Yoo 2010), resulting in a digital transformation of everyday life (Yoo 2010). At the heart of this digital transformation is the proliferation of personal data digitalization. By “personal data,” we refer to any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (data subject).<sup>2</sup> Drawing from Legner et al. (2017), we refer to personal data digitalization as the sociotechnical encounters associated with the conversion of personal data to digital form for use in digital technologies. The definition of personal data digitalization highlights that it is not the digitized personal data per se that transforms everyday life, but the ways in which such digitized personal data become incorporated into everyday decisions and activities. Personal data digitalization enables a plethora of benefits to individuals such as improved security (Clarke 2016), greater autonomy (Ozdemir et al. 2011), empowerment (Oh et al. 2013), self-expression (Choi et al. 2018), higher quality health care (Agarwal and Dhar 2014), and personalized service (Lehrer et al. 2018) as well as numerous downsides, including infringements of privacy and the potential for discriminatory treatment (Clarke 2016) and, more broadly, the potential to turn individuals into “slaves of unregulated innovations” with little, or no, free choice (Kass 2004, p. 173).

The type of personal data subject to digitization has expanded considerably over the years, starting with demographic data (e.g., name, home address, phone, e-mail, occupation) (e.g., Amaravadi 2003; Hann et al. 2007; Wakefield 2013), then extending to financial data, with a special emphasis on transaction and account data (such as purchases, bank account, credit cards, debts), followed by medical and health data (e.g., Dinev and Hart 2006; McKnight et al. 2002; Ozdemir et al. 2011). More recently, the personal data being digitized includes social media data, in terms of posts, likes, comments, shares, and photos (e.g., Choi et al. 2018; Salehan et al. 2017), behavioral data capturing daily activities (e.g., household and driving) and physical activities traced through wearable devices (e.g., Prasopoulou 2017; Warkentin et al. 2017), and also any type of digital traces resulting from user interaction with digital services (e.g., Lehrer et al. 2018; Newell and Marabelli 2015). Looking to the future, posthumanism foresees the technology-driven enhancement of three central attributes of human life—health, cognition, and emotion— with the aim to help individuals live longer, healthier lives endowed with the requisite cognition and emotion for responding to life situations and other people in a manner that enables human flourishing (Weiss 2014). These technologydriven enhancements are contingent upon digital personal data to assess, and in some cases regulate, the state of the human body, mind, and emotion.

As personal data digitalization permeates ever more areas of human existence, humans risk becoming artifacts of technology production (Demetis and Lee 2018). This concern brings to center stage the very notion of what it means to be human, a notion encapsulated in the term human dignity, defined broadly as the recognition that human beings possess intrinsic value and, as such, are endowed with certain rights and should be treated with respect (CBHD 2006). As the subject of intense interdisciplinary debate regarding its historical background, philosophical meanings, legal frameworks, and moral implications, among others, human dignity is a complex concept with several strands of meaning, which we will explicate as a foundation for our theorizing. Legal and philosophical discussions around human dignity have been growing concomitant with a surge in digital technologies that, for some, enhance the prospects for living with dignity, while, for others, threaten the very essence of human dignity (Benhabib 2013; Capps 2009; Gaita 2011; McCrudden 2008). However, without a shared understanding of the meaning of human dignity, the implications of new technologies for human dignity are difficult to disentangle. Given the importance of digital technology to human life, and the potential for digital technology to threaten human dignity (Kass 2004), we are in need of new theories that explore the various meanings of human dignity and shed light on the relationship between personal data digitalization and human dignity to ensure that personal data digitalization serves to promote, or at least not threaten, human dignity (Klang 2014).

IS research has a long tradition of investigating the interaction of humans (IT users) with IT (Sidorova et al. 2008). For instance, privacy calculus examines privacy risks and benefits as perceived in relation to personal information disclosure (e.g., Dinev and Hart 2006; Hann et al. 2007), identity theories are used to understand the bidirectional relationship of technology with a user’s identity (e.g., Carter and Grover 2015; Karahanna et al. 2018; Mishra et al. 2012), technology threat avoidance theory theorizes the means employed by IT users to avoid technological threats (Liang and Xue 2009), and IS design principles, such as the ones that highlight user participation (Briefs 1987) and ethical principles (Mumford 1981, 1983), aim to emancipate users (Hirschheim and Klein 1994). While the contributions of these theories are highly significant, they are fairly narrow in scope and have a limited ability to more broadly theorize the “societal grand challenge” (George et al. 2016) arising from advances in personal data digitalization.

With this in mind, we develop a grand theory that offers a multilevel dynamic equilibrium perspective of personal data digitalization. Our theory, CARE (claims, affronts, response, equilibrium), explicates the mechanisms tying personal data digitalization to claims, and affronts, to human dignity, uncovers several forms of dignity disequilibrium, and explains responses aiming to restore a dignity equilibrium. CARE also represents an “umbrella” concept that broadly accounts for different theoretical perspectives that capture (either implicitly or explicitly) some of its core dimensions (Hirsch and Levin 1999). CARE not only allows us to map various IS literature streams onto its constituent elements, but also aims to coordinate and encourage a dialogue among researchers from different theoretical perspectives (Hirsch and Levin 1999). We consider it imperative that IS scholars, developers, organizational and governmental policy makers, as well as users, reflect on the meaning and implications of personal data digitalization and human dignity before we reach a point, as feared by bioethicists, that we are no longer master of our own inventions (Kass 2004).

## Theorizing Approach

We followed an inductive top-down theorizing approach, depicted in Figure 1, because such an approach is particularly well-suited to research domains where the literature is “vast, dynamic, complex, and/or from disparate sources” (Shepherd and Sutcliffe 2011, p. 374) as is the case with both dignity, which covers a plethora of disciplines as well as time periods, and personal data digitalization, which includes many forms of data and technology. For the dignity literature, we read highly acclaimed books, recommended by colleagues in philosophy, and conducted forward and backward citation searches to expand our coverage of the dignity literature. For our search on personal data digitalization in the IS literature, we began with a search of the AIS Senior Scholars’ “basket of eight.” We constantly scanned the popular press as well, looking for examples that illustrated, or contradicted, the emerging theory, sparking further iterations with the academic literature. Both authors read all of the consulted literature in order to have a shared knowledge base.

Shepherd and Sutcliffe’s method is top-down in the sense that it begins with data contained in the literature as the starting point from which the theorist deduces a sensory representation, by which is meant the theorist’s perception of “tensions, oppositions, and/or contradictions among divergent perspectives and explanations of the same phenomena” (p. 361) in the literature. It is inductive in the sense that specific problems and solutions are abstracted from the sensory representation into conceptual representations, meaning the extraction of constructs and the formation of general abstract statements of relationships between constructs. Our initial sensory representations were based on the literature we consulted in the initial search. For each book chapter and article, we took copious notes of the content while also noting our impressions. We kept these content and impression notes in word documents with each author keeping separate documents for later comparison. Once our notes were complete, we then independently coded our notes and impressions into descriptive themes. These initial readings brought to our attention the need for additional readings, particularly in the areas of ethics, data analytics, big data and algorithms, topics that were frequently noted in the initial set of readings on personal data. As noted in Shepherd and Sutcliffe, theorists often iterate back and forth between the literature and sensory representation before proceeding to conceptual representation as the theorist’s attention becomes focused, not necessarily in the sense of more narrowly focused but in the sense of becoming focused on other aspects of the literature. In the next process of conceptual representation, we interpreted our notes on dignity and personal data digitalization. We iterated through various versions of our emerging CARE theory, each time going back and forth between our sensory and conceptual representations before reaching a point of coherence wherein the theory explained the tensions in the literature uncovered in our analysis.

As we evaluated our emerging theory, we kept in mind our goal of developing a grand theory. Grand theory is characterized by being broad and highly abstract with a view toward offering a general framework or overarching perspective from which to view a phenomenon across time and space (Turner and Boyns 2001). Grand theories are universal paradigms in a discipline that serve as a means of framing disciplinary knowledge and sparking mid- and micro-range theorizing (Higgins and Shirley 2000). For example, the symbolic interactionism theory (Blumer 1986; Mead 1934) is a grand theory in communications that has had tremendous influence on subsequent theorizing in many areas, such as identity theory (Stryker and Burke 2000), role behavior theory (Stryker and Statham 1985), and social movements (Turner 1983), among others. It is grand in that is broad, abstract, and general; it applies to all human communication contexts (Redmond 2015). Although the word grand might invoke expectations of complexity, in fact, grand theories tend to be characterized by a small number of broad theoretical principles (Redmond 2015). This is consistent with Shephard and Sutcliffe’s advice that theories are stronger when they are simpler, broader, and account for more evidence. Adhering to these tenets of grand theory, our theory is synthesized into three high-level statements, referred to as theoretical assertions, derived from our theorizing and intended to serve as the foundation for future proposition and hypothesis development in sundry contexts.

## Dignity

Human dignity, as the core of humanity (Düwell 2014a; Steenbakkers 2014), is inherent in each human being and in need of social protection (Pogge 2014). Three strands of the meaning of dignity can be found in philosophy: dignity as character or bearing, dignity as status, and dignity as intrinsic value (Rosen 2012). We refer to the first notion of dignity— dignity as living a life of virtue expressed in one’s character and bearing (Ober 2014)—as behavioral dignity. For the Greek philosophers, behavioral dignity was defined by the four cardinal virtues of wisdom, courage, moderation, and justice as laid out in Plato’s Republic and expanded upon by Aristotle. To Thomas Aquinas and the early Christian theologians, behavioral dignity consisted of individuals occupying their proper place in a divine order without complaint or resistance. Traces of behavioral dignity are also seen in the pre-European moral philosophy of the Aztec didactic oratorial moral texts advocating a life of self-control, respect, and tolerance (Pharo 2014) with an emphasis on moderate behavior and the qualities of humility, generosity, and courtesy (León-Portilla 2012). Similarly, in Confucianism, the emphasis on living a life of benevolence, righteousness, and integrity form a notion of human dignity as a code of conduct explaining what it means to live a dignified, fully human life (An’Xian 2014). Behavioral dignity is also seen in much later works, such as the political philosophy of De Toqueville, for whom dignity entailed accepting one’s station in life (Rosen 2012) and in the writings of the 18<sup>th</sup> century German philosopher, Friedrich Schiller, for whom behavioral dignity entailed a commitment and capacity to endure suffering with tranquility and to behave well in spite of natural inclination (Schiller 2016). Behavioral dignity is by nature a social construct because what has historically been considered virtuous is determined by society, family, and/or friends, and, specifically, by those with the power to influence others’ behavior through erudite argumentation, incentive, or force.

A second notion of dignity is that of meritocratic (or status) dignity wherein dignity denotes high social status and the honors and respectful treatment that are due to someone who enjoys such high status. In the Greco-Roman empires, meritocratic dignity was ascribed to a certain class of people , the guardian or warrior class, comprised of those individuals willing to risk their lives to defend the society (Fukuyama 2018). Meritocratic dignity was thus earned through certain behavior, but behavior that not everyone could successfully undertake. It is thus inherently unequal. In other civilizations, status dignity was reserved for those who observed certain religious practices. For example, in early Buddhist writings, the Buddhist counterpart of the word “dignified,” arhat, applied only to monks and was unattainable for laymen (Braarvig 2014). Likewise, in Islamic civilizations, human dignity was reserved for those following the commandments of Allah (Maróth 2014) and in Catholic societies, dignity only applied to those who embraced the teachings of the Catholic Church, with the reckless treatment of inhabitants of conquered territories being justified by the belief that “savages” or nonbelievers were not bequest of dignity (Pharo 2014). Over centuries, meritocratic dignity in Western societies became the reserve of the wealthy class and societal elites, no longer acquired by valiant courage in the face of battle or adherence to religious practices but rather associated with wealth and social standing. Because meritocratic dignity is scarce and fragile, holders of such status must remain vigilant against threats to their dignity as others seek to climb the ranks of meritocratic dignity (Ober 2014). As with behavioral dignity, meritocratic dignity is inherently social: to have status is to be recognized by others as superior in some capacity.

The third meaning of dignity, inherent dignity, was largely spurred by the philosophy of Kant, who is credited with being the first to argue that all humans had dignity by virtue of their rationality, by which he meant their possession of moral autonomy (Rosen 2012). Unlike meritocratic dignity, which was a property of social elites, the concept of dignity as intrinsic value in all humans extended dignity outward and downward such that it is common to all human beings (Rosen 2012). For Kant, all humans have dignity, even those who behave in an undignified manner (for example, by breaking civil laws). Thus, dignity came to be viewed as a universal social construct in the sense that all human beings are equally entitled to moral respect (Claassen 2014; Graumann 2014). Kant’s notion of dignity infers that humans should always be treated as ends in themselves and never as means. In other words, humans should never be treated as objects upon which to be acted in order to achieve some ulterior aim (Dürig 1956). In this sense, to profess human dignity is to recognize human equality and decry the “instrumentalization” of human beings (Düwell 2014a). Whereas Kant’s basis for ascribing dignity to humans is moral autonomy in the sense of the human capacity for moral reasoning, subsequent interpretations of inherent dignity consider human autonomy in the sense of freedom of an agent to choose his/her purpose without coercion, to present and express him/herself as he/she chooses, and to pursue a life of flourishing (Siegel 2012). These dual implications, being equal and having autonomy, of viewing humans as bearers of inherent dignity have enormous political, legal, and economic implications in addition to moral ones. Most fundamentally, the notion that each human being has dignity is tightly connected to human rights: it is because humans have dignity that they have basic human rights (Beyleveld 2014) and because all humans have dignity, they hold these rights equally (Brownsword 2014).

It is important to note that in the Confucian conception of dignity, the right to have one’s dignity respected is not inalienable: when an individual violates the norms of behavioral dignity, he can lose his dignity (An’Xian 2014). This suggests that in the Confucian conception, inherent dignity is contingent upon behavioral dignity. We will not engage in a debate here between Kantian and Confucian perspectives on the inalienability of human dignity, but for purposes of our theorization, we will accept the United Nations’ 1966 recognition that human rights “derive from the inherent dignity of the human person” (Beyleveld 2014, p. 24). Given the close association of human dignity with human rights, any affronts to human dignity are, therefore, threats to human rights, degrading humans’ capacities to pursue their own goals and increase the quality of their lives (Metz 2014).

Claims and affronts to dignity take different forms for the different meanings of dignity. Broadly speaking, a claim to dignity is treatment that enables behavioral dignity and/or treatment that recognizes the inherent or meritocratic dignity of the person whereas an affront to dignity is treatment that humiliates, insults, or injures the behavioral, inherent, or meritocratic dignity of the person. Specifically, for behavioral dignity, a claim to dignity is having access to resources required for virtuous living; an affront may be experienced when the conditions necessary for an individual to behave virtuously are compromised or eradicated. For meritocratic dignity, a claim to dignity is to be recognized as superior when one so merits; an affront may be felt when one’s wellearned status in a particular area is not recognized by others (Fukuyama 2018). For inherent dignity, the claims to dignity include being treated as a human, being recognized as equal to others, which includes being visible and having a voice, and being free/autonomous, which includes being able to selfexpress. Affronts to inherent dignity take the form of individuals being treated by others (including other individuals, organizations, institutions, or governments) as unequal in value or as objects, being subjected to humiliating or degrading treatment, or having autonomy constrained, manipulated, or coerced (Klang 2014; Neuhäuser and Stoecker 2014; Ober 2014; Pogge 2014).

In addition to the claims and affronts, there are also tensions across the forms of dignity. Meritocratic dignity largely runs contrary to inherent dignity because freedom and autonomy invariably create inequalities and hence, status differences. Recognition of the status of some may be humiliating for others, hence threatening the inherent dignity of the latter. Inherent dignity, through the claims of freedom and autonomy, might lead one to engage in behaviors that undermine one’s virtues. And the ability to live a life of virtue according to one’s own conception of virtue may be inhibited by those with status who try to influence the norms of virtue. ## The CARE Theory

Having developed our understanding of human dignity, we now craft our theory of human dignity amid personal data digitalization. We structure our theorization as follows: we first theorize the personal data digitalization encounters that produce claims, and affronts, to dignity resulting in three forms of dignity disequilibrium, namely intrapersonal, interpersonal, and associative. We then theorize the potential responses to dignity disequilibrium, and, finally, we theorize dignity equilibrium as dynamic.

## Personal Data Digitalization Encounters

Personal data digitalization (henceforth, PDD) takes four forms: knowing-self, showing-self, knowing-others, showingothers. We refer to these as PDD encounters because they each entail the personal data of an individual (the knowee/ showee) being digitized in a digital technology for use by a knower/shower that is either a micro-entity (i.e., an individual) or a macro-entity (e.g., an organization or government).

## Knowing-self

Knowing-self is a PDD encounter whereby aspects of an individual’s existence are digitized such that the individual is able to know more about himself or herself—his/her body, movement, emotion, brain activity, and more. To date, the type of personal data available for knowing-self includes behavioral data such as driving behavior (geolocation, velocity, travel date, time, distance, acceleration behavior, car type, and driver characteristics) (Kehr et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2012), habits and lifestyle data such as household activities (Warkentin et al. 2017) and carbon footprint data (Corbett 2013), physical activity data through wearable devices (Prasopoulou 2017), biological data and even brain activity data. Knowing self is an important psychological component of individuals need for self-definition and self-knowledge, which are tied to self-identity (Pierce et al. 2003) and inherent dignity (Fukuyama 2018). It is driven not only by the psychological need for self-knowledge, but also the neurobiological need of the brain to constantly seek ways to operate more efficiently (Hüther 2018).

Knowing-self potentially provides two claims to dignity: (1) being able to act with greater autonomy concerning decisions about the self and (2) having access to resources (in this case, knowledge about oneself, one’s health, and/or one’s behaviors) that might help one live a more virtuous life. As an example of the first, consider the case of Natural Cycles, a FemTech (feminine technology) cleared by both CE marking (a certification mark for products sold within the European Economic Area) and the FDA (Food and Drug Administration) for use as a digital method of birth control.

The app purportedly helps a woman know when best to try to conceive a child or, alternatively, how to avoid becoming pregnant without hormones,<sup>3</sup> by providing a woman information about her daily fertility based upon data entered by the woman—such as her basal body temperature taken every morning, luteinizing hormone test results, and intercourse dates—processed by algorithms (Sudjic 2018).<sup>4</sup> This can provide a woman with a sense of empowerment and freedom over her body, as least as far as pregnancy is concerned.

However, in order to experience a dignity claim from knowing-self, an individual must attend to and make sense of the personal digitalized data. This is a complex process involving attention diversion and the use of social comparison, either implicitly or explicitly, to acquire the requisite knowledge to interpret the data. In terms of attention diversion, the personal data digitalization encounter of knowingself focuses an individual’s attention on personal data that are currently amenable to digitization. Such data may, for some individuals, be diverting an individual’s attention away from areas of the self that are most important to an individual’s actual physical, mental, and emotional well-being and directing attention instead to those areas of the self that are digitizable. Control of attention is a powerful means of influencing behavior, in part because individuals have limited attention capabilities (Dukas 2004). With PDD, the individual’s autonomy is constrained in that someone else— in this case the organization that has developed the system for the personal data digitalization—is shaping an individual’s knowing-self through focusing attention on certain data. Returning to the Femtech example, the app has given many women a false sense of control, luring a woman into focusing on the data and displacing her own better judgement, hence affronting her autonomy. Indeed, a Swedish hospital reported many complaints of unwanted pregnancies by women who relied upon the Natural Cycle app.<sup>5</sup>

In terms of social comparison, even though knowing-self is personal, individuals must often rely upon comparisons with others to make sense of the data. Social comparison theory (Festinger 1954) posits that individuals are drawn to evaluate their capacities and characteristics against those of their peer groups (Chua and Chang 2016; Festinger 1954). Social comparison has likely been common since the dawn of behaviorally modern human beings (Fukuyama 2018) and is rooted in the brain’s constant search for better ways of doing things both as a survival mechanism and in the effort to operate more efficiently (Hüther 2018). In seeking to make sense of their own personal data, individuals will evaluate their data against that of others. In the case where the evaluation is largely positive, an individual’s sense of selfworth and identity will be confirmed but in the case where the evaluation is negative, an individual’s identity will be challenged, creating pressure on the individual to continually advance and to engage in behaviors, some of which may be unhealthy, in an attempt to compare favorably (Chua and Chang 2016). In this way, an individual’s autonomy is constrained (an affront to inherent dignity) as the individual engages in behaviors to compare favorably regardless of whether the behaviors are actually in the individual’s best interest. Because in the case of knowing-self, the knower and the knowee are the same person, the claims and affronts to dignity are experienced by the same individual, which we refer to as intrapersonal dignity disequilibrium. ## Showing-self

Showing-self is a PDD encounter whereby an individual displays, projects, or otherwise shares data about the self, including images, photos, opinions, preferences, knowledge, and comments through social media platforms (Choi et al. 2018; Krasnova et al. 2010; Lowry et al. 2011; Tow et al. 2010) as well as biological, location, and even health data over platforms with other users. Showing-self is driven by an individual’s need for recognition of the inner self, which she/he often perceives as suppressed by societal norms and expectations (Fukuyama 2018). With showing-self, individuals are able to show who they see themselves as, or who they wish others to see themselves as (Papacharissi 2010; Tufekci 2008). This results in a digital self that is, for some, not an authentic representation of who the individual really is, but a representation of either who the person wishes to be or wishes others to see him as (Chua and Chang 2016). Individuals desire not only to feel a sense of value (inherent dignity), but also long for others to recognize their inherent value (Fukuyama 2018). The desire for others to recognize one as equal in value is an important driver of human behavior (Fukuyama 2018) and drives, in part, showing-self: receiving thumbs-ups, likes, and comments in response to showing-self on a social platform are a means of feeling visible, of feeling recognized, of having one’s identity, and worth, affirmed by others (Chua and Chang 2016). Showingself via PDD thus enables the dignity claim of being visible and being free to self-express, both of which are claims to dignity as inherent value.

Yet, showing-self triggers potential affronts to the self, creating an intrapersonal dignity disequilibrium. As with knowing-self, these affronts occur as a result of the process of social comparison: individuals fear the humiliation of receiving dislikes, disparaging responses, or other indications of disapproval (thumbs-down and so forth) and therefore behave so as to ensure that their displayed self will meet with approval (Chua and Chang 2016). Take the case of teenage girls who engage in identity construction by manipulating their photos to meet the beauty standards set by their peers (Chua and Chang 2016). In this case their freedom of expression, and their autonomy to develop their own identity, is constrained because rather than presenting an image of themselves that is authentic, they are compelled to project an image that they believe others will accept and validate. This then reinforces the norm for appearances, opinions, or behaviors from which their showing-self might otherwise emancipate them. For those who go against the mainstream norms in their showing-self, they may experience humiliation if their photos, opinions, preferences, knowledge, or comments are disliked, maligned, or ridiculed by others who themselves are availing the opportunity of self-expression.

Equally insidious is the potential for unauthentic showing-self to confirm and reinforce oppressive societal norms. This creates potential affronts to the dignity of others (e.g., those who observe the showing-self of someone else or who are otherwise affected by the expectations it places on themselves by others). Dubrofsky and Wood (2015) argue that while some may view women’s agency to self-represent online as empowering, such self-representation is often intended as an invitation for male gazing and inadvertently reifies existing oppressive conceptualizations of women. Thus, in this way showing-self may also produce an interpersonal dignity disequilibrium in which the individuals experiencing the dignity affront from a PDD encounter are not the same as the individual experiencing the claim and indeed, the claim for the one individual is contingent on the affront to the others. ## Knowing-others

Knowing-others is the PDD encounter whereby aspects of one individual’s existence (the knowee) are digitalized such that another (the knower, which can be either an individual or an organization/government) is able to know more about the knowee. When the knower is an individual, we will refer to micro-level knowing-others. When the knower is an organization or government, we will refer to macro-level knowingothers. In both cases, the knowee is at the micro (individual) level.

## Micro-level Knowing-others

Similar to knowing-self, with micro-level knowing-others the knower is driven to know as much as possible based on both psychological drivers (the need for self-identity) (Pierce et al. 2003) and neurological drivers (the brain’s efforts to constantly find ways to operate more efficiently; Hüther 2018) because even though the data is from another (the knowee), the knower is the one using the data. With micro-level knowing-others, the knower is in essence learning about herself—for example, herself as a parent (monitoring their baby or teenage driver) or herself as a caretaker (monitoring their parents’ drug intake)—through data about the knowee. The knowing is then tied to behavioral dignity: the knower feels as though she is doing everything that can be done to be to perform the particular role (e.g., parent, caretaker) in an exemplary fashion, and thus verifies her role identity (Burke and Stets 1999). As an example, the Owlet baby sock is a digital sock worn by babies that monitors their heart rate and oxygen levels. The knowing-others (parents knowing their baby) made possible by the Owlet helps relieve some of the parents’ parenting stress. But as in the case of knowing-self, attention diversion is the mechanism through which microlevel knowing-others is tied to dignity affronts in the knower: parent users of the Owlet report that their attention is drawn to fluctuations in the baby’s data, changing how they care for their baby as well as how and when they physically engage with the baby (Wang et al. 2017). In this way, the knowing about the baby creates an affront for the knower (the parent) in the form of autonomy constraint, creating an intrapersonal dignity disequilibrium. ## Macro-level Knowing-others

Macro-level knowing-others began with individuals entering basic descriptive information about themselves into an information system often as a prerequisite to use a service or complete an online transaction (Culnan 1993; Culnan and Williams 2009; Dinev and Hart 2006; Dinev et al. 2008; Hann et al. 2007; Hui et al. 2007; Lee et al. 2011; McKnight et al. 2002). Purportedly, by having a record describing an individual, an organization (or government agency), the knower, is able to know important attributes of the individual that then enables the provision of personalized service (Tam and Ho 2005). Yet macro-level knowing-others, even when just limited to self-described personal data, also entails potential affronts to the dignity of the knowee, in particular a loss of autonomy concerning how the information is used and by whom (Conger et al. 2013; Hong and Thong 2013) as well as the potential for the knowee to feel treated as an object. Harms attributed to unauthorized access of personal data include damage to psychological well-being, changes to social status, and physical danger (Karwatzki et al. 2017; Warkentin et al. 2017).

An emerging trend in organizations is the use of tools to analyze and compare the digital behavior of employees: how much time each employee is spending reading and writing emails, making phone calls, browsing the internet (Rusli 2013). The software analyzes the digital behavior and can be programmed to send prods to employees who are exceeding the range of time the organization has determined is desirable for optimal productivity. An individual’s behavior is thus being known by the organization in a way that was heretofore unknown. And the individual’s attention is being shaped both by the data that is being gathered and the knowledge that it is being gathered (Stanko and Beckman 2015). Although these systems might be marketed as empowering the employee to obtain higher performance, they also threaten the employee’s autonomy, thereby creating an intrapersonal dignity disequilibrium.

Macro-level knowing-others frequently entails the analysis of the digital trace data of individuals without their awareness (Newell and Marabelli 2015) and the classification of individuals into categories using data analytics and algorithms (e.g., datification; Markus 2017) presumably for the purpose of more personalized service to the individual. Indeed, there may be claims to inherent dignity (for the knowee), specifically equal treatment, made possible by macro-level knowing-others. Yet macro-level knowing-others that categorizes individuals into social groups or behavioral categories also raises critical concerns for their dignity. As noted by Pullman (2001), the very act of labeling individuals through drawing inferences based upon personal data is a means of denying others their humanity and depriving them of equal moral consideration. While self-categorization enables individuals to join the groups that they feel they belong to (Hogg and Terry 2000), categorization by PDD can result in not just a loss of autonomy for the individual, but also discrimination against the individual based upon their being classified as part of an undesirable group (Clarke 2016; Marjanovic and Cecez-Kecmanovic 2017; Newell and Marabelli 2015) or not being classified as part of a desirable group. Such classifications often serve to reinforce existing power structures and suppress the most vulnerable (Khan 2015). To illustrate, consider the SAT adversity score. The SAT is a widely used college preparatory examination that heavily influences college admissions in the United States. Based on descriptive data entered by a high school student when registering for the test, the SAT organization computed an “adversity score” intended to indicate to college admissions’ offices the degree of hardship faced by the student.<sup>6</sup> The claim to dignity is for the recognition of the value of disadvantaged students (primarily minority and low-income). However, because the score relied on data input by the student and a nontransparent algorithm, there is a potential for students who were indeed disadvantaged receiving a low adversity score because they overestimated their parents’ income, or the zip code they lived in was typical of high-value houses even if their own family’s house was not high-value. Such an ecological fallacy creates a potential dignity affront for those students who actually were facing high adversity in life being classified as though they were not. We refer to this situation in which individuals belonging to one group experience a dignity claim from a PDD whereas individuals not belonging to the group (or not properly recognized as belonging to the group) experience a dignity affront, as associative dignity disequilibrium. The individuals feeling the disequilibrium are those experiencing the dignity affront. Table 5 summarizes our discussion of knowing-others.

## Showing-others

Showing-others is the PDD encounter whereby aspects of one individual’s existence are digitalized such that another (the shower, which can either be an individual or an organization/ government) makes visible aspects of the showee’s existence, such as the body, language, emotion, or behavior. Unlike showing-self, the showee may have little choice in the data being shown. When the shower is an individual, we will refer to micro-level showing-others. When the shower is an organization or government, we will refer to macro-level showingothers. In both cases, the showee is at the micro (individual) level.

## Micro-level Showing-others

Micro-level showing-others is similar to micro-level knowingothers in that it mostly entails a power imbalance between the shower and the showee and frequently confers claims to the former and affronts to the latter, forming an interpersonal dignity disequilibrium. As with showing-self, the tie to dignity claims of micro-level showing-others comes from self expression and the tie to dignity affronts through social comparison and attention diversion. We will not restate these mechanisms here but will offer the example of what Steinberg (2017) calls “sharenting,” the creation of digital versions of children by their parents, who post images and stories of their children to social media platforms as a means of expressing their identity, verifying their role identity as successful parents. Research is just now emerging on children who become teenagers discovering highly personal anecdotes about themselves posted by their parents and the frictions this creates between parents and the child but also between the child and friends, who compare the digital versions of themselves to see who is more important, more loved, more attractive, more exciting and so on (Steinberg 2017). In addition, the images and stories of the child, indelibly marked by the values of the parent, become part of the child’s digital footprint, thereby potentially constraining the child’s future autonomy. The liberty of self-expression of the shower (the parent in this example) thereby clashes with the future autonomy of the showee (the child), creating an interpersonal dignity disequilibrium. Ironically, micro-level showing-others can also result in dignity affronts to the shower, such as when the shower’s attention is so diverted by the digital recording of the “other” that the shower is distracted from fully experiencing the moment, creating a constraint on the shower’s autonomy. In this way, micro-level showing-others may also create intrapersonal dignity disequilibrium.

## Macro-level Showing-others

At the macro-level, there are numerous emerging technologies, such as facial scanners, body cams, digital secretaries, and text analytics as well as established technologies such as ultrasounds, body scanners, and lie detection technology, that enable the showing of others. As with macro-level knowingothers, it may not be as obvious how macro-level showingothers can result in dignity claims as much as dignity affronts, but in fact, the emergence of these technologies is largely premised on the protection of the well-being of individuals or promoting their performance. As an example, take the case of an organizational gamification system that attaches numerical scores to workers’ productivity and publicly discloses this to all the employees (Kim and Werbach 2016). In this case, the individual work performance data is the personal data that is being digitalized. One might view the system as a dignity claim for high performers in the sense of providing them recognition for their performance (meritocratic dignity), but also as an affront to dignity in the case of lower performers because the displaying of their performance data may be humiliating and may lack context. In this case, the dignity disequilibrium takes the interpersonal form, with high performers being visibly recognized and low performers being humiliated.

In some instances, showing-others is encouraged or mandated by an external agency and even though only displayed internally, can serve to benefit some groups over others. A case in point is the digitalization of domestic abuse victims by police as part of the process of filing claims. As described in Moore (2015), digital images of abuse suffered by light-skinned victims appear much more pronounced than do digital images of abuse suffered by dark-skinned victims. So even while both light- and dark-skinned women are treated equally in the sense of having their abuse digitalized for display to prosecutors and judges, the abuse of light-skinned victims is treated more seriously in part because the injuries, as recorded in digital images, are much more visible. While these images offer a dignity claim to light-skinned victims, displaying the violation of their dignity and prompting police agency response, it reinforces societal preferential treatment of lighter-skinned women, thereby further affronting the dignity of darker-skinned victims (Moore 2015). This associative dignity equilibrium is all the more troubling because, even if unintended, it reifies an historically-rooted meritocratic dignity wherein race confers status. The above theorization is synthesized into our first theoretical assertation:

Theoretical Assertion 1: The personal data digitalization encounters of knowing (self and others) and showing (self and others) produce claims and affronts to behavioral, meritocratic, and inherent dignity resulting in intrapersonal, interpersonal, and associative dignity disequilibrium.

Theoretical Assertion 1 is intentionally broad, providing a basis for numerous research questions around which midrange theories could be developed and empirically tested. Research questions might target specific PDD encounters, the interplay among PDD encounters, and/or the effect of a PDD encounter on those not directly involved in the PDD. Some of the theories that fall under the CARE umbrella may be useful to investigate these questions. For instance, privacy theories can partly help explain the interplay among PDD encounters, such as how the willingness to engage in showingself may be constrained by the anxiety of becoming a showee in some else’s showing-others. Generally, theories of identity, social comparison, and privacy might complement CARE to address challenges related to an “inappropriate” enactment of specific PDDs by an individual knower and/or shower, whereas theories of social categorization and surveillance might be useful when the knower and shower is at the macrolevel. ## Response to Dignity Disequilibrium

Responses to dignity disequilibrium may be undertaken by those experiencing the disequilibrium or those witnessing an affront to someone else’s dignity, as well as by groups, organizations, and governments. To identify potential responses to dignity disequilibrium, we undertook several iterations of inductive top-down theorizing (Shepherd and Sutcliffe 2011), this time consulting multiple significant bodies of work, including coping theory (e.g., Lazarus 1993; Liang and Xue 2009), stress response research (e.g., Taylor 2006), and institutional theory (e.g., Oliver 1991). We engaged in several cycles of comparing the various forms of responses emerging from these literature streams with the examples we had observed in our review of the dignity and personal data digitalization literatures as well as in media outlets. Through this, we derived the micro- and macro-level responses depicted in Table 9. The list of the both micro- and macrolevel responses is by no means exhaustive, and we would expect new responses to emerge through technology, judiciary, and social changes.

## Micro-level Responses

A forfeit response involves the abdicating of one dignity claim for another or the accepting of a dignity affront as necessary for a dignity claim. In some cases, the individual may be unaware of a dignity affront, also resulting in a forfeit response. In other cases, an individual may have no choice but to forfeit, such as when an employer has a policy mandating that employees be tracked in some manner. IS research has widely studied forfeit from a privacy paradox perspective in which individuals willingly disclose personal information in spite of the risks and violation to their privacy in order to gain certain positive outcomes (Choi et al. 2018; Culnan 1993; Dinev and Hart 2006; Kordzadeh and Warren 2017; Krasnova et al. 2010; Lowry et al. 2011). A risk of forfeiting one’s claim to dignity is that over time, one begins to accept the state as normal both for oneself and for others (Hüther 2018).

Unlike a forfeit response, fight and flight responses involve actions to eliminate or avoid a dignity affront. The fight response entails eliminating the source of the stress through aggressive behavior, often including confrontation (Taylor 2006). When the source of stress is PDD, fight may take the form of action against a personal data digitalization platform provider, such as the lawsuit initiated by two Uber drivers against Uber in order to force the company to disclose details of the enormous amounts of data collected about drivers and used in algorithmic decisions affecting their workload and pay.<sup>7</sup> Fight might also take the form of manipulating the data in such a way as to benefit the individual, such as when an employee participating in an organizational wellness program involving a wearable fitness-tracking device places the device on his dog in order to manipulate the data.<sup>8</sup>

In contrast to a fight response, a flight response refers to the act of coping by avoidance: one flees the source of stress (Taylor 2006). When the source of stress is PDD, an individual may avoid the stress by adopting alternative technological solutions, as for instance choosing to use DuckDuckGo as a search engine instead of the giant Google in order to not be tracked.<sup>9</sup> Another form of flight response is opting-out of the technology (Spiekermann and Korunovska 2017; Warkentin et al. 2017). While opting-out might be an effective measure, it also risks creating an affront to dignity in that one might lose access to the digital resources required for a virtuous life.

Whereas forfeit, fight, and flight are largely undertaken by single individuals experiencing the dignity disequilibrium, other micro-level responses—notably tending and befriending—involve multiple individuals, both some experiencing the dignity disequilibrium and some observing it. Tending refers to the protection of offspring as an affiliationbased response to stress (Taylor 2006). An individual who empathizes with someone else who is being subjected to humiliating or degrading treatment, such as through offensive comments in response to an image produced by the PDD showing-self, may engage in a tending response. To illustrate, if one’s friend is a victim of cyberbullying on online platforms, one can offer emotional support and can also report the content, such as image or offensive comments, to the digital platform provider. Befriending involves affiliating with others as a means of coping with a threat (Taylor 2006). It may take the form of joining an online community as well as of posting negative reviews in those communities against an organization that the individual feels is falsely using his data. To illustrate, r/AntiFacebook is an online community in Reddit. It currently has over 8K members and its description reads:

A community for posting articles about the problems of Facebook: its disrespect for privacy, widespread censorship, manipulation of users, and overall threat it poses to the freedom of the web and social relationships.<sup>10</sup>

An individual may join this group of people because he/she feels that Facebook has treated him/her as an object of manipulation with constrained liberty and autonomy (i.e., dignity affronts). While befriending does not change the reality, it may nevertheless be helpful in re-establishing emotional stability.

## Macro-level Responses

Macro-level responses, which are largely triggered by the micro-level responses, include actions taken by coordinated individuals (as in mobilize) as well as organizational and governmental policies and actions (as in comply, resist, and regulate). These responses overlap with the action repertoires of collective action (Taylor and Van Dyke 2004) and with organizational strategic responses (Oliver 1991).

Mobilize responses are initiated by a coherent group of individuals that shares a concern (such as through befriending at the micro level) and seeks to target the entities responsible for the “misuse” of PDD. In collective action (i.e., a form of mobilize), a group may engage in traditional or digitallymediated social action (Bennett and Segerberg 2012; Selander and Jarvenpaa 2016) to exert pressure on organizations, platform providers, and/or government to make changes to protect individuals’ dignity. For instance, to protect their private spaces, residents of a small UK town self-organized to halt a Google Street view car from taking photos of their houses and publishing them online.<sup>11</sup> Mobilizing can also take the form of a group of individuals pursuing legal claims against a PDD practice, such as the initiation of a class action lawsuit against the Australian government for unlawful debt collection, an automated process that relied upon a flawed and inaccurate algorithm.<sup>12</sup>

General public opinion and/or the interest group behind a mobilize response can be thought of as an institutional constituent that exerts pressure on organizations (Oliver 1991). When facing institutional pressure, organizations employ various response strategies, including acquiesce, compromise, avoid, defy, and manipulate strategies that vary by the degree of active organizational resistance in order to maintain stability and legitimacy (Oliver 1991). Drawing upon the resistance range from active to passive, we abstract the strategic responses into two superordinate level categories relevant to the PDD context: comply and resist. A comply response, which includes acquiesce and compromise strategies, denotes organizational attempts to undertake changes in line with institutional pressures. In the PDD context, an organization many enact a comply response by modifying the personal data digitalization platform or by changing its personal data policies. A resist response , which includes avoid, defy, and manipulate strategies, denotes organizational efforts to withstand external pressures. For instance, an organization might force individuals to consent to long, difficult to understand policy agreements, or might signal transparency by providing a difficult to comprehend explanation of the algorithms behind their personal data analysis (Newell and Marabelli 2015).

If the micro- and macro-level responses are strong enough or if there has been prominent media attention on the PDD and particular affronts, a government might then respond with regulation. For example, the New Zealand government recently produced a set of standards for the use of algorithms by public agencies,<sup>13</sup> and the European Union General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) of May 2018 aims to protect data privacy for all EU citizens by regulating how organizations handle personal data.

## Responding to Restore Equilibrium

Having discussed the range of behaviors that may be invoked in response to dignity disequilibrium, we now explain why a response is necessary. The state of dignity disequilibrium is experienced by the individual(s) perceiving a dignity affront. This individual may be the knower/knowee and shower/ showee in the case of intrapersonal disequilibrium, the knower, shower, knowee, and showee in the case of interpersonal disequilibrium, and the knowee, showee, as well as others by virtue of belonging to (or not belonging to) a group in the case of associative dignity disequilibrium. The associative dignity disequilibrium emphasizes that individuals who are not directly using a technology (either as knower/shower or knowee/showee) may nevertheless suffer dignity affronts.

An affront to one’s dignity is felt as unfair and a powerful feeling of resentment arises because the desire to be recognized by others as equal (also referred to as isothymia) and the desire to be recognized as superior (also called megalothymia) are both strong drivers of human behavior (Fukuyama 2018). When an individual feels that he is being disrespected, either not being treated as equal or not recognized as superior when he feels he merits such recognition, the individual experiences a range of negative emotions from depression and humiliation to anger and rage that provoke various responses (Fukuyama 2018). The neurobiologist Hüther (2018) claims that violations of dignity activate the same neural networks in the brain as injuries to the body, forcing the brain to respond. As an individual either finds justifications to rationalize the affront to his dignity or develops ideas on how to respond to the indignity, the brain creates connections across the nerve cells in the brain that are fixated on the indignity in an effort to return the brain to a state of equilibrium, for as long as the brain is confronting a sense of indignity, the energy consumption demanded of the brain increases and neuro attention is diverted from other areas (Hüther 2018). From a psychological perspective, an affront to one’s sense of dignity is a negative stimulus. Negative stimuli may create a sense of cognitive dissonance, prompting an individual to act in such a way as to reduce the dissonance (Festinger 1957). Research demonstrates that individuals pay more attention to negative, as opposed to neutral or positive, stimuli (e.g., Ito et al. 1998; Smith et al. 2003), creating an attentional bias whereby information with a negative valence receives more cognitive attention than do other types of information (Pratto and John 1991). Negative stimuli mobilize a cognitive–affective response designed to minimize the adverse impact of the negative stimuli (Taylor 2006).

Based on the above, we present our second theoretical assertation:

Theoretical Assertion 2: Intrapersonal, interpersonal, and associative dignity disequilibrium will trigger microand macro-level responses in order to restore equilibrium.

Theoretical Assertion 2 suggests numerous avenues for future research. In particular, there are questions concerning the relation of the responses to the forms of dignity and dignity disequilibrium, and questions concerning the intensity and effectiveness of the responses. Theories focusing on fairness, and how individuals respond to injustice, might provide a mid-range complement to CARE in addressing some of these questions. For example, justice theory (Greenberg 1987; Lind and Tyleer 1988) could help understand the conditions under which affronts to dignity result in more intense or more effective responses. Perhaps affronts are more acute when more than one form of justice—distributive, procedural, and interactional (Cropanzano et al. 2007)—are felt to be violated. Or perhaps individuals are more willing to accept a dignity affront as long as they perceive a distributive justice—that others suffer the same affront. ## Dignity Equilibrium as Dynamic

As a final step in our theorizing, we return to the meaning of dignity because the extent to which one experiences a dignity claim, or affront, depends on the meaning one ascribes to dignity. We argue that the meaning of dignity is not static and may be influenced by environmental and societal changes, including by the very knowledge acquired at the micro and macro levels from personal data digitalization.

From a historical perspective, dignity—to whom and what it applies—continues to evolve. Early references to human dignity and the special worth of humanity abound in the Middle Ages and are tied to the belief in man being created in the likeness of God (Dales 1977). This early notion did not consider the unique worth of each individual but only the collective worth of humanity. It was not until the Renaissance that particularist conceptions of human dignity arose (Pullman 2001). From here, the Enlightenment thinkers, sparked by the philosophy of Kant, began to identify human dignity with moral autonomy. Developments in the 20<sup>th</sup> century led to conceptualizations of dignity in terms of autonomy per se, and not just moral autonomy (per Kant). Dignity came to be associated with the right to self-determination and the right to the conditions necessary to live and flourish as an agent (Düwell 2014b). In contrast to Kant, whose notion of dignity applies only to the human species, the current notions of dignity consider other species as potentially worthy of dignified treatment (Pogge 2014). In light of advances in our understanding of the implications of genetic engineering on animals’ needs and behavior (Lansink 2019; Ortiz 2004), we are beginning to see the notion of dignity being applied to animals (Schaber 2014). Likewise, dignity is also being applied to nature as in the case, for example, of the granting of personhood rights to the Klamath River.<sup>15</sup>

Moreover, there are advancing notions of collective dignity. Neuhäuser (2011), for example, attends to the collective or social nature of shared dignity, suggesting that collective dignity, just like individual dignity, may be violated through direct, symbolic, and representative group humiliation (Werner 2014). Furthermore, recent emphasis on dignity has shifted from looking solely at how an individual is treated largely by government agencies and organizations, to how individuals treat others (Düwell 2014b). The others constitute not only humans living today, but also the future humans. With individuals’ autonomy and freedom come social responsibilities, implying that human behaviors should be restricted, if need be, in order to preserve the essential resources for future generations (Düwell 2014b).

Because of the far-reaching consequences of human actions for the future, the interaction of society and nature is a central theme in the discipline of sustainability (Kates et al. 2001). From a sustainability perspective, all developments should “meet the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs” (World Commission on Environment and Development 1987, p. 41). Guided by 17 sustainable development goals<sup>16</sup> that encourage both social development and environmental protection, organizations and governments across the globe are called to design and implement policies and regulations to avoid the risk of putting the future generation in peril. These sustainability concerns expand the meaning of dignity to support “third-generation” human rights such as the right to “an environment of a quality that permits a life of dignity and well-being” and a “responsibility to protect and improve the environment for present and future generations” (Hollenbach 2014, p. 255). Finally, the human emotions triggered by the “murder” of the Hitchbot—the hitchhiker robot<sup>17</sup>—suggests that we might also expect dignity to be considered for manmade objects in the future.

To the extent that this push is driven by the individual wanting to know more about his/her impact on others in order to pursue a life of dignity, this may be viewed as a positive development. To the extent that this becomes imposed on individuals by platform providers, organizations, or governments, such advancements in PDD may provoke new incidences of dignity disequilibrium and response.

## Implications

The CARE theory developed in this paper is a grand theory that theorizes the relationship of human dignity to personal data digitalization. Grand theories can be used as conceptual models to guide research and practice (Markus 2014) and can serve as the inspiration and foundation of mid-range theorizing (Gregor 2006). Here we highlight implications for IS research and practice.

## Implications for IS Research

CARE places human dignity at its core and sets the scene for future IS research to investigate digital technologies from a societal grand challenge perspective (George et al. 2016). Given the broader objective of CARE to better understand the new reality shaped by personal data digitalization and the significant contributions of the IS theories that partly map (implicitly or explicitly) to some of the CARE’s constitutive elements, as illustrated in Table 12, CARE theory can be construed as an “umbrella concept” (Hirsch and Levin 1999) under which theories that investigate related phenomena, albeit from less broad perspectives, are tied. We recognize that the list of theories currently situated under CARE in this paper is not exhaustive; indeed, we believe that other IS research streams may find common grounds with some of the CARE elements.

We invite IS researchers from different theoretical perspectives to revisit existing research based on the insights generated by CARE and to further position their work within the CARE theory. For instance, even though IS research has long studied the implications of IS use on users (e.g., on individual users disclosing personal information in privacy research, on IT users perceiving a technological threat, and on business users of an organizational system in IS design), this body of knowledge primarily overlaps to one of CARE’s dignity disequilibrium forms, that is, intrapersonal disequilibrium. CARE extends current IS research through the addition of two other disequilibrium forms—interpersonal and associative— denoting that personal data digitalization also has implications to others including the knower, knowee, shower, and showee as well as non-users who may be ingroup members or out-group members. CARE encourages IS researchers to consider implications on a broader scale such as the second-hand effect of IT use on indirect users (knowees and showees) as well as non-users.

Finally, considering that personal data digitalization encounters are grounded on self-identity, social comparison, and social categorization among others, it might be appealing to identity researchers to consult the CARE theory. For instance, internal identity asymmetry is the perception that one’s real identity is mistaken by others (Meister et al. 2014). Personal data digitalization can at once increase one’s capacity for selfpresentation by allowing an individual to selectively digitalize, or selectively erase, aspects of their work and lives, thereby providing an impression management tool to help individuals adjust the perception held by others, while also increasing one’s internal identity asymmetry by selectively digitalizing aspects of an individual’s work while ignoring others. Research is needed to understand the bidirectional relationship between personal data digitalization and identity. While we would normally expect users to know-self and show-self through system use, the users’ identity might also be shaped by other users—through the process of attention diversion and social comparison—as much as it is presented by the user. Across various streams of research, the use of CARE as an overarching theory can facilitate the accumulation of a comprehensive and cohesive body of knowledge in IS regarding the challenges and opportunities of personal data digitalization given the rapid technological developments and implications to human dignity.

## Implications for Practice

The CARE theory provides a number of implications for practice, namely for system designers, organizational and governmental policy makers, and individual users.

## Implications for System Designers

For IS design, this study suggests a focus on designing with dignity and designing for dignity. Designing with dignity entails thinking of ways to design a system while respecting the individuals who might use the technology, as well as those who will not use the technology per se but will be affected by it (e.g., the knowee and showee in cases of knowing-others and showing-others). Designing with dignity complements the framework of value sensitive design, which incorporates human values during the design process of a technology (Friedman et al. 2008). Of particular interest is the concept of digital nudging, a concept derived from behavioral economics, that refers to the use of design elements as a way to influence people’s behavior (Schneider et al. 2017; Weinmann et al. 2016). Although the intentions of nudging may be good—to help guide people toward a better way of living (e.g., living a healthier life) and toward socially responsible behavior (e.g., reducing CO<sub>2</sub> footprint)—nudging might potentially constrain one’s freedom of choice and autonomy (Schneider et al. 2017; Weinmann et al. 2016). Designing with dignity would suggest that designers customize digital nudges according to the values of an individual in order to respect the individual’s moral agency and freedom to choose what is meant by a “virtuous” life (i.e., behavioral dignity).

Not only might designers design with dignity, as espoused above, the CARE theory would urge designers to design for dignity, going beyond emancipation (Hirschheim and Klein 1994; Ngwenyama 1991) to pursue personal data digitalization with a specific objective to enable one of the three forms of human dignity. In particular, more design attention is warranted in regard to systems supporting behavioral dignity. To date, most instances of personal data digitalization have enabled claims to the third form of dignity—dignity as inherent value—and in particular, the promotion of autonomy. Design is needed that supports individuals in their efforts to live virtuously, with virtuous taking on various meanings including healthy, responsible, environmentally considerate, and waste conscious lives and even more nebulous meanings such as considerate, uplifting, thoughtful, and compassionate lives.

## Implications for Organizational and Governmental Policy

Organizations have long engaged in scientific management, carefully scrutinizing aspects of human work to produce the most effective outcomes as efficiently as possible (Perlow 1998). Personal data digitalization is enabling organizations to implement new initiatives that track employee behavior and performance, such as incorporating fitness trackers and competitions into wellness initiatives purportedly designed to improve the well-being of employees but also intended to reduce illness-related absenteeism and insurance premiums (Giddens et al. 2016) and gamification systems that keep track of employee work performance (Kim and Werbach 2016). Even though some of the coded information in a digital performance system may benefit the employee, omitted information results in an incomplete picture of the employee’s value to the organization, resulting in mixed emotions and a range of positive and negative responses (Stein et al. 2015). Also, as noted in the CARE theory, we would expect the employee’s attention to be focused on those aspects of his work that are being digitalized, perhaps at the expense of others. Thus, even though employee data digitalization is a means by which employers can shape employee thoughts and emotions, potentially rendering them more productive (Stanko and Beckman 2015), organizations will need to address with care the intrapersonal disequilibrium felt by employees, such as making sure that employees feel strong enough claims to their dignity to compensate for the coercion of their autonomy. And in cases where employees have customer facing positions, organizations should think of the implications for the customers when the employees’ focus is diverted to the digitalization of certain aspects of their work.

Moreover, because digitizing the work of an employee can be used as ground for the employee’s performance evaluation, it is all the more important for organizations to be certain that the behaviors being digitized are those that matter most to performance. Given that there will be norms of performance embedded in a digitalization initiative against which employees will compare themselves, organizations must work carefully with employees to determine the appropriate norm. Likewise, governmental institutions should be proactive in issuing rules, regulations, and policies to protect human dignity as a consequence of PDD and to strive toward a power balance between organizations and individuals (i.e., data users and data providers). The case of ninety Spanish citizens against Google’s indexing of personal information highlights the need for the court to sometimes intervene and protect “the right to the future” (Zuboff 2019). When acting as the knower and shower in the PDD encounters of knowing-others and showing-others, governments must ensure that their actions do not run counter to human dignity.

## Implications for Users

Our CARE theory suggests that users need to be aware that once they embark on knowing-self, the inability to un-know may itself prove an affront to their dignity in that the knowledge sets constraints on their behavior, creating a spiraling data race where the individual needs ever more sophisticated personal data to better understand the meaning of the current data. Likewise, once a user engages in showing-self, it may be very difficult to un-show, resulting in a reputation that is not easily shed. Because it is not always possible to un-know and un-show, users should be vigilant in how they use personal data digitalization in their everyday lives. Lastly, our theory emphasizes the effect of IT use on others. Users should also think beyond their own need for self-expression to how their self-showing affects others, something one might think of as the second-hand effect of one’s IT use. There is a fine line between negative self-expression (criticizing others in tweets, digital shaming of others, disliking others’ posts) and cyberbullying (Bedeley et al. 2019). Moreover, individuals need to understand that when they present unauthentic depictions of themselves, they are potentially hurting others, who see those representations and use them to judge themselves. Individuals should be mindful of others in their use of personal data digitalization.

## Conclusion

From a philosophical perspective, the concept of dignity dates back to Greek philosophers, and yet dignity continues to play a pivotal role in modern life with such notions as respect, human flourishing, empowerment, and self-control—notions that are part of the conceptual family of dignity—shaping everyday moral considerations (Neuhäuser and Stoecker 2014). The continued importance of human dignity is seen in its use in as the basis for both secular (Henkin 1979) and theological (Moltmann 1984) claims of universal and inalienable human rights. Human dignity is a unifying concept, transcending politics, religion, gender, age, and ethnicity (Hüther 2018) and providing a common moral ground upon which to understand, and interpret, the impact of external forces, including technology, on human life and the impact of human life on others, as well as on what it means to be human (Pullman 2001). Our CARE theory explicates how behavioral dignity, meritocratic dignity, and inherent dignity can be claimed, and threatened, through the personal digitalization encounters of knowing and showing of the self and of others. Of particular importance is the fact that dignity affronts are experienced not only by the data users themselves but also by others who might be non-users, resulting in the three forms of dignity disequilibrium: intrapersonal, interpersonal, and associative. Although technology is a morally neutral object (Kass 2004), the impact of technology on human life is not morally neutral and neither are those designing, using, and benefitting from such technology use. This is all the more reason to theorize around a concept that is linked to the very meaning of what it is to be human, that of dignity. Interpreting a new phenomenon—personal data digitalization— with an established concept—human dignity—provides a novel way to understand the use and consequences of information systems on human life.

## About the Authors

Dorothy E. Leidner is the Ferguson Professor of Information Systems at Baylor University. She is a Senior Research Fellow of Lund University in Sweden. Dorothy received her Ph.D. from the University of Texas at Austin, from where she also received an MBA with a concentration in information systems and a bachelor’s degree in the Plan II Honors program. She received an honorary doctorate from Lund University in 2018 and became a Fellow of the Association for Information Systems in 2011. Dorothy is currently the editor-in-chief of the Journal of the Association for Information Systems. She has previously served as editor-in-chief of MIS Quarterly Executive and Database for Advances in Information Systems. She has served as senior editor for MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and Journal of Strategic Information Systems. She directs the Ph.D. program in Information Systems at Baylor, a program she founded in 2008. It was a milestone for the University, being the very first PhD program offered by the Hankamer School of Business.

Olgerta Tona is an assistant professor at the department of Applied IT, University of Gothenburg, and a research affiliate at the Swedish Center for Digital Innovation (SCDI). She received her Ph.D. in information systems from Lund University in 2017 and was awarded the Börje Langeforspriset by Svenska Informationssystem Akademin for the best Ph.D thesis. Her research interests include business intelligence and analytics, personal data, and societal implications of algorithmic decision-making.
