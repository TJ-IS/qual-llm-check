# Healthcare IT Adoption: An Analysis of Knowledge Transfer in Socioeconomic Networks

GANG PENG, DEBABRATA DEY, AND ATANU LAHIRI

He received his Ph.D. from the Foster School of Business, University of Washington. His papers have appeared in Management Science, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Decision Support Systems, and Manufacturing & Service Operations Management.

ABSTRACT: Despite the potential of health information technology (HIT) systems to significantly reduce medical errors, streamline clinical processes, contain healthcare costs, and ultimately improve the quality of healthcare, their adoption by hospitals in the United States has been rather slow. To study this adoption process and get insights into the underlying mechanisms, in this work we synthesize the theories on social networks and knowledge transfer. We propose a research framework in which the absorptive capacity of a potential adopter and the collective disseminative capacity of connected adopters act as two key determinants of knowledge transfer in a socioeconomic network, and these two capacities substitute for each other in affecting HIT adoption. We also propose that, in a network setting, the mechanism of knowledge transfer manifests quite differently from that of social contagion in its impact on the diffusion process at different stages of adoption. Using a large longitudinal data set covering adoption decisions of more than five thousand hospitals across a thirteen-year horizon, we find strong support for our hypotheses. Our analysis shows that knowledge flow in provider networks plays a key role in fostering technology diffusion in initial years, allowing the contagion effect to set in sooner for quicker adoption in later years. Therefore, recent efforts at multiple levels to form integrated healthcare delivery networks should accelerate HIT adoption.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: absorptive capacity, disseminative capacity, healthcare, healthcare information system, healthcare information technology, healthcare information technology adoption, integrated healthcare delivery system, knowledge transfer, social network.

The healthcare industry in the United States today is plagued by numerous challenges, reflected in the rising cost of healthcare and endless political debates surrounding it. Currently, the United States spends \$2.7 trillion annually on healthcare alone, translating to about 18 percent of the country’s GDP [55], the highest percentage in the world. At the same time, the quality of care is toward the bottom of the scale when compared with other developed nations [28]. Indeed, the healthcare sector lacks effective mechanisms to coordinate patient care, share relevant information, and monitor compliance with relevant guidelines [49]. An effective way to meet these challenges would be to deploy integrated health information technology (HIT), which can reduce medical errors, streamline clinical processes, contain healthcare costs, and improve overall quality [6, 25, 33]. Recognizing this, in 2009, President Obama committed \$20 billion to computerize all medical records [34]. Despite such support, adoption of information technology in the U.S. healthcare industry lags far behind that in other industries in the country [3], as well as the healthcare industry in many other developed nations [23]. According to the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS), as of the first quarter of 2013, roughly one in eight providers were still without a clinical data repository (CDR) system—the most basic building block of an electronic health record (EHR) system and an essential step toward fully computerizing patient data. Furthermore, a large fraction of providers that now have a CDR system have not yet implemented other useful systems, such as computerized practitioner order entry or a clinical decision support system. Abelson and Creswell [1] reported that only 44 percent of all institutions have adopted basic electronic systems necessary for properly coordinating patient care.

It is, therefore, natural to ask why this sector has been so slow in adopting technologies, even those that have been around for decades [2]. There are several reasons for this apathy, a nice summary of which can be found in a report by the Congressional Budget Office [49]. First, a large portion of the savings from a new technology often spills over to patients and insurance companies, leaving little incentive for providers to invest in HIT. For example, less duplication in diagnostic tests stemming from HIT implementation would result in savings for the patient and the patient’s insurance company but, in fact, lower revenues for the provider. Likewise, HIT can improve the quality of healthcare, “but it is relatively rare for providers to be compensated for such improvements” [49, p. 20]. Worse yet, HIT may add to portability of health records, making it easier for patients to switch to a competitor. Moreover, because nonadoption has become the norm, there is little competitive pressure on hospitals to adopt a technology. Lack of standardization also adds to the uncertainty about the benefits, because many benefits often accrue from interoperability with other providers’ systems. Finally, concerns about implementation costs, patient privacy, loss of control from the physician’s perspective, and other unintended consequences further amplify this uncertainty (e.g., [5, 12, 19, 35]). Can endowing potential adopters with appropriate knowledge reduce the uncertainty, and, if so, how can its transfer be facilitated? Can the emergence of institutional norms or standards help? And what role, if any, do these factors play in different stages of adoption?

As set forth above, many of the impediments to HIT adoption and many of the ills contributing to the rapid increase in healthcare costs are so complex that they cannot be solved overnight. At the same time, they are so intricately intertwined that a problem patched at one place may magnify the effect of another or may create a completely new hurdle. Waiting for these issues to be resolved to one’s satisfaction, or for the adoption uncertainties to fully disappear, does not make much practical sense. Perhaps it is this dilemma that has gotten the healthcare sector into such a fix today. Solving these long-term structural issues would require much research in organizational, political, and economic domains and is outside the scope of a single paper. Our objective, therefore, is to look beyond such issues and identify factors that can accelerate HIT adoption within the current structure.

In fact, academic researchers have already taken up this important question of technology diffusion, albeit in more general settings, tackling it primarily from two different angles. On one hand, some researchers have employed knowledge transfer theory to study the underlying diffusion mechanism [42, 54, 60, 71]. This theory focuses on how information relating to a new technology flows from current adopters to potential ones, influencing their decisions to adopt. On the other hand, more recently, researchers have also started employing the social network approach, which recognizes that current and potential adopters are embedded in a web of relations, and the network connectivity determines how the actions of one actor in the network influence others [2, 29, 67, 69]. However, there is a notable disconnect —the knowledge transfer literature does not consider the role of networks, and the social network literature overlooks the issue of knowledge transfer. Thus, neither explicitly models nor investigates knowledge transfer in a network setting. This is the gap we seek to fill. We hasten to add that it is necessary to fill this gap. Healthcare providers usually belong to larger delivery networks. Because providers within the same integrated healthcare delivery system (IHDS) establish formal and informal communication channels, such as meetings, forums, and councils, to exchange information [43], the issue of knowledge transfer in a network setting is particularly relevant in the context of HIT adoption. Our research, in essence, offers a unified view and presents a comprehensive approach for understanding diffusion of technology in socioeconomic networks.

We study the diffusion of HIT empirically, using a large data set obtained from the HIMSS database. In particular, we examine the adoption of CDR, a technology that has been diffusing gradually over the past decade and has now reached a large majority of all kinds of providers. Its gradual diffusion over a decade-long period, along with its uneven adoption rate across different provider networks, affords us a natural setting and a rich longitudinal data set, which are necessary for a thorough empirical investigation. Indeed, our sample includes 38,506 observations for 5,171 hospitals across thirteen years (1998–2010). Drawing from and suitably extending the relevant theories, we hypothesize and measure the impacts of knowledge transfer in a socioeconomic network and compare them with a well-studied mechanism known in the social network literature as the contagion effect [2].

Technology adoption has ties to knowledge transfer [54, 71]—a process through which an adopter (knowledge sender) transmits pertinent information relating to risks and benefits of a new technology to a potential adopter (knowledge receiver). The existing literature on knowledge transfer argues that knowledge receivers typically differ in their absorptive capacity, which is the capability of effectively locating, acquiring, and using external information and knowledge that enables analysis, processing, interpretation, and understanding of stocks and flows of knowledge [13, 27]. Organizations with higher absorptive capacity are able to quickly identify and recognize the value of external knowledge and information and are, therefore, more likely to easily absorb and assimilate such knowledge and information; as a result, they are also likely to adopt a new technology sooner. We do consider the role of this absorptive capacity in HIT adoption, but also go beyond it and incorporate the concept of a disseminative capacity—a construct previously used only at a dyad level [45, 46]—in extending the existing theory on knowledge transfer to a network setting. This extension of the dyadic concept to the context of an interorganizational network simply recognizes that a “student” organization (knowledge receiver) may be exposed to the teachings of not just one “teacher” organization (knowledge sender) but a variety of them. Situated in such an environment, the knowledge acquired by the student ought to depend on the collective disseminative capacity of all its teachers. This way, our work enables one to study the adoption decisions of healthcare providers within the web of relations in a provider network.

Our study reveals several important insights. First, we posit and find significant empirical support that absorptive and disseminative capacities both play a major role in HIT adoption. Therefore, knowledge transfer in provider networks is critical in inducing HIT adoption. Second, our empirical analysis lends evidence that these two capacities—absorptive and disseminative—actually substitute for each other in affecting HIT adoption and that the disseminative capacity plays a more significant role for providers that have a lower absorptive capacity. Third, we show that knowledge dissemination is most effective in influencing adoption when it happens from senders to receivers that are quite similar in their business scope. Last, and perhaps most important, we demonstrate that the roles played by absorptive and disseminative capacities constitute a mechanism that is starkly different from the contagion effect. We empirically confirm that the impacts of these two capacities decrease over time, whereas that of the contagion effect actually increases. The important lesson here is that knowledge transfer plays a pivotal role in earlier years but gradually pales in significance in later stages when the contagion effect becomes more and more dominant. All these insights have important implications for healthcare organizations and provider networks, as well as for governments and public policy institutions.

## Theoretical Background

Technology and innovation are important vehicles that enable transformation of business processes, enhance firm productivity, and facilitate collaboration and transactions across organizational boundaries [42, 56]. Quick adoption and assimilation of technology and innovation are important for firms to gain competitive advantages and achieve business success.

Two theoretical perspectives have received increasing attention: the social network approach and the knowledge transfer theory. In the social network literature, actors are considered to be embedded in their social contexts and relations, which could either facilitate or derail economic exchanges and decision making [8, 20]. In this approach, what really matters is the position of the ego within the network, which is usually captured through metrics such as network centrality and closure [51]. In particular, scholars have investigated how network centrality—the number of connected actors within a social network—affects technology adoption in the healthcare industry. They have discovered that hospitals become more likely to adopt total quality management with increased number of previous adoptions [67, 69] and that hospitals in alliances with physicians are more likely to adopt new imaging technologies [29].

Although the social network approach to technology adoption has become popular lately, the influence of knowledge transfer on diffusion of innovation has a longer tradition [4]. Knowledge transfer has been defined as the process through which the knowledge state of one actor is affected by that of another. Although knowledge transfer can take on various forms, such as personnel movement, training, association, and alliance, the end result, in essence, is that knowledge is passed on from the source to the destination [60]. More importantly, it has been observed that technology adoption is an information-intensive endeavor, which is essentially a process of knowledge transfer from current to potential adopters [42, 54, 71]. Several other studies have expanded and enriched this learning perspective to technology adoption by focusing on the ability of an organization to overcome hurdles in order to adopt and assimilate technology successfully [48]. A key concept here is that of absorptive capacity, or an actor’s ability to identify, absorb, assimilate, and exploit external information and knowledge to gain competitive advantage [13, 27, 70]. Furthermore, realizing that organizational efforts in deploying and managing technology can yield strikingly different results, Boynton et al. [9] have argued that the absorptive capacity of an organization is truly the underlying force beneath successful adoption and deployment of new technologies.

However, a closer examination of the literature reveals a clear gap between these two theories of technology adoption. On one hand, the social network approach suggests that information channeled through the network can facilitate technology adoption, but it does not explicitly model the process of knowledge transfer from current adopters to potential ones. On the other hand, although the knowledge transfer approach admits that innovation diffusion is a process of mutual understanding and information exchange between current and potential adopters [54], it typically focuses on dyadic relationships, neglecting the web of network relations between these two groups of actors. By incorporating and generalizing the concept of disseminative capacity, we are able to build a unified research framework that bridges this discernible gap.

How does our framework differ qualitatively from the theory of social contagion? And, how does it allow us to paint a richer picture of the research context and generate newer insights about the main drivers of HIT adoption? Social influence often manifests itself in the form of a contagion effect—the larger the number of adopters in the network, the higher the chance of adoption or use (e.g., [11, 17, 61, 66]). Implicit in this treatment is the assumption that everyone is equally susceptible to social contagion and every carrier is equally infectious. In knowledge transfer terminology, this is equivalent to stating that the learning of a “student” is related only to the number of “teachers,” whereby every student learns the same way and every teacher exerts a uniform influence on the learning of a student. Such a point of view may be too restrictive to capture the reality of a context [58], because it tends to ignore the ability of a student to learn and the ability of a teacher to impart the knowledge. Of course, incorporation of absorptive capacity from the knowledge transfer literature allows us to readily capture the heterogeneity among students in their ability to learn.

The heterogeneity among teachers, or knowledge senders, is somewhat more difficult, however. In modeling this heterogeneity, Angst et al. [2], for example, noted that different organizations may have varied levels of influence on potential adopters and captured it by considering the “celebrity” status, size, and age of hospitals. Even there, however, the influence generating from a specific hospital is exactly the same on all potential adopters; the fact that the extent of influence of a current adopter may vary from one potential adopter to another is lost in that conceptualization. Now, although knowledge transfer theory considers this heterogeneity across every student-teacher pair, it views this relationship only as a dyadic one [46], effectively precluding more than one teacher from contributing to a student’s learning. Our unified framework also recognizes this heterogeneity for every student-teacher pair, but, in contrast, admits that a student could be exposed to a variety of teachers with varied levels of teaching abilities, which, taken together, constitute a collective environment that is conducive to learning. This subtle, yet important, distinction is at the core of our study and allows us to better identify the important drivers of HIT adoption that have been largely overlooked in prior research.

Finally, a word is in order regarding proximity, both physical proximity and social proximity, which has been considered in prior literature [2]. Although we capture social proximity through the interorganizational network to which a provider belongs, we do not consider physical proximity in this study. Physical proximity primarily manifests itself in creating opportunities for interaction and information exchange. Because our providers within an IHDS already have ample opportunities to establish formal and informal channels to exchange information, the role of physical proximity is likely not that important.

## Absorptive Capacity

The concept of absorptive capacity was originally developed to study how organizations can effectively identify, absorb, assimilate, and exploit external information to gain competitive advantage [13]. As suggested in the knowledge-based view of competitive advantage, the survival and prosperity of social actors essentially lie in their capacity to acquire information and create knowledge. Absorptive capacity has been examined from various angles, such as an actor’s receptivity to technological changes, the ability to use external knowledge, and the capacity to learn and solve problems [16, 37, 70]. However, it is most often conceptualized as the actor’s existing knowledge stock [13, 52].

Adoption researchers have quickly come to realize that the concept of absorptive capacity offers a promising theoretical lens for research on technology adoption and deployment (e.g., [9]). For example, absorptive capacity enables firms to acquire, analyze, process, interpret, and understand stocks of knowledge [27], and employees’ stock of knowledge becomes critical to identifying, appreciating, and implementing new technologies [10]. Organizations with higher absorptive capacity are, therefore, more likely to absorb and assimilate information about a new technology, which leads them to faster adoption. Formally, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1: The higher its absorptive capacity, the earlier a hospital adopts HIT.

## Collective Disseminative Capacity

Knowledge transfer theory suggests that the transfer of knowledge from a “teacher” to a “student” depends not only on the student’s absorptive capacity but also on the ability of the teacher to impart knowledge to that student [36, 45]. This ability of the teacher, referred to as disseminative capacity, is essentially a function of the studentteacher relationship [46]. Prior research has shown that the higher the overlap between the basic knowledge stocks of the student and the teacher, the more effective is the learning [36]. In some sense, this is quite intuitive. For example, if a business manager understands technology and a technology professional understands business processes, a conversation between them about a new technology will likely result in more effective sharing of knowledge [32].

Of course, sharing of knowledge between organizations can be intentional as well as unintentional. An organization may intentionally disseminate knowledge to encourage recipients to contribute to the knowledge pool, which, in turn, can be exploited by the sender to further improve its capability and competitiveness [68]. Firms may also deliberately disseminate technologies in the hope of creating an industry norm or a dominant design [59]. At the same time, knowledge dissemination may also be unintentional. For example, a current adopter may influence potential ones, unintentionally, through association, shadowing or externships, or joint problem solving [16]. Likewise, employee migration can bring knowledge and expertise of product lines to the hiring firm [4]. In fact, new firms often recruit employees from established ones to overcome resource constraints, triggering unintentional knowledge transfer. The existence of social relationships among employees from two or more organizations can also lead to unintentional sharing of confidential knowledge across organizational boundaries.

Although the concept of disseminative capacity has been studied at the level of a teacher-student dyad, it is important to understand that organizations are continually interacting with one another within their socioeconomic networks. Through this process, knowledge held by one actor can get disseminated to several others along its network ties. We therefore define the collective disseminative capacity as the combined ability of all current adopters in the ego network to efficiently and effectively pass on information about the technology to the ego, intentionally or unintentionally. Viewed from this perspective, the collective disseminative capacity that a potential adopter is exposed to is the sum total of the dyadic disseminative capacities of all the adopters in its ego network. By introducing this collective disseminative capacity, we essentially assert that the process of knowledge transfer has to be considered within the network comprising both current and potential adopters, and that each potential adopter is subject to knowledge dissemination from all of its connected peers when contemplating adoption of a new technology. This way, we are able to go beyond the dyadic analysis typically used in prior literature and extend it by incorporating the connectivity within a network.

We argue that the collective disseminative capacity plays a positive role in facilitating technology adoption. As noted by Rogers [54], technology adoption is essentially a process of communication and knowledge transfer. If current adopters do not have sufficient ability to transfer relevant information about a technology to potential adopters, the speed with which the technology spreads will be greatly reduced. Therefore, if current adopters are highly effective in imparting knowledge about the technology or influencing a potential adopter to use the technology, they will be able to change the potential adopter’s perception of the new technology, ultimately inducing its adoption. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: The higher the collective disseminative capacity of its connected adopters, the earlier a hospital adopts HIT.

## Interplay Between Absorptive and Disseminative Capacities

When two factors can facilitate the same outcome, they may interact with each other, resulting in either a complementarity or a substitution effect. For example, firms often substitute resource acquisition for innovation as a way to gain competitive advantage [24], or apply a capacity-building strategy as a substitute for resource picking to generate economic returns [40]. Examples also abound wherein two factors complement each other—R&D investment and network connectivity have been found to complement each other in facilitating firm innovation and performance [63]. Quite often, the substitution effect reflects a loss in value due to redundant influences from multiple resources [57]. The complementarity effect, in contrast, arises when multiple resources are potential enablers of one another [44].

In our context, the relevant question, then, is how effective the collective disseminative capacity will be if an organization already possesses a high absorptive capacity. We argue that the two capacities are substitutes for each other in their marginal effects. In our student-teacher metaphor, a student’s learning is influenced by his own abilities as well as the teacher’s. If the student is endowed with a lot of abilities, the student’s dependence on the teacher is likely to be smaller. Similarly, when the teacher is highly effective, students of all ability levels learn well, making their individual abilities a little less important.

In the process of technology adoption, if a potential adopter can recognize the importance and benefits of a new technology, and if the potential adopter, by itself, has the ability to effectively understand and absorb the technology, then the potential adopter is more likely to actively explore and apply the new technology on its own, even when situated in an environment that is not so favorable (e.g., [54]). When few others have adopted the new technology, the potential adopter does not have many outside sources to consult or learn from, and it is not affected as much by the adoption decisions of its peers. Under this circumstance, the ability and discretion of the potential adopter possibly play a bigger role in its adoption decision. Thus, a potential adopter with a stronger absorptive capacity can adopt the new technology early, without much intervention and help from its peers. Viewed differently, the marginal effect of a potential adopter’s absorptive capacity on technology adoption is likely to increase as the collective disseminative capacity decreases.

When the absorptive capacity of a potential adopter is not sufficiently high to fully recognize the benefits of a new technology, however, a strong collective disseminative capacity is likely to be more effective in communicating the benefits and converting the potential adopter to an adopter. Put another way, the marginal effect of the collective disseminative capacity on technology adoption increases as the potential adopter’s absorptive capacity decreases. This implies that, although both capacities can positively induce adoption, an increased exposure to both can potentially create more redundancies, thereby reducing their individual marginal effects. Hence, we posit:

Hypothesis 3: The absorptive and collective disseminative capacities substitute for each other in influencing HIT adoption.

## Impact of Business Scope Similarity

Existing research suggests that knowledge transfer is most effective when the knowledge sender and receiver share similar organizational structures and face similar issues and experiences [36]. These similarities, in essence, make it easier to develop a common language and a shared set of norms and mental models, which can significantly facilitate knowledge transfer [47, 48]. Moreover, social actors absorb cues and information to guide their behavior by looking at those whom they perceive to be similar. Such a situation arises when actors have imperfect information about the true value of a technology, so they infer about its utility from actions of others who need to address comparable problems. Prior studies have, in fact, confirmed that perceived relevance enhances outcome-based learning and that actions in similar settings receive closer attention [38]. Therefore, when an organization considers whether to adopt a technology, the decisions of other organizations that share a similar business scope become more salient, visible, and influential [41]. The higher the similarity in organizations’ business scope, the greater should be the overlap between their problem sets, and the more effective the impact of the collective disseminative capacity. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 4: The impact of the collective disseminative capacity on HIT adoption increases with the similarity between the business scope of a hospital and its connected adopters.

## Contagion Effect

Related to the process of technology diffusion is the widely documented contagion effect, which suggests that one’s adoption of a technology is primarily influenced by how many others have adopted the same technology. For example, individuals are more likely to adopt home computers in areas where greater numbers of other individuals have already done so [18]. Implicit in the contagion effect is the assumption that, at any time, all individuals in the network who are yet to adopt are uniformly exposed to the same amount of influence from current adopters [17].

As discussed earlier, absorptive and disseminative capacities are built upon the theory of knowledge transfer—certain actors are more likely to adopt technologies, because they have accumulated sufficient related knowledge [13] or because their peers possess the capacity to impart related knowledge [36]. The contagion effect, in contrast, occurs mostly because actors in a socioeconomic system resemble or mimic others for decision making in response to institutional isomorphism or normative pressures [15]; that is, actors imitate others to conform to shared norms or appropriate behaviors. When the contagion takes hold, things are done in a certain way simply because it has increasingly become the way of doing things [58, 66, 72].

Typically, technology adoption takes several stages, and often potential adopters at various stages possess different characteristics [54]. Early adopters tend to possess high levels of absorptive capacity, and they are more responsive to external stimulants such as new information and knowledge [39]. Studies have also revealed that early adopters are more rational—they are usually driven by a desire to improve performance out of rational considerations [15], and their acquisition of new technologies is contingent on their current technological capabilities [52]. In contrast, late adopters are often engaged in symbolic actions—they are merely seeking social conformity and are driven by considerations of legitimacy rather than performance improvement [39].

In early stages when a technology is first introduced, because social norms and technology standards have not been established, the absorptive capacity of a focal actor and the collective disseminative capacity of its peers should play a more critical role. Therefore, we propose that absorptive and disseminative capacities will be more effective in the early stages of adoption. The impact of the contagion effect, however, is expected to play out differently over time. As the technology gradually gains legitimacy, potential adopters should feel increased levels of social and cultural pressure to conform to the established practices [67]. Taking these factors together, we postulate:

Hypothesis 5: The impacts of the absorptive and collective disseminative capacities on HIT adoption decrease over time.

Hypothesis 6: The impact of the contagion effect on HIT adoption increases over time.

## Data Set and Variables

In general, HIT refers to a diverse set of technologies used for delivering healthcare and managing information used by patients, providers, and insurers/payers. Broadly speaking, HIT falls into two main categories: (1) HIT for diagnosis/treatment, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and echocardiology, and (2) transactional HIT, such as computerized practitioner order entry and EHRs. The first category improves the quality of medical services, and the second primarily improves the coordination and sharing of medical information across parties involved in the healthcare service chain. The foundation of EHR programs is the CDR system, a real-time transaction processing database that merges data from a variety of clinical sources to present a consolidated view of every patient’s clinical history. In this study, we examine the adoption of CDR by hospitals in the United States.

## Data Set

To investigate our research questions, we used the HIMSS database. The HIMSS database provides extensive information on HIT adoption by healthcare providers across the nation. This database is available from the HIMSS Foundation.<sup>1</sup> Our data set on CDR adoption covers a thirteen-year time period, from 1998 to 2010. Many types of healthcare providers are included in the data set, such as ambulatory, home health, hospital, and subacute providers. We restrict our analysis to hospitals (also called acute healthcare providers) only. Our final sample includes 38,506 observations for 5,171 hospitals.

It is possible that several hospitals in our final sample belong to an IHDS. At the same time, however, some of these delivery systems may have just one hospital. It is also possible that a hospital is affiliated with multiple delivery systems. Hospitals within the same IHDS establish both formal and informal communication channels to exchange information [43], so they are connected to one another within a socioeconomic network. In the HIMSS database, each hospital is assigned a unique identifier, which we use to match data records and construct the data panel. Our sample includes, as potential adopters, those hospitals that had not adopted a CDR system by 1998. We then tracked their adoption decisions until 2010. Once a hospital adopts, it is no longer considered a potential adopter for later years in the sample. This systematic exclusion results in an unbalanced panel with fewer records for hospitals that adopted sooner.

## Variables

The dependent variable in this study is a binary variable indicating whether or not a hospital adopts CDR in the observation year. There are eight different CDR adoption statuses in the data set. Among them, we regard the following four as adoption: (1) live and operational, (2) installation in process, (3) contracted/not yet installed, and (4) to be replaced. The remaining four—not automated, not reported, not yet contracted, and service not provided—are considered nonadoption. There are two key independent variables in this study, the absorptive capacity (ACAP) and the collective disseminative capacity (DCAP). We now discuss how these two capacities, as well as other independent variables in the model, are operationalized.

## Absorptive Capacity

As discussed earlier, ACAP represents the ability to absorb and assimilate external information, and it is most often conceptualized as organizational learning capability [37]. Learning is best characterized as cumulative and path dependent, because assimilating new knowledge requires related prior knowledge. Cohen and Levinthal [13, p. 132] asserted that “if external information is closely related to ongoing activity, then external information is readily assimilated.” That technology adoption is affected by the degree to which the new technology is related to the preexisting stock of knowledge is also evident from the fact that personal computers diffused more rapidly among firms that had prior experience with mainframes or minicomputers [13]. In this study, we use the current stock of HIT as a proxy for ACAP, because it is indeed consistent with these ideas. Specifically, to represent the stock of HIT, we count all the HIT applications that have already been adopted by a focal hospital currently without a CDR.<sup>2</sup>

Use of the HIT stock as an indicator of absorptive capacity is not commonplace and demands further explanation. First, as the stock of HIT applications within a hospital grows, so do the depth and breadth of expertise required to evaluate, acquire, deploy, operate, and maintain them [7]. Naturally, the ability to assimilate information about a new, related technology ought to grow as well [22]. Second, as experience with HIT grows over time, a hospital should develop processes, policies, and procedures to “identify and value external knowledge” as well as to share that knowledge internally; this way, it also “becomes skilled at using” related knowledge [37, p. 839]. These must then augment its absorptive capacity. Third, IT enables and transforms business processes, and it touches not just IT professionals but non-IT employees as well. As more HIT applications are introduced in a hospital, these employees can witness them in action, which increases their comfort level with HIT, adds to their overall IT competence, and also provides them with the ability to see its value within their current organizational context [7]. In short, they become attuned to the concept of more HIT and are “better equipped to identify opportunities offered by new technology” [10, p. 95].

Finally, the HIT inventory as a proxy for ACAP is also consistent with traditional measures in prior literature. For example, the R&D intensity, often expressed as the average annual R&D budget, has been used quite often in prior literature (e.g., [13, 36]). Within our context, a hospital’s IT budget could serve that purpose well. Interestingly, though, the annual IT budget is often closely related to the HIT stock, because the primary component of that budget is the personnel cost for operating and maintaining the existing systems. Then, why do we not use the IT budget in the first place? This, unfortunately, is the plight of a researcher working with secondary data. Although our data set includes the annual IT budget, a large fraction of the records there have missing values, making an analysis with that proxy practically meaningless. Fortunately, it is still possible to derive some comfort from the fact that the correlation coefficient between the annual IT budget and HIT stock is 0.34 and very highly significant $( p \approx 0 )$ .

## Collective Disseminative Capacity

In our model, DCAP represents the collective ability of knowledge senders to effectively impart knowledge to a receiver. We begin by defining the disseminative capacity at a dyad level, which should depend on synergies between the sender and the receiver [36]. Let $\mu _ { i j }$ be the dyadic disseminative capacity of sender j for receiver i. Of course, i and j must be connected for knowledge transfer to actually take place between them; in our context, this means that the two hospitals must be in the same IHDS to share relevant knowledge. We capture this by defining the following variable:

$$
I _ {i j} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1, & \text { if   } i \text {   and   } j \text {   are   in   the   same   IHDS   and   } j \text {   has   adopted   CDR }, \\ 0, & \text { otherwise }. \end{array} \right.
$$

Then, assuming that the influences from the current adopters are independent, a focal hospital i is exposed to a collective disseminative capacity of

$$
\mathrm{DCAP} _ {i} = \sum_ {j} \mu_ {i j} I _ {i j}.
$$

It is in this collective nature of the disseminative capacity within a network setting that we augment prior literature on knowledge transfer.

We now operationalize the dyadic construct $\mu _ { i j }$ . Prior research suggests that the “student” has the greatest potential to learn from a “teacher” who has a similar stock of basic knowledge [32, 36]. In fact, hospitals with a large number of common HIT applications accumulate similar sets of knowledge, procedures, and rules, which should allow them to effectively share with one another information about the new technology. Therefore, if an adopter hospital has more HIT applications in common with the focal hospital, the former would potentially exert a much stronger influence on the latter to adopt. Using this logic, we code $\mu _ { i j }$ as the number of HIT applications that are common between hospitals i and j. This characterization is also consistent with our earlier observation that knowledge transfer can be intentional or unintentional. For example, a current adopter with a similar stock of HIT might already have a level of business synergy with a potential adopter in terms of patient information sharing and collaborative workflows, which can be further enhanced if the potential adopter also adopts the new technology—clearly a case for intentional knowledge sharing. Likewise, unintentional transfer can happen when an employee migrates from a current adopter to a potential one; the knowledge the employee carries would tend to be more relevant to a potential adopter with a comparable stock of HIT.

## Similarity

Hospitals are of various types, with each type representing a different business scope. The variable similarity, denoted $\phi _ { i } ,$ measures on average how similar the CDR-adopting peer hospitals are in their business scope when compared with the focal hospital i:

$$
\phi_ {i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c l} \frac {\sum_ {j} \psi_ {i j} I _ {i j}}{N _ {i}}, & \text { if } N _ {i} > 0, \\ 0, & \text { otherwise }, \end{array} \right.
$$

where $N _ { i } = \sum _ { j } I _ { i j }$ is the total number of CDR-adopting hospitals that have ties to hospital i, and

$$
\psi_ {i j} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1, & \text { if   } i \text {   and   } j \text {   are   of   the   same   type }, \\ 0, & \text { otherwise }. \end{array} \right.
$$

## Base

Technology adoption is often influenced by social contagion attributable to institutional isomorphism or normative pressure—as well as network effects— whereby the current “popularity” as measured by the size of the installed base positively impacts the adoption decision [17, 30, 61]. To incorporate the contagion effect, we use the variable base, which is the total number of CDRadopting hospitals connected to the focal hospital. Thus, the base for a focal hospital i is simply $N _ { i } ,$ defined above.

## Other Regressors

The variable time is the calendar year of an observation minus 1998. Consistent with prior literature, we also control for hospital size and age in this study (e.g., [2, 50, 67]). The size of a hospital is measured by the number of beds in the hospital, and its age is measured as the time span (in years) since its inception to the observation year. In addition, we control for the type of hospital, because prior literature suggests that HIT adoption rates differ substantially across various types of hospitals (e.g., [62]).<sup>4</sup> A summary of all variables is provided in Table 1.

Table 2 provides descriptive statistics and the correlation matrix for our data set. This table is consistent with our basic understanding of the context, although the correlation coefficients require cautious interpretation. For example, from a distance, the negative correlation between base and time may look misleading—after all, the installed base is cumulative over time. An astute reader would, however, recognize that this happens because our data set tracks a hospital only until it adopts CDR. After adoption, it is no longer a potential adopter, and its growing base no longer a consideration. The negative correlation, therefore, implies that a late adopter is actually connected with fewer other adopters.

## Estimation Model and Results

We use a logit model to express the probability of CDR adoption by a hospital. The motivation for this model choice is as follows: The data set used in this study is best described as time-to-event, with the adoption of CDR representing the event of interest. To study this event, we need to use duration analysis, which could be either continuous or discrete [21]. Here, we must employ a discrete model because the observations are one year apart. When a discrete duration model is used with panel data, it becomes equivalent to a logit model; a more detailed discussion is available in [26].

To explicitly account for the simultaneity issue in examining network influence,<sup>5</sup> we take advantage of our longitudinal data set and employ the independent variables in one time period to predict the adoption of CDR in the following one [51, 64]. By doing so, we explicitly specify that the adoption decision of hospital i at time t is determined by the state of the network at time t 1 , and not vice versa. In other words, our regression model can be symbolically expressed as

$$
Y _ {i, t} ^ {*} = \mathbf {X} _ {i, t - 1} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \epsilon_ {i, t}; Y _ {i, t} = 1 [ Y _ {i, t} ^ {*} > 0 ],\tag{1}
$$

where Y represents the binary adoption decision, X the regressor vector, - the i.i.d. normal error, and $Y ^ { * }$ simply the latent variable. In essence, the resulting model is a spatial discrete choice lag model, in which adoptions in previous years influence current and future adoptions; such models are particularly suitable for studying technology adoption “across different individual establishment locations in different geographic areas” [31, p. 128].

To test our hypotheses, we apply an incremental regression strategy using Equation (1). The results are presented in Table 3. Model 1 represents the baseline and lists estimation results without the absorptive and disseminative capacities. This model controls for time, as well as for size and age of hospitals. The coefficient of time is estimated to be both positive and significant, implying that, with time, the hospitals in our sample were gradually becoming more open to using CDR. Absent this control variable, an analysis of the influence of the adopters would become systematically biased. The coefficients of size and age are also positive and significant. This is expected, as we anticipate larger and more mature hospitals to adopt a new technology sooner [2, 50]. The positive and significant coefficient of base is also expected [2], and it does reveal a strong contagion effect—as more hospitals in an IHDS adopt CDR, they tend to induce other hospitals in their network to adopt as well. Thus, the results from Model 1 are consistent with our basic understanding of the research context and, hence, are also reflective of the integrity of the data set and its appropriateness for the purpose of this work.

In Model 2, the two main regressors—absorptive capacity (ACAP) and collective disseminative capacity (DCAP)—are introduced. Their coefficients are found to be positive and significant, implying that Hypotheses 1 and 2 are both supported. As a robustness check, we note that, even in this extended model, the coefficients of the control variables all have their expected signs, and the contagion effect is significantly positive. Having established the importance of DCAP, we now test Hypothesis 3, which looks at the substitution effect between ACAP and DCAP. This effect is captured in Model 3 by the interaction term ACAP×DCAP. The coefficient of this interaction term is negative and significant, thereby providing support for Hypothesis 3, that ACAP and DCAP are, in fact, substitutes for each other. Of course, this does not imply that they offset each other; it only suggests that the marginal effect of one gets weaker as the other increases. Indeed, as evident from Model 2, the marginal effects of both are positive, implying that simultaneous increases in their values collectively increase the odds of a CDR adoption.

Model 4 tests the moderating effect of the business scope similarity on DCAP, and the positive and significant coefficient for similarity×DCAP supports Hypothesis 4. Thus, the role of the collective disseminative capacity is more pronounced when the current adopters are similar to the potential ones in their business scope. Model 5 compares the impact of the contagion effect (base) with those of ACAP and DCAP. In particular, it is aimed at testing Hypotheses 5 and 6. The negative and significant coefficients of ACAP×time and DCAP×time lend support to Hypothesis 5, that the impact of knowledge transfer attenuates over time, whereas the positive and significant coefficient for base×time supports Hypothesis 6, that the impact of the contagion effect accelerates. These results seem to suggest that knowledge transfer is critical in early stages of adoption, whereas the contagion effect becomes more dominant during the later stages.

Finally, Model 6 is a “kitchen sink” that includes all explanatory variables. Interestingly, it still yields very similar results as far as the signs and significance of all the important coefficients are concerned—the impact of DCAP remains significant, as do those of ACAP and base, and knowledge transfer continues to be more important in initial years, although contagion becomes increasingly dominant in later years of diffusion.

A word about a possible endogeneity issue is in order now. If a hospital acquires other HIT applications in anticipation of CDR adoption in near future, ACAP would become endogenous. To rigorously rule out this possibility, we need to find an appropriate instrument variable. It turns out that the number of full-time employees in a hospital can indeed be used as such an instrument for ACAP; the correlation coefficient between the two is 0.386 and significant. At the same time, the number of full-time employees is unlikely to be contingent on CDR adoption and thus should be orthogonal to the error term. The Durbin-Wu-Hausman test on Model 2 with this instrument indicates that ACAP is not endogenous (p 0:618).

## Discussion

Rogers [54, p. xxi] noted that “the challenge for diffusion scholars of the future is to move beyond the proven methods and models of the past, to recognize their shortcomings and limitations, and to broaden their conceptions of the diffusion of innovations.” To that end, we propose a new research framework, and our empirical results from the context of CDR adoption strongly support this framework. Our study makes several theoretical contributions.

First, we synthesize theories on social networks and knowledge transfer to develop a more unified view of technology adoption. The knowledge transfer approach postulates that the adoption decision depends largely on the ability of a potential adopter to absorb relevant information about risks and benefits of a technology. This approach, however, ignores the web of relations between current and potential adopters and the role they may play in enabling knowledge transfer. The social network approach, on the other hand, admits that social behavior and decision making are embedded in a network of relations, but it ignores the actual process of knowledge transfer. Combining these two theories, therefore, affords us the ability to paint a richer picture of the diffusion process.

Second, to combine these two theories, we generalize the concept of disseminative capacity in a network setting. In doing so, we explicitly recognize that knowledge transfer

—often viewed as a two-way process between a sender and a receiver—is potentially intertwined, in a network setting, with the network itself. That this marriage of theories is necessary is also evident from our empirical findings. Even when we control for both— knowledge transfer through the absorptive capacity (ACAP) and social influence through the contagion effect (base)—we find that the impact of the collective disseminative capacity (DCAP) is still positive and significant at the 1 percent level across all models in Table 3.<sup>6</sup>

Third, we identify that these two mechanisms—knowledge transfer and social contagion—manifest themselves quite differently in a network setting, even though they both represent external influences on technology adoption. Absorptive and disseminative capacities represent knowledge transfer and learning through which a potential adopter accumulates relevant information about a new technology. This mechanism recognizes that potential adopters are heterogeneous, as they possess different abilities to learn and are exposed to different levels of influences [51]. The contagion effect, in contrast, is primarily attributable to institutional isomorphism or normative pressure and treats all potential adopters uniformly [17]. At initial stages of technology diffusion, when adoption is sparse, potential adopters are quite heterogeneous and the normative pressure is not strong. As a result, the contagion effect is not significant, but the impact of knowledge transfer is. With time, however, as more hospitals start adopting, gradually the institutional norms are strengthened, and the contagion begins taking hold. At the same time, as the adoption level increases, potential adopters become more homogeneous and the impact of knowledge transfer attenuates. This is indeed borne out by our empirical findings. To illustrate, we consider the marginal impact of ACAP, DCAP, and base on adoption. From Model 5 in Table 3, for example, we get

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\partial Y ^ {*}}{\partial (A C A P)} = 3 0. 0 0 2 - 2. 5 7 9 t, \frac {\partial Y ^ {*}}{\partial (D C A P)} = 1. 3 3 3 - 0. 1 5 0 t, \text { and } \\ \frac {\partial Y ^ {*}}{\partial (b a s e)} = - 2. 3 7 2 + 2. 6 1 2 t. \end{array}
$$

Clearly, the impacts of ACAP and DCAP decrease over time, t, but that of base increases.

To visualize the temporal trend of these marginal impacts and their overall significance, we first calculate the standard error for each marginal impact as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname{SE} \left(\frac {\partial Y ^ {*}}{\partial x}\right) = \sqrt {\operatorname{Var} \left(\hat {\beta} _ {x}\right) + t ^ {2} \operatorname{Var} \left(\hat {\beta} _ {x \times \text { time }}\right) + 2 t \operatorname{Cov} \left(\hat {\beta} _ {x} , \hat {\beta} _ {x \times \text { time }}\right)}, \\ \text { for } x = A C A P, D C A P, b a s e, \end{array}
$$

where $\hat { \beta } _ { z }$ represents the coefficient-estimate for regressor z. Figure 2 plots these marginal impacts (solid line) over time, along with their respective 99 percent confidence intervals (dashed line). As can be clearly seen from this figure, the marginal impacts of ACAP and DCAP indeed decrease over time, with the impact of DCAP becoming statistically insignificant beyond the first five years of the horizon of our study. In contrast, the impact of base is insignificant during the initial years, and it becomes significant only after three years. This way, Figure 2 demonstrates that, even though the two mechanisms favorably impact technology adoption, they exhibit very different dynamics. Ignoring one or the other would, therefore, leave the picture incomplete.

Fourth, we posit and empirically confirm that absorptive and disseminative capacities are substitutes for each other in facilitating HIT adoption. The absorptive capacity is particularly important for actors with limited resources, but as actors become increasingly exposed to influences from peers, its importance diminishes. In addition, we identify similarity as an important moderator within the framework —increased similarity in the business scope between the focal actor and its peers strengthens the effect of disseminative capacity on HIT adoption. The implication is that, when analyzing the effect of the collective disseminative capacity, one should not only consider the ability of current adopters to assimilate a new technology and communicate relevant knowledge but also take a contingency approach that includes possible moderating factors for a deeper and more complete understanding.

Finally, our research complements other theories of knowledge diffusion, such as the classical diffusion and institutional theories. Diffusion models, much like our framework, also emphasize interactions between social actors in a network, but the primary focus there is on the change agent “who influences clients’ innovation-decisions in a direction deemed desirable” [54, p. 27]. Although it is true that change agents are important triggers for a new diffusion process, their role is often quite limited once the process takes off [65]. This is exactly where our research fits in, as it delineates some of the mechanisms that can both sustain and accelerate the process after the initial takeoff. The institutional approach, too, identifies similar mechanisms, but it assumes the preexistence of established institutions such as schemas, norms, and routines [53] and places the focal actor under a macro-level context wherein it chooses to adopt a technology out of pressures to conform or look legitimate [69]. In comparison, we place the focal actor in a micro-level setup, in which knowledge flows to it from connected adopters, and the aggregate level of flow depends critically on the abilities of the connected adopters to impart relevant information. We then study the adoption decision in light of the resulting knowledge dissemination. We do acknowledge that such knowledge dissemination may itself lead to the emergence of new institutions, leading to even higher rates of adoption as the technology matures. In fact, our empirical finding that points to a strong contagion in later years reflects this very possibility; see Figure 2(c).

## Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a new research framework to study the important topic of HIT adoption. Using this framework, we empirically verify complex interactions between knowledge senders and receivers and the role they play in influencing adoption. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to offer a conceptual framework for explicitly analyzing knowledge exchange with external constituents in a network setting and to establish that technology adoption can indeed be studied through the lens of absorptive and disseminative capacities of actors in a socioeconomic network.

A major insight that emerges from this study is that flow of knowledge—from the connected peers who have adopted a technology to potential adopters—can play a significant role in fostering diffusion. There are practical implications of this finding, as it suggests that a possible way of alleviating the problem of slow diffusion of HIT would be to encourage formation of new networks such as an IHDS. In fact, in recent years, the emphasis on the role of such networks has increased considerably. In a recent thoughtprovoking article in the New England Journal of Medicine, Crosson [14] argued that any movement away from the fee-for-service system, which simply incentivizes more consumption but not necessarily a better clinical outcome, would require integration of physicians from different specialties as well as hospitals to form new accountable systems of care. Also, the success stories of the Mayo Clinic, the Geisinger Health System, and Kaiser Permanente have inspired some local governments to take initiatives to form and grow their own networks in order to better coordinate delivery of healthcare and improve accountability, both critical to bending the ever-rising cost curve. An example of such state-initiated networks is the AccessCare network in North Carolina (www.ncaccesscare. org), which has now grown to a statewide network of over 300 primary care practices with 1,000 providers caring for more than 227,000 Medicaid enrollees as of April 2011. Smaller states, like Wyoming, have also started joining the fray.

Our finding about the role of the collective disseminative capacity is a clear indication that, in addition to serving the purposes for which they were set up, such networks can also serve as important vehicles of knowledge dissemination, providing a much-needed boost to the rather slow process of HIT diffusion. It is also apparent that lack of HIT adoption among independent providers, who do not participate in any integrated care network, can be partly addressed by incentivizing them to join a large network with a significant disseminative capacity. Doing so would not only improve the quality of patient care and reduce costs but also promote the cause of HIT adoption, leading to even better quality of care and even lower costs.

Another important finding is that the process of knowledge transfer in socioeconomic networks is starkly different from the contagion effect, even though they are both governed by the same network connectivity. The contagion effect and the collective disseminative capacity both accelerate adoption. However, the major difference between the two is that social contagion becomes effective in later stages of adoption, whereas the process of knowledge dissemination is useful earlier. Furthermore, the dissemination process is more useful for providers with low absorptive capacities, that is, those who have little experience with technology and are, therefore, less likely to embrace a new technology on their own. According to our study, the key to helping these providers adopt a new technology turns out to be placing them in networks with adopters that have a similar structural mold in their business scope and similar stocks of HIT. Thus, in years following the inception of a new beneficial technology, policymakers should pay particular attention to encouraging providers to join networks that already have adopters with similar business scopes and overlapping stocks of HIT. This would likely alter the trajectory of adoption in earlier years, allowing the contagion effect to set in sooner for an even quicker adoption in later years.

In summary, our study augments the literature on knowledge transfer, as well as emerging social network theory, by forging a relationship between the two. We distinguish between current and potential adopters and explicitly model the flow of knowledge along network connections from the former to the latter. This is an essential first step toward extending the knowledge transfer theory to network settings in which actors are embedded in a multiplicity of connections. By taking this step, we have also paved the path for further research on the vexing issue of slow HIT adoption.

Now, although we believe that IHDS networks represent a major channel for hospitals to communicate with one another, we do concede that there are other channels through which hospitals may share information. For example, two hospitals located in close physical proximity may end up sharing information even when they are not in the same IHDS. Our model ignores this issue and must be suitably extended to better understand the role and effectiveness of these additional communication channels. In fact, such an extension would become imperative if we were to generalize and validate this model in sectors other than healthcare.

In this study, we used an approach in which the adoption decision of a hospital is modeled as a function of its characteristics, implying that the hospital-level heterogeneity may not be fully accounted for. One could address this by incorporating hospital-level fixed effects. However, the time-invariant nature of the fixed effects would be hard to ascertain for a data set spanning more than a decade. Besides, given their large number, the binary nature of the adoption decision, and the unbalanced nature of the data panels, there would be additional and significant statistical challenges in estimating a panel model [21, pp. 689–710]. We are currently looking for ways to overcome these challenges.
