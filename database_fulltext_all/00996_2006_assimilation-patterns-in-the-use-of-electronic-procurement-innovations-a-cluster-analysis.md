# Assimilation patterns in the use of electronic procurement innovations: A cluster analysis

Arun Rai <sup>a,\*</sup>, Xinlin Tang <sup>b,1</sup>, Paul Brown <sup>c,2</sup>, Mark Keil <sup>d,3</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Computer Information Systems, Center for Process Innovation, Robinson College of Business,

<sup>b</sup> Center for Process Innovation; Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303, USA <sup>c</sup> Clark Atlanta University, Atlanta, GA 30314, USA

<sup>d</sup> Department of Computer Information Systems, J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303, USA

Accepted 28 August 2005 Available online 24 October 2005

## Abstract

Electronic procurement innovations (EPI) have been adopted by many firms as a means of improving their procurement efficiency and effectiveness, but little research has been conducted to determine whether the assimilation of EPI really increases procurement productivity and which factors influence its assimilation. Drawing on data from 166 firms, we conducted an exploratory study to address these questions, using cluster analysis that revealed four different clusters or patterns of EPI assimilation: none, focused niche, asymmetric, and broad-based deployment. The level of EPI assimilation was closely related to procurement productivity. Greater levels of EPI assimilation were associated with higher levels of top management support and greater IT sophistication. Also, interesting patterns emerged between the various elements of EPI infrastructure capability, specifically flexibility and comprehensiveness of standards, EPI security, and the level of EPI assimilation. <sup>#</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronic procurement innovations (EPI); Procurement productivity; Cluster analysis

## 1. Introduction

As companies strive to provide more value to customer by improving site performance and reducing costs, they are also turning their attention to the procurement process [34]. This process, which serves as the interface between an organization and its suppliers, used to be viewed as having little strategic importance [39,44,56]. Many purchasing departments were viewed as merely ‘‘buying’’ or ‘‘shipping’’ units. In addition, their function was inefficient [47], e.g., nearly 95% of the non-production goods, which account for a third or more of a corporation’s expenditures, are still acquired using paper-based processes [18]. The lack of efficiency is so bad that many companies spend far more on managing the procurement cycle than on the goods actually purchased [6]; e.g., on average it costs \$ 107 to process a paper-based purchase order with an average cycle time of 7.3 days from order to fulfillment [10].

IT provides a means to improve the procurement process by providing a digital infrastructure for collaboration [51]. General Electric (GE) in the USA is a company which is realizing benefits by using the Internet. Its trading process network (TPN) is an online business community that allows it to transact over \$ 1 billion worth of business with more than 1400 suppliers around the world. TPN simplifies the old time-consuming contract bidding and award processes. Unlike industry giants, most companies are using off-the-shelf solutions to facilitate their procurement process. Electronic procurement tools are targeted at procurement related activities that enable organizations to integrate processes with suppliers and yield benefits for participants in the value chain [11,13].

Benefits associated with investment in electronic procurement tools are supposed to include increased information visibility and reduced search time. However, these possible benefits cannot guarantee performance gains: a link should be established between technology adoption for different facets of the procurement process, how these facets are facilitated by technology, and performance improvement.

Since there has been no prior research on the usage patterns of EPIs across buyer organizations, we decided to conduct a survey to discover distinct usage patterns and investigate their relationship to procurement productivity and its technical and managerial enablers. Towards this end, we addressed the following three questions:

(1) What distinct EPI usage patterns can be observed by examining different levels of EPI assimilation across key EPI innovations?

(2) How do different EPI usage patterns relate to procurement productivity?

(3) How do IT-enabled process innovation enablers, specifically top management support, IT sophistication, and EPI infrastructure, influence EPI assimilation?

## 2. Electronic procurement innovation

To improve the procurement process, many organizations have started to use electronic procurement innovations (EPIs); these, when acquired and deployed, change how an organization conducts procurement. Core procurement processes include supplier selection, order placement, order fulfillment, and payment and settlement [57]. Each of these processes is supported through EPIs that have been developed for reverse auctions, catalog management, order fulfillment, and payment and settlement [29]. Table 1 provides the definitions for each of these four EPIs.

Traditionally, if a buyer needed some specific product or service, he or she had to investigate, qualify, and negotiate with several potential suppliers before selecting one. To facilitate this process, the online reverse auction was introduced to enable temporal and geographical convenience, reduced cost of contact, instant feedback, and privacy. In electronic reverse auctions, a buyer offers a tender to invited suppliers who bid for the contract at the lowest price, usually in a short time span (hours or minutes). By putting these auctions online, buyers can streamline the process. This creates the potential for savings by stimulating increased competition. Such auctions have increased in popularity, because they emphasize short-term price savings and easy negotiation. They have been found to achieve as much as 5–40% savings [52], with average savings of 15–20% [16]. They also drastically reduce the average time from 6 weeks to a few hours.

The electronic catalog provides an electronic representation of information about products and/or services offered by an organization [48]. In contrast to paper-based catalogs, they can carry an almost unlimited volume of product related information. They are also much easier to maintain; therefore, they usually carry more accurate, real-time product information. Web-based catalogs simplify searches, thus providing easy location and comparison of supplier goods [9,58]. Its use also creates an interorganizational system that allows organizations to exchange information in an automated, electronic, form [14].

Electronic order fulfillment innovations refer to the automation of processes conducted after a sale. Such innovations provide web-based interaction to customer processes so that buyers, sellers, and logisticians can coordinate their activities. Key processes include picking and shipping of orders, analyzing solutions to ship quantities, and tracking the orders when shipped. Its assimilation enables fast and continuous communication within and between firms, which helps both to avoid lost orders and to find and correct errors. It can also be used to speed delivery by tracking the location of products and the status of orders [40].

Electronic payment and settlement systems involve the issuing of bills, payment and reconciliation of accounts, and logging of credits and invoices between partners. They also support transactions associated with product returns. Electronic funds transfers are cheaper, safer, and easier to make and track. An electronic payment can be made for less than 2 cents, compared to 43 cents by check. Further, they will prevent checkrelated crimes, including mail theft and forgery [21]. Electronic payments benefit payers by cutting payment processing costs. Payees, on the other hand, have an alternative means to get their payment, which can be deposited directly into their account. In addition, time and cost of reproduction, retrieval, and distribution can be significantly reduced.

Two aspects of EPIs make their investigation unique. First, they represent a related set of partially complementary innovations which have potential synergies when deployed together. Second, when they integrate processes distributed across firms and suppliers, their deployment is shaped by both.

## 3. Assimilation of electronic procurement innovations

Assimilation of EPI is the extent to which an organization has progressed from understanding the concept to deploying it in their procurement. Prospective users encounter significant challenges in learning about the technology and re-conceptualizing their work process activities to use it [7,45]. Moreover, successful use often requires mutual adaptation of the technology and the organizations in which the technology is being introduced [30], as IT assimilation also involves managerial factors.

## 4. Research approach

A cross-sectional survey was used to collect information on a broad range of companies. The survey and a cover letter stating the purpose of this research was sent to 1200 senior procurement professionals randomly drawn from the membership database of the Institute of Supply Management (ISM). In the letter, they were asked to complete the survey if they were the most informed respondent for EPI assimilation in their organization. Alternatively, if they were not, they were asked to re-direct it to the appropriate individual in their organization. Our respondents included presidents, vice presidents, and operations and purchasing managers. The sample covered Industrial Machinery and Equipment, Electronics Equipment, Wholesale Trade of Durables, and Business Services, which are represented by US SIC codes, 35, 36, 50, and 73.

The initial survey was mailed in May 2003 and a postcard reminder was sent to non-responders in the first week of June. One hundred and sixty-six responses were received, representing a response rate of about 14%.

## 4.1. Constructs and measures

The survey instrument included measures developed to assess: EPI assimilation, EPI usage, procurement productivity, top management support, IT sophistication, and EPI infrastructure. After initial development of the instrument, multiple steps were taken to ensure its quality and accuracy. First, three faculty members with experience in survey development were involved in examining the survey. Then three purchasing managers scrutinized it to further ensure accuracy of its wording. Finally, the instrument was pre-tested with 35 purchasing professionals across the United States using both online and paper-based formats. Several survey questions were changed based on feedbacks.

## 4.1.1. EPI assimilation and EPI usage

Our first research question focused on identifying distinct patterns in the four EPI assimilations. A Guttman scale was used to define seven stages of EPI assimilation; respondents were asked to indicate their assimilation stage for each of the EPI. This scale is similar to, and based on, the scale used by Fichman [20] to measure the assimilation of software process innovations. Table 2 lists the criteria for each stage and the survey items used.

In addition, usage of each EPI for catalog management, payment and settlement, and order fulfillment was measured in two ways:

 What percent of the specific procurement activity was conducted through EPI?

 With what percent of suppliers did the buyer interact through EPI?

Measuring EPI usage from these two aspects should provide a more complete picture than from a single one. In the case of reverse auctions, respondents were asked to specify the number of them that they had electronically conducted since 1 January 2002. During the pilot, the purchasing professionals suggested this was an appropriate measure of usage, due to its novelty in the marketplace.

## 4.1.2. Procurement performance

Our second question concerned the relationship between patterns of EPI assimilation and procurement performance. There is much literature on the impact of

IT on organizational performance. Commonly used measures have included intermediate process-level measures, such as inventory turnover and process productivity, and organizational level measures, such as profitability improvement and competitive advantage [27]. The importance of focusing investigations concerned with value created by information technology innovations on appropriate process-level ‘‘intermediate performance’’ variables was noted by Rai et al. [42] and Mukhopadhyay et al. [35]. Since our focus here was on the specific procurement process, we used procurement productivity as the measure of performance, defining it as the total dollar value of goods organizations procured divided by the total number of individuals employed in the procurement unit [19].

## 4.1.3. Enablers of EPI assimilation

Our third question focused on technical and managerial enablers and their relationships with patterns of EPI assimilation. Recent studies on IS success concluded that IS usage behavior must focus both on the IT innovation and the task context in which it is applied [41]. Therefore, we focused on three factors: top management support, IT sophistication, and the EPI infrastructure capability. Though the first two factors have been proposed as enablers of technology assimilation, including inter-organizational data exchange [15], they have never been examined in the context of EPI for procurement.

The role of top management support has been identified as a critical factor in successful IT implementations [23,28,50]. A multi-item measure was adapted from Chatterjee et al. [12] and refined to assess top management support, as shown in Appendix A.

IT sophistication describes the extent to which IT is important for a firm to achieve its core business objectives. Firms with high levels are less likely to be intimidated by technology, possess a superior corporate view of data in information management, and have access to advanced technological resources. Therefore, the possession of and experience with technological resources may directly impact the likelihood of EPI assimilation. IT sophistication was assessed by using a multi-item measure adopted from Pare and Raymond [37].

EPI infrastructure capability refers to the degree to which the adopting firm believes that a digital infrastructure is in place to promote the success of EPI. The enabling role of the IT infrastructure in core business process innovation was suggested in [32,55]. Based on these, we defined a capable EPI infrastructure as one that was able to support procurement processes comprehensively, flexibly, and securely. To cope with an ever-increasing rate of change in the business environment, EPI standards should be both comprehensive and flexible to handle inter-organizational integration needs and the increasing variety of customer demands without substantially increasing costs [25,54]. Since the procurement process also involved private information exchange, organizations should be more willing to use EPIs if they addressed key security concerns [22,38]. Accordingly, three constructs were used: EPI standards comprehensiveness, EPI standards flexibility, and EPI security. Their definitions are given in Table 3.

Multi-item measures were developed for each of these constructs (see Appendix A for the items).

## 4.2. Sample demographics

Although the survey response rate (14%) was not high, Babbie [8] suggested that ‘‘a demonstrated lack of response bias is far more important than a high response rate’’. Therefore, two tests were conducted for nonresponse bias. Armstrong and Overton [5] suggested that respondents should be classified into three groups and a series of one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests be used to determine whether later respondents were systematically different from earlier ones with respect to the number of employees in the buyer organization. Such a test assumes that later respondents serve as a proxy for non-respondents. In addition, a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test that measures the difference between the expected and observed number of respondents over two-digit SIC codes was conducted to detect whether there were significant differences across the sampled industry groups. Tables 4 and 5 present the results of these tests, which suggested that there was no significant non-response bias.

The industry representation of respondent companies, as shown in Table 6, indicated that they represented a wide variation in sales revenue. Over half of the responding companies spend less than \$ 50 million on their annual procurement, while half of them have less than 10 employees (including management, purchasing agents/buyers, lawyers, and support staff) in the procurement department.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Cluster analysis of EPI assimilation stages

Cluster analysis was employed to analyze the data. This statistical technique allowed us to identify groupings of firms where variations in the assimilation across the EPIs is minimal within the group but maximal across groups [3,31]. This process involved deriving distinct and meaningful clusters from the assimilation of the four EPIs.

To identify the clusters, we followed a two-stage procedure recommended by Ketchen and Shook [26], and Merchant [33]. First, we inspected the Euclidean distances across the clusters in the dendrogram produced by Ward’s hierarchical clustering method. The results of this procedure suggested a four-cluster solution. Second, in order to evaluate the robustness of this solution, we generated two-, three-, four-, and fivecluster solutions by using the K-means clustering algorithm. The assimilation means for each of the four EPIs differed significantly across all clusters in the fourcluster solution. For all other solutions, either the discriminatory power was relatively weaker or the clusters produced were less meaningful. To further ensure the stability of the solution, we replicated the two-stage clustering procedure with several subsamples randomly selected from the sample. The same four-cluster pattern surfaced each time providing support for the stability of this solution. We therefore concluded that the four-cluster solution best captured the patterns of assimilation of EPIs.

## 5.2. EPI usage associated with EPI assimilation patterns

The assimilation characteristics of the four clusters can be visualized in the form of a star chart (Fig. 1), which reveals separate and distinct EPI assimilation patterns in our data. Cluster 1, which we labeled ‘‘no deployment’’, was comprised mainly of companies that were aware of the existence of EPIs, had the intention to deploy one or more of them in the near future, but had not yet done so. Cluster 2, ‘‘focused niche deployment’’, consisted of companies that had only implemented an electronic payment and settlement systems. Companies in cluster 3, ‘‘asymmetric deployment’’, had already made initial trials on all EPIs other than online reverse auctions. Cluster 4, ‘‘broad-based deployment’’, was made up of companies that used all four EPIs in a considerable part of their procurement activities.

## 6. Discussion and implications

Our results indicated that firms seeking to enhance their procurement productivity could do so by focusing on EPI assimilation. Also, the broad-based deployment cluster had the highest average procurement productivity and was significantly higher than that for both the no deployment and the asymmetric deployment groups. This meant that firms realized benefits from two sources: (a) economies of scale when they put more transactions online, and (b) effective management of the complementarity between catalog management, order fulfillment, payment and settlement, and reverse auctions through the assimilation of EPI in each of them. Additionally, focused niche deployment of electronic payment and settlement systems could bring significantly higher increases in productivity than no deployment. Given the large volume of financial transactions that are associated with the purchasing process, this is an area where a focused application of EPI can yield significant returns.

Our results on productivity gains realized by the broad-scale deployment of a partially complementary set of EPIs was consistent with past research which concluded that: (a) maximum gains in systems quality performance accrued by collectively using a partially complementary set of TQM practices [43] and (b) maximum gains in IT project management performance were realized by collectively deploying a partially complementary set of risk management practices [53].

By developing commitment from their top management, firms can facilitate the assimilation of EPIs. Our results offered insights on the extent of top management support needed to transition across the distinct patterns of EPI assimilation. Asymmetric deployment of EPIs needs considerably higher top management support than for the no deployment group. To implement all the EPIs and use them intensively requires even more top management support. It is quite interesting that focused niche deployment of electronic payment and settlement systems only needs marginally more support from top management than no deployment. This pattern is consistent with findings from a recent meta-analysis study, which concluded that greater levels of top management support are required for IT innovations targeted at tasks with high interdependence [49].

Our results suggested that asymmetric and broadbased deployment of EPI needed significantly more IT knowledge than either focused niche deployment or no deployment. Also, security of the EPI infrastructure was found to be a key concern for companies. Surprisingly, firms with a focused niche deployment have a lower level of security than firms with no deployment.

Firms that plan to adopt a range of EPIs need to be concerned about the flexibility and comprehensiveness of their EPI standards. Our results indicated that asymmetric and broader deployment was associated with higher requirement for infrastructure flexibility and comprehensiveness than either the no deployment cluster or the focused niche deployment cluster.

Second, it reinforces the importance of top management support and IT knowledge, constructs. Finally, it identifies three specific properties of the EPI infrastructure that facilitate the deployment of EPIs.

## 7. Conclusion

The value of electronic procurement innovations has been touted in the business world, but little research has been performed to investigate the benefit of these innovations. In our study, we explored the relationship between EPI assimilation patterns and procurement performance. We found empirical evidence that EPI usage patterns that increased digital interaction with suppliers and increased digitalization of core procurement transactions lead to higher procurement productivity. Further exploration of the factors revealed that top management support, IT sophistication, and EPI infrastructure capability were, as might be expected, needed for EPI assimilation. Establishing a secure digital infrastructure may be a pre-condition to the assimilation of EPIs. Developing flexible and comprehensive standards for digital interaction with suppliers apparently promotes higher levels of deployment of catalog management, order management and fulfillment, and reverse auctions, while tight standards that adhere to institutional regulatory requirements facilitate the deployment of electronic payment and settlement.

He has also served as an Associate Editor for MIS Quarterly, and as co-editor of The DATA BASE for Advances in Information Systems.
