# Evaluating Team Collaboration Quality: The Development and Field Application of a Collaboration Maturity Model

IMED BOUGHZALA AND GERT-JAN DE VREEDE

IMED BOUGHZALA (corresponding author: Imed.boughzala@it-sudparis.eu) is a full professor in the Information Systems Department at the Telecom Ecole de Management of the Institut Mines-Telecom, France. He holds a Ph.D. in computer science from Paris VI Pierre & Marie Curie University. His present research interests focus on digital transformation of organizations. He is leading the SMART<sup>2</sup> (Smart Business IS) Research Team and is working with several major French and international companies. He is the author of numerous scientific and professional articles and books, and is a member of the editorial board of Information and Management. He is also columnist at Harvard Business Review France.

GERT-JAN DE VREEDE is a professor at the Information Systems and Decision Sciences Department of the University of South Florida. He received his Ph.D. from the Faculty of Technology, Policy and Management of Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands. His research focuses on crowdsourcing, collaboration engineering, convergence, and the facilitation of group meetings. His research has appeared in journals such as Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly Executive, Journal of the AIS, Communications of the ACM, and others. He was named the most productive group support systems researcher worldwide from 2000 to 2005 in a comprehensive research profiling study.

ABSTRACT: The quality of collaboration directly affects the quality of an organization’s outcomes and performance. Trends like globalization and increased product and service complexity have pushed organizations to become more and more reliant on collaboration in distributed, cross-disciplinary, cross-cultural, virtual teams. The present research is based on an applied science/engineering (AS/E) research paradigm to address an important class of unsolved problems—measuring the quality of collaboration within and across organizational boundaries. This paper presents a collaboration maturity model (Col-MM) to assess an organization’s team collaboration maturity as a first step toward a generalizable solution to that class of problems. The Col-MM is intended to be sufficiently generic to be applied to different organizational and team settings and usable by practitioners for conducting selfassessments. The Col-MM was developed during a series of focus group meetings with professionals (business unit managers). The model was then piloted and subsequently applied in a field study in an automotive company. This paper reports on the development and field application of the Col-MM. It contributes to the collaboration science literature, theory, and practice through a detailed AS/E study that develops a maturity model and a system for administering it that provides proof of value and effective use in the field.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: collaboration maturity, collaboration maturity model, collaboration quality, collaboration technology, online collaboration, organizational performance.

In today’s global and knowledge-based economy, organizations have to survive and thrive in a socioeconomic environment that is increasingly unstable and competitive. To meet this challenge, it is critical for organizations to achieve successful collaboration within and among organizational teams and with their external partners to ensure their organizational performance and competitive advantage. Indeed, collaboration is one of the most critical ingredients of organizational life: Organizations form because people have to work together to create value that they cannot produce by themselves. When collaborative efforts are successful, organizations can aspire to be productive and profitable. The quality of team collaboration directly affects the quality of an organization’s outcomes [1, 28]. A recent survey among 946 decision makers reported that collaboration had twice the impact of strategic orientation and five times the impact of market and technological turbulence on organizational performance. Yet many organizations and teams struggle to make collaboration work. Poor collaborative practices hinder performance and have a negative impact on the organizational profitability. Collaboration challenges often faced by teams and organizations include (but are not limited to) establishing sharing understanding of process, goals, and information [61], seeking goal alignment between individuals’ private goals and team goals [52], thinking creatively to define and solve problems [51, 52], overcoming production blocking, premature convergence, and groupthink [15, 18, 27], choosing effective and efficient methods and techniques for collaborative efforts [31], and selecting among a plethora of electronic and nonelectronic support technologies [42].

Therefore, it is important for modern organizations to reflect on the quality of the collaboration within and between their teams. This can help to identify measures to enhance team collaboration quality and therefore lead to improvements in organizational performance. Several researchers have proposed models and approaches to assess either collaboration processes [14, 48] or collaboration technology [23, 47]. One way to assess the overall collaboration quality in terms of its key dimensions (people, process, leadership/management, information, and technology [59]) would be through a maturity model approach. Maturity typically refers to the extent to which processes and systems are formally organized and executed or operated to produce outcomes. The assumption behind maturity models is that higher levels of maturity imply higher levels of quality of outcomes [17]. For example, maturity models have been used extensively in quality assurance for product development [19]. Using maturity models as an assessment instrument enables an organization to measure operations and practices at certain intervals and see trends evolve. Maturity models are not intended to provide rigorous metrics but to help identify best practices and trouble spots, and stimulate discussion among practitioners to initiate activities for continuous improvement [19, 49].

Even if collaboration is and remains one of the most important ingredients for organizational performance, its study from a maturity model perspective is still an open question. Some authors have proposed maturity models for various aspects of collaboration, each of which contributes value for certain purposes and particular dimensions, but none of which is useful for comprehensively assessing the maturity of the collaboration and thus for measuring the quality of the overall collaboration [13, 21]. In this paper we will summarize the goals, utility, and limitations of these models, and propose the design of a new model—the collaboration maturity model (Col-MM). This model was developed in cooperation with a focus group consisting of professionals to maximize the relevance of the model and its practical applicability. The aim was to create a model that is sufficiently generic to be applied to different organizational or team settings and to be useable by practitioners for conducting self-assessments.

Thus, the present paper addresses the following main research question: Can we determine the maturity level of an organization’s team collaboration? Key subquestions include: What characteristics describe a team’s collaboration maturity? And, what steps need to be taken to assess a team’s collaboration maturity? To address these questions, we executed an exploratory study using an applied science or engineering (AS/E) paradigm to uncover what aspects are important for measuring collaboration maturity and developing and field testing a first version of a new collaboration maturity model (Col-MM). This paper therefore presents the results of our exploratory efforts to address an important class of unsolved problems— assessing the quality of collaboration within and across organizational boundaries. In the words of Stebbins, to understand a complex phenomenon one has to start looking at it in broad terms, from a broad perspective: “First observe the wood, then study its individual trees” [58, p. viii]. The Col-MM as presented in the paper represents the wood and the model elements the first outline of the constituent trees.

In reporting on the development of the Col-MM, the system for administering it (i.e., the Col-MM tool) and its application in the field, our paper aims to make several contributions. First, we propose a maturity model in the domain of collaboration quality that addresses a practical need and scientific void. Second, we propose a flexible structure for the Col-MM that allows it to be easily adapted to the situation in which it is applied. Third, we demonstrate that the application of the model yields useful results for organizations and their teams to perform an assessment with limited organizational effort. Finally, from a broader perspective, the Col-MM represents a holistic measure to inform comparative organizational level studies on the effects of various issues on collaboration quality such as organizational culture, leadership culture/styles, and team structures. In other words, the Col-MM may be used as a dependent variable for intra- and interorganizational collaboration quality studies.

## Background

## Maturity models

Maturity literally means “ripeness.” It describes the transition from an initial to a more advanced state, possibly through a number of intermediate states [19]. Paulk et al. [45, p.21] define process maturity as “the extent to which a process is explicitly defined, managed, measured, and continuously improved.” The evolutionary nature of improvement is also emphasized by Shapiro [55, p. 147]: “Some do it faster than others and with fewer detours, but fast or slow, every company that gets to world class must evolve through these stages to get there. There are no shortcuts.” According to Fraser et al. [19, p. 247] maturity can be seen “as a combination of the presence of a process and the organization’s attitude toward it.” The fundamental underlying assumption of maturity models is that a higher level of maturity will result in better performance [17]. Maturity models reflect the degree to which key processes or activities are defined, managed, and executed effectively and produce reliable results. They typically describe the characteristics of an activity at a number of different levels of performance [19]. Approaches to determine process or capability maturity are increasingly applied to various aspects of product development, both as an assessment instrument and as part of an improvement framework [17]. Most maturity models define an organization’s typical behavior for several key processes or activities at various levels of “maturity” [19]. Even though maturity models are declarative, that is, based on self-reports, they provide an instantaneous snapshot of a situation and a framework for defining and prioritizing improvement measures. The key strengths of maturity models include:

● They are simple to use and often require simple quantitative analysis;

● They can be applied from both functional and cross-functional perspectives;

● They provide opportunities for consensus and team building around a common language and a shared understanding and perception; and

● They can be performed by external auditors or through self-assessment.

One of the earliest maturity models is Crosby’s Quality Management Maturity Grid (QMMG) [12], which was developed to evaluate the status and evolution of a firm’s approach to quality management. Subsequently, others’ maturity models have been proposed for a range of activities including quality assurance [12], software development [45], supplier relationships [36], innovation [9], product design [19], research and development effectiveness [41], product reliability [53], strategic alignment [35], and knowledge management [26]. The best known models are the Capability Maturity Model (CMM) for software engineering (based on the Process Maturity Framework of Watts Humphrey, quoted in [45]), developed at the Software Engineering Institute (SEI), and the ISO 9001 standard developed by the International Standards Organization. Both share a common concern with quality and process management. Unlike the other maturity models cited above, CMM is a more extensive framework in which each maturity level includes a number of key process areas (KPAs) containing common features and key practices to achieve stated goals. A number of studies on CMM have shown links between maturity and software quality [22]. This model (with multiple variations) is widely used in the software industry as part of quality certification (SEI Certification).

Several maturity models have been proposed that aim at clearly identifying the organizational competences associated with best practices [19]. In practice, however, many maturity models are intended to be used as part of an improvement process, and not primarily as absolute measures of performance [19]. Few maturity models have been validated in the way of performance assessment. An exception is the study of Dooley et al. [17] that demonstrated a positive correlation between new product development (NPD) process maturity and outcome.

## Collaboration maturity models

The word “collaboration” derives from the Latin words com and laborare, which mean “to work together.” Researchers have defined collaboration in different ways. For example, Levan [33] argues that collaboration is a social phenomenon that involves several individuals when the action of only one does not achieve the expected result. Briggs and colleagues define collaboration in terms of efforts and purpose: “Collaboration is making a joint effort toward a group goal” [7, p. 46]. Koch and Gross [30] further specify this joint effort as acts of shared creation and/or discovery. In the context of this study, we define, more precisely, collaboration as a process in which two or more agents (individuals or organizations) share resources and skills to solve problems so that they can jointly achieve one or more goals. During this process, the agents communicate with each other to coordinate their tasks. Based on this definition of collaboration and in accordance with the overall dimensions’ collaboration, we define collaboration maturity as a team’s current maximum capability to collaborate (i.e., people) where team members effectively communicate (i.e., process), reach shared understanding (i.e., information), and adjust their tasks (i.e., management and process), behaviors (i.e., people), and means (i.e., technology and information) to produce high-quality outcomes.

A number of maturity models related to collaboration have been proposed, each of which contributes value in particular purposes, dimensions, and types, but none of which addresses the overall maturity of collaboration practices and structures:

1. The Fast Reactive Extended Enterprise-Capability Assessment Framework (FREE-CAF) model was initially suggested by [32] and further developed by Wognum and Faber [62]. The purpose of this model is to define the maturity level of concurrent engineering in a virtual company. It emphasizes the organization and management of new products and processes in temporary collaborative projects in two or more organizations. The model distinguishes four hierarchical maturity levels (repeatable, defined, managed, and optimizing). At each level, competencies are defined for each key process area (KPA). These competencies include inherent practices of the virtual organization, the partners, or both. The FREE-CAF model is interesting as it incorporates a wide range of KPAs, yet it is limited in that it covers only the initial phases (design and engineering) of the project life cycle. Because collaboration continues throughout a project, it would also be useful to have a maturity model that could cover the whole project life cycle. In addition, because collaboration occurs between individuals as well as among organizations, it could be useful to have a model to measure the collaboration maturity of teams.

2. The Collaboration Maturity Grid (CMG) was developed at Cambridge University to examine collaborative maturity in the context of issues confronting firms that outsource a significant amount of design or development activity to a third party [19]. The main aim of the model was to provide guidance to managers involved in collaborative projects. This model applies to the development of products in general and is not restricted to the domain of software. A limitation of the model is that it mainly focuses on the organizational-level collaboration between companies rather than on the team-level collaboration between individuals.

3. The model by Ramasubbu and colleagues [50] was developed at SAP AG in response to limitations with CMM. It emphasizes the dimensions of dispersion and collaboration of development teams. Neither CMM nor ISO 9001 addresses the key processes required to develop or evaluate distributed products such as establishing mutual knowledge and managing geographically dispersed social networks. A limitation of the model is that it mainly focuses on a particular collaboration in a particular domain.

The value of each of the models is that they emphasize and raise awareness on the issue of collaboration maturity in an organizational setting. Notwithstanding the individual strengths of each of the above models, several key limitations exist. Collaboration maturity models’ applications are specific for only certain types of collaboration (e.g. interorganizational, virtual organizations, or distributed projects), for certain application domains, or for certain project life-cycle phases. Third, most models are descriptive in nature, helping to identify collaboration-related problems without proposing solutions. Finally, little is known about whether the use of these models leads to actual performance improvements.

The main objective of the study reported in this paper is to present a new collaboration maturity model (called Col-MM) that addresses some of the limitations described above. Where the FREE-CAF model covers only the initiation phases of the project, Col-MM will cover the whole project life cycle. Where FREE-CAF and CMG models focus on the organizational-level collaboration between companies, Col-MM will focus on team-level collaboration between individuals. Where the model of Ramasubbu et al. [50] is only useful for software, Col-MM will generalize to collaboration in any domain. Table A1 (see Appendix) summarizes the comparison of the proposed Col-MM with other maturity models. Col-MM aims to be generic for all types of collaboration and can be used to assess the overall collaboration maturity of a given team. Further, it supports the development of recommendations to define and prioritize improvement measures of the quality of collaboration and thus organizational performance.

## Method

The present research is based on the design science research (DSR) method [24, 25, 39] as a case of AS/E research to solve an important class of unsolved problems— measuring the quality of collaboration within and across organizational boundaries. Indeed,

the goal of AS/E research is to use scientific knowledge to solve important practical problems. AS/E is distinguished from engineering practice in that engineering practice seeks to create a specific instance of a useful artifact to solve a specific problem . . . while AS/E seeks to create novel, generalizable solutions for an important class of problems, and to synthesize bodies of knowledge, construction principles, and generalizable work practices that can increase the likelihood that designed artifacts will meet design objectives. [6, p. 100]

According to Briggs and Schwabe [6], the research products of AS/E research are: (a) detailed descriptions of important classes of problems, and the contexts in which they emerge; (b) generalizable design objectives, constraints, and requirements for addressing a class of problems; (c) generalizable solutions for a class of problems; (d) expository instances of generalizable solutions; (e) proof-of-concept prototypes; (f) evidence that solutions are useful and generalizable; and (g) the elements that compose design theories for implementing solutions for a class of problems. The AS/E paradigm is well suited for our research since it aims to develop a new model to assess the collaboration maturity in an organization and it creates an exemplar instance of a generalizable solution empiri cally tested in the field to demonstrate proof-of-concept and proof-of-value.

In the context of our research, the exemplar instance of the solution would be represented by the collaboration maturity model (Col-MM) as follows:

● The Col-MM structure that describes the collaboration areas of concerns (topics) and their related criteria.

● The Col-MM questionnaire that includes questions, levels of ratings, and mathematical formulas for analysis.

● The Col-MM method that (a) defines the steps and provides guidance on how to run the Col-MM questionnaire in the field, and (b) supports the development of recommendations for defining and prioritizing improvement measures of the quality of collaboration.

● The Col-MM tool, which is a system for administering the Col-MM questionnaire according to the Col-MM method. It is a customized Microsoft Excel application that represents the implementation of the Col-MM, and enables the execution of a concrete assessment by allowing the collection and analysis of the questionnaire data (quantitative/qualitative). It provides different presentations of results (e.g., forms, statistics, individual and collective spider diagrams, and comparison curves) and subsequent reports.

The Collaboration Maturity Model (Col-MM)

The development of Col-MM

The Col-MM was developed in cooperation with a focus group consisting of professionals with leading positions (i.e., business unit managers). These self-declared collaboration and knowledge management (KM) experts were accustomed to meeting in the context of a business association: (a) to share their best practices regarding methods, techniques, and tools, (b) to get their peers’ feedback on case studies, and (c) to attend special presentations on the latest trends in the collaboration and KM areas. Below, we report on the focus group meetings that led to developing the Col-MM. The goals of these meetings were threefold. First, to build a generic collaboration maturity model for a global assessment of team collaboration (people, information, management, process, and technology) based on previous maturity models of software development and quality assurance. Second, to apply the model in practice through pilot and field studies. Finally, to provide guidelines and a system enabling practitioners to conduct self-assessments with the Col-MM.

## Participants

The focus group included 15 chief knowledge officers (CKOs) working for different companies of different sizes (ranging from 500 to 200,000 employees; including 10 multinational firms) in different sectors (including automotive, software, audiovisual, civil engineering, and telecommunications). The participants held at least a master’s-level degree (MSc or MBA; 4 held a Ph.D.) from different areas (including industrial design, mechanical engineering, human resources, management, computer science, and ergonomics). They had at least fifteen to nineteen years of work experience, with 50 percent of them having five to nine years as a CKO managing many different projects. The average age of the CKOs was forty-eight years-old, and 73 percent were male. All participants were French.

The focus group meetings were facilitated by one of the coauthors. The participants were interested in participating in the focus group for a variety of reasons. First, one of the CKO’s missions is to improve the organizational performance of teams through knowledge sharing [4], commitment [55], and streamlined knowledge-intensive processes. The participating CKOs felt that their teams in general were not efficient as expected. They assumed that a bad quality of collaboration is always a brake for a good organizational performance, while a good quality of collaboration is an enabler and driver for it. Second, some of the participants were faced with the need to enhance collaboration quality in their own company. Some of their top managers inquired about instruments for gauging collaboration quality in order to improve team performance and the quality of outcomes. Operational managers in their companies expressed a need for such instruments to guide them in effectively managing and fairly rewarding individuals and teams [57]. Finally, no business instrument was available to help them in such activity. They felt that existing human resource assessment instruments were more focused on individuallevel than team–level assessments.

## Focus Group Process

The development of the Col-MM took almost two years. The focus group process consisted of three-hour long monthly meetings per month. Seven meetings were used to work on the Col-MM, and five meetings for participants’ feedback on pilot studies. The main seven working meetings as the focus group process steps could be summarized as follows:

1. The first step consisted of the generation of the antecedents to collaboration quality. This first meeting was more like an open discussion and addressed the multidimensions of the collaboration: people, information, process, management, and technology. Initial antecedents included collaboration objective/ goal, commitment, interaction intensity, formalization of relationships, management style, leadership endorsement, collaboration technology, and so on.

2. The second step was to generate the requirements for a useful collaboration maturity model. The participants—with regard to their knowledge on the maturity models and their expertise related to KM and collaboration—were in agreement that the Col-MM should meet the following generalizable requirements:

● Resource efficient: The Col-MM should be fast to complete.

● Rich data: The Col-MM should report on different points of view and concerns from the workplace, using both quantitative and qualitative data.

● Limited need for further advanced data analysis: The supporting tool should provide integrated support for the interpretation of the results.

● Self-assessment: Practitioners should be able to apply the Col-MM themselves.

● Constructive learning: The Col-MM should promote team building and organizational learning rather than control and sanction.

● Quality improvement: The Col-MM application should inform initiatives that may influence team collaboration and, consequently, organizational performance. This could affect not only the process but also people, information, management, and technology.

3. The goal of the third step was to generate the constructs of the Col-MM in terms of criteria and levels of assessment. For this stage the researchers were invited to share insights from the collaboration science literature on critical aspects of organizational and team collaboration. At this step, the participants combined these insights with their personal perspectives to define a total of twenty-four criteria that were grouped in four areas of concern. A four-point scale was suggested to assess each criterion as for the previous MMs. These criteria were discussed among participants according to their relevance to the context of professional collaboration.

4. The fourth step focused on the development of the Col-MM questionnaire in terms of items (questions related to criteria, one question for each criterion), rating scales (analysis levels), and average calculations. The participants worked on the criteria one by one, and defined each criterion question and its levels of assessment. Attention was paid to the wording to ensure the clarity for future respondents.

5. The fifth step addressed the Col-MM method in terms of how to delineate the scope of the Col-MM project, how to choose the respondents, how to run the Col-MM questionnaire in practice in terms of steps and how to inform discussions in which the results would be reflected on for the purpose of developing improvement recommendations. Following this meeting, the Col-MM was tested in three pilot studies that were organized in the companies represented by three of the participants (a sound-engineering team in an audiovisual and communications company, a change management department in an information technology (IT) consulting company, and a rail transportation control service unit in a transport and freight company).

6. In the sixth step, the use of the Col-MM method was evaluated. Discussions were organized by the facilitator with the focus group to argue the pros and cons of the Col-MM method applications: relevance of the questionnaire and steps. It was decided to perform two additional pilot studies (from a quality control service unit in a civil engineering company and a radio performance and monitoring department in a telecom company). Upon completion of these final pilots, a few adjustments and improvements were proposed and discussed, in particular the clarity of the questions for each criterion, the rating scales, and the execution of interviews. Following this meeting, the Col-MM tool, a system for administrating the Col-MM, was designed (by a third party) as an Excel application.

7. In the final step, both the Col-MM method and tool were presented and adopted by the focus group in the presence of the chief executive officer (CEO) of a large company in the automotive field that was interested in hosting a Col-MM application.

## The Col-MM

The Col-MM presented in this paper aims to assess the overall collaboration maturity of a given team. It informs (but does not determine) the development of recommendations in the form of an action plan to define and prioritize improvement measures of the collaboration quality. Its applicability is not limited to a particular form of collaboration and the model can be used in different settings (e.g., project teams, virtual teams, organizational teams, cross-functional/organizational teams, or interorganizational teams). Depending on the selection of participants in an assessment using the Col-MM, its unit of assessment is a team, a functional part of an organization, or an organization as a whole.

## The Col-MM Structure

Inspired by the software Capabilities Maturity Model (CMM), Col-MM distinguishes between four maturity levels: ad hoc, exploring, managing, and optimizing. At the ad hoc level, processes are usually ad hoc and chaotic, and information/ knowledge is not managed. Success in these teams depends on the competence and heroics of some individuals. Teams often produce products and services that work; however, they frequently exceed the budget and schedule of their projects. The management of the collaboration practices is not based on a clear strategy. In sum, teams are collaboratively immature. Individuals have many difficulties communicating effectively, reaching shared understanding, and adjusting their tasks, behaviors, and means (e.g., technology).

At the exploring level, processes are characterized but not always respected or understood, and information/knowledge is at some point managed. Teams produce products and services that work; however, they are not effective. One strategy of collaboration management is announced but not always followed. In sum, teams are well aware of weaknesses in terms of collaboration quality. Individuals try to work together with the means at their disposal, but are faced with many collaborative challenges. Some initiatives to address these are attempted but without major impacts.

At the managing level, processes are well characterized and understood, and information/knowledge is managed. Individuals are able to accomplish products and services of good quality. The collaboration management is based on a clear strategy. Thus, the collaboration maturity of teams is good but there is still room for improvement.

At the optimizing level, processes are continually improved, and information/ knowledge is very well-managed. In sum, teams are collaboratively mature. Individuals work together optimally and are able to produce high-quality products and services. The management of collaboration is based on a proven and effective strategy.

Unlike the other maturity models discussed earlier, Col-MM explores the maturity of the collaboration of a given team from different perspectives related to its quality (i.e., people, information, process, management, and technology). The following dimensions, or areas of concerns, were considered essential by the participants in the focus group meetings:

● Collaboration characteristics: covers the descriptive features and attributes of the collaboration and collaborators’ participation.

● Collaboration management: covers the way in which the collaboration is managed.

● Collaboration process: covers how actors perform collaboration on a regular basis. It includes the technology dimension as technology is considered as a process resource.

● Information and knowledge integration: covers how actors manage the information and knowledge required for productive collaboration.

For each area of concern, a number of criteria were defined (see Appendix Table A2 for more details). These criteria represent the topics for the Col-MM questionnaire and were derived through the focus group discussion that was informed by a literature review from the researchers. Each criterion is represented by an item that is evaluated on a four-point scale (with 4 being the highest and 1 the lowest). To support the respondents, the levels of each criterion are described briefly, with examples wherever possible.

In essence, the Col-MM is structured as a library of criteria. In some situations, not all criteria may be relevant. So, an organization can decide which criteria fit better with their specific context. An organization can also decide to expand the set of criteria. Also, for some organizations certain criteria may be more important than others. In such situations, it is possible to assign different weights to the criteria.

## The Col-MM process

The Col-MM method defines the seven steps to perform the analysis (see Appendix Table A3).

At the scoping step, the purpose of the Col-MM analysis is delineated according to the organizational context and the business strategy. The boundaries of the analysis are precisely defined before starting. The reasons for performing the assessment should be communicated to all concerned individuals and teams. It is important to present this as an overall team collaboration assessment to improve organizational performance, rather than as an individual retrospective evaluation. At this step, the Col-MM structure could be revised with regard to the specificities of the context (i.e., in terms of areas of concerns, nature and number of criteria, and definition of levels of ratings).

The data collection is performed through individual and/or collective interviews based on the Col-MM questionnaire (quantitative data). The choice depends on parameters such as: social context of the project, availabilities of respondents, current team climate, time and project deadline, and so on. The selection of individuals should be representative of the target team (profile, position, age, experience, etc.). During interviews, qualitative observations should be collected to enrich the analysis and gain a deeper understanding of any perception differences that may exist.

The preparation of an interview plan/flow is suggested to ensure the homogeneity of all interviews. For a better understanding of the domain where the collaboration is applied (vocabulary/jargon, past experiences, etc.), we recommend for some cases, during interviews, the involvement of a person (e.g., internal auditor) from the company (but external to the assessed team). The recording of interviews is also very useful for the (qualitative) data analysis but one must get the respondent’s prior approval.

After the data collection, a first quantitative data analysis is performed using the Col-MM tool for administering the Col-MM questionnaire (described below). This analysis identifies individual perceptions about the collaboration maturity of the team. It also helps to highlight critical perception differences concerning the different criteria. The qualitative data analysis (which can be done using an additional content analysis based on the interview statements) facilitates a more in-depth understanding of these perception differences for each criterion or group of criteria (area of concern). Follow-up discussions and consensus-building efforts could be carried out for relevant scores, in order to settle on an acceptable assessment. The cross-data analysis may yield additional interpretations by combining criteria for specific measurements of capabilities according the goal of the assessment, such as knowledge management (criteria 20, 22, 23, and 24), value creation (criteria 4, 23, and 24) or organizational learning (criteria 10, 15, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, and 24).

The results can be presented in various formats using the Col-MM tool. Spider diagrams can be made individually for all criteria scores or grouped by area of concern. Superposition of individual spider diagrams can show the rating gaps on individual criteria or areas. To identify criteria for which it is necessary to collect additional qualitative information, comparison curves can be prepared that visualize perception differences between respondents regarding a criterion. Team spider diagrams of all criteria scores, individually or grouped by area, can represent the collective perception of the collaboration maturity/quality of a team. Finally, cloud matrixes can show combinations of criteria (not detailed in this paper).

The last step of the Col-MM method concerns the definition of an action plan. It helps in the framing of concrete recommendations in terms of actions to define and prioritize improvement measures of the collaboration quality and outcomes (not detailed in this paper). Such actions can involve a variety of initiatives, for example, the clarification of the strategic corporate objectives, training on facilitation techniques (e.g., for brainstorming), invited seminars on collaboration technologies, coaching/tutoring, the development of a shared knowledge repository, and/or group communication training. Table A3 (see Appendix) details the seven main steps of the Col-MM in terms of goal, procedure, tool, deliverable, and quality indicators.

## The Col-MM tool

The Col-MM Excel application, a system for administering the Col-MM, was specifically created to facilitate quantitative and qualitative data collection during the interviews, and to analyze the quantitative data automatically. It provides different presentations of results.

Using the Col-MM tool, data collection through interviews is performed with one or more respondents at the same time. The idea is to evaluate, on a four-point scale, each criterion of the Col-MM questionnaire through one Excel spreadsheet according to each respondent. Respondents are allowed to provide scores such as “0.5,” “1.5,” “2.5,” and “3.5.” When a respondent cannot answer, no score is recorded. This is thus not taken into account by the tool for the analysis.

The data collected may represent a considerable volume. It depends on the scope of the study and the number of persons/groups/teams to be interviewed. For that, the Col-MM tool is very useful for the storage, processing, and visualization of these data, particularly with spider diagrams (e.g., Kiviat diagrams) and curves. The visualization is very important for the synthesis of the results.

The more the scores tend to 4, the higher the level of team maturity increases.

The maturity of team with regard to a criterion $( m _ { c r i t e r i o n } )$ is obtained by calculating the average of respondents’ ratings:

$$
m _ {c r i t e r i o n} = \frac {\sum m _ {r e s p t}}{n _ {r e s p t}},
$$

where $n _ { r e s p t }$ is the number of respondents interviewed. The level of collaboration maturity of a team (M) is obtained by calculating the average of collaboration maturity values for each criterion:

$$
M = \frac {\sum_ {i} k _ {i} . m _ {c r i t e r i o n}}{\sum_ {i} k _ {i}},
$$

where $k _ { i }$ is the coefficient corresponding to the criterion i used for the average calculation. This coefficient is a weight given to the criterion according to its importance in the analysis. The weighting of criteria can be applied in some cases when necessary.

The calculation of points provides the level of maturity: ad hoc (< 20 percent), exploring (20–50 percent), managing (50–80 percent), and optimizing (80–100 percent).

## The Application of Col-MM: A Field Study in the Automotive Industry

Overview of need

A large multinational automotive firm had a desire to assess the collaboration quality of some of their distributed virtual teams. This company had previously established a new organizational matrix structure, based on the “management by project” principle. To assess the “fit” of this new structure in the context of a recent merger-acquisition and to see if all the constituent brands work as one single group, the company decided to assess the organizational performance in terms of synergy between the different sites and brands, productivity, quality of the products, and the balance between product diversity and process complexity. The collaboration maturity assessment was part of this wider organizational performance assessment.

As a first step, the Col-MM was applied to assess the collaboration maturity of one virtual team distributed over two European countries (two sites) with different cultures, different work habits, and different management styles. This team was in charge of the “Engine After Treatment System” (EATS) that was part of a wide development project of a new diesel engine that was taking place under the responsibility of a business unit distributed over three countries. The leading site in this project will be referred to as site A below.

## Field study steps

The field study followed the Col-MM method steps (Table A3 Appendix). As presented to the stakeholders, the objectives of the Col-MM analysis were to check:

● Whether the organization had adequate capabilities to effectively support high-quality collaboration.

● Whether collaboration technologies were provided and effectively used.

● Whether there were critical collaboration issues related to cultural differences (e.g., national, organizational, or technological), given the merger-acquisition context.

The selection of respondents was done with the assistance of the firm’s CKO (who was part of the focus group) according to criteria such as job position, responsibility, process step intervention, and working experience. All respondents had similar levels of education (MSc degree) but from different engineering fields: mechanical, electronic, electric, and industrial. The Col-MM questionnaire was sent to the respondents before the meeting with an introduction to the company’s Col-MM objectives. Anonymity and confidentiality of the treatment of the responses were formally assured.

Nine individual interviews were conducted face to face, by one of the coauthors and two research assistants, in the respondents’ native language in the two European countries (three at site A and six at site B). Next, two collective interviews (one for each site) were conducted to examine perception gaps on some criteria (e.g., 19. Information collection and 23. Knowledge reuse for site A, and 13. Rewarding, and 21. Information access for site B). Much more consensus was noticed through the ratings of the site A respondents. Each interview lasted about ninety minutes. During the interviews the Col-MM tool was used for data collection. All interviews were recorded for further qualitative data analysis.

After the quantitative analysis with the Col-MM tool, the qualitative analysis of specific statements, and cross analysis, a first report was sent to the respondents to solicit any corrections before the final report was prepared. A final presentation to the company’s top management was scheduled to report on the results and to provide input for a discussion on possible recommendations for future actions.

## Findings

The findings were reported in two forms: the actual aggregated results from the Col-MM (scores and respondents’ feedback) and a number of topics for further discussion. Discussion topics were identified based on the collected perceptions related to the Col-MM criteria and areas. Examples of findings reported to top management according to the four Col-MM areas of concern include:

● Collaboration characteristics: The findings showed similar understanding of collaboration objects (1) and team members’ commitment (7) for both sites, but different perceptions regarding interaction intensity (4) and formalization of relationships (6). In site A, reporting (to the group and hierarchy) is much more common and synergy (regular face-to-face meetings and interactions) is higher among individuals.

● Collaboration management: Respondents reported different perceptions between the two sites with respect to management style (10) and decision making (11) (hierarchical management vs. consensual management). Site B respondents felt unfairly rewarded compared to site A. They felt that because site A had the project lead, its employees always had an advantage. Incentives for collaboration efforts were not explicitly set up for either site (13. rewarding).

● Collaboration process: Respondents reported different perceptions between the two sites with respect to resource sharing (15). Findings showed that site B respondents experienced less awareness about the overall context (16) and different collaboration approaches to enhance team performance. Because of their positions and responsibilities in the process, they focused more on their individual contributions to the overall process rather than on developing collaborative relationships. Findings also showed differences in terms of conflict management (17) by the leadership in each site: Conflict management in site A was based on consensus while in site B it was based on hierarchical decision making and negotiation.

● Information and knowledge integration: Different perceptions were found regarding information structure (20) and access (21). For respondents in site A, access to information was not organized as well as they would have wished. Information is very scattered and access should be simplified. Consistent perceptions between the two sites were found regarding collaborative knowledge creation (24); both sites felt this process was well-organized. Respondents reported that a solid knowledge management strategy is available to best capitalize and reuse project knowledge. However, some differences between the sites were reported: site B appeared to be more stringent at this level.

An analysis of the qualitative feedback from the respondents revealed that some cultural differences between sites appeared to be related more to the organizational culture than to the national culture. For example, the perceived balance between private and professional life appeared to be different. One participant from site A commented: “I don’t think that culture has big role to play here but I think that there is a connection with the place of work in everyone’s life . . . it’s not because they spend more time at work, they are more productive.” Also, there was a different brand identity between sites: site B respondents felt they still belonged to their original brand (i.e., from before the merger), “We still have not digested this merger . . . the brand continues to reside in our subconscious.” Differences were also found regarding work attitudes: in site B respondents tended to be reactive, whereas site A respondents were more proactive. According to some respondents, this is because of their position in the project. Possible explanations could be related to their work contract type (tenure status) and social protection: “In the other site, it seems that they are always on hold . . . if they are not wooed, nothing happens. I wonder if this is because of their contracts and national social policy.”

The general findings from the application of the Col-MM can be summarized in three main points. First, respondents felt that collaboration is mainly based on “individuals’ goodwill” especially with respect to resource sharing and knowledge management. Second, the team is not as collaboratively mature as they expected themselves to be beforehand—they are at the exploring level (36 percent in total: site A = 46 percent; site B = 26 percent). Because of the asymmetric collaboration awareness between the two sites, their collaboration is mostly of a coordination nature. This makes it difficult to further improve the quality of their outcomes. Finally, the new matrix structure did not resolve all problems with respect to the imbalance between responsibility and authority.

Based on these findings a discussion was organized within the company and various options for improving collaboration in and between the teams were discussed. Five recommendations were reported to higher management:

1. Make collaboration a clear strategic goal in all project management initiatives.

2. Rethink the management of collaboration and provide training for managers.

3. Nominate team members or leaders to fulfill facilitation responsibilities to guide collaboration.

4. Take into account diversity aspects related to culture.

5. Recognize every team member’s contributions toward effective collaboration and goal attainment explicitly.

After six months, we learned that three of the suggested recommendations were followed-up with concrete actions:

1. The first recommendation was included and prominently mentioned in the company’s project management standard.

2. Following the third recommendation, one full-time collaboration facilitator was assigned to each business unit.

3. Following the fifth recommendation, a “collaboration capability” criterion was added to the annual individual performance assessment.

## Reflection on the field application of Col-MM

After applying the Col-MM steps during this field study, we gathered feedback regarding the use and usefulness of Col-MM. According to the respondents, the Col-MM analysis was useful and correctly represented their perceptions. It focused on real collaboration problems and allowed traditionally ‘unspoken issues’ to surface. Respondents were also satisfied with the feedback provided to top management and the subsequent actions that were taken related to the assessment’s recommendations. According to the operational managers, the results were relevant. Furthermore, most of them felt able to reuse the Col-MM by themselves in the future. According to the top managers, the study was satisfactory in terms of results and recommendations, as they confirmed and reinforced some of their own perceptions. This allowed them, for example, to focus more on the organizational culture than on national culture and to understand the problems related to the project-based new organizational structure. A few respondents provided some recommendations on the Col-MM questionnaire such as the request to clarify a few criteria or to add additional criteria to the library. Finally, most respondents expressed that they preferred the use of collective rather than individual interviews as this would enable a faster application of the Col-MM process.

Based on the experiences and feedback from this field study, we make the following observations regarding the extent to which Col-MM meets its initial critical requirements:

● Resource efficient: Col-MM appears to be resource efficient. A total of 36 hours were spent: 1.5 hours for the assessment preparation, 16.5 hours for the interviews (engineering profile), 3 hours for the CKO profile, 3 hours for the top management profile, and 12 hours for the analysis and report preparation. We feel that this is a comparatively modest and reasonable effort in terms of resources spent.

● Rich data: The combined use of quantitative and qualitative data analysis resulted in rich findings. We felt that qualitative observations enabled us to better uncover and interpret the various points of views expressed by the respondents through the Col-MM questionnaire.

● Limited need for further advanced data analysis: The analysis needs in the field study were limited and the Col-MM tool provided sufficient support (among others, the report generation).

● Self-assessment: The operational managers expressed confidence that they could perform future applications of the Col-MM themselves.

● Constructive learning: The respondents’ feedback shows that when the Col-MM study is carefully communicated, participation can be effective and generate discussions on real problems that further facilitate the acceptance of proposed solutions. In this respect, anonymity and confidentiality seem to be crucial.

● Quality improvement: The Col-MM application informed spirited discussion on possible improvements in areas that receive low or differing scores. It allowed the teams at both sites to focus on ways to change their practices and collaboration management.

## Discussion and Conclusions

Organizations often experience a distinct need for structured assessment methods concerning the quality of their collaborative processes. To address this need, we executed an exploratory research study following the AS/E paradigm to uncover what aspects are important for measuring collaboration maturity and developing and field testing a first version of a collaboration maturity model, the Col-MM. The specific aim of the Col-MM was to address a class of unsolved problems as expressed by fifteen CKOs and other organizational managers who are regularly confronted with such challenges. Our experiences show that the Col-MM can be applied in a resourceefficient fashion and yields results that are useful for organizations.

The contribution of our research is both theoretical and practical as we propose a model, an application method, a supporting administration system, and an empirical evaluation of their application. First, our research aims to contribute to the domain of maturity models that support the assessment of performance in a particular area, in our case, team collaboration. As our review on maturity models shows, there is limited and insufficient research knowledge available regarding collaboration maturity models. Second, the nature of the Col-MM is such that it can be easily adapted to the different settings in which it is to be employed. This versatility enhances the model’s practical utility. Third, our field experiences demonstrate that the application of the model yields useful results for organizations and their teams to perform an assessment of the quality of their collaborative practices and structures, with limited organizational effort. Finally, we submit that the Col-MM represents a way to holistically measure organizational or team collaboration quality and as such can inform studies that compare the effects of organizational or team structures, practices, culture, and norms on the quality and maturity of their collaboration. In this manner, the Col-MM can serve as a high-level dependent variable for intra- and interorganizational collaboration quality studies.

There are several limitations related to this study that provide direction toward a program for future research. First, our model was developed from two perspectives: a practical perspective (the focus groups) and a theoretical perspective (the identification of key criteria from the literature). Additional work to cross validate these two perspectives may help strengthen the foundation of the model in terms of its comprehensiveness to create a holistic set of collaboration quality criteria. This would include additional theoretical work on the inclusion (or exclusion) of certain areas of concern and their constituent criteria. It may also focus on investigating the usefulness of weighting of criteria (i.e., certain criteria may contribute more points than others, thereby influencing which maturity level is reached).

Second, our empirical evidence is limited to a field study involving a small number of teams in a single organization. To study the broad applicability of the model, it has to be applied in different types of organizations for different types of teams. Particular care has to be taken to ensure that Col-MM can take into account different levels of collaboration and different collaboration processes that occur in a variety of settings, such as teams of various sizes, crowdsourcing, and cross-cultural teams.

Third, the Col-MM cannot be used to investigate the relationship between collaboration maturity levels and organizational/team performance, although it is based on the assumption that higher levels of maturity will have a positive effect on performance. The Col-MM does provide a first step toward assessing the effect of collaboration quality on team performance insofar as it provides a benchmark measure to quantify collaboration quality at different points in time. Therefore, we plan to develop specific organizational and team performance measures to enable an analysis of the relationship between collaboration quality and organizational productivity. This will facilitate quantitative studies to examine the relationship between Col-MM factors (criteria), maturity levels, and team or organizational performance.

Finally, while the field experiences with Col-MM pointed at a number of strengths of the model itself and the approach to applying it, there are also a number of inherent weaknesses that may be the focus of future research. First, at first glance, the customizability of the model in terms of the criteria to be used during a specific maturity assessment could be considered a valuable asset. Yet it may also present a complication if researchers or practitioners want to compare teams or organizations in terms of their collaboration maturity: If the organizers of the effort do not include the same criteria in the maturity assessment, a comparison across different populations becomes impossible. Future research could investigate the possibility of defining a minimum base set of criteria that must be held constant so that comparisons can always be done along these more limited dimensions.

Second, as evident from the field application that was reported in this paper, the maturity assessment itself (i.e., the application of the Col-MM) does not automatically provide a team or organization with insights on how to fix any problems. It merely signals areas of concern that then need to be investigated in more detail to determine the exact problems and possible solutions. Future research could focus on developing standard guidance and best practices on how to improve collaborative practices along each of the criteria or groups of related criteria.

Finally, the essence of the Col-MM is the self-reports from the selected population on various criteria. This reliance on self-reports may limit the applicability of the Col-MM across cultures. For example, experiences with self reports in East Africa [60] regarding the application and appreciation of electronic meeting technologies showed that some respondents wanted to provide only what they considered socially desirable answers rather than frank assessments. While the current version of

Col-MM allows for anonymous responses, it cannot mitigate this risk completely, which limits its application to cultural environments where respondents are comfortable providing straightforward feedback.
