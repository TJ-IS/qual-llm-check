# Sustaining Web 2.0 services: A survival analysis of a live crowd-casting service

Sungjune Park <sup>a</sup>, JinKyu Lee <sup>b</sup>, MinJae Lee <sup>c,</sup>⁎

<sup>a</sup> Belk College of Business, University of North Carolina at Charlotte, 9201 University City Blvd., Charlotte, NC 28223 USA

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 24 October 2011 Received in revised form 17 September 2012 Accepted 25 November 2012 Available online 7 December 2012

Keywords: Web 2.0 User created content Lifespan Survival analysis Motivation Popularity Social network

## a b s t r a c t

Securing continuous in<sup>fl</sup>ows of user created content and promoting them to the potential consumers are basic yet critical tasks for many Internet businesses to succeed in Web 2.0 environments. Managers of Web 2.0 services need to quickly identify value-adding content and encourage the content contributors to keep supplying valuable content. This study surveys the previous literature to explain the lifespan of user created content and proposes a theoretical framework that considers three major stakeholders in the Web 2.0 business environments — content contributors, content consumers, and service platform providers. Based on the theoretical understanding, a model that explains the survivability of a user created content is developed and empirically tested using three months of log data collected by an Internet personal broadcasting service provider. The proposed theoretical framework provides researchers with insights into the interworking of the three major players in the novel business environments. The results of the empirical analysis suggest that content providers' commitments and positive rewards to intrinsic motivations, content consumers' acceptances and perceived popularity of available content, and the service platform provider's support for social network capabilities and popularity signaling mechanisms are important factors that Web 2.0 service providers must carefully manage in order to improve the longevity of value-adding user created content.

## 1. Introduction

The notion of Web 2.0 has been discussed a great deal among people in the online service industry, but there is little academic research that systematically investigated this topic [12]. The term Web 2.0 was coined by O'Reilly and Medialive International<sup>1</sup> as the name of a conference<sup>2</sup> in 2004. The term did not have a clear de<sup>fi</sup>nition at the inception and thus has often been used as a buzzword without a coherent meaning. Nevertheless, the recent discourse among industry leaders has narrowed down the scope and substantiated, to some extent, its meaning. Some of the characteristics of Web 2.0 services [27,38] that have been agreed to among industry experts include:

• Web as platform, software as a service (SaaS) — Core competences reside in web-based service provisioning (e.g., Amazon product recommendation, Facebook friend suggestion/personalized Ads.), as opposed to static products/services sold online.

• Crowd-sourcing — Harness collective intelligence and user created/ controlled content (e.g., Wikipedia, Linux developer communities, YouTube, Twitter).

• Dynamic content — Remixable data source and data transformations resulting in “mashup” web contents/applications (e.g., Blogger, Facebook news feed).

• User-centered design — Architecture of user participation and collaboration (e.g., Wikis).

The above-mentioned characteristics resulted in socio-technical changes such as power decentralization in the IT and media industries, software above the level of a single device, cost-effective scalability, and rich user experience.<sup>3</sup>

An important implication of this paradigm shift is that the ability to elicit value added content from users and the ability to promote the user created content (UCC) to their potential consumers are critical necessities for Web 2.0 service providers. Accordingly, understanding the factors and mechanisms to secure a stable supply of UCC that would draw the interest of content consumers is of utmost importance in the Web 2.0 service environment. Yet, little is known about these factors and mechanisms. Past research on web-based services has mainly focused on the potential users' acceptance or intention to use the services at the site level (TAM-based site studies, e.g., [52]). In those mainstream e-commerce studies, the existence and provision of a site-level service (e.g., web-based online store, product review/recommendation systems) are taken for granted. Questions about the sole supplier's performance often fall outside of the scope of those studies. Some media studies [3,6,20] that attempted to explain the popularity or hit period of pop arts/media products (e.g., songs, movies, TV shows, CDs) paid attention to the differences of the artists and producers (e.g., gender, castings, promotions). However, these studies assumed that all content creators/sellers (e.g., musicians, movie producers, TV stations) are equally and strongly motivated to market their products. These assumptions in many e-commerce and media studies completely overlook the supply side of Web 2.0 services: the factors associated with content contributors who actually create value of Web 2.0 services. On the other hand, recent studies of open source software (OSS) projects [22,23,28] focus on effective incentive mechanisms for software developers' voluntary participation. However, the demand side factors (e.g., popularity of an OSS, competition with other OSSs) often fall outside of the focus. Therefore there is a need for a more balanced theoretical framework that takes into account both the supply and demand sides of Web 2.0 services. Furthermore, we need to realize that many web-based services are now dynamic in nature, while most previous studies in the area have dealt with static content (e.g., merchandise, recorded songs, movies) of which properties do not change over time. The content presented to consumers of many <sup>fl</sup>ourishing Web 2.0 services changes constantly (e.g., results of Google/YouTube searches, potential friends/wall postings on Facebook, Top tweets). As more and more dynamic services emerge with an ever changing set of content, we need to extend our understanding of online service success in such transient service environments.

To <sup>fi</sup>ll the gap of knowledge in the area, we identify the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the lifespan of UCC in this paper. These factors are related to three major stakeholders in the Web 2.0 environment: 1) content contributors, 2) content consumers, and 3) service platform providers (Fig. 1). These factors address both the supply and demand sides of service environments. In addition, we propose to develop and empirically test a model that uses the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed factors to predict the survival of UCC in a very transient, live crowd casting service environment. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a crowd casting service (CCS) as a type of Web 2.0 service that provides a platform on which individual users can contribute audio/video content which can then become available to other users on the platform. CCS can be categorized into two types: on-demand CCS and live CCS. Examples of on-demand CCS are the current form of YouTube video and iTunes Podcasting services. On the other hand, live CCS services, such as iTunes Radio and SHOUTcast Radio Directory are specialized in providing temporary content that is available to other users only when they are being transmitted by the contributors. We focus on live CCS in this study, analyzing the services and data from Afreeca (www.afreeca.com), a Korea-based personal TV broadcasting service platform. In this live CCS context a user created live content (UCLC), such as a series of personal TV shows, should be conceptualized as a packaged experience good that consists not only of the multimedia content but also the unique presentation style and perspective of the content creator, the airtime/program schedule, the mode of interaction between the creator and consumers, as well as the observable behavior of other content consumers. For example, there can be multiple UCLCs built on the same base media content (e.g., a presidential election campaign video). However, each of the UCLCs will provide a different experience to its viewers because the individual broadcaster can edit and mash up the base content with his/her own perspective, subjective interpretation, as well as supportive and/or critical comments that can be very biased. In addition, the composition of the viewers and resulting live interaction among them (e.g., supportive discussions vs. hostile debates on a chatting screen) will also differentiate the UCLCs.

Content contributors, content consumers, and service platform providers represent the three major stakeholder groups that have distinct interests and needs in the Web 2.0 environment. The content contributor group is the major player that affects the success of live CCS on the supply side while the content consumer group is the player that affects the success on the demand side. Yet, the platform providers may be able to mediate or moderate the effect of the perceptions and behaviors of their content contributors and consumers in the cyberspace under their control. Accordingly, we adopt a multi-stakeholder framework that takes into consideration the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of all three major stakeholders on both the supply and demand sides of live CCS success. This framework combines personal, social, and managerial perspectives to examine the interests and needs of the three stakeholder groups, respectively. Building on the theories of motivation and popularity, and mapping previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings to our live CCS context, we develop, in the next section, a model that can predict the survival of UCLC with a few commonly available site statistics. The model is then tested against log data of Afreeca users collected for three months.

This study extends the past research by addressing both the supply and demand sides of Web 2.0 services and building on a theoretical foundation for the new business environment where success of an online service is determined by the interaction among content contributors, content consumers, and platform providers. By theorizing Web 2.0 success and testing a model of UCLC survivability, this study will help Web 2.0 businesses evaluate their contributed content and improve their services for both content providers and consumers. Researchers in the e-commerce area will also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the novel multi-stakeholder view of Web 2.0 services and the hazard model proposed in the study.

In the following section, we lay out a theoretical foundation that will clarify relevant concepts and their relationships. Next, we present a hazard model that can predict the lifespan of UCLC based on the theoretical foundation. Then the model testing procedure is described and the data analysis results are presented. The paper will conclude with a discussion of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 2. Theoretical foundation

The <sup>fi</sup>eld of media study offers various models developed to predict the lifespan or popularity of experience goods such as pop music [3], movies [17] and TV/radio stations [8]. Although the previous studies have suggested a large number of potential antecedents and useful prediction models, many of them are data driven and do not accompany elaborate theoretical discussions as to why the specific set of variables/factors would predict or determine the hit/survival of the media products. Thus, in order to predict the survival of a new breed of media products such as UCLC in the Web 2.0 environment, we <sup>fi</sup>rst need to elucidate the theoretical rationales behind the variety of predictors suggested in the past research.

## 2.1. Lifespan of active UCLC threads

With the peculiarity of Web 2.0 environments and new business models in mind, we need to rethink what we mean by the success of online services. We propose to adopt the lifespan of an active UCLC as a criterion variable in this study. The unit of analysis is a thread of UCLC which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a series of live content supplied by one contributor for an uninterrupted period of time. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition of UCLC threads, however, is concerned with the supply side of Web 2.0 services only. Since many UCLC threads would never generate pro<sup>fi</sup>table demand from the perspective of live CCS platform providers, we limit our scope to only the UCLC threads that achieve a meaningful level of consumption to take into account the demand side of live CCS success. Having said that, we use the term “active UCLC thread” in this paper to refer to a UCLC thread that generates a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned level of consumption for an uninterrupted period of time. Accordingly, the lifespan of an active UCLC thread can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the time period during which a UCLC contributor continues to make valuable contributions to content consumers. This conceptualization incorporates both the supply side (i.e., continuous contribution) and demand side (i.e., measurable consumption) performance of UCLC threads, both of which are critical for the success of a live CCS. The lifespan of an active UCLC thread can be affected by a variety of factors through multiple paths (i.e., direct/indirect causal relationships). In this paper, we focus on the motivational factors that affect content contributors' voluntary contributions, the social cues and pressures on content consumers' content choices, and the technical factors that allow platform providers to facilitate or manipulate the cognitive processes of the other two stakeholder groups.

## 2.2. Supply side mechanisms: motivation, commitment, and achievement

Unlike earlier Internet business models where website operators are responsible to <sup>fi</sup>ll up their website with their own goods and services, Web 2.0 service providers let their users contribute content to their platform, which in turn will attract other users. Therefore, the supply side of most Web 2.0 services relies heavily on Internet users' voluntary contributions. OSS development research emphasizes the importance of the voluntary contribution and interest of participants for the success of OSS development projects [22,23,28]. Those studies, building on motivation theories, have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed various types of motivators that encourage software developers to contribute their expertise, time, and effort to OSS development projects [22,46,50]. Most taxonomies to date follow the conventional dichotomy at the highest level: intrinsic motivation vs. external rewards [16], both of which are geared toward ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of human needs [32]. Intrinsic motivation seeks the origin of the motive inside the person in relation to the activity in question (e.g., receive recognition, feel competence or self-determination by contributing to an OSS project). On the other hand, external rewards involve consideration of the consequences of the activity (e.g., monetary compensation, credit for promotion), rather than the activity itself.

In the OSS context, “innate desire to code” [22 p.28], desire to help others (altruism), and desire to identify oneself with the OSS community were found to be good sources of intrinsic motivation for OSS contribution [22]. Altruism can be considered as a type of intrinsic motivation or personal disposition that enables an individual to do something for another at some cost to himself/herself [40]. Community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is also a type of altruism toward a community with which an individual identi<sup>fi</sup>es himself/herself. When an individual identi<sup>fi</sup>es their self as a part of a community, he/she considers the other members of the community as kin and therefore is willing to help them [24]. This is a form of obligation/community-based intrinsic motivation. Enjoyment-based intrinsic motivations such as personal enjoyment, interest, and recognition for self-esteem have also been found to be very strong motivators for voluntary contributions in related domains [22,23,46,50]. External rewards can motivate OSS developers as well. Some examples of external rewards for OSS developers are professional recognition, experience, and better credentials for future career, improvement of their favorite OSS, and indirect revenue from other OSS related products and services.

A previous OSS study suggests that internal factors (i.e., intrinsic motivation) are most in<sup>fl</sup>uential to hobbyists and students, while external (e.g., work-related user needs) and professional (e.g., status) factors may have a strong effect on paid contributors [50]. Accordingly, we expect that intrinsic motivation factors exert strong effects on most Web 2.0 services that capitalize on voluntary content contribution. This includes our live CCS case where hobbyists and students make up a large portion of the UCLC suppliers. For example, the sheer enjoyment of broadcasting a TV program, the sense of connection to or being loved by many people, the feeling of attention and achievement from an increasing number of viewers can be important enjoyment-based intrinsic motivators for the armature broadcast jockeys (BJs) at Afreeca. Community-based intrinsic motivation (e.g., altruism toward a community) was actually one of the major thrusts that have ignited the explosive growth of Afreeca in South Korea. The Korean press was often accused of losing its impartial voice when the conventional news media covered little about the government's suppression of candlelight protests against a new government policy in May 2008.<sup>4</sup> In response to the allegedly biased mainstream media, many citizens took their webcam and Wi-Fi/3G equipped laptops to the street and broadcasted the true scene live on the Afreeca platform.<sup>5</sup> The major motive there was community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation — let other fellow citizens know what is really happening in the country.

The organizational and personal psychology literature suggests a causal relationship between motivation and achievement, offering an explanation of the prolonged voluntary contribution of UCLC contributors. A number of previous studies on employee turnover [2,7,13,19,25,48,51] have stressed that both job satisfaction and organi zational commitment are negatively related to employees' intention to leave and actual turnover behavior. Job satisfaction can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “one's affective attachment to the job,” [48, p. 261] while organizational commitment is “the strength of an individual's identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with and involvement in a particular organization” [43 p. 604]. The concept of commitment can refer to “an enduring desire to maintain a valued relationship” [36 p.316] when applied to interpersonal relationships (e.g., marriage or customer relations). Although job satisfaction and organizational commitment have been suggested as two independent concepts, they can, and often do affect each other in a feedback cycle. Organizational commitment is said to consist of three components: affective (i.e., emotional attachment to the organization), normative (i.e., a sense of moral obligation), and continuance (i.e., the opportunity costs of switching) components [43]. It should be noted that Meyer and Allen argue that all three of these components represent attitudinal commitments [33]. According to their model of organizational commitment [33], attitudinal commitment affects on-the-job behavior (e.g., job performance, absenteeism, and organizational citizenship) as well as employee turnover. The resulting on-the-job/turnover behavior then provides feedback to yet another type of commitment: behavioral commitment, which in turn reinforces the affective component of attitudinal commitment in the next cycle. This view is in line with goal setting literature.

It has been widely accepted that a challenging yet achievable goal can lead to a higher level of task performance than an easy goal or a best effort pledge can do [23,30]. This is because there is a feedback loop, called the high performance cycle [30], between goal and performance. Self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “a person's belief in his/her own abilities to successfully perform a task” [4 p. 217], plays a pivotal role in the high performance cycle of goal setting and performance. When a person has con<sup>fi</sup>dence in his/her capability, he/she is more likely to accept/set a high-level goal. This will then help to regulate his/ her efforts and keep them focused to achieve the goal which in turn will result in a higher chance to achieve the expected level of performance. When the person actually achieves the challenging goal, the sense of achievement enhances his/her self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy further, inducing an even higher level of goal, commitment, performance, and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in the following round of the high performance cycle [30]. This suggests that the correlation between goal level and performance level can further be strengthened by providing the performing person with speci<sup>fi</sup>c feedback on his/her performance toward the set goal. OSS studies also show positive and cyclical causal links from an OSS developers' contribution level to their performance rankings and back to their subsequent social/professional status motivation [44,50]. Live CCS differs from on-demand CCS in that a live CCS requires continuous and regular contribution of user created content. The extended period of contribution implies that there will be a feedback loop for voluntary participation evolving through the period of participation. Accordingly, BJs with strong motivation and attitudinal commitment will have a chance to correct/adjust their behavior (i.e., behavioral commitment) such as spending more time to interact with their viewers in response to positive feedback (e.g., a sign of increasing popularity of their TV station). This ongoing motivation– commitment–positive feedback loop will put the contributors in a high performance cycle and increase the chance of survival of the UCLC thread.

## 2.2.1. Supply-side intervention of platform providers

Web 2.0 service platform providers have almost complete control over the design of the platform system (i.e., website), which becomes a virtual place where their users, both content contributors and consumers, interact with each other. Therefore, the platform providers can and should implement mechanisms to support their users' perceptions and behaviors. For the supply side support, platform providers need to provide features that enable their content contributors to motivate themselves, enjoy their contribution activities, and get feedback and support from other users.

Since social networking is a core motive of Web 2.0 services, it is logical to expect for content contributors to form a social network or community of like-minds, which in turn can provide positive community experience and reinforce their contribution for successful Web 2.0 services [31]. For this reason, many Web 2.0 sites attract users with similar interests and objectives (e.g., to become or feel like a popular news anchor, seasoned programmer, or a TV/pop star) and foster interpersonal relationships among the users. MySpace and MMORPG.com (Massively Multiplayer Online Role-Playing Games) are good examples of such Web 2.0 services that capitalize on similar interests and friendship. Live CCS is not an exception. For the fun seeking hobbyist BJs at Afreeca, connecting and networking with other people can be a terminal objective as well as the means by which they achieve their initial objective (i.e., own and run a personal TV station) [27]. Many Afreeca BJs broadcast for fun, rather than pursuing a serious career or making a living out of their TV stations. Thus, membership in a social network of UCLC contributors (BJs) will make the online broadcasting more enjoyable and thus strengthen their motivation to contribute to UCLC.

Social networks can also promote the UCLC themselves. Viral promotion is a unique characteristic of Web 2.0 services [27]. In order to make viral promotion happen, the platform provider needs to provide a means to spread the word about content by enabling syndication of similar interests/tastes. Syndication in the Web 2.0 context refers to a loose, unincorporated af<sup>fi</sup>liation of entities (people or organizations) intended to facilitate distribution of media material [12] such as UCLC. There can be many forms of syndication that can handle (re) publication, promotion, and distribution of content [12]. In our study case, a syndication of BJs/broadcasting stations called club offers an effective mechanism for viral promotion. For most casual viewers on Afreeca, sifting through the huge number of personal broadcasting stations to <sup>fi</sup>nd one that <sup>fi</sup>ts their taste is a daunting task. A club network is a form of content syndication that provides the member stations with cross references to each other's station. This enables stations of similar characteristics to easily be exposed, promoted, and enjoyed.

As discussed above, motivation theories suggest that various intrinsic motivation factors can affect the lifespan of a UCLC thread by in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the UCLC contributor's attitudes and behaviors. We have also emphasized that the role of platform service providers in realizing these motivators is utterly important in the Web 2.0 environment. The <sup>fi</sup>rst half of Table 1 summarizes the supply-side factors that we propose to consider as major antecedents of successful Web 2.0 services.

It should be noted, however, that motivating content contributors covers only half of the story. Unlike the organizational context where an individual worker's commitment, attitude, and intention can determine the worker's performance and actual turnover behavior [29,34,39,48], survival of a UCLC in Web 2.0 environments requires more than mere determination of the content contributor. In addition, we also need to take into consideration the acceptance of potential consumers of the contributed content. In the following section, we turn our attention to the demand side of Web 2.0 service success.

## 2.3. Demand-side mechanisms: general acceptance and perceived popularity

Success of Web 2.0 services is dependent as much on consumers' choices and actual consumption, as it does on content contributors' voluntary and continuing contribution. To understand the demand side of Web 2.0 success we bring in the concept of popularity in addition to user acceptance which has long been a central piece of mainstream IS research (e.g., technology acceptance, diffusion of innovation, IS success). Popularity has also been an important focal point of various research streams (e.g., developmental, educational, organizational) in psychology [9,14,37,47]. Popularity is often de-<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree of someone being generally accepted by one's peers [47], while the dictionary's de<sup>fi</sup>nition suggests that the subject of popularity does not have to be a person. Accordingly, we conceptualize the popularity of a UCC as the degree to which the UCC is being accepted by its potential consumers, whether the UCC is a discrete item (e.g., single song, music album, video clip) or a continuous stream of items (e.g., podcast series, regularly aired Internet TV/radio shows).

The broad conceptualization of UCC popularity above resembles the user acceptance concept measured at the individual level in many IS studies. From a psychological standpoint, however, popularity is a socially embedded concept [45]. That is, popularity is determined by a referent group, which in turn can be perceived by an individual in the group. Therefore, there must be a mechanism that signals the referent group's acceptance of the subject to the individual members in the group [9]. An important point is that, depending on the <sup>fi</sup>delity of the popularity signaling mechanism, an individual's perception of the popularity of a subject may or may not closely correspond to the actual, unbiased level of general acceptance of the subject. This is particularly true in the Web 2.0 context where platform service providers can have almost complete control over the information <sup>fl</sup>ow among their users. Accordingly, we decompose the concept of popularity to general acceptance and perceived popularity.

In this paper, general acceptance of a UCC refers to the actual, unbiased level of acceptance of the UCC users (potential consumers). We posit that the platform provider can approximate it by summing up an individual level of acceptance indicator (e.g., # of viewers, total view time, # of bookmarks, channel subscription/favoriteregistration). Individual acceptance can be understood as “interpersonal liking” relaxed to include non-human objects (e.g., UCC). Interpersonal liking is determined by an individual's attitude toward the other person in a dyadic relationship, regardless of the general opinion of the individual's referent group [11]. The general acceptance for an object will regress to the unbiased group-level sum of individuals' attitudes toward the object. On the other hand, perceived popularity is a function of available information about the general acceptance. The person–situation perspective of behavior [35] suggests that popularity at work is determined by the attributes of the subject (e.g., core properties of him/herself) and situational position (e.g., centrality in the network of coworkers) [47]. The former re<sup>fl</sup>ects the quality of the subject, and thus acceptability of the subject, while the latter emphasizes the signals about the quality propagating through the referent group.

The quality of attributes can be re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the subject's reputation. Reputation is a multi-dimensional concept that can refer to one of many positive or negative attributes that characterize an entity (e.g., an online vendor or a content contributor). For example, the popularity of an Internet TV station may be attributed to a good reputation for its highly interesting and enticing stories, but may or may not be affected by the BJ's reputation for his/her frequent provocative comments, political/religious prejudice, etc. Different individuals may weigh the subject's attributes differently and determine their attitudes toward the subject, of which result becomes the individual acceptance. We have a relatively good understanding on the key dimensions of reputation that strongly affect online vendors' general acceptance and popularity in a conventional e-commerce context. For example, reputation for low price, high product quality, fast shipping speed, or a hassle-free return process will signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase the popularity. However, in the new Web 2.0 context, especially when the service involves customized or trendy experience goods, reputation of a UCC in terms of a static set of content attributes may not be a good predictor of general acceptance or perceived popularity. Regardless of what dimensions are considered important at a given time, UCLC with more preferable attributes for that moment's potential consumers will achieve a higher general level of acceptance, increasing the chance for the UCLC to survive as long as the taste of the consumers do not change. As discussed above, this factor is determined by the attributes of the UCLC directly observed by the content consumers. However, perceived popularity of the UCLC cannot be realized without the platform provider's intervention.

## 2.3.1. Demand-side intervention of platform providers

The other component of popularity (i.e., perceived popularity) is a social factor that can have an extra effect on the demand side of Web 2.0 service above and beyond the general acceptance which is a personal factor. A recent study about a video-on-demand system [53] examined two types of platform provider generated chart effects. The results show that whether a movie appears on the lists or not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the popularity of the movie [53]. The study also indicates that the chart effect is independent of the actual popularity of the movies on the charts. Thus, mere inclusion in the platform provider's recommendation list can drastically prolong the lifespan of a UCLC. This chart effect can be explained by mismatch anxiety [5]. In a study that used functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to examine the brain parts associated with social in<sup>fl</sup>uence (e.g., song preference decision), the anxiety generated by a mismatch between personal acceptance and the perceived popularity was found to be a principal mechanism that drove individuals to change their original acceptance decision and conform to the perceived popularity of the song(s). Therefore, appearance on a popularity chart will have a strong positive effect on the survival period of the UCLC (e.g., broadcasting stations) above and beyond the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the actual popularity (i.e., general acceptance) or the quality of the UCLCs.

A Web 2.0 platform provider (e.g., Afreeca in the studied case) can help its content consumers perceive the popularity of its content by publicizing various metadata on the home page or an easily accessible/high-traf<sup>fi</sup>c area. One form of such metadata widely used in many Web 2.0 sites is popularity scores or ranking charts. It is important to note that the popularity of UCC in Web 2.0 service platforms can be easily reinforced or weakened by the platform provider. Suppose a situation where a platform provider publicizes a list of popular content on its website. If the company decides to list the top 10 popular UCCs on its main page, the popularity effect for the 11th popular UCC will be seriously lower than that of the 10th popular UCC. Therefore, a platform provider can exert in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the demand side of its service, and thus on the survival of its UCLC threads by strategically and selectively publicizing its proprietary information about the UCLCs and their general acceptance. In this sense, perceived popularity of Web 2.0 content is a result of interactions among the three stakeholders where managerial intervention of the service platform provider can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the results of the popularity contest.

## 3. Research design and methodology

This study attempts to empirically validate the theoretical propositions by applying them to a real Web 2.0 service example. For this purpose, the researchers partnered with Afreeca (www.afreeca.com/), a leading Internet broadcasting service platform provider in Korea that generously provided real service usage data. The researchers met representatives from Afreeca and sought consultation with regard to how the theoretical propositions can be applied to Afreeca's service platform. This was a critical step for both sides to understand potential implications of the technical aspects of the service platform, managerial practices, and user behavior in terms of the theoretical concepts. Upon a clear understanding of the study context, the research team developed a data collection strategy that included a list of data items to be measured or calculated, the scope and length of data collection activities. Since the data collection involved ad-hoc database queries and new data re-processing procedures, for both user privacy protection and longitudinal data collection, the research team went through several cycles of dry runs followed by data collection query/procedure modi<sup>fi</sup>cations. The resulting dataset, after a data cleaning, included more than 17,000 active UCLC threads with various data items for one dependent (i.e., lifespan) and six independent variables over a 3 month period. The six independent variables are Rank improvement rate, Message board use, Club membership, Number of favorites, Viewer size, and Chart appearance. In the following subsections, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y introduce Afreeca and its Web 2.0 services to help us relate the six context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables to the theoretical concepts, and then present a model that tests our theoretical propositions against the Afreeca data, along with the measurement items for the model.

## 3.1. Afreeca — the research context

Afreeca, implying “A free [TV broad]ca[sting],” is a market pioneer and leader in the live CCS business in Korea. The company started a beta service of then one-of-a-kind personal TV broadcasting services in May 2005 followed by the of<sup>fi</sup>cial launching of a full service platform in March 2006. According to internal statistics, the average number of visitors per day exceeded 500,000 in 2009, with up to 300,000 concurrent viewers. During the same period approximately 2500 stations on average (3000 MAX) were broadcasting concurrently with an average of 7.1 hours of broadcasting time, making the daily average channels served by Afreeca 50,000 as of June 2009. Unlike conventional, on-demand video sharing services such as YouTube, Afreeca empowers individual Internet users to stream video content and interact with the viewers while the video is being broadcasted. As a result, the unpredictable and transient aspects of live interaction add an extra value in the UCLC, distinguishing Afreeca's live CCS from many other on-demand CCSs. The Afreeca platform has been adopted by various large public events such as Seoul World DJ Festival 2007 (Fig. 2) and Yonsei–Korea university bedlam games,<sup>6</sup> attracting publicity and drawing more amateur BJs.

The vast majority of Afreeca BJs are individual hobbyists who run a TV station for fun (e.g., by interacting with viewers). Some BJs produce their own video content while others replay or relay existing video. A broadcasted video can therefore be anything from a simple rerun of a recorded TV episode to an original live talk show featuring multiple audio/video clips. As described earlier, we consider a replay of existing video user created content if the BJ transforms the video and/or allows live interaction among the viewers to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the viewers' experience. That said the proportion of UCLC, as opposed to simple reruns with no mashup, was estimated to be 55% and growing in Afreeca as of 2012.

The length and schedule of broadcasting also widely vary from BJ to BJ. Both BJs and viewers are constrained by the schedule if we consider how live content is created from the perspective of mashup. BJs need to attend their program all the time while broadcasting, unless they play a simple rerun with no live interaction with their viewers. Depending on their lifecycle, viewers also will have different sets of BJs and peer viewers available to interact with. Video on demand (VOD) services can be supported by Afreeca, but they are rarely used, partly due to the lack of social interaction that BJs value and partly due to the additional copyright issues that VOD services have to deal with.

## 3.1.1. Station/BJ ranks and feedback

The Afreeca platform keeps track of viewer counts and view time per broadcasting TV station and calculates daily ranks which become available to the station operators (i.e., BJs). Accordingly, any noticeable change in the popularity of a TV station over time can be important feedback for the BJ, in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the BJ's sense of achievement, satisfaction, and future commitment. If the feedback was positive and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence was strong, the station would have a higher chance to enter a high performance cycle resulting in fast improving ranks. Therefore, the rate of rank improvement up to the highest rank achieved of a station will indicate the level that the station has achieved in the high performance cycle as well as the BJ's level of motivation, commitment, and achievement.

## 3.1.2. BJ–viewer asynchronous communication

Another indicator of BJ's commitment, especially behavioral commitment, is the use of a message board. Each TV station on Afreeca has its own message board where the BJ can disseminate information about the TV programs and communicate with its viewers outside the broadcasting hours. Afreeca also provides a live-chat function while the station is on-air, but since the majority of Afreeca BJs do not broadcast 24 hours every day, the live chat may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for BJ–viewer interaction. Furthermore, users often bypass the station page and jump into a live studio via other channels available in Afreeca. For this reason, not all BJs post messages to their broadcasting pages. Given the context, one way that committed BJs can differentiate their TV stations is to use the message board function and offer an asynchronous communication channel to his/her (potential) viewers.

## 3.1.3. Social networks among BJs

BJs can create or join a Club, a social network of BJs or a syndication of broadcasting stations on the Afreeca platform. This capability is similar to the Groups in Facebook in that one BJ can participate in multiple Clubs or choose not to join any. These Clubs are usually small in size, therefore BJs that are members in a club have a good chance to develop close social relationships (e.g., friendship, mentorship) with each other. In addition, club member stations are cross-referenced on other member stations. If desired, they can also share their content and spread the workload over member stations. Considering birds of a feather <sup>fl</sup>ock together, a club membership can be a great means to enable viral promotion as well as a major source of enjoyment and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

## 3.1.4. Favorite stations

Viewers on the Afreeca platform can register their favorite TV stations. This function is similar to the channel subscription on YouTube or the bookmark function on Internet browsers. Viewers use this function to keep a record of TV stations that they liked, so that they can easily <sup>fi</sup>nd and access those stations when they are on. While a viewer can register multiple favorite stations, a favorite registration indicates that the viewer has accepted the TV station and is likely to watch the channel again. From a BJ's perspective, the number of users who registered the BJ's station signi<sup>fi</sup>es the fan base and general acceptance of the station. The information about individual viewers' registered Favorites is not visible to other users. Therefore, the number of Afreeca users who registered a particular station as a favorite would not affect the perceived popularity of the station.

## 3.1.5. Viewer counts

Viewers tend to switch from one station to another whenever they lose interest in the program because it is likely that there are other broadcasts that they would like more. Maintaining a good number of viewers indicates real time quality and acceptability, and thus increases the odds to survive. Afreeca can constantly monitor and record the number of viewers for each broadcasting station. The average number of viewers of a TV station, for example, can be an objective measure of general acceptance of the station. Since many users may accept a station yet do not register the station as a favorite, viewer size can be a good indicator of the general acceptance of a station among casual viewers.

## 3.1.6. Popularity rank charts

Afreeca calculates multiple ranking charts based on BJ popularity, viewer acceptance, and cumulative viewer counts which are calculated based on seven (7) day log data. The charts are updated daily and presented on the front page of the Afreeca site as well as on the viewer application. Viewers can select a channel to watch on the popularity charts. Since it is impractical to build a comprehensive chart that includes every broadcasting station and dynamically update it at all times, only a small fraction of the concurrently transmitting stations at a particular given moment are listed on the charts. Afreeca has arbitrarily set the cutoff lines for the charts, which may have also been changed a few times in the past. Therefore, an appearance on a rank chart provides the charted BJ station with a disproportional advantage over the large number of less fortunate stations in terms of perceived popularity.

## 3.2. Test model and measurement items

This study adopted a survival analysis technique and developed a hazard model that includes the six context speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors as the independent variables and estimates for the lifespan of active UCLC threads. The six context speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors can be categorized into supply side factors and demand side factors as shown in Table 2. In another dimension, the model presents two content contributor oriented (BJ) variables, two content consumer oriented (viewer) variables, and two platform provider oriented (Afreeca) variables. The operationalization of these variables is described below. Although a vast variety of data can be extracted from the service logs of a Web 2.0 site, the researchers tried to extract only the most objective data as free of complication as possible for the dependent and independent measures in the model.

## 3.2.1. Dependent measure — lifespan of active UCLC threads

We have argued that success of a live CCS largely depends on continuous in<sup>fl</sup>ux of UCLC that the users are willing to consume. We have observed that a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of Afreeca UCLC is created by BJs who contribute over an extended period of time. Moreover, Afreeca users tend to prefer the content created by those regular contributors, suggesting us to use the thread as our unit of analysis and the lifespan of active UCLC thread as the dependent variable of our survival model in this study. The longer an active UCLC thread survives, the more value the platform provider (e.g., Afreeca) can create from the users, thereby increasing the likelihood of the live CCS success.

While the minimum level of supply–demand activities necessary for a Web 2.0 business to sustain its services would differ from one Web 2.0 business to another, we adopted the following criteria after a consultation with Afreeca in determining whether a UCLC thread is active or not.

• Supply side stability: the number of UCLC contributions (i.e., broadcasts) in the past 7 days should be greater than or equal to 3

• Demand side stability: the average number of peak concurrent consumers (i.e., viewers) in the past 7 days should be greater than or equal to 20

With this operational de<sup>fi</sup>nition, our dependent measure is the number of consecutive days that a UCLC thread was active during our 3 months of data collection period. When a UCLC thread had become inactive for one or more days and then later re-entered an active state with the same BJ ID, they were considered as two separate threads. We do not consider the case where one person is broadcasting multiple TV stations using different BJ IDs. It is practically impossible to identify users with multiple IDs, while many Korean Internet service providers, including Afreeca, require a citizen registration number (CRN)<sup>7</sup> when a new user creates an account.

## 3.2.2. Independent measures

Rank improvement rate is calculated by dividing the difference between the initial rank and the best rank of a BJ by the number of days to reach the best rank. With this operationalization, a high improvement rate suggests a strong motivation, commitment, and achievement of the BJ, especially in the early stage of the active UCLC thread.

Message board use denotes whether the BJ uses a message board and is measured by examining if there is at least one notice or announcement posted by the BJ. If there is a BJ message, we considered that the BJ was using the message board, regardless of the number of posts. A large number of BJs never used the message board function. We determined that the comparison between users and non-users would result in more meaningful outcomes than the different number of BJ messages.

Number of favorites that re<sup>fl</sup>ects general acceptance (i.e., objective popularity) of a station was measured by counting the number of Afreeca users who had registered the station as a favorite on the Afreeca system.

Viewer size denotes the average number of viewers during a broadcasting session. It is calculated by dividing the cumulative total number of viewers by the number of broadcasting sessions during the data collection period.

Chart appearance denotes whether a station has been ranked in a ranking chart. Since Afreeca maintains multiple charts, we considered any station that appeared at least once on any of the charts as a station that appeared on a chart. Afreeca did not change the cutoff rank for the charts during the data collection period.

Club membership denotes whether a BJ is a part of broadcasting syndication in Afreeca (a.k.a. Clubs). We measured whether the BJ is a member of at least one club or not. We did not include any demographic data of the BJs in our model because Afreeca personnel considered such data as age and gender unreliable. This is related to the practice of CRN collection. Many underage users submit their parents' CRNs to use the Afreeca services. Many Korean online service providers, including Afreeca, are aware of this practice and consider age and gender statistics inaccurate.

## 3.3. Data collection and analysis

Data on the usage of UCLC service have been collected from Afreeca over a three (3) month period. One of the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in collecting the data for live content is that the usage data is dynamic by nature. The ranking of BJs changes every day and viewer size changes every second during certain broadcasting sessions. Such real time statistics as viewer size were collected by injecting additional codes in their server applications while most of the data was obtained from the server log. For the completeness of the dataset, every active UCLC thread was examined; however, we excluded threads that had already been active before the study period because there was no data on how long these had been active. As a result, for this research we examined a total of 17,325 UCLC threads that had become active during the study period. Among these threads, 14,841 threads had become inactive and 2484 UCLC threads remained active. That is, 14.3% of the UCLC threads survived our study period but the lifespan of these threads is not available. In survival analysis, we call these censored data. Since we are interested in investigating the antecedents of active UCLC lifespan, we adopt survival analysis as our research method. Survival models are used in the study of occurrence and timing of events using longitudinal data and thus are adequate to use in our study context. There are other advantages of survival models, in particular the Cox proportional hazards model. The Cox proportional hazards model does not require speci<sup>fi</sup>c distributional assumptions in the data. Most importantly, survival models enable us to address features of the data that cannot be handled with conventional statistical methods such as regression. These features include the ability to use censored data (i.e., including cases where the event has not yet occurred in the analysis) and time dependent covariates [1]. In our study, censoring in survival models allows us to accommodate the fact that a successful UCLC will not reach its demise by the end of the study period. Survival analysis also allows us to use time dependent covariates. In our model, many research variables are potentially time dependent.

A hazard ratio of an interaction term less than 1 (i.e., $\tilde { \beta } _ { j }$ is negative) indicates that the effect of corresponding covariate on the probability of survival increases as time goes on. Therefore, if a covariate and its time interaction term have hazard ratios greater than 1 and less than 1, respectively, the negative effect of the covariate on the survival at time zero becomes diluted with time. Conversely, if a covariate and its interaction term have hazard ratios less than 1 and greater than 1, respectively, the positive effect of the covariate on the survival at time zero becomes diluted with time and possibly turns to negative after a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient time.

The hazard ratio of 0.50 indicates that the lifespans of UCLCs of which BJs use message boards are twice longer than the UCLCs that the BJs do not use their message boards. The use of message boards also had a contradicting time-interaction effect, but the effect was not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at pb0.05. These results clearly suggest that the content contributor's commitment and positive feedback can help the UCLC thread survive longer. However, these content contributor oriented motivational factors may lose their thrust as time goes on. Especially, fast rank improvements may exert a negative effect as the UCLC thread passes its peak in ranking. We interpret this phenomenon in a couple of ways. First, because there is limited room for rank improvement, all threads with increasing rank will experience decreasing speed of the rank improvements followed by a downhill ride in the rank. People tend to apply different baselines when they evaluate options for positive and negative outcomes [49]. Therefore, when a UCLC rank rises from 50 to #40 in a week, the BJ may expect to be ranked at #30 in the next week. If it is ranked at #35, the BJ may perceive the decrease in ranking from 10 rank/week to 5 rank/week as a loss. Similarly, if the rank falls back to #40 the next week, the BJ may frame it as a ranking loss of 10 from the previous week rather than a ranking gain of 10 from #50 three weeks ago. This inevitable disappointment could cause the negative time interaction effect on the survival of UCLC threads. Another possible explanation is the burnout [26] effect. Some hobbyist BJs may be excited by the fast increase of their ranks and put excessive effort into their content. Such over commitment may result in con<sup>fl</sup>ict with other personal needs and withdrawal of their efforts. In any case, it seems that motivational factors to encourage BJ's content contribution have a limit to their effect.

## 5. Conclusions and discussions

This paper introduces some idiosyncratic characteristics of the Web 2.0 service environment and builds a theoretical foundation to predict, and to some extent explain, the success of Web 2.0 services based on previous research in various disciplines. The study also applies to the theoretical understanding of a real Web 2.0 service context (i.e., Afreeca live CCS) and examines six Web 2.0 success factors using a survival analysis technique (i.e., Cox proportional hazards model). The data analysis results con<sup>fi</sup>rmed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects of all six factors on the lifespan of UCLC threads. Interestingly, the results suggest that some of the effects change over time while others are consistent throughout the lifespan.

This research bears two important academic implications. First, this study <sup>fi</sup>lls the gap in the online business/service research by integrating the perspectives of three major stakeholder groups in the Web 2.0 service environment. Previous studies in the motivation, media studies, and e-commerce areas have provided theoretical and/or data-driven understanding of related topics such as employee (or volunteer) performance and turnover, popularity of pop art products, and online shopping behavior. However, this research is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst that combines multiple research streams to explain the phenomena in the radically new Web 2.0 service context.

The second important implication of this study is the <sup>fi</sup>nding of time varying effects of Web 2.0 service success factors. Our results suggest that the effects of a Web 2.0 success factor can be strengthened, weakened, or even overturned as time goes on throughout the lifespan of UCC. This <sup>fi</sup>nding can explain why some potential determinants/predictors may show inconsistent effects across studies or contexts in Web 2.0 environments. In addition, this paper demonstrates a process and set of guidelines to apply to the proposed theoretical foundation of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context (e.g., Afreeca live CCS). The application of theory via a hazard model testing rendered empirical support to our theoretical understanding of Web 2.0, while the test results offered baseline numbers to compare similar Web 2.0 service platform providers (i.e., live CCS). Thus, this research contributes to both practical and managerial implications.

The current research proposes, based on the aforementioned theories, that survival of a UCC in a Web 2.0 service environment can be affected by 1) intrinsic motivations of the content providers reinforced by their behavioral commitments and high-performance cycle, 2) general acceptance by casual content consumers as well as repeated consumers, 3) popularity signaling mechanisms of the platform providers that increases the perceived popularity among content users, and 4) social network capabilities of the platform provider which encourages viral promotion, peer-support and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation among likeminded UCC contributors. These four sources of in<sup>fl</sup>uences re<sup>fl</sup>ect both the supply and demand sides of Web 2.0 success, while taking the three major stakeholders (i.e., content providers, content consumers, and service platform providers) into consideration.

Our survival analysis results supported the theoretical foundation developed in this study. When we examined the hazard ratios of six variables mapped to the supply demand and three stakeholder oriented factors in a live CCS context (see Table 2 context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factor mapping), all six variables demonstrated signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on the survivability of UCLC threads. More importantly, the results indicate that the effects of these factors can change over time. Rank improvement rate exerts positive effects on the survivability of UCLC in the early stage of the UCLC lifecycle (i.e., active threads). However, a rapid rank improvement becomes a hazard to the UCLC thread after a certain period. A similar pattern was observed in the chart appearance effect. Appearance in a popularity chart has a very strong positive effect on the survivability of UCLC threads, but that effect fades away and eventually turns to negative. On the other hand, club membership appears as a negative factor which was contrary to our expectation in the beginning; however it turns out to be a positive factor after several days. Other factors such as message board use, registered favorites, and viewer size show consistent positive effects on survival throughout the lifespan of UCLC threads.

Another signi<sup>fi</sup>cant yet practical result of this research comes from our adoption of survivability (i.e., lifespan) of active UCLC as the criterion variable. There can be many dimensions of Web 2.0 business success. Survivability, a rarely accounted concept in online service research, was operationalized based on practical needs and its managerial relevance was reaf<sup>fi</sup>rmed in a post study interview with Afreeca managers. A Web 2.0 business can generate revenue directly from the interactions among its users. For example, a live CCS platform provider can sell cyber gifts that UCLC consumers can buy for their favorite BJs. This kind of contributor–consumer tie requires time to build but once established, both parties are likely to continue the relationship, resulting in a long stream of revenue for the platform provider. It is needless to say that such mutually captivated users present many other <sup>fi</sup>nancial and strategic opportunities (e.g., reach for online ads, network externality) to the platform provider. Afreeca managers emphasized that establishing long lasting fan communities around UCLC providers (i.e., BJs) has been their business priority, as fans discourage BJs' defection. They also suggested that enthusiastic viewers bring in a disproportionately high pro<sup>fi</sup>t, while regular and seasoned BJs can nurture such enthusiasm much better than sporadic broadcasters can. Our operationalization of the dependent variable takes into account the persistence of BJ contribution and the existence of the viewer base. Therefore, our survivability concept is a good indicator of community establishment that can also be used to estimate the potential value of each UCLC thread.

Web 2.0 businesses can use the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the current study to review and improve their platform design. The design and algorithm of popularity charts must be thoroughly tested to balance its positive (e.g., high performance cycle) and negative (e.g., burnout) effects. For example, Afreeca could award an honorary title (e.g., hall of fame, Golden BJ) to high-performing BJs and exclude them from the rank charts after certain on-chart period, which will offer a long lasting sense of pride without putting endless pressure on BJs to keep a high ranking. Web 2.0 businesses that rely on value added UCC must pay attention to contributor support/development functions, such as a mentor network or tech support group, so new contributors can quickly learn and start to generate attractive UCCs. On the other hand, an effective community building mechanism should be provided to active UCC contributors. Such a mechanism can also leverage some social network functions. For example, fan registration, shared status, group/personal messaging, and event organizer functions will help UCC contributors develop a community of their content consumers. A <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentive system for UCC contributors (e.g., virtual gift with monetary value) will facilitate the community building effort, while the platform provider can make a pro<sup>fi</sup>t from the exchange of cyber goods among the users. As with many other types of online services, search and recommendation systems play a critical role in the success of individual UCLC providers as well as the platform service provider. One strategy that a live CCS platform provider may consider is to recommend a group of potential stars, like a venture capital, rather than established top performers. A platform provider can identify a group of UCLC providers (e.g., BJs) who have shown strong commitments (e.g., persistent and frequent UCLC contribution and interactions with their UCLC consumers, etc.) but do not have a stable fan community yet. Then, the platform provider can use a recommendation system to direct drifting (i.e., undetermined) UCLC consumers to the potential starts in a revolving manner. An optimization algorithm may be implemented to maximize the number of recommended UCLCs, each of which achieves a minimum number of UCLC consumers that can satisfy the UCLC provider. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the perceived quality of UCLC content can be extremely subjective and personal, and that a discussion of matching algorithms for search and recommendation systems requires a whole new stream of research. The next section discusses other limitations of this study.

## 5.1. Limitations and future research

While this study offers <sup>fi</sup>ndings with strong empirical support that can serve as a stepping stone for future Web 2.0 research, our theoretical framework and the novel approach to test the propositions leave some room for improvement, as all exploratory studies do. One issue that needs to be further articulated is the de<sup>fi</sup>nition and operationalization of Web 2.0 success. We used the lifespan of active UCLC threads as a precursor to Web 2.0 success. Nevertheless, as stated earlier, there can be many other equally good measures to represent the success, depending on how you de<sup>fi</sup>ne the concept of success in the Web 2.0 environment. Therefore, we expect to see a follow-up study that investigates multiple key indicators of Web 2.0 success and their inter-relationships, as DeLone and McLean did for general/organizational IS success [18,42].

We tried to develop a theoretical framework and propositions that can be applied to a wide range of Web 2.0 services. While this highlevel, inclusive perspective conferred a higher level of generalizability to our theory, it also imposed a limitation, especially on the practical implication side. Since the theoretical propositions use abstract concepts (e.g., intrinsic motivation, behavioral commitment, social network supports), researchers who want to develop testable hypotheses based on our propositions need to interpret the concepts in the propositions through their contextual lenses and construct measurable variables. We demonstrated this process using live CCS (i.e., Afreeca) data in this paper in order to provide guidance to future research, however follow-up studies that develop context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c measures are highly anticipated.

In terms of our empirical analysis, we can envisage several alternatives that could complement our study. We used objective serverlog data collected, transformed, and reprocessed over an extended period of time. This type of server log data is very precious and hard to collect unless the researcher has a strong support from a Web 2.0 service platform provider. We were very fortunate to have such a supporter who also guaranteed autonomy and independence of the researchers. Nevertheless, some factors could be better measured using a multi-trait/multi-method (MTMM) technique [10]. A user survey coupled with server log data will be particularly bene<sup>fi</sup>- cial for more reliable measures of the psychological/sociological variables such as intrinsic motivation, commitment, community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and peer-support. As computing power grows and new techniques of handling dynamic and large data evolve, more detailed data will become available to better estimate the theoretical factors. For example, we measured BJ's message board use instead of live chat volume to approximate the BJ's commitment. This substitution was in part due to the prohibitive system load that live chat tracking would generate on the Afreeca server. With the rapid developments in big data analysis, such rich data will shed additional light on the nature of interaction between UCLC providers and consumers. Different analysis techniques should also be utilized for new measures in the future. This study adopted Cox proportional hazards model, which is a non-parametric technique for survival analysis. Thus, a future study may employ a parametric survival model. If more reliable multi-item measures are developed, a structural equation modeling can be a good technique to better explain the causal relationships between behavioral variables.

It is also needless to say that more replication studies are needed in different domain/service contexts to clarify the boundary condition for our propositions and empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. In particular, comparison studies with on-demand CCS platforms (e.g., YouTube) or more conventional Web 2.0 services (e.g., image sharing, online forums, free/shareware) will shed light on critical common success factors as well as the distinct needs of different types of Web 2.0 services. We hope our study offers a good starting point for many new exciting research projects for online services as new environments evolve.
