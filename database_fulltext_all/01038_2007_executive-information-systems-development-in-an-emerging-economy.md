# Executive information systems development in an emerging economy

David Arnott <sup>a,\*</sup>, Waraporn Jirachiefpattana <sup>b</sup>, Peter O’Donnell <sup>a</sup>

Decision Support Systems Laboratory, Monash University, Melbourne, Australia

School of Applied Statistics, National Institute of Development Administration, Bangkok, Thailand

Available online 15 December 2004

## Abstract

This paper addresses executive information systems (EIS) development in an emerging economy. In particular, it examines EIS development in Thailand, a nation that is more representative of the majority of emerging economies in the South East Asian region than the four Asian Tigers (Singapore, Hong Kong, Taiwan and South Korea). Case studies of the development of four systems in large Thai organizations are presented. The analysis of the cases and their comparison to a benchmark study gives rise to the concept of EIS cultural fit, a concept that adds to our understanding of the reasons for the success and failure of EIS projects in emerging economies. The cases also raise questions about using outsourcing as a development strategy for EIS in emerging economies.

Keywords: Executive information systems; Developing country; Systems development

## 1. Introduction

Executive information systems (EIS) support the work of senior management by providing rapid access to critical information. Most of the studies of EIS development have been in large organizations in Western industrialized countries. Many developing countries are investing heavily in information technology and, in particular, the emerging economies of

South East Asia depend upon information technology (IT) to support their market position in an extremely competitive commercial hemisphere. It follows that many large organizations in emerging economies will have implemented, or are considering the development of EIS to support their senior executives. This paper addresses EIS development in Thailand. Thailand has a population of 64 million with a per-capita income of US\$2,200 compared to the USA with US\$36,300 per-capita income. A concern faced by Thai organizations that implement EIS is the possible challenge to their management culture from a technology that was designed for, and has matured in, Western organizations.

The next section of the paper presents four EIS case studies. The case descriptions address the social and cultural context of the projects and the systems development methods that were used. The third section of the paper analyzes the cases using Poon and Wagner’s critical success factor (CSF) model [9]. The analysis suggests that cultural fit should be added to their three meta-success factors of championship, resources and link with organizational objectives.

## 2. Four cases of EIS development

The cases all involve EIS development in large Thai organizations. Four organizations that had used EIS for at least one year were identified and approached; all agreed to take part in the research. We believe that the four cases are representative of EIS development in large Thai organizations. The identity of the cases has been disguised and they have been titled the <sup>b</sup>transport company<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>bank A<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>bank B<sup>Q</sup> and the <sup>b</sup>energy company<sup>Q</sup>.

Customized questionnaires were used to gather data from EIS developers, EIS coordinators and executive users. These instruments have been used to study EIS development in Western organizations [12]. The questionnaire for each group was distributed to the subjects by the EIS coordinator in each organization. The executive users were asked about the success of the EIS, while the developers were questioned about the development process. The questions for the coordina tor focused on system initiation as well as the general nature of the development of the EIS. In most cases, the coordinator was also a developer. All relevant EIS coordinators and developers participated in the study. Once the questionnaires were returned, the responses were analyzed and detailed follow-up questions were prepared. These questions were addressed in structured interviews. Two organizations did not return the questionnaires by the due date and the subjects were personally interviewed using the questionnaires. Pub lished information about the organizations (e.g. annual reports) and system documentation was also consulted. Only two executives (from different organizations) answered the executive questionnaires. This might reflect a cultural bias where managers, no matter how senior, are uncomfortable with criticizing the initiatives of their organizations [13]. Managers did provide anecdotal comments on system performance in each case. Success was gauged by the level of use, the technical performance of the systems, the degree to which they met their project objectives, and the perceived satisfaction of the sponsors and managerial users. The transport company case was successful, the energy company case a moderate or qualified success, bank A a qualified failure and bank B a failure. Table 1 provides an overview of the cases.

## 2.1. The social and cultural context of EIS development

The social and cultural context of EIS implementation in Thailand is radically different to that in the West, especially in organizations dominated by the Chinese. Martinsons and Westwood [7] provide an analysis of the effect of Chinese business culture on management information systems (MIS). They argue that, <sup>b</sup>MIS stems largely from the cultural values and attitudes that are associated with Western (and particularly Anglo–American) philosophical beliefs<sup>Q</sup> (p. 216). In typical Western organizations, job positions have generic descriptions that are used to frame the selection of workers and then guide them in their activities once they have attained a position. Chinese organizations, however, are typified by personalism where the person largely defines a role or position. This is a consequence of the Confucian value system where the family is the principal economic group [3]. Within and between families, there are complex networks of personal relationships that drive economic activity. In Chinese organizations, management is on the one hand informal and implicit and on the other hand hierarchical in respect of kinship relationships. Paternalism is a key element of Confucian management style with the father figure regarded as the source of legitimate power [10]. The information that flows through a Chinese business is therefore different in nature and transmission than the West. Chinese managers tend to keep information close to their in-group. Information is passed through the informal networks to promote family harmony. In contrast to the Western ideal of the rational or scientific decision maker, Chinese managers prefer to dissolve a decision situation rather than actively <sup>b</sup>make<sup>Q</sup> a decision.

First, many of the managers felt that it was <sup>b</sup>beneath<sup>Q</sup> them to be personally involved in EIS development, even though they were the ultimate users of the systems. A consequence of this cultural value is that the developers of the EIS may never be able to effectively identify the information requirements for the system. The second adverse effect is a cultural resistance to the drill-down feature of EIS. Drill-down means that an executive can disaggregate data to discover what is contributing to a particular high-level figure. This common feature of an EIS can offend Thai attitudes to hierarchy and harmony. The Confucian management style was prominent in all of the management systems of the cases. It was weakest in the transport company and strongest in the two banks.

Unlike other South East Asian countries that use English as an official language, Thai is the only official language in Thailand. Only highly educated Thai read and understand English and virtually all corporate and government data is recorded in the Thai language. The only exceptions are data collected for international organizations, where a company has a significant foreign shareholding, or performs most of its work outside Thailand. The transport company is the only such organization in this study and English was the language of management. This dependence on the Thai language could be a major obstacle for EIS development as all major EIS software is English-based. Bank

A and the energy company used non-EIS software that was able to manage Thai characters and grammar.

## 2.2. EIS systems development

The cases exhibit major differences in the strategies adopted for the development of EIS (see Table 2). Three distinct development strategies were used: inhouse evolutionary, in-house traditional systems development life cycle (SDLC) and outsourcing.

## 2.2.1. In-house evolutionary development

The transport company is the only case that clearly applied an evolutionary approach to EIS development. The initial system involved management information about traffic, production, finance, marketing, competitive position and customer service quality. It was a vehicle for EIS developers to understand the way that the executives managed the business. At the same time, the executives learned about the nature and possibilities of EIS. The development process of this company involved the iterative non-linear performance of the following processes: planning and research, requirements analysis and logical design, physical design, construction and testing, implementation and maintenance. The operation or application of these phases was not isolated but overlapped in time and occurred in different sequences. For some tasks, the development phases were repeated several times and the cycles only slowed when the task was relatively well understood. The major forces behind the general iterative process were the difficulty of identifying the requirements of executives and a lack of knowledge about EIS. The transport company’s EIS project is similar to those in Western organizations in terms of development philosophy, methods and user participation [4,11,12]. Its success confirms the applicability of evolutionary development to EIS projects.

## 2.2.2. A traditional information systems approach

Bank A employed a traditional waterfall development approach to direct and manage the EIS project [2]. The in-house development process involved a largely linear execution of requirements analysis, system development, user acceptance, system installation, use and maintenance. The low usage rate of this system is, in large part, due to an inflexible technical architecture. The combination of a traditional SDLC method and tools and applications designed for operational transaction processing systems means that there are no information systems procedures to enable the EIS to be easily modified with changing executive requirements.

## 2.2.3. Outsourcing

Outsourcing is a term that covers a wide range of contractual arrangements ranging from contract programmers to third party facilities management [6]. The development of EIS in bank B and the energy company were to some extent outsourced. However, the activities that were outsourced and the rationale behind the adoption of outsourcing differed between the two cases. In bank B, a major US-based consulting company was contracted to provide requirements identification, general system design, system architecture design and construction. The formal reason for employing the consultant was that the internal staff lacked experience in the conceptual design of data presentation for executives. Even though the bank has strongly invested in IT, the installed systems were focused on the operational services rather than management support. It is surprising that such a large bank did not recruit IT staff with specialist management support skills. After interviewing the EIS Coordinator, it became clear that it was actually internal political conflict within the organization that was the major reason for the outsourcing decision. A proprietary development methodology supplied by the consultant was used for the EIS development. Although the methodology used terms like prototyping and module delivery, it can best be termed a variant of the waterfall model. This reflects its development from the consulting firm’s large-scale operational systems methodology and also reflects the one-shot nature of many consulting engagements.

In the energy company, outsourcing is the standard development policy of the IT department. The department believes that this is the way to increase its productivity with limited personnel. Rather than allocating most professional staff to one major development project at a time, the department allocates professional IT staff to manage and coordinate several projects with different vendors. However, the department has determined that requirements analysis and system standards are the responsibility of its own staff. The EIS development approach of the Energy Company is best classified as linear SDLC development, rather than evolutionary, even though the EIS is approaching its third version. Each version has been a completely different system with different technical and application environments. The internal IT staff prepared the formal requirements, while database design and construction were performed by a contractor. The IT staff were not able to interview many executives and the CIO reported that <sup>b</sup>the executives could not sacrifice their time<sup>Q</sup>. This lack of direct contact between developers and the executive users is not confined to developing countries. Nandhakumar [8] reported a similar situation in the European business of a large multinational corporation.

The experiences of the energy company and bank B call into question the use of outsourcing for EIS development. Ketler and Walstrom [5] advise against the outsourcing of decision support systems (DSS) and EIS for strategic reasons. Alpar and Saharia [1] argue that information systems functions that are stable and routine are the best outsourcing candidates. EIS need frequent revision to accommodate changes in the organization’s environment and the wishes of individual executives. In both cases, system evolution has been seriously constrained by the absence of the primary developer after the initial development. In both cases there has been no transfer of skills and knowledge to the host organization. For outsourcing to be effective for EIS, it is essential that client IT personnel are trained and motivated to provide on-going support and system development.

## 3. Critical success factors for EIS development

Poon and Wagner [9] identified 10 CSF that successful projects mostly achieve. They also hypothesized that there are a small number of meta-success factors that <sup>b</sup>if managed correctly, result in all others to go right as well<sup>Q</sup> [9] (p. 406). The meta-success factors they identified are championship (a combination of executive and operating sponsorship), availability of resources and link with organizational objectives.

Table 3 shows an analysis of the Poon and Wagner CSFs in the four Thai cases. Examining the metasuccess factor scoring shows that the projects in the transport company, bank A and bank B should be successful, and the energy company project should be unsuccessful. These predictions are only 25% correct. The transport company system was successful (correct prediction), both bank systems were unsuccessful (incorrect predictions), and the energy company project was successful (incorrect prediction). The meta-success factors are therefore not convincing predictors of EIS development success in these four Thai projects.

We believe that the reason for this predictive failure is found in Poon and Wagner’s discussion of the reason for the failure of two projects in their study. They related that <sup>b</sup>. . .interestingly, they represented the only two organizations with a Chinese management system in place<sup>Q</sup> (p. 406). Poon and Wagner acknowledged that this difference in management systems was a separate factor to those in their CSF set. They believed that the mismatch of the information system with the organization’s management system led to the executives wanting the EIS projects to fail. This mismatch is actually a component of a larger concept that can explain Poon and Wagner’s finding and also explain what happened in the Thai cases. We call this concept <sup>b</sup>EIS cultural fit<sup>Q</sup>.

EIS cultural fit is the degree of similarity of both the organization’s social and cultural context and its IT policies and methods with that of the large western organizations where the concepts that underlie EIS were originally created. Table 4 shows an assessment of the degree of EIS cultural fit in the Thai cases. A high cultural fit indicates that this aspect of the project was very close to that experienced in EIS projects in large Western organizations. A low cultural fit indicates that there is little similarity between the Thai case and an archetypal Western case. In our study the transport company has the highest degree of EIS cultural fit. It was the most successful project and was the only case where the meta-success factors correctly predicted the system outcome. Both banks have low EIS cultural fit scores and both had unsuccessful projects despite meeting all meta-success factors. The energy company has a medium rating for EIS cultural fit and was a qualified success.

The analysis of the Thai cases suggests that Poon and Wagner’s meta-success factors are only useful predictors when EIS cultural fit is high. The most successful systems are likely to meet all meta-success factors and score highly on cultural fit. Where there is a conflict between the EIS cultural fit measure and the meta-success factors measures the Thai case studies show that the forces behind EIS cultural fit overwhelm the processes behind the meta-success factors.

## 4. Conclusion

This paper has addressed the development of EIS in large Thai organizations. Two important findings emerge from the analysis of the four cases. First, for EIS outsourcing to be effective it is essential that client IT personnel are trained and motivated to provide on-going support and system development. Special care should be exercised when engaging foreign, particularly Western, outsourcing consultants. Second, when assessing EIS in emerging economies a fifth meta-success factor, EIS cultural fit, should be added to the well-accepted Poon and Wagner critical success factor set.
