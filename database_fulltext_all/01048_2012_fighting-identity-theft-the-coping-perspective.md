## a b s t r a c t

The proliferation of the online business transaction has led to a large number of incidents of identity theft, which have incurred expensive costs to consumers and e-commerce industries. Fighting identity theft is important for both online business and consumers. Although the practical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of <sup>fi</sup>ghting identity theft has been of great interest, empirical studies on identity theft are very limited. Drawing upon coping behavior theories, this study examines two types of coping behaviors to <sup>fi</sup>ght identity theft (i.e., conventional coping and technological coping). Following structural equation modeling approach, we test the model using data collected from 117 subjects through a survey. The results reveal that both conventional coping and technological coping are effective to defend against identity theft. Technological coping is determined by an individual's conventional coping, self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, perceived effectiveness of coping, and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence. This study presents a timely empirical study on identity theft, and provides valuable insights for consumers, government agencies, and ecommerce industries.

## 1. Introduction

The proliferation of the online business transaction has led to a large number of incidents of identity theft. Personal identity-related theft was “at a record high” [42] and happened every 3.5 s on average [81]. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) reported 8 million identity theft victims in 2006 [31] and a similar <sup>fi</sup>nding of 8.1 million victims was found in 2007 [67]. The number of identity theft victims climbed to 9.9 million in 2008, with an estimated total cost of \$48 billion to consumers [17,93]. Another survey found 11.1 million victims with \$54 billion cost in 2009 [93]. To date, electronic data breaches account for 80.7% of identity theft events and paper breaches account for 19.3% (http://www.idtheftcenter.org/).

The impact of identity theft is evident because it occurs in any industry such as general business, educational institutions, government/military, healthcare, and banking/credit/<sup>fi</sup>nancial services. Berghel [8] pointed out that “identity theft can destroy personal credit and potentially lead to very expensive litigation that may take years, or perhaps decades, to fully correct” (p. 19). Rusin [81] and Mercuri [63] reported that identityrelated thefts cost more than \$50 billion annually, or \$6000 per victim. Identity thefts may severely hinder online transactions of e-commerce. For example, census data has shown more than three-fourths of Internet users are concerned about the security of their online private information [6]. 66% of adults in the U.S. reported that identify theft was one of their major concerns in the daily life [82]. 75% of Internet users bought fewer items online because of security concerns [41].

Although identity thefts can originate from illegal and immoral organizational practices [12,16,48], consumers' personal naiveté about their identity and privacy is usually the main source [52,76]. FTC has recommended various practices for consumers such as increased guardianship of personal identi<sup>fi</sup>ers. However, it is not clear what factors may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the consumers' use of these practices. Academic research on identity theft is still in its infancy [4]. Very little empirical and behavioral work on this subject has been conducted [4,69,84,86,88]. To <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap, our study aims at proposing and testing a theoretical model to explore the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers to adopt various identity protection practices. We also study how these practices reduce occurrences of identity theft. Different from previous technology adoption and acceptance studies in the literature, our study introduces a research model based on coping behavior theories [55,60,80,91].

Our study is organized as follows. We present the background of this study and literature review. We then describe the research model and develop research hypotheses. Next we report an empirical study to test the model, followed by data analysis and results. We conclude by discussing the major <sup>fi</sup>ndings and implications for research and practice.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Definition of identity theft

The Identity Theft Resource Center (ITRC) de<sup>fi</sup>nes identity theft as “a crime in which an impostor obtains key pieces of personal identifying information (PII) such as Social Security numbers and driver's license numbers and uses them for their own personal gain” (http:// www.idtheftcenter.org/). FTC de<sup>fi</sup>nes identity theft as “identity theft occurs when someone uses your personally identifying information, like your name, Social Security number, or credit card number, without your permission, to commit fraud or other crimes” ([33], p. 6). Organisations for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) de<sup>fi</sup>ned identity theft as “ID theft occurs when a party acquires, transfers, possesses, or uses personal information of a natural or legal person in an unauthorized manner, with the intent to commit, or in connection with, fraud or other crimes” ([72], p. 3).

According to ITRC, individual identity theft includes three categories [78]:

(1) Financial identity theft: the thief may impersonate the victim to get a loan from a <sup>fi</sup>nancial institution, take over the victim's bank accounts, pass bad checks, etc.

(2) Criminal Identity Theft: for example, a criminal identi<sup>fi</sup>es himself to authorities as another individual in order to commit a crime, get special permits, commit acts of terrorism, and so on.

(3) Identity Cloning: the thief uses another's information to assume his or her identity in daily life, for such purposes as opening a new phone or wireless account and getting utility services.

Javelin Strategy and Research's 2009 report [68] revealed that stolen wallets and physical paperwork accounted for 43% of all identity theft; 38% of identity theft victims had their debit or credit card number stolen; 37% of identity theft victims had their Social Security number; 24% of identity theft victims had their <sup>fi</sup>nancial account numbers compromised; and 59% of new account fraud that occurred in 2008 involved opening up a new credit card and store-branded credit card accounts. FTC's reported that “Credit card fraud (20%) was the most common form of reported identity theft followed by government documents/ bene<sup>fi</sup>ts fraud (15%)” and “fraudulent tax return-related identity theft, a subtype of government documents/bene<sup>fi</sup>ts fraud, has increased nearly six percentage points since calendar year 2006” ([32], p. 3).

Identity theft can happen both of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online [72]. Identity thieves can get hold of an individual's identity using conventional methods (of<sup>fl</sup>ine) and advanced technological methods (online) [72]. Conventional methods include rummaging through rubbish for personal information (dumpster diving), skimming information from bank or credit cards, stealing mail/wallet/purse, Nigerian letter, and so on [72]. Technological methods include spoo<sup>fi</sup>ng/phishing, hacking and malware (e.g., spyware, Trojan, worm) [9,23,61,72]. The FBI has referred to phishing as “the hottest, and most troubling, new scam on the Internet … contributing to a rise in identity theft …” [28]. Through bogus e-mails, the phishermen attempt to make victims believe that they are receiving e-mails from trusted sources and to direct the victims to visit bogus web sites, which look like legitimate web sites of banks and other <sup>fi</sup>nancial service institutions (e.g., PayPal) [9,23]. On the bogus web sites, the phishermen may harvest the victims' personal and/or <sup>fi</sup>nancial information, thus enabling them to hijack the victims' accounts to commit <sup>fi</sup>nancial frauds or other forms of identity theft [11].

## 2.2. Related studies

The increasing reliance on information technologies also leads to mounting attacks and increase in the number of security incidents and damage [70], identity theft in particular. Most information security studies were subjective–argumentative (i.e., mathematics) [86], like encryption algorithms. However, encryption techniques are often not utilized due to a lack of awareness or expertise [24,40]. In contrast, there is little research from a behavioral and managerial perspective to examine behavioral, organizational, and social issues [21,86,88]. Ng et al. [70] stated that “there are relative few research studies of security behaviors of computer users and how behaviors can be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to practice security countermeasures” (p. 816).

Among the behavioral studies of information security, Chan et al. [13] examined the effects of social contextual factors on employees compliance with organizational security policies. Li et al. [59] examined how employees' intention to comply with Internet use policy was driven by cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t assessments, personal norms and organizational context factors. Several studies examined technical countermeasures of information security (e.g., anti-spyware and anti-phishing tools). For example, Gurung et al. [38] analyzed the factors that motivate the consumer to adopt and use anti-spyware tools. Dinev [23] and Kruck and Kruck [50] technically demonstrated how spoo<sup>fi</sup>ng works and suggested several ways to avoid spoo<sup>fi</sup>ng. Berghel [9] described how phishing is performed. Lynch [61] reviewed phishing technologies and presented a framework for phishing crime control. Teer et al. [92] empirically examined the security practices (e.g., anti-virus programs, <sup>fi</sup>rewalls, and patching) performed by undergraduate university students. Ng et al. [70] investigated the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing computer security behaviors (i.e., exercising care when reading e-mails with attachments) in a working environment.

However, this stream of research is still limited. Gurung et al. [38] stated that “yet little research has been done to scienti<sup>fi</sup>cally investigate the motivation for consumers to use anti-spyware tools and those factors that lead to the adoption of anti-spyware tools for consumers” (p. 478). Ng et al. [70] called for “more research studying the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence individual's decision on practice computer security” (p. 824). The research on the consequences of these countermeasures is also rare. Newman and McNally [69] stated that “there is very little formal research on identity theft per se” (p. 1).

Our literature review also revealed that there are few studies on identity theft. Eisenstein [26] used a system dynamics model to explore policy options dealing with identify theft. Kahn [46] attempted to model identity and its use in credit transactions to study the equilibrium incidence of identity theft. Identity theft has not been examined from the behavioral perspective, except for Anderson [4] and Milne et al. [65]. Using the FTC's 2003 identity theft survey data, Anderson [4] described the relationship between demographic characteristics and the likelihood of identity theft. Milne et al. [65] reported consumer's adoption of suggested risk-avoidance practices.

Identity theft can occur not only due to computer-related threats (e.g., phishing, spoo<sup>fi</sup>ng, and viruses) but also due to consumers' conventional behaviors (e.g., not shredding credit card/bank statements and releasing social security number without cautions) [33]. It is important for individuals to <sup>fi</sup>ght identity theft using both technological and conventional behavioral countermeasures. However, the extant research is primarily on technological countermeasures. To complement the works of previous studies, we rely on coping theories to develop an integrative research model which includes both technological and conventional countermeasures.

## 2.3. Coping theories

In the psychology literature, there are two different views of coping — the process view vs. the style or personality view. The process view suggests that coping is a process of managing dif<sup>fi</sup>cult circumstances where an individual is faced with fear, stress, and threat [55]. As Lazarus [55] stated, “from a process standpoint, coping is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ongoing cognitive and behavioral efforts to manage speci<sup>fi</sup>c external and/or internal demands that are apprised as taxing or exceeding the resources of the person” (p. 237). In contrast, the style or personality view is focused on an individual's personal style of managing and defending one's psychological integrity against fear, stress, and threat. The process perspective of coping behavior has advantages over the more limited and static view of the personality perspective. This is because the process view suggests that coping is a dynamic process “in accordance with the situational contexts in which it occurs” ([55], p. 235).

Coping theory from the process perspective suggests two major functions of coping, i.e., problem-focused and emotion-focused [55,60,80,91]. Emotion-focused coping deals with the strategies an individual undertakes, in order to change his feelings and emotions toward the threat and crisis, so that the individual becomes less sensitive to the threat. Problem-focused coping deals with the cause of the problem by taking action on individuals themselves, or on the environment, in order to change the maligned relationship between the individual and the environment. In this paper, we emphasize problem-focused coping because problem-focused coping has been consistently favored over emotion-focused coping in the literature. Lazarus [55] concluded that “taking action against problems rather than reappraising the relational meaning seems more desirable” (p. 238). Further, we believe problem-focused coping is more relevant and effective for <sup>fi</sup>ghting identity theft than emotion-focused coping. Tangible actions and practices can be recommended if we have a better understanding of problem-focused coping.

Following Lazarus's seminal work on coping with stress [54], several theories and models have emerged in the literature from different disciplines. Protection motivation theory [79,80] was <sup>fi</sup>rst developed to explain fear appeals, and then extended to a general theory of persuasive communication. According to the theory, an individual is involved in two cognitive processes that appraise threat and coping simultaneously in a parallel manner, before reaching the intention (protection motivation) to protect oneself from threats. The individual considers four salient factors in the appraisal process: perceived severity of the threat; perceived probability of the occurrence of the threat; the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the recommended preventive behavior (perceived response ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy); and perceived self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. An individual considers the former two factors in the appraisal process of threat, while the latter two factors are used to appraise coping. Studies from various disciplines (e.g., Marketing [91], Psychology [47], and Information Systems [2,44,57]) have found support for this theory to explain different types of behaviors.

In an extension of the protection motivation theory, Tanner et al. [91] suggested an ordered or sequential appraisal process instead of the parallel process of threat and coping appraisal. In the extended model, threat appraisal occurs prior to coping appraisal. In addition, two important variables (emotion, and social norms and values) are introduced into the model. The concept of social norms and values brings in the social normative view to coping theory, which suggests that an individual's appraisal of threat and coping, the formation of protection motivation, and the choice of several coping behaviors cannot be separated from the social context where the coping process occurs.

In the area of technology threats, Liang and Xue [60] developed a technology threat avoidance theory based on the earlier coping literature, theories and models (see Fig. 1). In their technology threat avoidance theory, Liang and Xue [60] suggested that an individual's coping with a technology threat is also determined by threat appraisal and coping appraisal. In the alignment of the process view of coping with technology threats, Liang and Xue [60] developed a variance model to describe the factors contributing to an individual's appraisal of threat, coping, and coping behavior. According to the variance model, perception of threat in the threat appraisal process is determined by perceived susceptibility and perceived severity. This threat appraisal is similar to the factors contributing to the threat appraisal in the protection motivation theory [79,80]. On the other hand, the perception of avoidance in the coping appraisal process is determined by the perceived effectiveness of the safeguard measures, perceived costs, and an individual's self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, which are also similar to the coping appraisal factors in the protection motivation theory [79,80]. Liang and Xue's [60] theory is used as our overarching framework.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

The technology threat avoidance theory [60] outlines both threat appraisal and coping appraisal in the process of coping. Because there is a wide range of technology threats available in the context of identity theft, it is not feasible to investigate the effects of different threats on threat perception in the process of threat appraisal. Instead, we focus on the factors that contribute to the perception of avoidability in the coping appraisal process, rather than on the threat appraisal process. In addition, we investigate coping behavior instead of coping motivation. Actual behavior is the ultimate variable to be observed, although motivation or intention is an intermediate determinant of actual behavior [1]. In addition, the literature has suggested that there is a gap between intention and actual behavior (e.g. [1]). Finally, we focus on problem-focused coping rather than emotion-focused coping, as mentioned above. The research model is shown in Fig. 2.

In order to investigate what coping actions an individual takes in order to <sup>fi</sup>ght against identity theft, we identify two types of problemfocused coping behavior — technological and conventional. Technological coping indicates the action an individual takes (in the interactions with computers) when the individual is dependent on the information technologies. Ng et al. [70] stated that “it is clear that technology solution alone is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient [for information security]” (p. 824). Conventional coping means the behavior an individual performs (in a conventional context) to protect the individual's identity, regardless of whether the individual uses a technology or not.

Identity-related information can be stolen by using such traditional techniques vehicle break-ins, shoulder sur<sup>fi</sup>ng, home invasions, stolen wallets, as well as pretending/pretexting, skimming, and dumpster diving [30]. For these common and widely recognized techniques, consumers have exercised common practices (conventional coping strategies) to minimize occurrences of identity theft. Examples of such practices are shredding bank statements, monitoring credit history, and reviewing bank and credit card statements [29,30].

With computer technology advances, more techniques are emerging and are prevalent for today's identity theft incidents [64,65,85]. These techniques include computer viruses, phishing, scamming, and hacking [9,23,50,61,63]. Accordingly, consumers have generally exercised some technological coping strategies to avoid and reduce occurrences of identity theft. These technological coping strategies include updating/patching computer operational systems and using <sup>fi</sup>rewall and anti-virus software [92].

We expect that using these two types of coping can reduce occurrences of identity theft. This expectation leads to the hypotheses:

H1. The exercise of conventional coping can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce occurrences of identity theft.

H2. The exercise of technological coping can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce occurrences of identity theft.

In addition to the direct effect on identity theft, conventional coping may have an impact on technological coping. Thus, there may be an indirect effect of conventional coping on identity theft via technological coping. According to protection motivation theory [80] and the ordered protection motivation model [91], an individual may learn from observations and his or her own experience, and apply this learning in the formation of coping behaviors in the new context. In the context of <sup>fi</sup>ghting identity theft, prior knowledge and a past experience of dealing with identity threat, regardless of the technology environment, should lead to an individual's coping in the technology environment. If an individual has developed very effective conventional coping behaviors of protecting themselves in the traditional environment, the individual may be able to apply the principles and guidance of conventional coping behavior to the computer environment. Thus, an effective technological coping may emerge.

H3. The exercise of conventional coping is positively related to the exercise of technological coping

Self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is an individual's belief that the individual is capable of performing and executing the course of behavior to attain certain goals [7]. In coping theory, self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is an important factor to explain an individual's adoption of coping behavior to defend against threats [55,60,80,91]. According to coping theory, self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy indicates the level of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in one's ability to undertake a coping behavior to prevent oneself from threats [80]. Protection motivation theory proposes that individuals' self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is a major factor to predict intention to protect themselves against threat [80].

In the technology adoption literature, self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is also a precedent of an individual's belief about controlling adoption and use of computer technologies, according to social cognitive theory [7] and theory of planned behavior [1]. Abundant literature has shown that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy was positively related to a variety of behaviors related to IT adoption and use. For example, Hill, Smith, and Mann [39] found that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy positively related to the adoption of a wide range of technologically advanced products. Tan and Teo [90] reported that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy can facilitate the adoption of Internet banking. Gurung et al. [38] reported that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy could promote users to use anti-spyware. In Liang and Xue's [60] technology threat avoidance theory, self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is posited to in<sup>fl</sup>uence perceived avoidability, which in turn determines avoidance behavior. In the context of <sup>fi</sup>ghting identity theft, an individual who is con<sup>fi</sup>dent of having information security skills would be more inclined to exercise identity protection practices and relevant coping behavior because the individual is comfortable in exercising such practices. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H4. The greater the self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy toward computer security technologies, the more likely that technological coping will be exercised.

According to protection motivation theory [80], another salient factor in predicting the intention to cope with fear and threat is perceived response ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy — the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the recommended coping to respond to the threat. In the context of coping with IT threat, Liang and Xue [60] propose perceived effectiveness of the safeguards as a major antecedent of one's coping appraisal and coping behavior. In this study, we follow the de<sup>fi</sup>nition provided by Liang and Xue [60]; perceived effectiveness is therefore “an individual's subjective assessment that the safeguarding measure can effectively avoid the threat of malicious IT” (p. 81). This definition re<sup>fl</sup>ects an individual's overall judgment of the outcome of coping with IT threat. The concept of perceived effectiveness is also similar to perceived usefulness in the Technology Acceptance Model [18], which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which an individual believes that using a particular computer technology would enhance his performance of using the computer to <sup>fi</sup>nish a job. Perceived usefulness has been consistently shown to be a major determinant of user intention of adopting and using IT. Further, perceived effectiveness is also similar to performance expectancy in the Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology [94], where the authors develop the concept of performance expectancy based on such constructs as perceived usefulness, outcome expectations, and relative advantage.

H5. The greater the perceived effectiveness of computer security technologies, the more likely that technological coping will be exercised.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Measures

To enhance content validity, we designed the questionnaire based on a review of prior literature. Whenever possible, existing measures were adopted. The questionnaire was then reviewed by several researchers with expertise in information security and methodology. To further validate the measures, we conducted two rounds of pretests using respondents who were not included in the main sample. Several items were removed due to insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant loadings towards the designated constructs. The <sup>fi</sup>nal questionnaire is shown in Appendix A. All the items were anchored on a 7-point Likert scale.

Identity Theft: The respondents were asked how frequently they encountered identity-theft related incidents. The types of identity theft incidents were adapted from Swartz [89], including illegal use of credit cards and social security number. Conventional coping: The respondents were asked to indicate how frequently they shred bank statements, review bank statements, monitor credit records, etc. These items were compiled from various sources, such as Privacy Rights Clearinghouse [77], FTC [33], FBI [29], and Department of Justice [19,20] in regard to identity protection. Technological Coping: The respondents were asked to indicate the degree they technically protect their identity, such as using anti-virus and <sup>fi</sup>rewall tools and frequently updating computer operations system. These items were compiled from FTC [33], FBI [29], Privacy Rights Clearinghouse [77], and Teer et al. [92]. Self-Efficacy: The respondents were asked four questions to indicate the degree to which they perceived that they were con<sup>fi</sup>dent with security-related technology and issues. The four questions were adapted from Lassar, Manolis, and Lassar [53], Tan and Teo [90], and Venkatesh et al. [94]. Perceived Effectiveness: Measures were adapted from Dinev and Hu [25], and Srite and Karahanna [87]. The respondents were asked to indicate the degree to which they perceive the usefulness of computer-related tools for computer security. Social Influence: The respondents were asked three questions to indicate the degree to which they agreed that their social network (e.g., family, friends, and peers/colleagues) in<sup>fl</sup>uenced their practices of securing their identities. The measure was adapted from Venkatesh et al. [94].

The respondents were asked to indicate their intervals of age and income. These intervals were adapted from Anderson [4] because it was reported that people who are between the ages of 18 and 24 had less risk than those who are between the ages of 25 and 54, and people with income level of \$75,000 or higher had higher possibility of identity theft.

## 4.2. Sampling and data collection

The subjects were undergraduate students of a large 4-year public university in U.S. The subjects were sampled from juniors who were taking core business courses. The participants were non-traditional students who were also full-time employees with several years of working experience. A total of 155 questionnaires were distributed during classes. Individuals were informed that participation in the study was voluntary and that their responses would be kept con<sup>fi</sup>dential and would be analyzed only at the aggregate level. A total of 126 questionnaires were returned. Nine of the questionnaires were returned with substantial missing values and were consequently excluded from the data analysis. This resulted in a total of 117 usable responses (75.5%). The respondent pro<sup>fi</sup>le is presented in Table 1.

## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Partial Least Squares (PLS)

We used the Partial Least Squares (PLS) method in the data analysis. PLS is a causal modeling technique that analyzes a measurement model and a structural model simultaneously. PLS is widely adopted, along with standard regression and other structural equation modeling (SEM) techniques (such as LISREL and AMOS [36]). PLS also explicitly models measurement errors in observed variables, which is preferable to regression analysis which does not consider such errors. Although LISREL is useful for con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory studies with strong theoretical support, PLS is preferred as a tool for theory development because it is more concerned with the causal relationships between constructs [14]. Furthermore, PLS can handle formative, single-item, and summated scales [14,35], and does not require a large sample size or the assumption of the normal distribution of the source data [14]. PLS was deemed the most appropriate choice for our data analysis.

We used PLS-Graph 3.00 to assess the measurement model and structural model. A bootstrapping estimation procedure was used to examine the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the factor loadings or weights of the scales in the measurement model and the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients in the structural model [36].

Based on the measurements reported in Tables 2 and 3, we analyzed convergent validity, discriminant validity, and reliability for all re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs following the guidelines in the literature [35,36].

Unlike SEM, which provides goodness-of-<sup>fi</sup>t indices, PLS provides t-statistics for factor loadings. We assessed reliability in terms of item reliability and composite reliability. Item reliability was assessed by the factor loading of each item of the construct. It is widely accepted that items with loadings of 0.7 or higher have an adequate reliability [35]. As shown in Table 2, all of the factor loadings for re<sup>fl</sup>ective construct items were above the 0.7 threshold. In addition, the t-values of all of the loadings were higher than 1.96, which also supports item reliability [36]. Composite reliability (C.R.) is similar but superior to Cronbach's alpha, because it considers actual factor loadings rather than assuming an equal weight for each item [35]. As shown in Table 2, the C.R. values all are above the recommended value of 0.70 [71], suggesting an acceptable level of reliability.

For re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs, we further assessed convergent validity in terms of the average variance extracted (AVE). Convergent validity requires an average variance extracted of no less than 0.50 [35]. As shown in Table 2, all of the AVE values demonstrate adequate convergent validity.

Discriminant validity was assessed at both construct and item levels [15]. First, at the construct level, we compared the AVE of each construct with the shared variances between that construct and all of the other constructs. An AVE higher than the shared variances suggests discriminant validity [35]. Table 3 shows the inter-construct correlations on the diagonal of the matrix, indicating all of the correlations are less than the corresponding square roots of the AVEs on the diagonal, suggesting that adequate construct-level discriminant validity was achieved. Second, at the item level, we calculated crossing-loadings following the established procedure [36]. As shown in Table 4, each item has higher loading on its own construct than on other constructs, thus the item-level discriminant validity was achieved [15].

We also followed the established procedure to assess the formative construct (i.e., identity theft) [5,37]. We also checked the path weights. First, all weights were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at pb0.01 level. Second, the sign of all path weights were consistent with our theoretical expectation. Third, all path weights were greater than 0.1. In addition, we calculated variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF) for each indicator following Gefen and Straub's analytical procedure [36]. As shown in Table 2, VIF values ranged from 1.028 to 2.726, which were lower than the cutoff value of 3.3 suggested by [22]. Therefore, all indicators of this formative construct met the requirements of indicator validity and were thus considered appropriate.

As the correlation matrix (see Table 3) does not show any exceptionally correlated constructs, there is little evidence of common method variance (CMV), which usually gives rise to extremely high correlations of 0.9 (cf. [27,74]). The potential of CMV is therefore not a major concern in this study.

## 6. Discussions

The results reveal that both conventional coping (H1) and technological coping (H2) are effective in reducing occurrences of identity theft. Relatively speaking, technological coping has stronger direct impact on identity theft than conventional coping $( \beta = - 0 . 4 0 5 { \mathrm { ~ v s . ~ } } \beta = - 0 . 2 7 0 )$ However, it would be misleading to conclude that technological coping is more effective than conventional coping because conventional coping also has an indirect effect on identity theft through technological coping (H3). Calculating the total effect of conventional coping on identity theft $( - 0 . 2 7 0 + 0 . 3 2 7 * - 0 . 4 0 5 = - 0 . 4 0 2 )$ , we <sup>fi</sup>nd that conventional coping has a similar level of effect to that of technological coping (−0.405).

Our results do not imply that we should rely more on technological coping practices. As Berghel [9] pointed out, “perhaps our greatest mistake is excessive reliance on technology solutions” (p. 25). Lynch [61] argued that “we are all potential victims of identity theft, no matter how computer savvy we are and no matter how much we guard our personal information and credit card numbers” (p. 274). We suggest individuals pay attention to both technological coping and conventional coping in defending identity theft. In fact, conventional coping is also an important determinant of technological coping (H3) $( \beta = 0 . 3 2 7 )$ That is, the more an individual knows about how to cope with conventional issues of identity theft, the more likely the individual applies technological coping strategies. The development of an individual's capability of applying technological coping strategies cannot be separated from the individual's conventional capability of coping.

In addition, self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy also has a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on technological coping $( \beta = 0 . 3 9 6 , \ \ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that people with higher technical skills tend to have greater tendencies to apply technological coping to <sup>fi</sup>ght against identity theft. These individuals choose such methods as <sup>fi</sup>rewalls, anti-virus software, and system patching. The role of self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in coping has been well-established in previous coping research [80,91], which suggests that individuals with high positive self-evaluations see themselves as capable of dealing with stress and threats. The <sup>fi</sup>nding is also consistent with previous IT adoption literature (e.g., [73]).

Perceived effectiveness is also found to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on technological coping $( \beta = 0 . 3 5 9 )$ . This suggests that an individual's perception of the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the suggested solution to <sup>fi</sup>ght against identity theft was very important in an individual's coping appraisal and coping behavior, which is consistent with protection motivation theory [80,91] as well as with technology threat avoidance theory [60]. The <sup>fi</sup>nding is also consistent with the technology adoption/acceptance literature which suggests that perceived usefulness or performance expectancy increases the adoption/acceptance of information technologies [15,69].

The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence (β=0.255) suggests that social pressure may force individuals to approach technological coping as a strategy for securing their identities. The reasons for this impact are twofold. First, due to the prevalence of computer security software (e.g., anti-spam software, <sup>fi</sup>rewall, and anti-virus software), individuals may feel out-of-fashion if they are not familiar with these technologies. Therefore, they may be “forced” to practice new technologies in order to be considered “normal” in their social network. Second, social pressures may arise from the success of computer security software. Individuals may simply imitate practice, following perceived positive outcomes from technology [58,66]. Today's computer security software is, so far, both prevalent and successful (to a certain degree) in daily use. Individuals therefore may choose to exercise these technologies to protect their identities.

Although social in<sup>fl</sup>uence also has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on technological coping, the magnitude of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence is lower than that of self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and perceived effectiveness $( \beta = 0 . 2 5 5 \mathrm { v s . } \beta = 0 . 3 9 6$ and 0.359 respectively). This suggests that an individual's coping with identity threat is more a personal issue – relating to an individual's own abilities of coping and his or her assessment of the effectiveness of the safeguard practices – than a social issue, where the individual seeks social supports and/or receives in<sup>fl</sup>uence in his or her social networks. This is also consistent with the propositions suggested in previous coping theory (e.g. [91]).

## 7. Conclusions

## 7.1. Implications for research

First, our theoretical and empirical study of identity theft is a timely study. Different from studies based on existing technology adoption and acceptance theories and models, we present one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst research models based on the theoretical support of coping theories [55,60,80,91]. We believe that investigating identity theft from the coping approach is more relevant to issues under study than previous approaches, and therefore has brought some fresh ideas to the literature [40].

Second, we present one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical studies to demonstrate the effectiveness of the coping approach to explain how an individual deals with technology threats. While the coping approach has well-established support from other academic <sup>fi</sup>elds such as psychology, healthcare, organizational behavior, and consumer research (e.g. [47,55]), it is relatively new in the IS/IT <sup>fi</sup>eld, with exception of two recent empirical studies (i.e. [56,70]). Ng et al. [70] and Lee et al. [56] examined the antecedents of coping behaviors. Ng et al. [70] examined what factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence conventional coping behaviors in a “work [organizational] environment”. Lee et al. [56] investigated what factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence technological coping behavior, virus protection in particular, in a “non-work environment”. Our study extended both Ng et al. [70] and Lee et al. [56] by integrating both conventional and technological copings and both antecedents but also consequences of both copings.

Third, our model presents a progress of the general conceptualization of the technology threat avoidance theory [60]. We have delineated two types of coping strategies (technological and conventional) in the empirical study. To the best of our knowledge, conventional coping has not been examined speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in the literature. Previous research focused only on how to use technologies to <sup>fi</sup>ght identity theft. By introducing conventional coping, our study provides a more complete picture. Conventional coping is technology-independent and habitoriented, and thus has broader and deeper impacts on <sup>fi</sup>ghting identity theft. In addition, in pursuit of antecedent factors of technological coping, our research model provides a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed view of the impacts of an individual's own capability, experience, and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Our analyses have revealed that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, perceived effectiveness, and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence were key factors to foster technological coping.

## 7.2. Implications for practice

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have the following implications for practice. First, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of conventional coping on <sup>fi</sup>ghting identity theft is consistent with the coping literature [55,80,91]. This suggests that the traditional wisdom (i.e., conventional coping) is a key variable of <sup>fi</sup>ghting identity theft. Therefore, practitioners need to stress the important role of an individual consumer's learning and knowledge in the conventional context and that individual consumers can apply and transfer these knowledge to the electronic commerce context. Government agencies and mass media need to educate and promote the traditional wisdom. Good habit-oriented coping practice can complement technological coping practice.

Second, online business and other practitioners need to educate consumers to apply technological coping which is more important than conventional coping. In order to encourage technological coping, practitioners need to provide suf<sup>fi</sup>cient technical and computer trainings for individuals to develop stronger self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and computer related speci<sup>fi</sup>c self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, which is found to be the most effective driver of technological coping in our study. Security service providers, government agencies, and mass media should foster a belief that securing computers and networks is everyone's responsibility and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy should be developed.

Third, when an individual's self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is questionable, the individual should be encouraged to seek help from the social network, where social in<sup>fl</sup>uence can complement the de<sup>fi</sup>ciency of ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. Government agencies and educators should advocate the importance of an individual's social network in <sup>fi</sup>ghting identity theft. Service providers should design some communication mechanisms and networks among individual users, so that any incident of an individual consumer can be rapidly reported to other individuals among the communication network. This will help individual users be more informative and knowledgeable. Further, online business and security service providers should broadcast through the social network any emerging identity theft schemes and the technological bases that combat these schemes.

Finally, online business practitioners and security service providers should improve the quality of their security products and service, because perceived effectiveness of the security software is the second most effective variable for developing technological coping. Security service providers (e.g., anti-virus software companies) should strive to develop useful and effective computer security software and emphasize the severity of identity theft. Government agencies (such as the FBI and FCC) and mass media should also consistently evaluate the effectiveness of security software and disclose the evaluation report to the public.

## 7.3. Limitations and future research directions

Although this study provides valuable insights for research and practice, we note that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings must be interpreted in light of the study's limitations. First, we considered only factors relevant at the individual level because our study was focused on individual users' personal use of technologies. In order to extend the research model to organizational and governmental contexts, organizational and governmental factors should be considered for identity protection [10]. For example, it would be worthwhile to study what factors can facilitate companies to develop an appropriate privacy policy, what factors can in<sup>fl</sup>uence government regulations regarding identity theft, and how companies and government interact with customers to protect identity. In addition, cultural and economic factors may also need to be considered in future studies. It is worthwhile to examine whether identity theft occurrences are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by social dynamism and uncertainty, and if consumers from different countries behave differently for identity protection.

Second, in exploring the factors leading to coping, our focus was on coping appraisal in pursuit of model parsimony and simplicity. As we mentioned above, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to examine threat appraisal because of the existence of so many threats. Therefore, our research model is not complete compared with the general notion of coping theory which examines both threat appraisal and coping appraisal [55,60,80,91]. Future studies could investigate factors from the threat side of coping theory.

Third, our study was based on responses from nontraditional students. While this may produce results that are similar to the results derived from the general population [49], further studies may need to adopt other randomly selected samples in order to address the rigor of to the research model. Responses from the broader society at large could be considered. We are also cautious about our relatively small sample size, although the analysis tool that we used in the study is able to deal effectively with small sample sizes [36].

Finally, we used self-reported measures for two types of coping and identity theft. Future studies can gather objective data of identity theft incidents (such as website data) and collect subjective data from those users by means of survey.
