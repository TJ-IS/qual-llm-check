# Research Note

# Information Technology, Customer Satisfaction, and Profit: Theory and Evidence

Sunil Mithas

Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742, smithas@rhsmith.umd.edu

M. S. Krishnan

Technology and Operations, Stephen M. Ross School of Business, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, Michigan 48109, mskrish@umich.edu

Claes Fornell

Business Administration, Stephen M. Ross School of Business, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, Michigan 48109, cfornell@umich.edu

his paper studies the effect of aggregate information technology (IT) investments on customer satisfaction and profits at the firm level. Using data on 109 U.S. firms for the 1994–1996 and 1999–2006 periods, we find that aggregate IT investments have a positive association with customer satisfaction. However, the strength of the relationship varied across the 1994–1996 and 1999–2006 periods. Specifically, IT investments had a more positive influence on customer satisfaction for the 1994–1996 period than for the 1999–2006 period. Conversely, IT investments had a positive effect on profits in the 1999–2006 period, but a negative effect in the 1994–1996 period. These findings extend prior discourse in the information systems literature on the role of customer satisfaction as a mechanism that explains how IT-enabled benefits are “passed on to consumers” [Rai A, Patnayakuni R, Patnayakuni N (1997) Technology investment and business performance. Comm. ACM 40(7):90]. Our additional exploratory analyses showing that IT investments had a stronger effect on perceived quality than on perceived value provide an explanation for some of the observed effects of IT on customer satisfaction and profits. Together, these contributions and implications provide new insights to assess returns on IT investments by focusing on customer satisfaction, an important intangible and leading measure of firm performance, stock returns, and stock risk.

Keywords: information technology; customer satisfaction; intangibles; firm performance; profit; business value of IT; customer relationship management

## 1. Introduction

Understanding the payoffs from information technology (IT) investments has been an enduring theme in the information systems (IS) literature (see Hoadley and Kohli 2014, Mithas and Lucas 2014). Although recent research has made important advances in understanding the strategic value of IT at the firm level in terms of tangible measures such as profitability and market value (e.g., Kohli et al. 2012, Mithas and Rust 2016), our understanding of how IT influences key intangibles is in a nascent stage. For instance, IT investments and customer satisfaction have emerged as critical resources for firms; we know little about the relationship between these two resources. Recent work (e.g., Mithas et al. 2012, Rai et al. 2012) shows the importance of IT-enabled relational value and revenue growth for superior performance and implies that focusing on customer relationships may provide significant opportunities for sustainable sources of differentiation and competitive advantage. In particular, customer satisfaction has been identified as an important intangible and leading measure of firm performance (e.g., Fornell et al. 2006, Gupta and Zeithaml 2006, Lev 2001, Rust et al. 2004, Srinivasan and Hanssens 2009).

Despite an acknowledgement of the importance of customer satisfaction as an important and intangible measure of firm performance, few studies have examined the effect of IT investments on customer satisfaction. Previous research in IS acknowledges and argues that IT investments can influence intangible measures such as greater responsiveness to customers, overall customer experience, and customer satisfaction (Rai et al. 1997). For example, Bharadwaj et al. (1999, p. 1020) call for modeling “IT’s impact on intangible dimensions of [firm] performance such as 0 0 0 customer service, and customer relationships.” In addition, although literature on the business value of IT has made significant strides in relating IT to productivity, profitability, and market value (Kohli et al. 2012, Mithas and Rust 2016, Mithas et al. 2012, Tafti et al. 2013, Tambe et al. 2012), few, if any, studies explore the effect of IT on customer satisfaction.

Our primary goal in this study is to propose a theoretical framework that links aggregate IT investments to customer satisfaction at the firm level. A related but secondary goal is to assess the extent to which the effect of IT on customer satisfaction helps to explain prior findings relating IT with profits. We test the framework by collating archival longitudinal data on IT investments, customer satisfaction, and profit measures for U.S. firms for the 1994–1996 and 1999–2006 periods; this is a unique data set and perhaps the most comprehensive data set ever assembled to answer the research questions posed in this study. Because the effect of IT on customer satisfaction and related measures may vary by industry sector and time period, we explore the moderating effects of industry sector and time period in our models. Our data set also contains measures of perceived quality (a proxy for differentiation in firm strategy) and perceived value (a proxy for cost or productivity emphasis in firm strategy) from a customer’s perspective. These measures can potentially allow us to infer the relative extent of the effect of IT on these two determinants of customer satisfaction, complementing other studies that tend to infer such relationships indirectly (Bloom et al. 2012) or based on managerial perceptions (instead of customers’ perception) (Rust et al. 2002). Given the significant role played by customer satisfaction in today’s businesses, we shed new and important light on IT payoff by incorporating the impact of IT on customer satisfaction. Together, these contributions extend emerging literature on IT payoffs that investigates the effect of IT investments and capabilities on intangibles such as product variety, innovation, and patents (e.g., Kleis et al. 2012, Ravichandran et al. 2015, Saldanha et al. 2016).

## 2. Background and Theoretical Framework

As the locus of power across industries and businesses continues to shift toward customers because of overcapacity across industries and resulting hypercompetition, the need for managing customer relationships becomes increasingly important. It is not surprising then that firms are attempting to move from a product-centric approach to a customer-centric approach to sense and respond to customer demands for changes in product and service features, distribution channels, and pricing structure.

Although early literature in marketing focused on the individual or transaction-level customer satisfaction, beginning with Fornell (1992), several studies have measured firm-level customer satisfaction and its consequences for firm performance (e.g., Anderson et al.

1994; Fornell et al. 2006, 2009b; Lariviere et al. 2016). Higher customer satisfaction can reduce costs related to warranties, complaints, defective goods, and field service (Fornell 1992), as well as the likelihood of customer defection (Anderson and Sullivan 1993, Mithas et al. 2008). Related research suggests that customer satisfaction has significant implications for consumer spending, price elasticity, repurchase intentions, and returns in equity and bond markets (Anderson 1996, Anderson et al. 1997, Anderson and Mansi 2009, Anderson and Sullivan 1993, Fornell et al. 2010, Lariviere et al. 2016, Ngobo et al. 2012). The importance of achieving and maintaining high levels of customer satisfaction is underscored in the research on the customer lifetime value models (Gupta et al. 2006, Gupta and Lehmann 2003, Hogan et al. 2002, Rust et al. 2000, Villanueva and Hanssens 2007). Fornell et al. (2006) show that a portfolio of high-customer-satisfaction firms provides higher stock returns with less risk, a finding corroborated by subsequent studies (e.g., Aksoy et al. 2008, Fornell et al. 2009a, b). To improve their customer satisfaction, firms are making greater use of IT in their internal and customer-facing business processes as part of their digital business strategy (Bharadwaj et al. 2013, Mithas et al. 2013, Rai et al. 2012, Ray et al. 2005, Rosenbush 2015).

Our review of relevant prior work suggests that, to date, much of it has focused on customer satisfaction with IT-enabled products or services (e.g., customer relationship management (CRM), software products, enterprise systems, websites, cell phone services, auctions) at the individual customer, employee, or transaction level.<sup>1</sup> However, few studies focus on the effect of IT investments on customer satisfaction at the firm level, with some notable exceptions (Devaraj and Kohli 2000, Mithas et al. 2005, Rai et al. 2012). Some studies have examined how the adoption or use of IT may influence consumer or buyer surplus (Mithas and Jones 2007), though they do not indicate how firms’ IT investments influence customer satisfaction with their products and services, something that we do in this study. Although consumer surplus and customer satisfaction are related, they are distinct concepts: consumer surplus is the difference between consumers’ willingness to pay and price, whereas customer satisfaction is customers’ perception of the overall quality of their experience with a product or service, which includes monetary as well as nonmonetary aspects of their consumption experience.

## 2.1. IT and Customer Satisfaction

Linking IT investments to customer satisfaction requires an understanding of the antecedents of customer satisfaction at the firm level. Prior research points to three determinants of customer satisfaction at the firm level: perceived quality, perceived value, and customer expectations (see Fornell et al. 1996). Perceived quality, which captures recent consumption experience, has two components: (1) customization (i.e., the degree to which the firm’s offering is customized to meet heterogeneous customer needs) and (2) reliability (i.e., the degree to which a product or service is standardized and free from deficiencies). This notion of perceived quality implicitly accounts for innovation because perceived quality can have several dimensions, such as customization and product variety, among others. Perceived value refers to the perceived level of product quality vis-à-vis the price paid. Finally, customer expectations refer to customer perspectives on prior consumption experiences as well as customer beliefs in the firm’s ability to deliver quality in the future. Because the effect of customer expectations in directly affecting customer satisfaction is at best “mixed” (Yi 1990), we develop our arguments for understanding how IT influences customer satisfaction based on the effect of IT on the perceived quality and perceived value of a firm’s offerings.<sup>2</sup> Because prior research in marketing literature already documents the effect of perceived quality and perceived value on customer satisfaction, we examine the effect of IT on perceived quality and perceived value here only to assess whether IT has a stronger effect on perceived quality or perceived value.

We posit that IT applications have the potential to enable firms to influence the perceived quality of goods and services. IT can enable both the determinants of perceived quality (i.e., customization and consistency of consumption experience) by capturing customer information and using such customer information to customize a firm’s offerings and by providing a seamless service experience to customers. For example, by collecting customer-specific data through and then relaying it efficiently within the firm and to business partners, firms can customize their offering for each customer. Such customization has the effect of enhancing the perceived quality of products and services from a customer viewpoint. In addition to enhancing the customization-induced increase in perceived quality of the offering, IT systems allow firms to improve the reliability of consumption experiences by providing a consistent experience every time a customer interacts with the firm. Improvement in the ability to customize and deliver a consistent consumption experience enhances perceived quality of a firm’s offering, which in turn has a positive effect on customer satisfaction. In addition, by facilitating the seamless flow of information in an organization (Mithas et al. 2011), IT enables business process transparency and the efficient allocation of resources, shorter response times, and improved quality. This, in turn, leads to improvement not only in the quality of a firm’s core products or services but also in the quality of after-sales support provided to customers. Furthermore, IT facilitates business process innovation by redefining and redirecting business relationships and core processes through new channels, leading to significant improvements in total customer experience (Tallon 2008). These outcomes may enhance the perceived quality of a firm’s customer service.

In addition to its impact on perceived quality, IT may affect the perceived value of a firm’s offering. For example, IT can help automate business processes, leading to efficiency gains and cost reductions. Firms can also let customers serve themselves by defining the service and its delivery to suit their own needs (Bitner et al. 2000). Such IT-enabled efficiency gains and cost reductions, if passed on to the consumers in the form of price reduction, may enhance the perceived value of a firm’s offerings. In addition, even if the price of a firm’s offering remains the same, IT investments in supply chain and other systems with end-to-end integration have the potential to improve perceived value from a customer viewpoint through quicker responses to customer inquiries and consistent order fulfillment processes.

The preceding arguments we have made find support in both the marketing and the IS literature, which point to underlying dimensions of service quality as in the SERVQUAL model (Parasuraman and Grewal 2000) and the role of IT in addressing service gaps (Krasnikov et al. 2009). For example, virtually all of the dimensions of service quality and all of the service quality gaps are aided by technology infusion, resulting in enhancements in perceived service quality. Meuter et al. (2000, 2005) and Beatson et al. (2006) discuss several examples of how investments in self-service technologies can help with service innovation trial, adoption, satisfaction, and commitment.

## 2.2. The Moderating Role of Industry Sector

In addition to considering the effect of IT investments on perceived quality, perceived value, and customer satisfaction, we consider the moderating influence of the industry sector (i.e., type of firm offering) in these relationships because of significant differences in consumption experience for goods versus services (Mithas et al. 2006–07, Rai and Sambamurthy 2006). We argue that IT investments will have a stronger effect on customer satisfaction for the manufacturing firms than for services firms for three reasons. First, IT systems are generally more effective in situations that are highly codified and standardized, a situation that applies more strongly to manufactured goods than to services. Services are relatively harder to codify and standardize because of some of the unique characteristics of services, which include intangibility, heterogeneity, and inseparability (Parasuraman et al. 1988, Rust and Chung 2006). Although some research shows a positive effect of IT on productivity (Triplett and Bosworth 2004), service-producing sectors had lower rates of productivity improvements compared to productivity improvements in good-producing sectors, especially before 1995. Because productivity in services generally lags behind productivity in manufacturing, but real wages grow at about the same rate in the two sectors, one would expect the unit cost of services to increase over time, and this phenomenon of growth in costs of services relative to the rate of inflation came to be known as “Baumol’s cost disease” (Baumol 2012). Baumol’s disease is of particular concern for services that are highly customized and whose perceived quality is typically associated with a significant expenditure of human labor; Baumol (2012) characterized such services as stagnant services. To the extent services are harder to standardize (or need higher customization for the same level of improvement in customer satisfaction), we would expect IT to have a stronger effect on customer satisfaction for manufacturing firms than for services firms.

Second, IT systems may be more effective to help with improvements in conformance quality than with improvements in adaptive quality, and conformance quality may be more important for products than for services. The customer view of quality may differ between products and services (Prahalad and Krishnan 1999), and customers who purchase a manufactured product may regard the conformance of the product features to specifications as important. However, in service businesses such as hospitality or airlines, customers may consider the “adaptive view of quality” (i.e., the flexibility to respond to the specific needs of individual customers) as equally or more important. Based on similar reasoning, Rust and Miu (2006) and Rust et al. (2000) also argue that the drivers of customer equity may be different across sectors.

Third, compared with manufactured goods, it is much more difficult to evaluate services using objective criteria because the consumption experience is subjective. Johnson and Fornell (1991) use this reasoning to argue that average customer satisfaction should be higher for goods than for services. Therefore, to the extent that prior research reports generally higher levels of IT-enabled productivity gains in the manufacturing sector and it is easier to specify quality for manufactured items in advance, a baseline hypothesis would be that there is a positive relationship between IT investments and customer satisfaction for manufacturing firms.

However, we also acknowledge arguments that suggest why IT can play a more important role in enabling service sector companies to improve their customer satisfaction. Anderson et al. (1997) argue that IT may help achieve customer satisfaction in service businesses that are more information intensive. Services require adaptation to individual customer requirements, and this can be accomplished by leveraging the IT capability to customize product delivery and consumption experience in real time. In addition, the nature of the service business is such that consumers may experience the effect of IT-enabled business process flexibility and convenience more directly. For example, IT applications can be used in the hospitality industry to track the preferences of each customer on the basis of past experiences and tailor new service features that will delight these customers on their next business encounter. By contrast, for manufacturing firms, a larger proportion of IT investments may be deployed to streamline backend business processes, such as production planning and supply chain management. Although these systems may not have a direct and visible impact on customer service and the consumption experience, they can still influence perceived quality, perceived value, and thereby customer satisfaction.

## 2.3. The Moderating Role of Time Period

We also consider how the relationship between IT and customer satisfaction (and related measures) may be moderated by time period. The nature of IT has undergone significant changes over time, particularly after 1998, when firms began using modular and Internetbased channels. We argue that the nature of IT services and their production function has undergone significant changes since 1998 in at least three ways. First, use of Internet-era open standards<sup>3</sup> and service-oriented architecture facilitates a relatively seamless integration of disparate systems, thus reducing the complexity of overall IT infrastructure (Tafti et al. 2013). Before 1998, many firms were still using legacy pre-Internet-era IT systems, and these systems did not allow as much connectivity and integration as the open standards–based systems that emerged subsequently (for a discussion, see Andal-Ancion et al. 2003). Second, firms made much greater use of package applications such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) and CRM systems in the post-1998 period (Liu et al. 2013). Although these commercial off-the-shelf enterprise systems still require significant investments in process design, workflow planning, and development by vendors, they require relatively less effort in roll-out phase at a customer’s end compared with the fully proprietary and custommade software development projects that were more prevalent until approximately 1996. Finally, firms made significant use of IT outsourcing and offshoring services after 1999 to leverage enhanced software development quality and reduced labor costs at vendor firms located abroad (Han and Mithas 2013, Ramasubbu et al. 2008). Mani et al. (2013) report that more than 90% of the 100 largest outsourcing contracts by contract value signed during 1996–2005 in their sample belonged to the period after 1998 (IDC started tracking large contracts only after outsourcing gained significant momentum). Thus, beyond the continuation of Moore’s law and the associated decline in hardware prices, the application of newer types of IT systems and declining labor costs due to outsourcing motivate us to explore the moderating effect of time period in our analyses.

Although because of higher maturity and stability of technology in the post-1998 period and better availability of IT infrastructure and networks, it is possible to expect IT to have a stronger effect on customer satisfaction in the post-1998 period, there are also arguments that imply a stronger effect of IT on customer satisfaction in the pre-1998 period. First, although the IT systems before 1998 might not have been as sophisticated, they were relatively stable. Initial implementation difficulties and disruption of business processes as firms were implementing and learning to work with new IT systems in the post-1998 period may have had an adverse impact on customer satisfaction. Preoccupation with e-business transformation, year 2000 (Y2K) compliance, ERP implementation, and associated organizational disruption may have shifted attention away from customers to cause a weaker relationship between aggregate IT investments and customer satisfaction in the post-1998 period. A second reason to expect a weaker relationship between IT investments and customer satisfaction in the 1999–2006 period is that more sophisticated IT systems and greater use of specialized vendors for IT and business process outsourcing may have allowed firms to appropriate a larger share of the value created by IT in this period. Together, some of these factors may have allowed firms to become more productive and, at the same time, capture greater buyer and consumer surplus, which may have affected customer satisfaction (for a discussion, see Grover and Ramanlal 1999).

## 2.4. IT Investments and Profitability

Understanding the effect of IT on customer satisfaction can also explain prior findings that show a null or negative effect of IT investments on profitability in the period before 1997 (e.g., Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996, Rai et al. 1997) but a positive effect of IT investments on profitability in the period after that (Mithas and Rust 2016, Mithas et al. 2012). Researchers conjectured that one reason for the observed lack of relationship between IT investments and profitability in the period before 1997 is that firms may have competed away all of the IT-enabled gains and consumers realized most of these gains in terms of improved customization and customer convenience, as reflected in the overall customer satisfaction (Rai et al. 1997).<sup>4</sup> As Rai et al. (1997, p. 90) note, “IT is said to enhance organizational capabilities, resulting in improved 0 0 0 customer satisfaction 0 0 0 0 However, such improvements are often not reflected in improved financial performance, as benefits may be 0 0 0 passed on to consumers.” In other words, lower profitability may have been a result of higher customer satisfaction or more benefits going to consumers. If this argument is correct, we should observe a positive relationship between IT investments and customer satisfaction in the pre-1998 period when researchers did not find evidence for the positive effect of IT on profitability.

Conversely, subsequent research using post-1998 data has demonstrated a positive association between IT and profitability (Mithas et al. 2012), which would lead to the conjecture that firms may have had more success in appropriating benefits or gains from IT, leading to higher IT-enabled profitability. This may be due to a relatively weaker positive association between IT and customer satisfaction in the post-1998 period. Continued investments in IT and experience in managing IT systems may have improved the firms’ capabilities to leverage information and strengthen other organizational capabilities (El Sawy and Pavlou 2008; Grover and Ramanlal 1999, 2004; Mithas et al. 2011; Rai et al. 1997, 2012; Saldanha et al. 2016) to realize higher gains from IT. In addition, the purchasing power of a dollar of IT has changed significantly over time, in line with Moore’s law. Thus, with growing absolute IT investment over time coupled with findings that report higher payoffs for IT capital (Anderson et al. 2006), we should expect higher profitability in the post-1998 period. Newer technologies and complementary changes in business processes and incentive systems may also have allowed firms to make better use of IT to strike the right balance between customer satisfaction and profitability (Fornell et al. 2006, 2009a; Grover and Ramanlal 1999; Mithas and Jones 2007; Mithas et al. 2005) such that firms were able to not only satisfy customers but also to convert that into higher profits (perhaps through higher retention rates, cross-selling, or charging price premiums). Therefore, we should expect a relatively weaker IT–customer satisfaction relationship in the period after 1998 (compared with the 1994–1996 period) as indirect evidence for these conjectures.<sup>5</sup>

On the basis of the foregoing discussion, we posit the following two baseline hypotheses formally for the ease of describing our main results:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> IT investments are positively associated with higher levels of customer satisfaction, and we expect the effect to be stronger in the 1994–1996 period than in the 1999–2006 period.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> IT investments are positively associated with higher levels of profits, and we expect the effect to be stronger in the 1999–2006 period than in the 1994–1996 period.

Although, for ease of exposition and to retain sharper focus on main hypotheses, we do not posit formal hypotheses for the effect of IT on perceived quality and perceived value, we will discuss the differential effects of IT on perceived quality and perceived value to explain the effects of IT on customer satisfaction and profits in the 1994–1996 and 1999–2006 periods. Likewise, although we expect the industry sector to moderate the effect of IT investments on customer satisfaction and profits, we do not posit formal hypotheses for the same, but will incorporate such effects in our empirical models.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Data

The study uses data from multiple sources for the period from 1994 through 2006 (except 1997 and 1998) by assembling a unique and perhaps the most comprehensive data set ever compiled to test the effect of IT investments on customer satisfaction and profits at the same time. We obtained firm-level IT investment data from the InformationWeek annual surveys of top IT managers in North America. InformationWeek is considered to be a reliable source of information, and previous academic studies have also used data from InformationWeek surveys (Kudyba and Diwan 2002, Rai et al. 1997, Santhanam and Hartono 2003). The data on customer satisfaction come from the National Quality Research Center (NQRC) at the University of Michigan’s Ross School of Business. The NQRC tracks more than 150 U.S. firms on their customer satisfaction performance through its American Customer Satisfaction Index (ACSI). The IT and customer satisfaction data are matched to the other financial databases such as Compustat for data on control variables.<sup>6</sup> The data set operationalizing IT investments as aggregate annual IT budget as a percentage of revenue comprises an unbalanced panel during the period, for which aggregate IT investment and ACSI data are available, yielding a total of 433 firm-year observations. We next provide a brief description of key variables used in this study.

Customer satisfaction (CS) refers to the ACSI tracked by the NQRC. An individual firm’s ACSI score represents its customers’ overall evaluation of their total purchase and consumption experience (for details of ACSI measurement, pattern, and range across industries, see Fornell et al. 1996). The ACSI covers more than 40% of the U.S. gross domestic product, including both the public and private sectors. Each year, the NQRC surveys a total of about 50,000 customers who purchase products from over 150 companies (200–250 customers from each company) and asks these consumers to score companies on a scale to determine overall customer satisfaction for a company. Use of the ACSI methodology ensures a uniform, independent, customer-based, cumulative, and comparable firm-level customer satisfaction measure across industries and sectors of the U.S. economy. Customers are contacted by random digit dialing, and each respondent answers questions on 15 measurement variables (using 10-point scales) that are used as indicators of six latent constructs, including overall customer satisfaction, perceived quality, and perceived value. The ACSI is considered a reliable indicator of a firm’s customer satisfaction. These data have been used in several academic studies in both the accounting and the marketing literature (Anderson et al. 2004; Anderson and Mansi 2009; Fornell et al. 1996, 2006; Ittner and Larcker 1998; Lariviere et al. 2016; Morgeson et al. 2011).

Profit is the operating income before depreciation and taxes (in millions of dollars). Use of this variable allowed us to specify a more flexible specification and to easily interpret the results. Similar measures have also been used by other researchers (Bertrand et al. 2002).

IT investments 4ITINVPC5 refer to the level of IT investment as a percentage of the firm’s sales revenue. Several prior studies (e.g., Aral and Weill 2007, Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Chari et al. 2008, Tafti et al. 2013) have also used a similar measure to investigate the effect of aggregate IT investments on firm performance.<sup>7</sup> Because we do not use a production function approach, we follow the convention in other studies that relate flows of IT investments to other flow-type measures, such as profitability or market value (Aral and Weill 2007, Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Rai et al. 1997, Tafti et al. 2013). Rai et al. (1997, p. 91) argue that use of such flow-type measures of IT is important in an environment of accelerating “technological obsolescence and in which current expenditure has a significant role in producing short-term business benefits.” Recent studies have argued that such flow-type measures may be better than the “capital stock” types of measures because flow measures avoid some types of measurement errors involved in aggregating capital stock (Chwelos et al. 2010). Kleis et al. (2012) also prefer a flow-type measure in their recent work linking IT to intangibles such as patents. To ensure exogeneity and to avoid reverse causality, we use IT investment data for the year previous to that of the corresponding customer satisfaction–related variables.<sup>8</sup>

MFG is an indicator variable representing whether the company belongs to the manufacturing sector or the service sector (0 = service, 1 = manufacturing). The service sector includes firms from the hospitality, airline, and financial services industries, and the manufacturing sector includes firms that manufacture durable and nondurable goods. The manufacturing versus services distinction is based on their primary North American Industry Classification System code, and we recognize that this distinction is somewhat malleable in cases where manufacturing firms may have significant service operations.

PERIOD is an indicator variable (0 = 1994–1996, 1 = 1999–2006). Because firms were investing aggressively in IT to use new Internet-based channels for conducting business in the late 1990s, and some reports suggest that this trend accelerated in the 1999–2006 period (The Economist 2004), it is important to consider any shifts in the role of IT investments over time. Ravichandran et al. (2009) quote a 2003 Meta Group (acquired by Gartner in 2005) study that reported that IT investments as a proportion of firm revenues nearly doubled in 1999 compared with 1996. Although IT spending did decline in the aftermath of the dot-com bust and the 2001 recession, it recovered and then continued to grow with about the same trend line in the growth of IT spending as in the late 1990s (The Economist 2004). In our data set, firms increased their IT spending from 2.2% of their revenues in the 1994–1996 period to 3.3% in the 1999–2006 period. The use of this period dummy indicator along with an interaction term involving this dummy and ITINVPC in our regression models enables us to control for any systematic effects during the two time periods (1994–1996 and 1999–2006).

We also use other variables from ACSI (see Fornell et al. 1996) in our supplementary analyses. Perceived quality (PQ) refers to the perceived quality of a firm’s offerings and was obtained from the NQRC. In the ACSI methodology described previously, perceived quality is measured by asking customers to rate their recent experience with a product or service in terms of an overall post purchase evaluation of perceived quality, perceived customization, and perceived reliability. Perceived value (PV) refers to the perceived value of a firm’s offerings and was obtained from the NQRC. Perceived value is assessed as perceptions of quality relative to price and price relative to quality. Customer expectations (CE) refers to (1) overall expectation of quality, (2) expectation regarding customization, and (3) expectation for reliability. This firm-level variable was obtained from the NQRC.

Table 1 provides a summary of the variables and data sources used in the study. Table 2 provides descriptive statistics for the key variables used in the study. Our sample consists of large U.S. firms that are tracked by both the ACSI and InformationWeek. These are large and well-known firms, and many appear in lists such as the Fortune 500 or S&P 500. In our sample, on average, firms spent 2.91% of revenue in total IT expenditures. About 36% observations belonged to the manufacturing sector, and the remaining belonged to the service sector, whereas about 39% observations belonged to the 1994– 1996 period, and the remaining 71% belonged to the 1999–2006 period. On average, the customer satisfaction, perceived quality, and perceived value scores were 76.06, 81.86, and 76.35. The firms reported a mean profit of \$6.09 billion (minimum value was loss of \$1.29 billion, and the maximum profit was \$53.9 billion; all monetary figures were deflated to 2005 dollars). Further analyses of summary statistics indicates that firms had average revenues of \$33.5 billion with assets of \$83.3 billion. Firms were spending, on average, 2.2% of their revenues on IT in the 1994–1996 period, and investments on IT were generally higher (3.3%) in the 1999–2006 period. However, average customer satisfaction was generally lower in the 1999–2006 period than in the 1994–1996 period (75.3 in 1999–2006 and 77.9 in 1994–1996). Although on average sales and profits were higher in the 1999–2006 period than in the 1994–1996 period, the differences are statistically insignificant, and firms have similar representation across industry sectors in two time periods.

## 3.2. Empirical Models and Econometric Considerations

We use a linear model estimation approach to relate aggregate IT investments to customer satisfaction and other dependent variables. We specify the following equation for the panel models:

$$
Y _ {i t} = X _ {i t - 1} \beta + u _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

where Y represents the dependent variables such as CS and profits for our main analyses; X represents a vector of firm and environmental characteristics such as IT investments, industry sector, and time period; $\beta$ are the parameters to be estimated; i and t are subscripts that refer to firms and time periods, respectively; $u _ { i }$ represents unobserved time-invariant fixed factors associated with a firm $i ;$ and  is the error term associated with each observation. These models implicitly control for firm size because we use IT investments as percentage of sales (we also explicitly include another proxy for firm size as a control in some of our models as a robustness check, as we discuss in §4.4).

Because of the presence of unobserved time-invariant fixed factors $u _ { i }$ in the composite error term ${ v _ { i t } } \ ( u _ { i } + \varepsilon _ { i t } ) .$ the $v _ { i t }$ are likely to be serially correlated across time for the same firm. One way to eliminate the $u _ { i }$ is to use the first differences of all variables. An alternative method, called the fixed effect or within transformation, often works better if data are available for more than two time periods for the same firm. This method subtracts the time averages for each variable and then estimates the model rather than subtracting the value of prior observations for a firm as in first differencing. This approach uses only the variation within a firm across time to estimate regression coefficients, and it provides consistent estimates even if any unobserved variable in the error term is correlated with the focal variable, such as IT investments. We use the fixed-effects models that handle unobserved heterogeneity by computing within-firm estimates of coefficients.

Panel models (e.g., fixed-effect models) assume exogeneity of Xs $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } E [ \pmb { \varepsilon } _ { i t } \mid X _ { i } , u _ { i } ] = 0 )$ . In our context, endogeneity can arise from two sources: reverse causality (i.e., firms may pursue IT to help increase customer satisfaction) and omitted variables. Our use of lagged IT investment measures helps address concerns due to reverse causality. As an additional way to rule out endogeneity of IT investments due to reverse causality, we followed the approach used by Black and Lynch (2001) and Doms et al. (1997). Instead of using values of dependent variables (e.g., customer satisfaction) for time t, we used lagged values. We did not find any evidence that current-year IT investment is related

Our use of fixed-effects models accounts for many time-invariant sources of unobserved heterogeneity, thus ruling out omitted variable bias as a serious concern (Wooldridge 2002). Nonetheless, we conducted a formal Hausman test to assess potential endogeneity of the IT investments variable following a procedure that Wooldridge (2003) recommends. In this procedure, we regressed the value of IT investments on lagged values of IT investment, customer satisfaction, and profits. We used predicted value of IT investments from this model to compute predicted residuals for IT investments. We then used this predicted residual in the firm performance models along with the regular IT investments variable. Because this predicted residual was not statistically significant at the conventional $p < 0 . 0 5$ level in the firm performance models, this test reduces concerns about the endogeneity of the IT investments variable.

Although the increased price will influence perceived value to some extent, if that loss in perceived value is made up by a gain in perceived quality, then firms can have acceptable satisfaction and higher profits at the same time.<sup>9</sup>

4.4. Additional Analyses and Robustness Checks We conducted additional analyses and robustness checks. First, in our customer satisfaction and related models in Tables 3–5, we used a normalized measure for IT (IT as a percentage of sales) that implicitly controls for firm size. The inclusion of an additional control variable (natural log of total assets) also yielded generally similar results. Second, to alleviate any concerns about omitted variable bias, we estimated our customer satisfaction models including the customer expectations variable (see Table A5 in the online appendix). As we expected, customer expectations do not have an impact on customer satisfaction; hence, we continue with more parsimonious models. Third, because market concentration in an industry may influence customer choice and eventually affect customer satisfaction, we accounted for industry concentration in our models for customer satisfaction (in place of industry sector) by using the Hirschman–Herfindahl index, one of the more widely accepted measures of market concentration, and obtained broadly similar results. The results of these additional analyses raise confidence in the main findings of this study. Finally, we conducted exploratory analyses to consider the effect of more proximate IT investments (e.g., CRM systems) on managers’ assessment of improvement in customer satisfaction as a complementary measure. We found that “focused” customer-facing IT applications (e.g., CRM) are more effective at influencing customer experience than relatively “broader” IT systems (e.g., ERP) because the effect of ERP systems is statistically nonsignificant (see the online appendix).

## 5. Discussion

Our main goal in this research was to study the effect of aggregate IT investments on customer satisfaction. Our analysis of an unbalanced panel of 109 firms indicates that aggregate IT investments had a positive association with customer satisfaction; the relationship was stronger in the 1994–1996 period and for manufacturing firms than it was for the 1999–2006 period and for service firms. Further analyses indicate that the IT investments had a negative association with profits in the 1994–1996 period, but a positive association in the 1999–2006 period. Although we knew that IT had a null effect on profitability before 1996 (e.g., Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996, Rai et al. 1997) and a positive effect after 1998 from other studies (Mithas et al. 2012) that used IT data from separate sources, this study used a longitudinal panel for the 1994–2006 period to document that relationship using a consistent measure of IT investments coming from the same data set for the entire duration. Overall, our findings provide a plausible explanation for the so-called profitability paradox observed in other studies and corroborative evidence for conjectures by Rai et al. (1997, p. 90) that suggest how IT can contribute to “improved 0 0 0 customer satisfaction.”

This study makes important contributions by approaching the business value of IT investments using customer satisfaction, an intangible and important leading indicator of firm performance, to provide a complementary way of judging returns from IT. We theorized and empirically validated the relationship between IT and customer satisfaction. Our results suggest that IT applications affect perceived quality and, to a lesser extent, perceived value of a firm’s offerings. Our findings linking IT to customer satisfaction, coupled with recent findings that customer satisfaction has a favorable association with market value, stock returns, and risk (Fornell et al. 2006, 2009a; Lariviere et al. 2016; Srinivasan and Hanssens 2009; Tuli and Bharadwaj 2009), provide a more complete understanding of how IT investments and IT capabilities influence firm performance. We also conducted exploratory analyses to study the effect of CRM systems on managerial assessments of improvement in customer satisfaction, to respond to calls to study more proximate IT systems and to take into account managerial perceptions of improvement in customer satisfaction (Schryen 2013). By identifying and testing the effects of aggregate investments and specific IT systems on customer satisfaction, this paper complements and contributes to the growing body of work that has identified other mechanisms that explain the link between IT and firm performance through various organizational capabilities such as alliance management capabilities, performance management capabilities, process integration capabilities, customer management capabilities, and process management capabilities (El Sawy and Pavlou 2008, Mithas et al. 2011, Pavlou and El Sawy 2006, Rai et al. 2006, Saldanha et al. 2016, Tafti et al. 2013). Our findings in this study linking IT investments with customer satisfaction complement findings in a related study that showed a strong linkage of IT-enabled information management capability with customer management capability that was shown to influence almost every measure of firm performance in panel models (e.g., customer related, financial, human resource related, and organizational effectiveness related; Mithas et al. 2011).

Before considering the implications of the findings, it is useful to point to some limitations of the study that present opportunities for further research. First, this study focuses on relatively large U.S. firms that are tracked by both the ACSI and InformationWeek; thus, our findings are likely to generalize to such very large firms in Fortune 500– or S&P 500–type firms. For example, the average revenues of firms in our sample in 2006 were about \$40.7 billion, which compares well with average revenues of large firms in Fortune’s Global 500 firms in 2006 (the average revenues in the Global 500 in 2006 were at about \$42 billion; Demos 2007). Although such firms may only be a small fraction of the overall population of U.S. firms, they contribute significantly in terms of overall economic activity, employment, and output (Vigna and Shipman 2010). These firms are some of the most global in nature; approximately 48% of the revenues of S&P 500 companies in 2008 came from abroad (Vigna and Shipman 2010), which helped provide significant employment opportunities to U.S. workers and helped raise profits of these firms. There is a need to validate the results of our study for midsized and small firms and across multiple countries for further generalizability.

Second, although we used a unique and one of the most comprehensive data sets to date, which includes consistent measures for IT and customer satisfaction over a long period, to answer our research questions, and although archival data are less prone to reactive measurement and researcher bias, our data do have limitations. For example, given our sample size, we use lagged IT investments to alleviate concerns about reverse causality, and because we do not have a long enough time series, it is difficult to conduct analyses for other time lags. Longer time series, primary data, and case-based research within firms can help us better understand how specific IT investments affect customer satisfaction across industries and related time lags. Such studies will enhance our understanding of the situational and contextual factors that are difficult to control for in large-scale archival research.

## 5.1. Implications for Research

From a theoretical perspective, the study provides evidence for arguments made in previous research suggesting that IT investments did not affect profitability in the period before 1997 because the IT-enabled gains were “competed away” (Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996, p. 135) and were “passed on to consumers” (Rai et al. 1997, p. 90) in terms of improved customization and customer convenience, as reflected in the overall customer satisfaction with a firm’s products and services. By showing a positive association between IT investments and customer satisfaction for the 1994–2006 period, we lend credence to some of these arguments. In addition, the findings provide evidence for theoretical arguments linking IT investments and IT resources to firm performance and firm risk because many of these prior studies suggested links between IT and firm performance using customer satisfaction and customer relationship management processes (e.g., Bharadwaj 2000, Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Kim and Mithas 2011, Mithas et al. 2012, Tian and Xu 2015).

We observed a weaker relationship between aggregate IT investments and customer satisfaction in our data in the 1999–2006 period, which suggests at least two explanations. First, although firms were aggressive in preparing for Y2K and acquiring the latest information technologies, such as CRM and ERP systems in the early years (e.g., 1999–2000), many of them experienced implementation difficulties and disruption of business processes as they were implementing and learning to work with new IT systems. It is also important to note that firms made excessive investments in IT systems during the 1999–2000 period that did not directly improve the consumption experience, and a lack of integration among IT systems may have distracted firms from focusing on customers. In our sample, firms significantly increased their IT investment as a percentage of sales from 2.2% during the 1994–1996 period to 3.4% during the 1999–2000 period, an increase of about 50% within a short time. In the 1999–2000 period in particular, firms aggressively invested in IT assets and adopted a plethora of hardware and software standards and platforms as part of their e-business strategies. In addition, firms made significant investments in Y2K-related activities by upgrading their back-end IT infrastructure significantly to alleviate widely prevailing investor and customer concerns, resulting in the observed weaker relationship between aggregate IT investments and customer satisfaction.

A second explanation for a weaker relationship between aggregate IT investments and customer satisfaction in the 1999–2006 period may be that firms learned how to appropriate a larger share of the value created by IT in this period (for a discussion, see Grover and Ramanlal 1999). Furthermore, firms have made greater use of IT outsourcing and offshoring services to leverage enhanced software development quality and reduced labor costs at vendor firms located abroad (Chang and Gurbaxani 2013, Han and Mithas 2013, Ramasubbu et al. 2008, Whitaker et al. 2011). This explanation appears plausible and may account for a positive association between IT investments and profitability in the post-1996 period, as observed in recent studies (Mithas et al. 2012), unlike the null or sometimes even negative relationship between IT investments and profitability from studies for the prior period (e.g., Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996).

In addition, as we argued earlier, firms used ERP and CRM systems in the post-1998 period, and these systems may have had some implications for firm profitability because many firms reported troubled enterprise systems implementations in early phases. Despite reported difficulties in rolling out ERP or CRM systems, it appears that with passage of time and by using implementation partners, firms eventually achieved success with their implementation efforts. However, the implications of these systems for firm profitability still remain an empirical question. On one hand, these systems can provide a competitive advantage to firms if such an advantage is not nullified by similar investments by their competitors. On the other hand, one would expect any advantages to be short-lived unless the firm finds a way to integrate such systems with unique and nonreplicable advantages.

One can make similar arguments for IT outsourcing, which was more prevalent after 1998. Understanding the effect of IT outsourcing on firm profitability and the extent to which that depends on how outsourcing is managed and how outsourcing strategy fits with the overall business strategy of the firm are fertile opportunities for research. Although we are not aware of studies that have directly investigated these issues, some studies have investigated the effect of IT outsourcing strategic posture on market value (Mithas et al. 2013) and the effect of IT outsourcing on non-IT operating costs (Han and Mithas 2013) without considering overall business strategy.

Among other implications, the relatively weaker effect of IT on customer satisfaction for services requires further research, particularly because increasing digitization of the economy is fueling a rise in services and the phenomenon called “Baumol’s cost disease,” which afflicts many services such as healthcare, education, legal services, and public services. A key question in this context is whether and what types of IT strategies, IT resources, software development practices, and IT-enabled interventions can help improve productivity, customer satisfaction, and innovation in service firms (Kude et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2015). At the same time, the role of IT and e-government services to improve access, quality, and citizen satisfaction needs further investigation, particularly in emerging economies where governments are often viewed with suspicion (Morgeson and Mithas 2009, Pang et al. 2013).

## 5.2. Managerial Implications

Our findings show how firms can use potential improvements in customer satisfaction as a justification for returns on their IT investments. Because customer satisfaction has an effect on the market value of firms (Anderson et al. 2004), chief information officers and IT managers can use potential improvements in customer satisfaction as a justification for returns on their IT investments.<sup>10</sup> Because IT investments also have other benefits such as revenue growth (Mithas et al.

2012), differentiation through customer knowledge and one-to-one marketing effectiveness (Mithas et al. 2005, 2006), cost reduction or productivity improvement (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996, Han and Mithas 2013, Rai et al. 1997, Tambe and Hitt 2012), profitability (Mithas et al. 2012), innovation (Kleis et al. 2012, Ravichandran et al. 2015, Saldanha et al. 2016), decrease in firm risk (Tian and Xu 2015), market value (Kohli et al. 2012, Mithas and Rust 2016), and improvement in organizational capabilities (Mithas et al. 2011; Pavlou and El Sawy 2006, 2010; Rai et al. 2006; Saldanha et al. 2016; Tafti et al. 2013), managers should be aware of these other benefits also to make a more complete assessment of the benefits from IT systems.

The quantitative assessment of the intangible value created by IT investments through improvement in customer satisfaction can be particularly useful when firms realize the importance of investing in IT to improve their customer satisfaction and yet find it difficult to justify such investments using traditional metrics such as return on investments. Recent research suggesting that high-customer-satisfaction firms not only beat the market but also do so at less risk provides further validation for why firms should consider the customer satisfaction benefits to justify and evaluate the benefits of IT (Fornell et al. 2006, Tuli and Bharadwaj 2009). This is not to suggest that IT will always have a positive association with customer satisfaction or that managers should maximize customer satisfaction, because there can be diminishing returns, and at some point, overinvesting in IT or customer satisfaction may not be profitable or value adding from a shareholder perspective (Fornell et al. 2006). That said, in most practical situations, underinvesting in IT and customer satisfaction is more likely the norm, and managers would do well to guard against this shortcoming to avoid losing their competitive advantage.

Because IT has a stronger effect on perceived quality than on perceived value, and perceived quality appears to have a stronger effect on customer satisfaction than that of perceived value, managers may be better off directing their IT systems to improve customization and reliability of consumption experience than reducing costs, particularly for firms that aim to improve customer satisfaction through superior quality. Furthermore, improvement in perceived quality can act as a leading indicator of a firm’s customer satisfaction, and by tracking such measures, managers can monitor whether their IT investments are likely to improve customer satisfaction and market value.

## 6. Conclusion

To conclude, this study investigates the effect of IT investments on customer satisfaction and provides a test of explanations for the lack of IT-enabled profitability in the period before 1998. Using longitudinal data on 109 U.S. firms, we document a positive association between aggregate IT investments and customer satisfaction for the pre-1998 period, but a relatively weaker association in the post-1998 period. The findings provide a new lens through which to assess returns on IT investments and provide guidance for managerial action in using IT for improving customer satisfaction and market value of firms. We hope that this study will encourage researchers to explore linkages between IT and other intangible measures, such as organizational capabilities, including process management and innovation capabilities. Despite the challenging nature of executing such interdisciplinary research, the exploration of such linkages is crucial for understanding the strategic impact of IT to create intangible assets and competitive advantage.
