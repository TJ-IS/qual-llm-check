Integrating Social Networking Support for Dyadic Knowledge Exchange: A Study in a Virtual Community of Common Practice

## Abstract

Virtual community of practice (VCoP) is an important form of knowledge management systems. Would the integration of social networking support into traditional forum-based VCoP benefits knowledge exchange in the community? If so, why? Based on social capital theory and transactive memory system research, this study suggests that social networking support will enhance knowledge exchange in VCoP. In a field study, this study found social networking support intensifies dyadic knowledge excha d reduces the probability that dyads stop knowledge exchange. These findings empirically confirm the for practitioners.

Keywords: Virtual community of practice, knowledge management, online social network, knowledge sharing, knowledge exchange.

## 1 Introduction

Typical employees spend a third of their time looking for information (Davenport & Lawrence, 2000). Managers spend up to two hours a day on this activity (Accenture, 2007). Facilitating knowledge seeking and sharing is an important element in boosting productivity Various knowledge management systems (KMS) have been deployed in organizations to support the capturing, storage, and exchange of knowledge (Xu, Kim, & Kankanhalli, 2010-2011). However, the usage of KMS often sees only limited success, plagued by problems such as low knowledge contribution intention (Kankanhalli, Tan, & Wei, 2005), low usage (Gray & Durcikova, 2006), and a cold environment where the interpersonal relationship between knowledge sharers and seekers is hard to maintain (Xu et al., 2010-2011).

#

While it is well known that interpersonal relationship is an integral part of knowledge management, how to boost interpersonal relationship in KMS remains a challenge. As observed by Hertzum and Pejtersen (2000), seekers do not rely on the documents in KMS but use KMS as a means to locate experts for offline interaction, because KMS do not satisfy their needs to build a closer relationship (Xu et al., 2010-2011). Even when an expert because their relationship with the expert is not close enough to overcome various concerns.

To address these challenges, Xu et al. (2010-2011) suggested there is a need to build “relational knowledge management systems,” whereby the management of interpersonal relationship is well supported along with content management. Online social networking, a prevailing technology available nowadays, is considered as an effective mechanism to instill relationship management into KMS. While some organizations have already adopted this practice, including IBM, Ford (Ellis, 2001), and Caterpillar (Powers, 2004), many are still lagging behind.

Does the integration of social networking support really help knowledge exchange? Unfortunately, there is very little empirical evidence in the literature. Past research on knowledge management have uncovered various motivational, social-psychological, and environmental factors in knowledge sharing (Kankanhalli et al., 2005) and seeking (Agarwal, Xu, & Poo, 2011; Xu, Tan, & Yang, 2006), but there is a scarcity of research that investigates the effect of technological mechanism, particularly online social networking support, on knowledge exchange.

We define knowledge exchange as the dyadic knowledge sharing and seeking between VCoP members. We differentiate knowledge exchange from knowledge sharing. Knowledge sharing could be one-way, while knowledge exchange indicates a reciprocal relationship. Knowledge sharing could occur in a broadcast fashion (Zhang & Watts, 2008), while knowledge exchange occurs at dyadic level, although others could observe such exchanges. Knowledge exchange is a component of knowledge sharing. This study focuses on dyadic

#

exchanges because this study is theoretically based on an interpersonal perspective, and the notion of dyadic exchange aligns with this perspective.

To address the research gap, this study seeks to (1) provide a theoretical justification of why online social networks boost knowledge exchange, and (2) provide a field study to empirically verify the impact of social networking support on knowledge exchange in the (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998) and the transactive memory system research (Jarvenpaa & Majchrzak, 2008; Wegner, 1987), we propose that the integration of social networks and KMS brings about three benefits. (1) It makes strangers in the community start sharing. (2) It makes weak ties in the community share more frequently. And (3) it mak haring relationships less likely to break up. These three benefits represent a comprehensive set of criteria to evaluate knowledge exchange outcomes of system integration. We collected second-hand data from a large VCoP before and after its implementation of social networking functions. Our results support the effectiveness of integration on all three criteria.

The main contributions of this study are as follows. First, we propose a set of dyadic behavioral measures that captures the birth rate, intensity, and death rate of dyadic knowledge exchange. Second, while past literature has suggested the impact of social factors on knowledge sharing (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chen, 2007; Chiu, Hsu, & Wang, 2006; Choi & Scott, 2013; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), this study investigates the impact of an actual social technology that supports relationship management, namely social networking support. Finally, while past literature has investigated individual knowledge sharing intention and behavior, this study takes dyad as unit of analysis, and looks into the dyadic behavior of knowledge exchange. Dyadic behavior outcomes are more direct measures of community outcomes than do individual behavior outcomes.

The paper is organized as follows. First, we review the literature on knowledge sharing and virtual community of practice. We then propose our hypotheses of the effectiveness of social networking integration in KMS based on social capital and transactive memory system literature. After that, we report our empirical study, followed by discussion and implications.

## 2 Conceptual Background

## 2.1 Virtual Community of Practice

In the age of Web 2.0, users not only receive knowledge online, but also create and transmit content. A VCoP is such an online community for like-minded individuals in geographically dispersed areas (Fang & Chiu, 2010). A VCoP is an emergent online community where professionals in an industry share and seek knowledge related to their practice (Hsu, Ju, Yen, & Chang, 2007). In accordance with the characteristics of conventional community of practice, it is a place where a rich shared repertoire community identity (i.e., common interest, theme, organization) and individual member identity (i.e., moderators, experts) are developed through social interactions. Different from online commonplaces, a VCoP is “as much a space for knowledge creation as for knowledge sharing” (Zhang & Watts, 2008). For example, StackExchange is a VCoP where programmers create and exchange coding knowledge. VCoPs are especially celebrated for the generation and dissemination of tacit knowledge.

VCoPs leverage various information technologies for knowledge sharing, including blogs, wikis, forums, chat rooms, and question-and-answer systems (Moore & Serva, 2007). A typical technological infrastructure of VCoP is forum, whereby content is organized into topics, and topics comprise of threads which include posts and all the discussion raised by these posts. Members typically post information to a suitable thread, and other members can respond to or comment on the post. In this study, by default we refer to forum-based VCoP. While popular forum software such as Discuz!, vBulletin, and Webcrossing traditionally did not support social networking, some incorporated social networking in recent years. For example, Discuz! incorporated social networking in 2010, as a result, many VCoPs based on Discuz! now have social networking support in addition to the traditional forum functions.

Like other KMS, VCoPs face the challenge of knowledge sharing. Dixon (2000) pointed out that “build it and they will come” is hardly the truth for knowledge sharing. First of all, with no offline communication, the relationship between members in VCoPs is inevitably

#

more fragile than in traditional organizations (Hsu et al., 2007). These weak ties discourage voluntary participation, hence fail to stimulate individuals to share knowledge (Chiu, Wang, Shih, & Fan, 2011). Furthermore, Garfield (2006) proposed ten reasons that people do not share knowledge in VCoPs. Particularly, they are not motivated to share and do not see of knowledge (Szulanski, 1996). On the other hand, there is a considerable codification effort even if one is willing to share (Kankanhalli et al., 2005). These and other reasons scare individuals away from knowledge sharing in VCoPs (Brown, 2013). Consequently, even after initial acceptance, most VCoPs suffer from the lack of continuous member participation, which threatens their eventual success (Chen, 2007).

## 2.2 Knowledge Exchange in VCoPs

Knowledge creation and exchange are identified as hallmarks of VCoPs (Zhang & Watts, 2008). Typical knowledge creation activities in VCoPs include writing initial and creative posts and providing constructive comments to a discussion. Knowledge exchange typically occurs in the form of question-and-answer (Zhang & Watts, 2008). Another typical form is post-and-comment. An initial post is often detailed, while a comment is often brief. However, a comment, even brief, shall not be dismissed as of no value. Cross and Sproull (2004) found that actionable knowledge includes indication of (dis)confirmation, approval, and support. Therefore, both a comment with technical content and one indicating support and confirmation are knowledge exchanges. The knowledge exchange process is also a part of the knowledge creation process. The discussion among members, including approvals and disapprovals, manifests a knowledge co-creation process which is beyond individual creativity.

It is important to understand the underlying motivation for knowledge exchange. Numerous researches have investigated the antecedents of knowledge sharing behavior in the context of VCoPs from various theoretical perspectives. For instance, Chiu et al. (2006) examined knowledge contribution in VCoPs from the social capital theory perspective. They identified individual motivational factors (i.e., reputation, enjoy helping), structural capital (i.e., social interaction ties), cognitive capital (i.e., shared vision and shared language), and relational capital (i.e., trust, norm of reciprocity, identification) as significant motivators of knowledge sharing. Based on the social cognitive theory and social exchange theory, Chen and Hung (2010) identified reciprocity, interpersonal trust, self-efficacy, and perceived relative advantage as significant factors for knowledge sharing behaviors in professional VCoPs and the major antecedents identified.

The literature reveals that the antecedents for knowledge sharing in VCoP can be grouped into three categories, i.e., social factors, cognitive factors, and technical factors. Social factors pertain to the relational motivations and intrinsic motivations in knowledge sharing. Individual motivators include altruism, reputation, identification, and commitment (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu et al., 2006; Choi & Scott, 2013; Fang & Chiu, 2010; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), and relational factors include social interactions, trust, reciprocity, and justice (Ardichvili, 2008; Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chen & Hung, 2010; Chiu et al., 2011; Fang & Chiu, 2010). Cognitive factors pertain to the factors that affect the absorption of knowledge, such as shared language and knowledge quality (Chen, 2007; Chiu et al., 2006; Chiu et al., 2011). Finally, technical factors pertain to the online system that supports knowledge sharing. Usefulness, ease of use, and the ability to track knowledge are such factors (Lin, 2006; Phang et al., 2009; Sharratt & Usoro, 2003). Collectively, these variables represent factors pertaining to three fundamental building blocks of VCoP, namely, user and her community (i.e., social factors), the knowledge (i.e., cognitive factors), and the underlying technology (i.e., technical factors).

Previous literature has laid a valuable foundation for us to understand factors that facilitate knowledge exchange in VCoP. To further advance the research, we believe the literature also reveals a few important research gaps. First, there is a lack of attention to technical factors besides the typical TAM factors. This is an important limitation because practitioners seek guidance on the application of new technologies in VCoP. Some general technical functions have been proposed, such as providing search functions and allowing users to track knowledge activities (Phang et al., 2009). While the importance of using technology to facilitate social interaction is recognized (Phang et al., 2009; Xu et al., 2010-2011), no actual technical function has been tested. Second, there is a lack of study that focuses on dyadic knowledge sharing over time. Ultimately, the effectiveness of VCoP and their strengthened knowledge exchange lasts over time. While some research has investigated dyadic knowledge seeking (Xu, Zhang, & Zhang, 2010), no technological mechanism has been tested.

To fill in this research gap, this study tests the effect of social networking support, a technological mechanism that is now mature, on knowledge exchange in VCoP.

## 2.3 Social Networking Support in VCoPs

Traditional VCoPs organize knowledge into themes. Topics are grouped together into a theme hierarchy. Posts and their threads on similar topics are organized in a chronological order. Members typically browse the theme hierarchy, identify the theme of interest, find an interesting topic, and follow its threads. Alternatively, a member may search with keywords to find interesting posts, or directly delve into a topic that is promoted to the front page because of its quality or recency. Internet forum technology is the typical underlying technology which provides all these functionalities for VCoPs. Since members of a common interest are drawn together by topics, such a community can be regarded as a theme-oriented community.

Traditional VCoPs are structured based on the ideology that knowledge is an object (de Lima, de Carvalho, & Ambrósio, 2010). Hence it can be coded, classified, sorted, and searched. This view, however, overlooks the social nature of knowledge exchange. As explicated in our literature review, knowledge exchange is a social process. If so, the technological infrastructure of VCoP shall incorporate supports for social processes.

Online social networking support is a technology with three basic functions: (1) It allows users to explicitly organize their social relationships in a form of friend list. (2) It provides a

#

relationship-oriented organization of knowledge. (3) It supports relationship building. First, with social networking support, a friend list makes an easy entry for a VCoP member to find and consult her friends. Second, the member could now read a friend’s posts in one place, and be informed of new posts from the friend. It becomes easier to know a friend’s expertise in a topic area. One could also have her own posts organized in one place (Ma & Agarwal, 2007) (i.e., on her profile page), as well as her recent dyadic communications. Finally, one could show support for friends, so as to strengthen interpersonal relationship. In sum, the integration of social networking support into a VCoP not only enhances interpersonal relationship, but also provides a new way to organize knowledge.

Our analysis focuses on the effect of the integration of social networking support for friends in a VCoP. By friends, we refer to dyads who are interested in each other, in terms of the knowledge they share and an intention to build a closer relationship. In a VCoP, although some members may read and comment on other’s post, they may have little intention to recognize others as a friend. For such dyads, social networking integration would have little effect on them. It is when two members add each other as a friend, even though it is a weak tie with no offline relationship, that such a technically explicit friendship link would become conducive for future interactions. Just as Ren et al. (2012) proposed, a theme-oriented community may not inspire friendship in the same way as does a social networking site.

To verify the effectiveness of social networking support for friend dyads, we propose three criteria. First, we define the birth rate of knowledge exchange as the probability for dyads who have not exchanged knowledge before to start to exchange knowledge. Second, we define the activeness of knowledge exchange as the frequency of knowledge exchange between a dyad. Finally, we define the death rate of knowledge exchange as the probability for dyads who exchanged knowledge before to stop doing so. If social networking support is effective, its integration into traditional forum technology shall increase the birth rate and activeness of knowledge exchange, but decrease the death rate.

## 3 Theory and Research Model

## 3.1 Social Capital Theory and Transactive Memory System

To better understand the effect of social networking support on knowledge sharing, we draw on the social capital theory (SCT) (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998) and the transactive memory research (Jarvenpaa & Majchrzak, 2008; Wegner, 1987). Following Nahapiet and dimensions: structural, relational, and cognitive capital. Structural capital is defined as “the overall pattern of connections between actors” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Structural capital represents the pool of one’s access to others in time of need. Relational capital represents the kind of personal relationship people have developed with each other through interactions (Granovetter, 1992), mainly mirrored by trust, norm of reciprocity and identification (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Numerous studies have demonstrated a positive relationship between relational capital and knowledge contributions (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu et al., 2006; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Relational capital indicates the degree that one can mobilize her relationships in the relationship pool. Cognitive capital manifests as shared knowledge representations, languages, and visions (Chiu et al., 2006; Cicourel, 1974) which encourages a common understanding of collective goals and proper ways to behave in a social system (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Cognitive capital influences one’s difficulty in exchanging and absorbing information (Chiu et al., 2006), thus is also a critical factor impacting knowledge exchange behaviors (e.g., Chang & Chuang, 2011; Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

While the SCT offers a typology of social capitals at the individual level, the transactive memory system (TMS) sheds light on the collective effect of knowledge sharing. TMS refers to a cooperative division of labor within a team for encoding, storage, communication and retrieval of knowledge (Wegner, 1987). Through a TMS, team members know who knows what and who knows who knows what and so on (Jarvenpaa & Majchrzak, 2008). Therefore, teams with an effective TMS are better at utilizing members’ expertise and realizing the value of tacit knowledge (Lewis, 2004). Ultimately, such teams are more effective in knowledge

sharing among team members (Akgun, Byrne, Keskin, & Lynn, 2006; Choi, Lee, & Yoo, 2010; Moreland & Argote, 2003).

## 3.2 Research Model and Hypotheses

In the light of SCT and TMS, we believe that the social networking integration into theme-oriented VCoP would produce a positive effect on all three criteria. Figure 1 summarizes our hypotheses.

Social networking integration boosts the birth rate of knowledge exchange for friend dyads. In the contexts of VCoP, a friend is often a person who has the knowledge that one needs, and she does not have to be an actual offline friend. Although such a relationship is often considered a weak tie (Chiu & Wang, 2011), it serves two important purposes. First, it represents a list of experts that one could consult. Second, this weak tie serves as a starting point to develop a closer interpersonal relationship, making future knowledge exchange more easily.

#

Social network establishes the structure of social capital. In theme-oriented VCoP, one cannot “bookmark” a friend, because individual profiles are not public. In an integrated VCoP, one can “collect” and “bookmark” friends. Although such friendship might be a weak tie in the beginning, the recognition of each other still has a profound impact. First, for a knowledge seeker, a friend list provides a pool of experts that one could potentially consult. This pool is those friends as an expert in a topic area. As suggested by relational communication theory (Walther & Burgoon, 1992), when one seeks knowledge from another, one signals trust in the other party, and an intention to establish a stronger relationship (Xu et al., 2010-2011). One also signals future reciprocity. For the knowledge source, accepting a friend relationship indicates the willingness to communicate, to share, and to offer help in time of need. Finally, friend list makes an easy entry for one to read a friend’s posts in one place. Therefore, they would have a better picture of what the friend knows, and what she does not. This knowledge helps to establish a cognitive background for future communication, hence enhances the cognitive capital. Together, social capitals are enhanced through social networking support even when a dyad has not started knowledge exchange.

Non-friend dyads do not have the benefit of social capital based on an explicit friendship link. Therefore, we propose:

H1. After social networking integration, the birth rate of knowledge exchange in friend dyads is higher than that in non-friend dyads.

Social networking integration boosts the activeness of knowledge exchange for friend dyads. For friend dyads who have started exchanging knowledge before the integration, the technically explicit friend list still helps. First, it is now easier to locate and exchange with a friend. One does not need to search for a request post or feedback post in the haystack of threads. Personal profile is an easy entry where the recent dyadic communications are listed (Ma & Agarwal, 2007). The salience of communication with friends enhances a sense of reciprocity and interpersonal relationship. Second, as one gets to know who knows what among one’s friends, in time of need, one needs not to resort to the whole community to post

#

a request, and hope some kind stranger would offer a hand. Instead, one could directly resort to friends for knowledge. A friend is likely to feel more obliged to exchange knowledge. This is the advantage of established relational capital and TMS. Finally, it is more natural for one to show emotional support for friends. Encouragement messages, virtual gifts, and other relationship building techniques help friend dyads to enhance relationship, which in turn facilitates knowledge exchange.

Non-friend dyads would not have the benefit of easy access to each other’s knowledge, transactive memory, or social capital based on explicit friendship link. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2. After social networking integration, the increase in the activeness level of knowledge exchange in friend dyads is higher than that in non-friend dyads.

Social networking integration reduces the death rate of knowledge exchange for friend dyads. In a VCoP, it is typical that one’s knowledge demand changes when the practical needs of a member change. Since members are mostly working employees, when a project finishes, or when job assignment changes, they need new knowledge. Consequently, they may not need to exchange knowledge with previous friends who do not have the appropriate domain knowledge. Therefore, there is a natural death rate to dyadic knowledge exchange. However, social networking integration could reduce this probability. First, an easy access to friends’ profile makes one knows “who knows what” better. Therefore, if one’s knowledge demand changes, one may resort to another friend who has the knowledge, instead of resorting to a stranger. This makes the knowledge exchange more likely to occur among friends. Even if none of one’s friends has the knowledge one needs, a friend might recommend her friends to the seekers, and draw friends of friends closer as direct ties. This establishes a tighter relational network where mutual learning and discussion are encouraged. As a result, even if the friend is not the main knowledge source, she would be more likely to stay in the loop of knowledge exchange.

Non-friend dyads would not have an easy access to transactive memory based on explicit friendship link. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H3.After social networking integration, the death rate of knowledge exchange in friend dyads is lower than that in non-friend dyads.

## 4 Methodology

## 4.1Data Collection

Logclub.com. Logclub was a VCoP established in January, 2003. It was based on the popular open source forum technology Discuz!. It provided a traditional discussion forum with a focus on third-party logistics, supply chain management, and other logistics-related topics. Consistent with Zhang and Watts’ definition for VCoPs (2008), the content of Loglub was tightly organized around the central theme of logistics. Its content was organized around industry-specific logistic issues, specialized areas of logistics, and industrial trends. The website also had a core team of moderators and experts, who encouraged creative contents and moderated theme-irrelevant contents. Among the members who reported their job position, 88% were directly related to logistics. The rest were mainly comprised of students. At the time of study, it had more than 180,000 users and 460,000 threads. Activity level was quite high during our research time.

Logclub initially adopted traditional forum technology as its technical infrastructure. On March 2, 2010, it introduced a new function called “Social Network Home”. This function allowed members to establish a basic personal profile. One can maintain personal information and a friend list. However, at this stage, one still cannot see her own posts and friend’s posts at this home. Even if one clicked into a friend’s profile, one could not see the friend’s posts either. The social networking functions and the forum functions were basically disjointed, although they were hosted under the same roof. Nevertheless, members started to add friends. On February 8, 2011, almost a year later, Logclub finally integrated the two, and members could now see her own posts on the profile page. They could also click into friends’ profile and see theirs. Interactions and updates of friends were also pushed to one’s profile. At the

same time, members could still use the traditional forum interface to read topics and follow threads.

The staged integration of social networking support and forum provided us a unique quasi-experiment opportunity to test our research model. Assume the social networking support were integrated with forum all at once, and assume members had added each other as friends and started to exchange knowledge frequently, such frequent dyadic interactions could be attributed to four possible reasons. (1) Because they were friends, they would naturally communicate more often than non-friend dyads. This could be regarded as the friendship effect. (2) The intensified communications could be due to the integration of forum and social network technology which could enhance social capitals and transactive memory, as explained in our hypotheses. This is the integration effect. (3) The intensified communication could be due to history effect. For instance, a major event in logistic industry after the integration might cause a lot of discussion in the VCoP. (4) Members might gain experience in the VCoP and hence communicate more often. This is the maturation effect. The staged integration had a unique advantage. First, it allowed us to rule out friendship effect. The one year delay offered ample time for friends to recognize each other. Therefore, by focusing on this group of dyads who revealed their friendship before the integration (i.e., friend dyads in this study), and comparing their behavior before and after the integration, we could rule out the friendship effect. Second, it allowed us to rule out history effect. Because both friend dyads and non-friend dyads were subject to the same history effect, the change of interactions between non-friend dyads serves as a measure of natural change that would happen over the time period. Finally, we could compare the length of membership between friend dyads and non-friend dyads to control for the maturation effect. Friend dyads, however, due to the integration effect, were expected to be more active in knowledge exchange than non-friend dyads after controlling the other three effects.

We extracted data from the server database. We first defined two periods, each spanned three months. The first period was before the integration, starting from October 1 to December 31, 2010 (hereafter T1). The second period was after the integration, starting from

April 1 to June 30, 2011 (hereafter T2). The reason that the first period stopped not before the day of integration (i.e., February 8th, 2011) but about one month before, was to avoid the Chinese New Year. The reason that the second period did not start right after the integration, but started about two months after the day of integration, was to allow users to get to know the integrated function, till it reached a natural and stable usage level. No other significa indicators for online communities (Cothrel, 2000), i.e., PV (i.e., page view) and UV (i.e., unique visitor) of Logclub. In both T1 and T2, the two indicators were stable.

Active members were defined as those who wrote a post, made a comment or added a friend in both T1 and T2. In the beginning of T1, there were 71,992 active members. We defined friend dyads as pairs of active members who added each other to their friend list before the beginning of T1, and they maintained the friendship throughout the whole data collection periods. Our sample employed all members from the database, as long as they fulfilled our selection criteria (i.e., being active). Our sample excluded members who became inactive in T2. This selection criterion ensured all members in the sample could participate in the VCoP if they wished, and ensured the change of knowledge exchange behavior was not due to external factors that prevented them from participating in the VCoP.

Non-friend dyads did not naturally exist in database. For an active member, her non-friend dyads were defined as all possible pairs between her and other active members who were not in her friend list. Therefore, there were many more non-friend dyads than friend non-friend dyads was consistent over T1 and T2, hence there was no change in sample size over time.

Knowledge exchange in a dyad was measured by the number of exchanges between the two, including all post and comment items the two exchanged. These exchanges might be in the form of question-and-answer or post-and-comments. We did not differentiate their roles as knowledge seeker or source, for they could co-create the knowledge and both benefit from the exchange.

The server database also recorded user’s registration time, number of posts, number of friends and number of threads. Since our unit of analysis is dyad, the dyadic differences of the four factors were included as control variables. The difference in registration time was defined as the absolute number of days between one’s registration date and the others. Similarly, the number of posts, number of friends, and number of threads were defined as their absolute difference at the beginning of T1 respectively. We included these control variables because the homophily argument in organizational communication (Tsui and Egan, 1992) suggests that people of similar attributes tend to have a stronger affinity in their communication. Other demographic information of users, such as age, gender, and position in firms, were not included due to the prevalence of missing data.

We then report on the test of hypothesis 2. The Difference-in-Differences (DID) approach was used. This approach is particularly suitable in a quasi-experiment setting when the data is subject to history effect, such as the effect of exogenous events in a time period (Stewart & Swaffield, 2008). This method is considered more efficient than instrument variable approach since it eliminates the need for strong exclusion restrictions (e.g., Blundell & Costa Dias, 2000; Girma & Görg, 2007). In our case, we were basically comparing the difference in dyadic exchange for two groups (i.e., friend dyads and non-friend dyads) over time (i.e., before and after integration). Let the activeness of friend dyads at T1 be $\mathrm { Y } _ { f I }$ and that of non-friend dyads be $\mathrm { Y } _ { n I }$ , similarly, let the activeness of friend dyads at T2 be $\mathrm { Y } _ { f 2 }$ and that of non-friend dyads be $\Upsilon _ { n 2 } , ( \Upsilon _ { f 2 } - \Upsilon _ { n 2 } ) - ( \Upsilon _ { f l } - \Upsilon _ { n l } )$ is the deference-in-difference. The change of gaps between the two groups represents the effect of “treatment” , i.e., the social networking integration effect which is applicable only to friend dyads. In other words, the non-friend dyads served as a control group while the friend dyads were the treatment group. The change in the non-friend dyads could be attributed to the history effect which the friend dyads were also subject to.

While the DID model controls the history effect on friend and non-friend dyads, it does not control for the maturation effect of members in the two groups. Therefore, it is necessary to ensure that the two groups were subject to the same maturation effect. In our sample, the 41 friend dyads were composed of 32 unique members, while the 142 non-friend dyads were composed of 112 unique members. We tested the maturation effect of the two groups by group size, we conducted 20 bootstrap samples of a size of 32 from 112 members in the latter group. The 95% confidence interval for the mean registration time of the latter groups contained the mean registration time of the former group. Hence we concluded that the two groups were subject to the same maturation effect.

Therefore, with the appropriate controls for history effect and maturation effect, the expected larger gap between the knowledge exchanges in friend and non-friend dyads could be attributed to the “manipulation”, i.e., system integration.

Statistically, the DID interpretation is equivalent to the following regression model (refer to Abadie, 2005; Meyer, 1995):

$$

$$

where i denotes a dyad, t denotes the time period (t=1 in $\scriptstyle \mathrm { T } 1 ; t = 2 \sin \mathrm { T } 2 )$ ). $F r i e n d _ { 1 }$ is the treatment dummy variable which is 1 for a friend dyad and 0 for a non-friend dyad. is a dummy variable denoting the time before or after social networking integration. It is 0 for the period prior to integration, and 1 after. $\mathbf { X } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ represents a vector of control variables with $\delta$ being their corresponding estimated coefficients. $E _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ is the error term to control for unobserved heterogeneity (Rishika, Kumar, Janakiraman, & Bezawada, 2013). $\beta _ { 1 }$ represents the friendship effect. $\beta _ { 2 }$ represents the history and maturation effect that occur over time. $\beta _ { 3 }$ , the main parameter of interest, represents the social networking integration effect. It represents the additional effect of system integration after partialing out the main effects of friendship and time difference.

## 5 Discussion and Implications

## 5.1 Discussion of Findings

This study seeks to propose a model to explain the effect of social networking integration on knowledge exchange in VCoPs. Based on social capital theory and transactive memory system research, we hypothesize that the effect of integration would be particularly salient for friend dyads in the community. Social networking support would make friends who did not communicate more likely to start knowledge exchange, it would make friends who had prior knowledge exchange more active in the activity, and it would make friends who had prior knowledge exchange less likely to stop exchange.

Our analysis of a real VCoP lent strong support to all three hypotheses. The differences in the birth rate, activeness, and death rate of exchange clearly indicated the effect of system integration. The unique staged implementation of social networking support in the VCoP and the use of difference-in-difference analysis allowed us to exclude alternative explanations of friendship effect and history effect. In sum, we empirically confirmed the effect of social networking technology on knowledge exchange because it can enhance members’ social capital and transactive memory.

With regard to the control variables, a larger difference between two participants the earlier member’s interest. A later member might judge the interest of an early member by her posts. However, the earlier member might have changed her interest. This mismatch in interest would decrease interactions. The difference in the number of threads had a positive effect on knowledge exchange. A plausible reason is that one member of the dyad is likely to be very active. Her activeness helps maintain an on-going exchange relationship between the two.

## 5.2 Theoretical and Practical Implications

This study offers interesting theoretical insights. First, it enriches the social view of knowledge management by investigating the dyadic outcome of social relationships. Past literatures have confirmed the impact of social factors on knowledge sharing at individual level (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chen, 2007; Chiu et al., 2006; Choi & Scott, 2013; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), but few have analyzed their dyadic outcome. However, in a social network, individual level behavior does not imply the same aggregate outcomes, due to the interdependent and sub-additive nature of collective action (Libai, Muller, & Peres, 2010). It is therefore important to provide direct evidence at the aggregate level. This study takes the research one step further in this direction.

Second, this study investigates the impact of an actual social technology on knowledge sharing. Usability and sociability represent two fundamental value dimensions of virtual communities (Phang et al. 2009). Both usability and sociability can be greatly facilitated by technologies. However, except for a few limited investigation of TAM factors (Lin, 2006; Phang et al., 2009; Sharratt & Usoro, 2003), the literature surprisingly shies away from more in-depth study of new technologies such as social networking support. This lack of research

#

leaves practitioners to wonder what they shall do in order to enhance system usability and sociability. This study contributes to bridging this gap by confirming the effectiveness of social networking technology. It showcases one effective route to relational knowledge management systems (Xu et al., 2010-2011).

For practitioners, this study first provides a justification for technological investment Because the return of KMS is often hard to measure, the investment on KMS requires more direct justifications beyond members’ subjective perceptions. For the investment of social networking support, this study provides a few measurable outcome indicators which IT managers could use for justification. Second, we propose a set of outcome measures that captures the birth rate, intensity, and death rate of dyadic knowledge exchange. This set is relatively comprehensive. It is not only useful for the justification of social networking support, but also useful for evaluating other technological enhancements if they aim to enhance interactions among members. Finally, this study suggests that social networking support is a functional technological approach to enhance knowledge sharing in VCoP.

## 5.3 Limitations and Future Research

Our findings shall be interpreted with limitations of the study. First, this study focuses on the quantity of knowledge exchange. Future research may investigate on the quality of knowledge exchange as well, because knowledge quality is also an important outcome when a community becomes mature (Chiu et al., 2006). The second limitation is that the psychological mechanism of social networking integration is not directly verified through members’ self-report. As a quasi-experiment study, we did not have the luxury to survey members at the time of the system integration, let alone manipulation checks. Future research may use other research method to further verify the psychological mechanisms in such a process. The third limitation is the lack of robustness check with other time spans. This study compared knowledge exchange three months before and after system integration. Although the UV and PV (Figure 2) in the 3-month spans suggested that at the aggregate level member behavior was stable over time, it would be ideal to try other time spans for robustness check. We tried 2-month spans, but the sample size was too small for statistical analysis. A 4-month span was infeasible due to another significant system adjustment in Logclub. Finally, this study focused only on one outcome of social networking support for VCoP, which is dyadic knowledge exchange. Since knowledge sharing could occur in a broadcast fashion, and the audience of dyadic exchanges is beyond the dyad, the ripple effect of social networking support can be much larger than its immediate effect at the dyadic level. Future research could also explore the broader impact of social networking support.

## 6 Conclusion

Would the integration of social networking support and forum-based VCoP benefit knowledge exchange in the community? If so, why? This study provides an answer to these questions. Based on social capital theory and transactive memory system research, this study suggests that social networking support will enhance knowledge exchange in VCoP. In a field study with a unique and advantageous data set, this study found that social networking support intensifies dyadic knowledge exchange among friends, and reduces the probabilities that dyads stop exchange. Our findings empirically confirm the effect of social networking support in enhancing social capitals in VCoP, provide a justification for investment in such a technology, and offer guidance for practitioners to evaluate the outcomes of a technology investment in the context of knowledge management in VCoP.

● We provide a theoretical justification of why online social networks boost knowledge exchange based on social capital theory and the transactive memory system research.

We propose a set of dyadic behavioral measures that captures the birth rate, intensity, and death rate of dyadic knowledge exchange, which are measurable outcome indicators for future knowledge sharing research.
