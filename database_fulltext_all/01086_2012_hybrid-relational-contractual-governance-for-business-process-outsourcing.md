Abstra ct: We examined 335 business process outsourcing (BPO) ventures to understand the effect of contractual and relational governance factors on BPO satisfaction from the client’s perspective. While both contractual and relational factors explain significant variance in BPO satisfaction, relational factors dominate. By examining interactions between key contractual and relational mechanisms, we found that elements of the two governance approaches operate as substitutes with respect to BPO satisfaction. Specifically, the relational mechanism, trust, was found to substitute for contractually specified activity expectations, goal expectations, and contractual flexibility. Similarly, the relational mechanism, information exchange, was found to substitute for contractually specified activity expectations and goal expectations. Finally, the relational mechanism, conflict resolution, was found to substitute for contractually specified goal expectations. Our results can be applied to more effectively realize controls in outsourcing contexts and to design governance systems that integrate contractual and relational governance mechanisms based on the characteristics of client–vendor relationships.

Key w ords a nd ph ra ses: business process outsourcing, controls, formal contract, hybrid governance, relational governance, services.

Business processes involve interrela ted a ctivities that transform information inputs to meet business objectives, thereby creating business value [76]. Given the central role of information in business processes, we have witnessed rapid-fire information technology (IT) innovations for the execution and management of business processes. Indeed, developments in IT are one of the major enablers of the disaggregation of business processes and their outsourcing (e.g., [78, 103]). Capitalizing on the opportunities offered by the IT innovations, specialized services providers for a vast range of business processes have emerged. These specialized service providers possess expertise in the business domain of the processes (e.g., accounting rules, regulations, and standards for an accounting process) as well as in the IT domain (e.g., the technologies that can support the management and execution of activities). The phenomenon of business process outsourcing (BPO) has evolved to where the outsourcing of entire business processes by clients to vendors is suggested as a means for clients to achieve strategic and nonstrategic benefits [11, 26, 37, 75, 109].

The upside potential of BPO is accompanied with downside risks that have challenged firms from realizing benefits [5, 31]. A Gartner study [23] reports that organizations often accomplish initial cost savings by reducing vertical integration but then fail to realize benefits because of inadequate governance capabilities. Similarly, Demong [20] reports that because of inadequate understanding on how to govern BPO arrangements, the average client company experiences significant “value leakage,” thereby dramatically negating gross benefits. In particular, the complexity of business processes, the lack of BPO management experience, the tendency to focus on precontract negotiation while neglecting the need to manage the relationship dynamically, and the risk of vendors’ poaching sensitive customer information are complex issues challenging BPO governance [5, 75].

While past work on IT outsourcing is relevant for how BPO governance should be managed, there are important distinctions between BPO and IT outsourcing that make it important to examine BPO governance and elaborate our understanding of effective governance for the BPO context [21, 37, 59, 62, 76]. First, while IT outsourcing has been primarily motivated by a client wishing to focus on core competencies or reduce IT costs [61, 113], BPO is pursued by clients for a broader range of business objectives, including cost reduction, innovation, and business transformation [76, 111]. Second, while IT outsourcing focuses on the client’s IT resources and activities (e.g., infrastructure, operations, applications development), BPO pertains to the client’s core business. Third, the specialized knowledge to be integrated between the client and vendor are different for BPO and IT outsourcing. For IT outsourcing, the client and vendor need to integrate specialized knowledge for the IT resources and activities to be coordinated across the two firms [84]. For BPO, the client and vendor need to integrate (1) the client’s knowledge about its instantiation of the business process and corresponding BPO requirements with (2) the vendor’s knowledge about the business process (accumulated in working with clients) as well as how to execute and manage the business process with IT. Also, unlike information systems (IS) project outsourcing that has discrete start and end points (although there can be schedule slippages), BPO relationships are likely to be less well defined in terms of a start and end point and could be ongoing for a long duration with contract renewals.

Because BPO has the unique potential to offer strategic and nonstrategic benefits, comes along with distinctive risks, and has salient characteristics that differentiate it from IT outsourcing, organizations must carefully design governance mechanisms for their BPO initiatives [5, 75]. Contractual mechanisms, which have played an important role in IT outsourcing, should still continue to be important because they legally define obligations and provide exchange parties with an instrument of control and escalation. However, contractual governance alone is probably insufficient for BPO as complete contracts are most effective when uncertainty is low [27] and output measurability or task programmability is high [82], and this is almost certainly not the case with respect to BPO. Indeed, prior research suggests that these “ideal situations” seldom occur in embedded interfirm relationships in general (e.g., [46, 99]) and in strategic outsourcing arrangements, such as BPO, in particular [22, 38, 75, 112].

Since BPO benefits depend heavily on leveraging capabilities by aligning structures, processes, and resources between exchange parties, intensive cooperation should be necessary to achieve them. Consequently, in the BPO context, social embeddedness between the client and vendor should be critical, as relational norms and practices enable clan control, exchange of private information and knowledge, and trust-based mitigation of risk. Not surprisingly, leading BPO vendors are focusing not only on contracts but also on cooperative governance practices to achieve targeted business outcomes for the client. Best-practice vendors’ focus on cooperative governance practices is based on their recognition that the client relies on the vendor and the vendor relies on the client to develop shared understanding, implement changes, and achieve business outcomes [51]. For example, Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), a leading BPO vendor, uses a mix of contracting and collaboration practices to manage risks, work closely with the client, understand its business processes, and provide effective BPO solutions. In remarking about Allianz Life Insurance’s successfully outsourcing processes (i.e., policy administration, benefits claims, and audits for its variable and fixed business lines) to TCS, Allianz’s Senior Vice President, Cathy Mahone remarked, “What I’m most excited about in working with TCS is that they are side by side with us figuring out how we can get even better—how can we improve our service for our customers and reduce our cycle times. So we’re very excited about this partnership and where we can take things” [104].

Given the importance of understanding how to combine contractual and relational governance, the literature on interfirm governance suggests that the two forms of governance (i.e., relational and contractual) should not be simply construed at a coarse level as substitutes and that it is critical to understand at a nuanced level how discrete elements of relational and contractual governance interact with each other to affect key outcomes [46, 99]. In probing the nature of these interactions for BPO governance, we focus on client BPO satisfaction (hereafter referred to as BPO satisfaction) as our outcome variable of interest as it is arguably one of the key outcomes in interfirm relationships.

As both forms of governance are likely to be important for BPO, it is essential to understand how to combine elements of relational and contractual governance in order to produce greater client satisfaction with BPO ventures. However, while past research on interorganizational relationships has elaborated on the factors of relational governance (e.g., [108]) and contractual governance (e.g., [71]), there is limited understanding of how relational and contractual governance factors interact in specific contexts to affect critical outcomes (e.g., BPO satisfaction). Although arguments and some evidence have been offered for viewing relational and contractual governance mechanisms as complements (e.g., [38, 87]), equally persuasive theorizing and findings support the perspective that contractual and relational governance operate as substitutes (e.g., [10, 32]). This tension suggests that a fine-grained approach is required to examine the interaction of relational and contractual governance factors with respect to a given outcome measure. Accordingly, our focus is to understand how best to influence BPO satisfaction by using a mix of both relational and contractual governance mechanisms, leading to the following research question:

RQ: How do relational and contractual governance factors interact to affect BPO satisfaction?

In order to control for industry and cross-country cultural differences, we analyzed BPO arrangements in the banking sector within a single country to address our research question [13]. The financial services sector was a logical choice, as it is the second largest buyer of outsourcing services [23]. Based on an analysis of 335 BPO ventures at 215 German banks, we observed that both relational and contractual governance factors explain substantial variance in BPO satisfaction. Most important, we found that relational and contractual factors interact with each other to affect BPO satisfaction, such that certain relational governance mechanisms function as substitutes for particular contractual governance mechanisms in their effect on BPO satisfaction. Our results provide insights on how to design governance mechanisms that increase the client’s satisfaction with BPO ventures.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We first synthesize the literature on BPO satisfaction, and relational and contractual governance, to develop our model and hypotheses. Next, we describe the empirical study, and the procedures used to test our hypotheses. We then discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our study.

## Theoretical Foundations

A business process is defined a s a set of logica lly rela ted ta sks performed to achieve a defined business outcome [19]. BPO is defined as the delegation of one or more business processes to third-party providers, including the software and hardware that support these processes [21].

## BPO Satisfaction

According to Dibbern et al. [21], outsourcing success can be viewed either as satisfaction, the realization of objectives (e.g., cost savings), or the performance of processes/ operations that are outsourced. We focus on satisfaction of the outsourcer with the outsourced process [81, 100], as it is an important, simple, and well-defined measure of success (see, e.g., [41, 64]).

Figure 1 presents our research model, which includes both the direct effects of relational and contractual governance factors on BPO satisfaction and the interaction effects that we posit and set forth as hypotheses. However, we do not state formal hypotheses for the direct effects; these effects are less interesting and comparatively better understood as we discuss in the next two sections where we introduce the contractual and relational factors. In contrast, the joint influence of relational and contractual factors remain relatively unexplored, creating the need for us to elaborate our theoretical understanding about how they need to be combined in the context of BPO arrangements or to develop a midlevel theoretical model about how relational and contractual governance factors together influence BPO satisfaction [110]. Our hypotheses therefore focus only on the interactions of specific relational and contractual governance mechanisms, as this is where our primary contribution lies. In the sections that follow, we introduce the specific contractual and relational governance factors that are of interest, provide the rationale for their selection, and focus on the theoretical justification for the hypothesized interaction effects.

## Contractual Governance Constructs

Contracting theory has evolved from an initial focus on designing complete contracts to recognizing, and addressing, the challenges of designing complete contracts for the exchange of products and services when the good (or service) to be exchanged cannot be fully described as it depends on a future state that is yet to be realized (as is the case for BPO services that are experienced based on future states of the client and vendor). Traditional contracting theory, focusing on complete contracts, considered it costless for parties to identify contingencies and design suitable covenants, with the guiding principle that contracts can never be too detailed or long [105]. Based on principles of bounded rationality and the derivate implication that there are significant costs to gathering and processing information, the feasibility of designing complete contracts has been challenged with the argument that all contracts are incomplete for goods (services) that are exchanged at a future state that is unknown [45].

Related to the idea of incomplete contracts, Fried’s [28] promise-centered view of contracts is based on the premise that it is impossible to design contracts that address all future contingencies and uncertainties. According to this view, the very nature of contracts is to provide a catalog of promises (i.e., overall goal and individual activity expectations) that exchange partners have agreed on. In principle, the promise-centered view draws on classical contract law, by constituting legal rights for exchange partners, that has informed current thinking on contractual governance (e.g., [33]). It is also consistent with Macaulay’s [70] and Macneil’s [71, 72, 73] perspectives on relational contracting in that reliance on legal boundaries decreases the benefits of interaction with partners as a result of reduced flexibility. Hence, while tight contracts are favored as single control mechanisms for one-time, discrete transactions, looser contracts are preferred in embedded relationships where uncertainties often arise during the course of the exchange and must be responded to if performance objectives are to be realized.

Drawing on the promise-centered view of contracts, we focus on three contractual governance factors: goal expectations, activity expectations, and contractual flexibility (see Table 1). These factors represent the main purpose (goal expectations), performance targets for execution (activity expectations), and the ability to modify contracts (contractual flexibility). Because BPO services are complex, ex ante hard to describe completely, and exchanged at uncertain and unknown future states of the client and vendor, BPO governance should be facilitated when the exchange parties establish foundational shared understanding about the purpose (i.e., goals expectations) and the standards for conduct (i.e., activity expectations) [37, 113]. By explicating the purpose and the code of conduct, contracts can play a pivotal role in establishing shared foundational understanding among decision makers and staff members from the client and vendor firms, thereby providing them with a clear framework for direction, action, and evaluation [15, 37]. Also, given the strategic, economic, and technological uncertainties that characterize a BPO relationship, BPO governance should be facilitated when the exchange parties include in the contract change provisions to make adjustments given foreseeable contingencies and to renegotiate and redefine the contract given unforeseen outcomes and circumstances [12, 37, 113]. Accordingly, we expect goal expectations, activity expectations, and contractual flexibility to be relevant in influencing BPO satisfaction. However, the literature does not identify how each of these contractual factors interact with relational governance factors, thereby leaving a gap in our understanding on how specific contractual factors should be (de)emphasized in the co-presence of specific relational governance factors.

## Relational Governance Constructs

Despite the importance and value of contracts, outsourcing research has acknowl edged that reliance on a legal contract alone is insufficient, given the complexities of real-life outsourcing arrangements and the rapid changes in technology and organizational environments [54, 62]. Specifying long-term exchange relations, such as outsourcing arrangements, is complex and inherently incomplete because they have to cover unanticipated obligations. Thus, governing beyond traditional contractual clauses toward a closer relationship-focused management that operates “within the spirit of the contract” is inherently necessary [25, 56, 87, 103]. For example, Unilever Europe and IBM’s touted long-term collaborative BPO relationship was established when Unilever Europe wanted to simplify and standardize its fragmented financial processes and IT (i.e., general ledger, fixed assets, accounts payable, expense claims processing, bill to cash, and higher-level dispute resolution) across 24 countries. The industry recognized, award-winning governance of this BPO relationship has accommodated flexibility in goals and activities to develop an effective solution and achieve the buy-in of various stakeholders. It also established important committees (e.g., joint executive committee, service delivery committee, customer advisory board) to promote the sharing of information, development of trust across stakeholders, and the handling of complaints or disputes [89]. As this example illustrates, relationally governed exchanges rely on social processes that ensure fulfillment of obligations [109]. Of course, formal contractual elements can influence the nature of social processes in interfirm exchanges [38].

Several approaches have been put forth to describe relational governance (e.g., [46, 112]). One conceptualization that has received significant attention is Uzzi’s [109] categorization, which synthesizes the literature of relational embeddedness and isolates information exchange, trust, and conflict resolution as key properties of relational governance (see Table 1). Indeed, in the context of interfirm relationships for IT products and services, these relational governance factors have been shown to be important for both IT outsourcing and offshoring success [38, 90]. Trust has been found to be influential in the success of IT outsourcing and offshoring relationships as well as BPO relationships because it alleviates concerns about opportunism, reduces the burden of monitoring, provides confidence in the partners’ competence, and promotes partners’ pursuing opportunities and sharing risks [41, 53, 64, 90]. Information exchange has also been identified as a key determinant of IT outsourcing and offshoring success because of the importance of communication and sharing of private information to develop and evolve partners’ shared understanding about needs, capabilities, and performance [41, 90]. Finally, conflict resolution has been shown to influence positively the outcomes for BPO relationships since conflicts are inevitable given partners’ goal disparities, cross-cultural and organizational culture differences, and changes in partners’ resource bases (e.g., personnel, IT resources) [37]. Accordingly, we expect information exchange, trust, and conflict resolution to influence BPO satisfaction and focus on uncovering how they change the influence of the contractual factors on BPO satisfaction.

## Substitutive Effects Between Relational and Contractual Governance Factors

Past research on interorganizational relationships has explored the interplay of relational and contractual governance (see, e.g., [42, 87]), and there is some evidence suggesting that, in general, relational and contractual governance can act as substitutes (e.g., [10, 32]). As noted earlier, however, there is also some evidence for viewing relational and contractual governance mechanisms as complements (see, e.g., [42, 87]). This tension suggests the need for a more fine-grained approach to examine the interaction of relational and contractual governance factors. The espoused “substitution view” suggests that relational norms are a less expensive alternative to contracts and that contracts can even be interpreted as a sign of mistrust causing (instead of protecting from) opportunistic behavior [32, 69]. Extending this line of work, our hypotheses pertain to the specific relational and contractual governance factors that, we argue, should operate as substitutes in affecting BPO satisfaction, a key psychological outcome reflecting the client’s accumulated experiences in the relationship. Therefore, we now examine the substitutive effects of specific relational and contractual governance factors, beginning with information exchange and goal expectations.

## Goal Expectations and Information Exchange

The goals of a BPO venture can be specified in the contract and can be used to achieve shared understanding about the expectations of the client and vendor. For example, BPO contracts can specify key goals to be cost reduction, service quality, and access to expertise, and they can include clauses to provide specificity so that goals are unambiguous. By explicating overall goals and objectives in contracts, parties can rely on agreed courses of action that are designed to generate benefits for both parties [71]. It should be noted that explicating goals and objectives does not mean creating rigid contracts that prevent adjustments in the face of contingencies that may arise, a point that we discuss later. What it does mean is specifying goals and objectives so as to create a shared understanding of what is to be accomplished. Written goal expectations can serve as an explicated assessment criterion for evaluating the benefits achieved by the BPO venture and can also help guide day-to-day operations.

However, it is unclear as to how the importance of goal expectations should change as a function of the level of information exchange in a BPO relationship. Prior research has shown that detailed or fine-grained information sharing has a positive effect on the success of IT outsourcing ventures (e.g., [41, 63]). In the BPO context, plans and procedures regarding changes in process inputs, outputs, or technological uncertainties are more likely to be adapted effectively when parties exchange information on process specifics and particularities. Since BPO ventures are subject to technological and business uncertainties [75], it is difficult to specify goals completely for them or to adjust goals quickly when contingencies and unforeseen outcomes arise. Under such circumstances, information exchange can clarify goals and reduce goal ambiguity [48, 53], thereby substituting for goal expectations. For example, the prioritization of goals for the duration of the contract may not be fully specified in the contract. Over the course of a BPO contract period, unforeseen events and changes in partners’ strategies and resource conditions may necessitate the reprioritization of objectives in response. Business recovery or continuity responses, as an illustration, may require the outsourcing partner to trade off cost reduction objectives for process performance goals. An acceptable reprioritization, from the client’s perspective, may be a function of what is currently happening in other areas of the business. Facilitating an understanding of whether and how to reprioritize the objectives for the outsourced business process as a result of contingencies depends on exchanging information between the business and its outsourcing partner. Such information exchange also fosters mutual understanding on the objectives of the relationship [90], reducing the importance of completely specifying the relationship’s goals ex ante, Accordingly, we expect the influence of contractually specified goal expectations on BPO satisfaction to be diminished as information exchange between the vendor and client increases. Thus, we posit a substitutive relationship between information exchange and goal expectations in terms of their impact on BPO satisfaction:

Hypothesis 1: Information exchange substitutes for goal expectations in increasing BPO satisfaction (i.e., the positive effect of goal expectations is diminished as information exchange increases).

## Activity Expectations and Trust

Activity expectations (as specified in service-level agreements, or SLAs) help outsourcers to precisely define expectations at a more detailed level. By assigning granular obligations, opportunistic behavior that could have a deleterious effect on outsourcing satisfaction can be safeguarded against through the ongoing monitoring of the performance of activities. SLAs have been identified as an important contributor to success in the IT outsourcing context [49, 57].

While there is an explicit recognition in the literature that activity expectations can safeguard against opportunism and enhance client satisfaction, it is unclear as to how this relationship changes as a function of the level of trust that operates in a BPO relationship. In IT outsourcing studies, trust has been identified as the predominant relationship factor [64, 97]. Zaheer et al. [115] suggest that trust allows parties to focus on long-term objectives, with less worry about day-to-day issues. It suppresses opportunism, increases cooperation, and enables risk taking. This long-term perspective and the ability to develop clan control align with the characteristics of the BPO context (e.g., inherent incomplete contracting, strategic dependence, and risk of poaching sensitive information). In the BPO context, we suggest that trust and SLAs should operate as substitutes with respect to BPO satisfaction. One explanation for this relationship is that the monitoring of SLAs for complex business processes places a significant administrative burden on managers who must oversee, in detail, routine operational activities, thus decreasing their satisfaction. In the absence of trust, activity expectations specify lower-level objectives that must be monitored to avoid the moral hazard problem in interorganizational relationships [49]. When trust is present, however, it enables the governance of these lower-order activities without as much emphasis on the ex ante specification of activity expectations and also enables a focus on higher-level objectives. Consequently, trust can substitute for contractually specified activity expectations by replacing contractual obligations through “obligations by handshake” [1, 25]. However, it is unlikely that outsourcers will rely exclusively on either trust-based or obligation-based governance mechanisms [75]. If trust is low, then the need for activity expectations to safeguard against opportunistic behaviors should be high. In contrast, if trust is high, then the need for activity expectations should be low. Thus, we expect a substitutive relationship between trust and activity expectations with respect to BPO satisfaction:

Hypothesis 2: Trust substitutes for activity expectations in positively influencing BPO satisfaction (i.e., the positive effect of activity expectations is diminished as trust increases).

## Goal Expectations and Trust

As discussed earlier, well-specified goals and objectives can reduce ambiguity [68] and increase performance outcomes [67], but in BPO contexts, which are marked by complexity [5], partners may not be able to specify a complete set of goals and objectives for the relationship [77]. Still, the outsourcing firm faces the threat of opportunism when goals are not explicated and the vendor and client have divergent organizational interests that can lead the vendor to act with self-interest that is detrimental to the client’s goals [70]. As such, trust substitutes for the need to specify goals completely, ex ante, in a contract for two reasons: (1) it reduces the likelihood of the vendor acting in self-interest that is detrimental to the client [46], and (2) it enables the partnering firms to pursue a strategy of mutual adjustment in refining and pursuing goals, knowing that “obligations by handshake” are likely to be honored [83]. This leads us to hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: Trust substitutes for goal expectations in positively influencing BPO satisfaction (i.e., the positive effect of goal expectations is diminished as trust increases).

## Goal Expectations and Conflict Resolution

Sooner or later, some conflicts arise in interorganizational relationships [24], and the way conflicts are settled has great implications for future collaboration [4]. Given the complexity and interdependence of business processes, conflict resolution is particularly important in BPO ventures. Having well-defined procedures to resolve conflict enables BPO partners to communicate more effectively, solve problems, and learn from experience. Reference to agreed-upon, contractually specified goal expectations can be an effective means for resolving disagreements [28, 37], as defining the BPO’s goals with specificity reduces the variance that is acceptable [63]. However, given the complexity of the BPO context, it may be difficult to specify the goal clearly for every contingency that may arise. In such situations, relational mechanisms for resolving disputes through a process of give-and-take between partners, in which parties negotiate their respective interests in order to reach a consensus or compromise, can be effective [95]. For example, partners may develop a process of negotiation in which a fair and complete hearing of the respective positions of both the client and the vendor is the norm. Such a process of facilitating open dialogue to arrive at an appropriate solution can, to some extent, replace the need for formally specified ex ante goals. In this way, conflict resolution can substitute for the contractual specification of goal expectations by providing the ability to resolve imminent disputes in the course of the BPO relationship:

Hypothesis 4: Conflict resolution substitutes for goal expectations in positively influencing BPO satisfaction (i.e., the positive effect of goal expectations is diminished as conflict resolution increases).

## Activity Expectations and Information Exchange

Activity expectations provide a more granular elaboration (than goal expectations) of how operational activities and performance are evaluated in the exchange relationship [37]. As contingencies arise during the outsourcing contract, new cooperation and shared understandings are needed to rebalance operational expectations [83]. One way to develop shared understanding of evolving circumstances is by sharing credible, private information among partners [90]. Information exchange represents the extent of proactive sharing of meaningful and timely information between exchange partners [57]. New shared understanding based on the exchange of relevant and welltimed information should compensate for a lack of codified activity expectations in understanding how well operational tasks are being executed [25, 108]. As such, established procedures for information exchange between partners that dynamically update operational expectations should replace the need to formalize activity expectations, leading us to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5: Information exchange substitutes for activity expectations in positively influencing BPO satisfaction (i.e., the positive effect of activity expectations is diminished as information exchange increases).

## Contractual Flexibility and Trust

Much has been written about the incompleteness of contracts [33, 44]. In the BPO context, we expect business or technological uncertainties to arise. This requires structural provisions of flexibility in contracts so that business partners can make the necessary adjustments to deal with unanticipated situations. For example, a flexible contract may include a meet-or-release clause based on changes in pricing and supply conditions in the BPO market. Alternatively, it can include a clause on risk sharing as a result of delays in project completion or quality problems or on mechanisms to accommodate a change in the client’s business requirements. Contractual flexibility is a codified means of providing for the give-and-take necessary for responding to the uncertainties in an outsourced business process environment. In dynamic environments, such as BPO, decisions may need to be revisited, and in order to respond to unforeseen contingencies, flexibility needs to be designed into the contract [12]. Despite contingency clauses that specify procedures for proposing change, there is not a formalized procedure for how the partners can arrive at an equitable outcome. Trust, a relational governance factor, enables partners to make adjustments in response to uncertainties because it fosters a long-term orientation in interorganizational relationships, encouraging investments specific to the relationship and the development of rich interaction routines between partners [83]. Moreover, a long-term orientation reduces the risk of opportunistic behavior and increases partner confidence that problems in the relationship will be resolved in a satisfactory way to the benefit of all partners in the relationship [29]. As such, trust operates as a governance mechanism in interorganizational relationships through informal arrangements and self-reinforcement rather than strict formal control [93]. As a result, trust substitutes for the necessity to monitor the vendor’s activities and the need to design detailed contract clauses on partners’ contingent actions and responsibilities [42]. In other words, given the inherent uncertainties that BPO relationships must confront over time, “if one party trusts the other, there is simply little need for contractually specifying actions” [87, p. 711]. Thus, trust should substitute for the effect of contractual flexibility on BPO satisfaction:

Hypothesis 6: Trust substitutes for contractual flexibility in positively influencing BPO satisfaction (i.e., the effect of contractual flexibility is diminished as trust increases).

## Empirical Design

## Data Collection

Our sam ple wa s draw n from a popula tion of 2,344 ba nks that were registered to conduct business in Germany. From this population, a sample of the 500 largest banks was selected based on total reported assets. The cumulative assets of these banks account for more than 90 percent of total assets in the German banking industry [8, 52]. As the unit of analysis is an outsourced business process, we identified BPO of four banking processes for investigation: settlement of securities, consumer credit, credit cards, and domestic payment. These processes are ideal candidates for BPO as they are digitally enabled and represent core processes that banks typically outsource [62].

Our questionnaire was sent to managers of Germany’s top 500 banks who were responsible for one of the four identified business processes. To ensure that the questionnaire was targeted to the most informed respondent, all the banks were contacted by phone to identify the managers responsible for each of the four business processes. Finally, 1,931 questionnaires were sent out. Because not all the processes in each bank are outsourced, the questionnaire first asked managers to indicate whether the business process was outsourced. The managers who indicated that it was outsourced were requested to complete the rest of the questionnaire, while the others were requested to simply inform us via e-mail or telephone that the process was not outsourced. Overall, we received information that 1,027 processes were outsourced and 904 were not. In total, 335 usable questionnaires from 215 banks were returned. Considering 1,027 outsourced processes, this implies a response rate of 32.6 percent.

The primary reasons for nonresponse were lack of time or interest. To test for nonresponse bias, we compared early and late respondents in terms of the mean values of demographic variables and study constructs. Results of one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed no mean differences in these variables across early and late respondents (Satisfaction: F = 0.002, p = 0.96; Information Exchange: F = 1.23, p = 0.29; Trust: F = 0.21, p = 0.65; Conflict Resolution: F = 0.93, p = 0.34; Goal Expectations: F = 0.07, p = 0.79; Activity Expectations: F = 0.06, p = 0.81; Contractual Flexibility: F = 1.71, p = 0.20; BPO Experience: F = 1.19, p = 0.28; Organization Size: F = 0.28, $p = 0 . 6 0 )$ , suggesting that nonresponse bias was not a significant threat.

## Hierarchical Regression Analysis for Hypotheses Testing

To test the hypotheses, we applied hierarchical regression analysis using ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation. We examined if OLS estimation was suitable for hypotheses testing because in our sample there are firms that provided information on one BPO arrangement and firms that provided information on more than one BPO arrangement. While we did include relevant firm characteristics as control variables as discussed earlier, we examined if there were differences in the variance of the residuals between these groups of firms, but we did not observe this to be the case. Also, we did not observe the residual term to be correlated with the dependent variables, leading us to conclude that OLS was suitable to estimate our model [2] (see Appendix C). As per the hierarchical regression analysis procedure, predictor variables and theorized interactions were included in steps to assess their effect on the explained variance of the criterion variable. We first assessed the impact of the control variables on BPO satisfaction; next we added the relational and contractual governance constructs as main effects and then introduced the interaction terms one at a time. As recommended by Aiken et al. [2] and Cohen et al. [17], we mean centered variables prior to computing interaction terms so as to alleviate multicollinearity that can be introduced. We did not detect any indications of harmful collinearity, with none of the variance inflation factors (VIFs) above 4.0. Results for each of the models are shown in Table 5.

As shown in Table 5, the control variables alone (Model 1) explained 34 percent of the variance in BPO satisfaction. Process type, ownership structure, IT embeddedness, and outcome measurability were found to be significant predictors. Balance sheet totals, equity holding, BPO experience, and process standardization were not found to be significant. Adding the relational governance factors alone (Model 2) to these controls resulted in a near doubling of the explained variance (66 percent) in BPO satisfaction while adding the contractual governance factors alone to the controls resulted in an explained variance of 49 percent in BPO satisfaction. This suggests that these particular relational governance factors are collectively more powerful predictors of BPO satisfaction than the collective set of contractual governance factors considered here. By including both the main effects of the relational as well as the contractual governance factors in addition to the control block, we are able to explain 68 percent of the variance in BPO satisfaction. It is interesting to note that when we include the main effects of both the relational and contractual governance factors, nearly all of the control variables become insignificant with the exceptions of IT embeddedness and outcome measurability.

Models 5a–f show the results of adding in the two-way interactions one at a time, which represent our substitution hypotheses. The significant negative interactions between goal expectations and information exchange, goal expectations and trust, and goal expectations and conflict resolution were consistent with H1, H3, and H4, respectively. The significant negative interactions between activity expectations and trust and activity expectations and information exchange were consistent with H2 and H5, respectively. Finally, the significant negative interaction between contractual flexibility and trust was consistent with H6. Each of the hypothesized substitution effects was therefore supported, and including these two-way interactions in the regression improves our ability to predict BPO satisfaction.<sup>2</sup> The graphs of the substitution effects are shown in Figure 2.

## Implications

Our findings ha ve severa l im porta nt th eoretica l im plica tions for our understanding of BPO governance and, more broadly, for our understanding of the governance of the sourcing of IT-enabled services. First, our results indicate that a parsimonious set of relational and contractual governance factors are predictive of BPO satisfaction, a key outcome variable with downstream effects on customer loyalty and share of wallet [91]. Second, we found that the positive influence of the contractual factors on BPO satisfaction is reduced as the level of various relational factors increases. These substitutive effects contribute to our theoretical understanding of BPO governance, offering rich insights into how clients and vendors should deemphasize contractual governance factors given the levels of the relational governance factors. Thus, our findings provide a nuanced view of how hybrid governance mechanisms should be designed in order to maximize the client’s BPO satisfaction. Third, our granular approach to considering how specific governance factors jointly affect a key outcome illustrates how nuanced insights can be generated for the governance of emergent services enabled by IT. We now expand on these theoretical implications.

## Relational and Contractual Factors for BPO Governance

We extend past IS research that has identified various aspects of contractual and relational governance in contexts such as IT outsourcing [12, 37, 79], IT offshoring, and sourcing of IT-enabled services (e.g., logistics and supply chain management) [50, 57] by isolating a parsimonious set of governance constructs that are relevant to the BPO context and demonstrating that they are predictive of BPO satisfaction. Specifically, we isolate three relational factors (i.e., information exchange, trust, and conflict resolution) and three contractual factors (i.e., goal expectations, activity expectations, and contractual flexibility) that are important to govern BPO relationships and promote BPO satisfaction. These factors explain a significant amount of the variance in BPO satisfaction above and beyond the control variables that we included in our analysis.

Among the three contractual factors, goal expectations had the greatest effect on BPO satisfaction. Among the three relational factors, trust had the greatest effect on BPO satisfaction, a finding that is consistent with previous studies in IT outsourcing and interorganizational relationships of IT-enabled services that have found it to be a strong predictor of outsourcing performance (see, e.g., [57, 60, 93]). Our findings affirm that trust is also critical in the BPO context as it establishes the client’s beliefs that the BPO service provider will not behave opportunistically and has a commitment to the success of the relationship, enhancing BPO satisfaction as a result.

While trust and information exchange have been considered extensively in IS studies that have examined the influence of relational governance on client and vendor outcomes (e.g., [57, 83, 90]), the role of conflict resolution has not been broadly considered (see [37] for an exception). Our findings clearly indicate that conflict resolution also plays an important relational governance role. Thus, scholars interested in the governance of interorganizational relationships for the exchange of services enabled by IT should consider how conflicts can be resolved during the course of the relationship.

## Substitutive Effects of Relational and Contractual Factors

The substitutive effects of relational and contractual factors on the client’s BPO satisfaction represent our central research contribution. Specifically, the influence of each of the contractual governance factors on BPO satisfaction is reduced as relational governance factors increase, as we had theorized. In this way, we extend the prior literature on relational and contractual governance for the sourcing and offshoring of IT services and IT-enabled business processes (e.g., [37, 90, 113]). Prior work, which had tended to focus on contractual and relational governance at a rather coarse level (with the exception of [37]), offers only limited insights on how specific elements of relational and contractual governance serve as substitutes in influencing key outcomes (e.g., client satisfaction). The importance of examining the substitutive or complementary nature of formal and informal governance factors at a granular level (i.e., consider the specific factors and outcomes) has been demonstrated in the context of IS project management by Tiwana [106], who found that a pair of formal and informal controls can operate as complements with respect to one outcome and as substitutes with respect to another. Taking a similar granular approach, we theorized six interaction effects in which specific relational factors serve as substitutes for contractual factors with respect to the client’s BPO satisfaction. We found that each of the six theorized interactions increased the explained variance of BPO satisfaction in comparison to a direct-effects only model that included the relational and contractual governance factors (see Model 4 versus Model 5a–f in Table 5). To develop a nuanced view of the pattern of each of the six substitution effects, we plotted the interaction effects at three levels of the moderator (one standard deviation below the mean, the mean, and one standard deviation above the mean) and also examined the significance of the simple slopes at each of these levels [2, 88] (see Figure 2 and Table 6). Our work contributes to the literature on BPO by extending the work of Wüllenweber et al. [113], who examined the effect of business process standardization on BPO success (not BPO satisfaction) and included information exchange (labeled “communication”) in their research model, but did not examine the interactions of relational and contractual governance factors. Our work also complements and extends the work of Goo et al. [37], who focused on the direct effect of contractual factors on relational factors. While Goo et al. also included trust, conflict resolution, and contractual flexibility among the factors in their model, and did examine some interactions between relational and contractual governance factors, they did not test the specific interactions that we hypothesized and found support for.

## Substitution of Goal Expectations by Relational Factors

Our results support the hypotheses that the relational factors (information exchange, trust, and conflict resolution) substitute for goal expectations (H1, H3, and H4). Strikingly, the positive effect of goal expectations on BPO satisfaction attenuates as the level of each of the relational factors increases, becoming insignificant when the relational factors operate at a high level (see Figure 2 and Table 6). The core implication is that the importance of using contracts to establish the foundational aspects—specifically, purpose—of the BPO relationship becomes less important as the relationship is more developed (as represented by higher levels of the three relational factors).

Our findings suggest that in the context of BPO relationships, information exchange can substitute for the need to contractually explicate goals ex ante by promoting the ongoing development of shared understanding between the client and vendor on the objectives of the relationship. When information exchange operates at a high level in a relationship, the client’s expectations are unlikely to be influenced by ex ante specification of goal expectations and very likely to be influenced by the ongoing information exchange that occurs during the course of the relationship. Thus, while past IS research has found information exchange in IT sourcing relationships to improve client satisfaction and other project-level outcomes (e.g., [90]), our results suggest that information exchange also reduces the need for contractual governance through goal expectations. As such, information exchange among key stakeholders (e.g., decision makers, staff) involved in a BPO relationship provides clarity to them on the purpose of the relationship, thereby reducing the need for the up-front explica tion of goals in contracts.

Our findings also suggest that as the client’s trust in the BPO vendor increases, it reduces the client’s reliance on the ex ante specification of goals to safeguard against opportunism and ensure commitment by the vendor to the objectives of the relationship. Indeed, when high levels of trust have developed between the client and the vendor, goal expectations no longer remain instrumental in promoting BPO satisfaction. Here again, while past IS research has shown that trust creates a positive environment for partners to pursue opportunities and mitigate opportunism [57], our findings reveal that trust reduces the importance of contractual emphasis on goal expectations. As such, trust among partners reduces the need to explicate the purpose of the relationship in the form of contractual obligations and penalties.

Finally, our findings suggest that as the client and vendor develop conflict resolution mechanisms (e.g., joint problem solving [79]), the utility of ex ante goal expectations in providing the foundations to address disagreements decreases. As a result, partners can rely on well-developed relational conflict resolution mechanisms to defuse tensions, negotiate disagreements, and guide the relationship forward.

While contractual specification of the business objectives of the relationship (in the form of clearly articulated goal expectations) can be a powerful governance mechanism, given the complex and dynamic nature of the BPO context, it may not be possible to specify fully such expectations in advance. We therefore identified three relational governance mechanisms that can, as each is developed over time, compensate for incomplete or unclear formal specification of goal expectations. Given the substitutive effects we have identified, trust can be a powerful relational governance mechanism when goals and objectives cannot be clearly specified in advance to make sure that one party does not take advantage of the other and a mutually acceptable goal set is determined over time. Likewise, under conditions of incomplete goal specifications, partners can rely on information exchange and conflict resolution to reduce the ambiguity and to manage any resulting tensions that may arise from unclear goals or objectives.

## Substitution of Activity Expectations by Relational Factors

Our results support the hypotheses that the relational factors (information exchange and trust) substitute for activity expectations (H2 and H5). Strikingly, the positive effect of activity expectations on BPO satisfaction is observed only when information exchange and trust operate at a low level (see Figure 2 and Table 6). At moderate levels of these two relational factors, we do not observe a significant effect of activity expectations on BPO satisfaction. Interestingly, at high levels of trust, activity expectations have a negative effect on BPO satisfaction. Thus, while there has been a focus on examining the direct effects of SLAs on various IT sourcing outcomes in different contexts (e.g., [90]), our findings suggest that the influence of activity expectations on outcomes depends on the level of relational factors that are present. Indeed, excessive monitoring through SLAs may not only be ineffective (when information exchange and trust operate at moderate levels) but also turn counterproductive (when trust operates at high levels, conceivably because of the unnecessary administrative costs for the both the client and vendor that are incurred in the process).

Activity expectations are a specification of the level of task performance rather than a statement of overall relationship objectives, and monitoring adherence to these expectations creates administrative costs. Trust creates the expectation that the partner will operate in the best interest of the relationship, averting the moral hazard problem in interfirm relationships [49], and in this way, trust can substitute for contractually specified activity expectations. Likewise, under conditions of incomplete activity expectations, partners can rely on another relational governance factor, information exchange, to reduce the ambiguity that may arise from unclear activity expectations. Thus, our results suggest that information exchange can reduce ambiguity arising from an incomplete specification of activity performance.

## Substitution of Contractual Flexibility by Relational Factors

Our results support the hypotheses that trust substitutes for contractual flexibility (H6). Interestingly, the positive effect of contractual flexibility on BPO satisfaction attenuates as the level of trust increases, becoming insignificant when trust is at a high level (see Figure 2 and Table 6). Given the dynamic nature of the technological and business environment in which BPO relationships are situated, provisions for flexibility in the contract enable partners to make adjustments as foreseen contingencies present themselves or unforeseen outcomes are confronted. However, there are significant contracting costs that are incurred in ex ante identifying a vast number of contingencies from the perspectives of both parties in an IT sourcing relationship [12]. Our results suggest that such costs are unlikely to be beneficial, at least from the perspective of client satisfaction, when trust is high. Our results also suggest that when contracts are incomplete with formal clauses not accounting for contingencies and unforeseen outcomes, trust (which supports informal arrangements and mutual commitment) compensates for contractual flexibility in addressing these scenarios effectively.

## Realizing Controls in Outsourcing

Our findings also provide insights on how the challenge of realizing effective controls can be overcome for BPO and outsourced IS projects. In a comparative study of the performance implications of controls applied in internal and outsourced systems development projects, Tiwana and Keil [107] found controls to be ineffective or even detrimental to performance for outsourced systems development projects. (Their conceptualization of controls was based on Ouchi’s [82] control theory, similar to other key studies on controls for IS projects. For outsourced projects, they found outcome, behavior or clan controls to have a nonsignificant effect on performance, and self-control to have a negative effect.) Their conclusion was that when systems development is outsourced, concerns of opportunism motivate the client to establish controls (greater than for internal projects); yet it is much more challenging to meet the informational demands required to realize formal controls and the social requirements to realize informal controls when projects are outsourced than internal. In a related study of IT outsourcing projects, Rustagi et al. [96] found trust, technical knowledge, and relationship management knowledge to reduce the amount of formal controls. They concluded that knowledge and relational factors enable clients to use fewer controls more efficiently and to motivate and influence vendor behaviors with fewer controls. Our findings extend this discourse and suggest that informational demands and social requirements for the effective governance for BPO and IT outsourced projects are more likely to be met by combining relational with contractual governance mechanisms rather than singularly emphasizing formal contractual mechanisms. We now discuss the implications that emerge from our findings on how specific contractual and relational governance jointly affect clients’ BPO satisfaction.

## Designing Governance Mechanisms for Services Enabled by IT

Finally, our findings collectively have implications for how governance designs for services enabled by IT should be conceptualized and examined. Consistent with Bardhan et al.’s [9] perspectives on understanding service systems enabled by IS, we examined BPO governance by (1) defining multiple stakeholders (i.e., clients and vendors), (2) isolating the effects of technological change (i.e., BPO contracts are necessarily incomplete because of business and technological change during the course of a BPO relationship), and (3) adopting different theoretical perspectives (i.e., contractual and relational governance of sourcing relationships). Susarla et al. [101] found that effective governance of software-as-a-service required achieving fit between the type of client–vendor relationship (i.e., arm’s-length or embedded relationships) and the type of incentive (i.e., high- or low-powered incentives) for a service given service characteristics. Our study suggests that nuanced insights can be achieved for the governance of services enabled via IT by (1) elaborating the type of client–vendor relationship to specific relational governance factors (e.g., information exchange, trust, and conflict resolution) and (2) considering the contractual characteristics that establish foundations (i.e., goals and standards of conduct) and incorporate change provisions (i.e., contractual flexibility).

## Practical Contributions

Our study suggests that BPO relationships should be governed by using a strategy that applies relational governance mechanisms as substitutes for certain contractual governance mechanisms. Clients should evaluate their trust in the vendor, the costs of information exchange (e.g., on-site meetings, monitoring, etc.), and their ability and motivation to resolve conflicts with the vendor. Such an assessment will be useful to determine how incomplete contractual governance can be offset through relational governance mechanisms and the resources that should be invested in specifying ex ante various contracting parameters related to goals, activities, and contingencies. In particular, managers should be mindful of the trade-offs involved in under- or overinvesting in contracts. Overrelying on relational governance and underinvesting in contractual governance could be detrimental because it takes time for relationships to develop to the point where relational governance becomes practical. On the other hand, downplaying relational governance and overinvesting in contractual governance may prove unnecessary or even counterproductive because of the substitutive effects identified in our study (as well as the complexities and uncertainties of the BPO context, and the costs associated with creating and enforcing contract provisions).

Our findings suggest that while both relational and contractual governance mechanisms can be effective in the BPO context, relational governance is the more potent tool and that it can even substitute, to some degree, for contractual governance. Managers should therefore consider hybrid strategies that combine relational and contractual elements for BPO governance. Of course, managers from client and vendor firms are only going to be in a position to design such hybrid governance mechanisms when the relational factors that characterize the client–vendor BPO relationship have evolved sufficiently to be able to serve as substitutes. In the meanwhile, they can work toward developing the relational factors, recognizing that not only will these factors be directly beneficial in promoting positive experiences and outcomes for the client but also in being able to substitute for the role of contracts in very specific ways in the governance process.

## Limitations and Extensions

Our study focused on BPO sa tisfa ction from the client perspective. While satisfaction is an important outcome measure, it will be useful to examine different measures of BPO performance. In addition, it will be useful to examine differences in perspectives between the vendor and client to develop a better understanding of mechanisms considered to be important by each party to the exchange relationship. Because our data are cross-sectional, we examined the effect of governance factors on BPO satisfaction at a single point in time. The evolving and dynamic nature of these relationships should be examined in future research through a longitudinal analysis. Further research is also needed to evaluate the role of process contingencies, such as the degree of business or technological uncertainty, and knowledge characteristics of the client and vendor firm in BPO governance.

Our findings related to contractual governance provide valuable insights for future studies on BPO contracts: many researchers have argued that confidentiality issues impede in-depth analysis of contracts (e.g., [21, 65]). By assessing contracts using the three contractual governance constructs, future researh can overcome confidentiality issues since neither service nor price level are exposed.

While our findings suggest that certain relational governance factors can serve as substitutes for particular contractual governance factors, it is important to remember that trust, conflict resolution, and information exchange may be constrained based on the selected vendor, the experience of the client with BPO and outsourcing, and the organizational culture and work practices of the client and vendor. Clearly, the effectiveness of relational governance depends to a great extent on the development of trust between parties. Such relationships do not develop instantaneously and may require considerable time to become established. Thus, it may be unlikely that relational governance factors would be effective substitutes for contractual governance factors at the outset of a BPO initiative. Further, relational factors may tend to be somewhat dynamic and this should also be taken into account. That being said, the substitutive effects we found are interesting and worthy of further research to understand how the joint effects of relational and contractors evolve over the course of the client’s and vendor’s experiences with BPO and, specifically, with each other as BPO partners. The issue of temporal dynamics and when substitutive effects can be observed represents a promising avenue for future research.

Finally, our findings reinforce Tiwana’s [106] conclusion about the usefulness of adopting a granular approach to understand how governance mechanisms (e.g., formal or informal controls; contractual or relational governance factors) operate as complements or substitutes for a particular outcome (e.g., BPO satisfaction) in a given context (e.g., BPO versus outsourced IS projects; internal IS projects versus outsourced IS projects). Such a granular approach is likely to reconcile inconsistencies on why certain governance factors may operate as substitutes for one outcome (e.g., client satisfaction) but as complements for another (e.g., vendor commitment), thereby elaborating our understanding on how to design governance for services that are enabled by IS and also for outsourced IS projects.

## Conclusion

Th is resea rch sh eds new ligh t on th e governa nce of BPO initia tives by identifying the substitutive effects that exist between specific relational and contractual governance factors. Our results suggest that deficits in certain contractual governance factors can be offset by specific relational governance factors. In particular, trust, information exchange, and conflict resolution can each serve as viable substitutes for goal expectations. Information exchange and trust can substitute for activity expectations. In addition, trust can substitute for contractual flexibility. These findings suggest that hybrid strategies that combine relational and contractual elements may be particularly effective for BPO governance. The substitutive effects identified in this study can be applied to designing hybrid governance mechanisms that will maximize BPO satisfaction.
