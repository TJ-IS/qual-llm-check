# DESIGNING PROMOTIONAL INCENTIVES TO EMBRACE SOCIAL SHARING: EVIDENCE FROM FIELD AND ONLINE EXPERIMENTS<sup>1</sup>

Tianshu Sun Marshall School of Business, University of Southern California Los Angeles, CA, U.S.A. {tianshus@marshall.usc.edu}

Siva Viswanathan Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland College Park, MD, U.S.A. {sviswana@rhsmith.umd.edu}

Ni Huang C. T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston Houston, TX, U.S.A. {nhuang5@uh.edu}

Elena Zheleva Department of Computer Science, University of Illinois at Chicago Chicago, IL, U.S.A. {ezheleva@uic.edu}

Despite the increasing connectivity between consumers and the large volume of social shares supported by digital technologies, there is an absence of research systematically investigating how firms can design promotional incentives that jointly consider their consumers as both purchasers and sharers. In this study, we examine whether and how firms can leverage consumers’ social connections and engage consumers to share promotional incentives. In collaboration with a leading online deal platform, we report a large-scale randomized field experiment to test the effectiveness of different incentive designs (varying in the shareability and scarcity of promotion codes) in driving social sharing senders’ purchases and successful referrals. We find that the different incentive designs have distinct impacts on the purchase and referral outcomes. Specifically, providing senders with one non-shareable promo code significantly increases their own purchase likelihood, compared to the other experimental groups. In contrast, senders who receive one shareable promo code are less likely to purchase themselves yet are more likely to make successful referrals. Surprisingly, the incentive design with two promo codes containing one non-shareable code and one shareable code increases neither the senders’ purchases nor their successful referrals. Managerially, we estimate that although the one nonshareable promo code group derives the highest net revenue for the experimental period, the one shareable promo code group derives the highest customer lifetime value for the firm from the new customers acquired through the successful referrals. We further conducted two online experiments on Amazon Mechanical Turk that replicate the field experiment’s findings and explore the underlying mechanisms of the observed relationships. We find that exclusivity perception and social motives triggered by the incentive designs with one promo code mediate their effects on senders’ self-purchases and successful referrals, respectively, and explain the ineffectiveness of two promo codes. Our study contributes to the bodies of literature on IT-enabled social sharing and social promotions, providing implications for firms on how to design promotional incentives that accommodate the dual role of consumers as purchasers and sharers.

Keywords: Social sharing, incentive design, social promotion, randomized field experiment, online experiments

## Introduction

Developments in information technologies have significantly boosted the prevalence of social sharing by making it convenient for consumers to disseminate digital information within their social connections (Gerlitz and Helmond 2013; Nielsen 2016). Recognizing this opportunity, major online retailers and e-commerce platforms such as Amazon and Groupon have integrated social features and channels on their product pages to facilitate the effortless sharing of commercial information (Huang et al. 2018). While the direct impact of social sharing has been extensively studied in prior literature (Godes and Mayzlin 2009; Gu et al. 2012; Susarla et al. 2012; Yin et al. 2014, 2016; Geva et al. 2019), there is limited research on how firms can design promotional incentives to leverage ITenabled social sharing. <sup>2</sup> For instance, many firms still follow traditional ways of designing promotions that focus on isolated purchase decisions of individual consumers without considering the new opportunities derived from social sharing among consumers. Similarly, despite extensive research attention on the role of firms as observers and moderators of online social sharing and word-of-mouth (e.g., Godes et al. 2005, Berger 2014), there is surprisingly little understanding on how firms can embrace social sharing to improve on their influence in actively engaging with potential customers via promotional incentives. Given the increasing connectivity among consumers and the low cost of social sharing enabled by digital technologies, it is paramount for firms to revisit their current promotional strategies in the social media era. Keeping the above in mind, our study aims to address the following research questions:

Should firms engage consumers as isolated and independent purchasers, or as both purchasers and sharers that are interconnected to each other?

• How can firms design incentives differently to engage their consumers across both roles?

What are the mechanisms underlying the effectiveness of such incentive designs?

Considering the dual roles of consumers as purchasers and sharers in social sharing, we propose that firms can design incentives along two dimensions: shareability and scarcity. Prior research on IT-enabled social sharing has primarily focused on the sharing of information among consumers (Ryu and Feick 2007; Hong et al. 2017) and the consequences of such actions (e.g., Godes and

Mayzlin 2004; Gu et al. 2012; Susarla et al. 2012). However, past work on information sharing information may not be applicable to the sharing of incentives in our study because, unlike information, promotional incentives incur financial costs to the firm and often tie to specific users, which are not always readily duplicable or shareable across consumers (Aydinli et al. 2014, Goldfarb and Tucker 2019). Therefore, incentive shareability becomes a strategic design choice for a firm. Specifically, although making the promotional incentive shareable may encourage sharing (Andreoni 1989), it may risk lowering a consumer’s own purchase likelihood because those who would be interested in purchasing themselves would need to sacrifice their own benefits by passing on the incentive (Andreoni 1990).

Meanwhile, another important design choice is incentive scarcity. A higher number of promo codes (thus, less scarcity) in the incentive design removes the resource constraint and allows consumers to share the promotion and draw additional customers without forgoing the option to use the incentive for themselves. At the same time, such incentive design with a lack of scarcity in promotion codes may create the impression that the platform is trying to liquidate its inventory, leading to a perception of low quality and thus jeopardizing consumers’ purchases as well as referrals (Nelson 1970; Cao et al. 2018). In addition, the lack of scarcity may also mitigate the perceived social motives inherent in the act of sharing since consumers who share the incentive no longer need to give up their own benefits (Ariely et al. 2009; Andreoni and Bernheim 2009). Lastly, incorporating either shareability or quantity into the incentive design may incur a loss of perceived exclusivity, as the promotion code is no longer designed just for the focal consumer, leading to lower redemption rates (Dreze and Nunes 2008; Feinberg et al. 2002).

In this paper, we report a large-scale randomized field experiment in collaboration with a leading daily deal platform and two online experiments on Amazon Mechanical Turk to identify the causal effects and underlying mechanisms of incentive designs on consumer purchases and successful referrals. In particular, in the randomized field experiment (i.e., Study 1), we vary the shareability and scarcity of promotion designs with an eye toward their effects on the purchase behaviors of consumers involved in social shares and any successful referrals attributed to these consumers. We find evidence that incentive designs that vary in shareability and scarcity have different impacts on the likelihood of the senders’ purchases and successful referrals.

First, compared to the other incentive designs, offering one non-shareable promo code to a sender significantly increased senders’ purchase likelihood in the experiment. Second, providing one shareable promo code to a sender led to a significant increase in the probability of the sender’s successful referrals, in contrast to the other incentive designs in the field experiment. Third and more importantly, based on our estimations, the economic effects of the incentive designs on the net revenue combining both senders’ purchases and successful referrals are not only statistically significant but also economically meaningful. Specifically, compared to the control group, the one non-shareable code group derived at least 9% higher net revenue, and the one shareable code group derives at least 5% higher net revenue in the short term. Moreover, in the long term, our results suggest that the one shareable code group would derive the highest lifetime value for the firm based on the new customers that the shareable incentive attracts (\~50% of the successful referrals are new users who purchase for the first time). We replicate the main findings in online experiments and also explore potential mechanisms.

Our study extends prior research on IT-enabled social sharing that has focused on the sharing of information to the domain of sharing incentives. Specifically, we contribute to the literature on IT-enabled social sharing by focusing on the sharing of incentives and investigating the causal effects of the incentive designs on consumers’ purchases and referral outcomes. Furthermore, our work adds to the stream of literature on social promotions by empirically examining the purchasing outcomes and profitability (i.e., net revenue) of shareable incentives in the field setting. Prior research on the profitability of social promotions has been analytical in nature due to the unavailability of proprietary data on promotion operations and customer purchases, and our empirical study addresses this gap in the literature. We theoretically analyze and empirically demonstrate that firms can profit from IT-enabled social sharing with the proper incentive designs, which helps overcome the information asymmetry issue based on consumers often having more information about their friends than the firm (Van den Bulte et al. 2018). Additionally, by allocating the promotion to the right consumers with optimal preference fits, shareable incentives could effectively improve both firm profits and customer welfare. Finally, from a practical perspective, our study offers actionable implications for firms in that they can utilize the incentive designs to leverage social sharing to increase purchases as well as successful customer referrals. This is important because the majority of prior studies have focused on the firm’s role in encouraging and moderating online social sharing (e.g., Godes et al. 2005; Ryu and Feick 2007) and have overlooked the ability of firms to use incentive designs to directly target and convert consumers who engage in social sharing. Our study demonstrates that firms could benefit from designing appropriate incentives that leverage the dual role of customers as both purchasers and sharers.

## Overview of the Related Literature

## IT-Enabled Social Sharing

This work contributes to the prior literature on IT-enabled social sharing (Wasko and Faraj 2005; Aral and Walker 2011; He et al. 2017, Lee et al. 2018; Qiu et al. 2015; Sun et al. 2016; Shi et al. 2014). Previous research on social sharing has focused on understanding the antecedents and consequences of sharing information, examining research questions such as how to effectively motivate consumers to make recommendations to their social circles (Ryu and Feick 2007; Huang et al. 2018), how to better design customer referral programs (Hong et al. 2017; Sun etl al. 2019; Sun et al. 2021), and the impact of word-of-mouth on others’ purchase behaviors (Kim and Srivastava 2007; Aral and Walker 2011; Huang et al. 2019). However, there is limited research on the implications of sharing incentives in online commerce. The IT-enabled sharing of incentives could be similar to the sharing of information in terms of convenience and instantaneity. Notably, information sharing is also fundamentally different from incentive sharing because while information might have a near-zero marginal cost of duplication and redistribution (Aydinli et al. 2014, Goldfarb and Tucker 2019), incentives are likely unduplicable and their redistribution often incurs a financial trade-off for the sharers. As a result, from a consumers’ perspective, incentive sharing often imposes a choice between the consumer using the incentive for self-benefit and the consumer passing on the incentive for the benefit of others.

Considering the fact that the findings in prior literature on information sharing cannot be directly generalized to incentive sharing, our study advances prior work by empirically investigating the impacts and mechanisms of promotion designs with incentive sharing on consumer purchases and referral outcomes, effectively extending the social sharing literature.<sup>3</sup> Furthermore, our study makes a pioneering effort to explore the platform’s ability to leverage IT-facilitated social sharing traffic with different incentive designs, contributing to the broad stream of literature on social sharing and social influence (Aral and Walker 2011; Bakshy et al. 2012; Bapna and Umyarov 2015; Iyengar et al. 2011, 2015). Whereas prior work has focused on the implicit influence of the focal consumers’ purchase on the subsequent product sales through social learning (Bapna and Umyarov 2015; Huang et al. 2019), we show that, in the incentive sharing scenario, consumers may have an explicit impact on the others’ purchases through sharing promotional incentives. Specifically, we show that firms can target consumers with novel promotion designs to engage consumers across their dual roles as purchasers and sharers. This not only incentivizes them to purchase but also motivates them to use their social connections to generate successful referrals. We demonstrate that a shareable incentive activates a sender’s social connections, resulting in increased referrals from the senders. Also, during this process, consumers also face a trade-off between their own purchases and their influence on others’ purchases.

## Social Promotions

Our study adds to an emerging stream of literature on social promotions (e.g., Kumar and Rajan 2012; Subramanian 2012; Edelman et al. 2016; Hu and Winer 2017; Hanson and Yuan 2018). Different from traditional promotions that offer reduced prices to individual consumers (Blattberg et al. 1995; Banks and Moorthy 1999), social promotions emphasize the interpersonal aspect of sales promotions and exhibit a variety of formats, such as group discounts (Subramanian 2012; Edelman et al. 2016) and shareable coupons (Hanson and Yuan 2018). Prior work on social promotions can be categorized into two main camps. One group of studies focuses on the antecedents of sharing social promotions and suggests that the sharers of social promotions are driven by intrinsic (e.g., altruism, selfexpression, social image) and extrinsic motivations (e.g., economic rewards) (Tang et al. 2016; Subramanian and Rao 2016; Li et al. 2019). Meanwhile, the other body of research focuses on the redemption of social promotions (Ashworth et al. 2005; Luo et al. 2014; Cao et al. 2018).

Our study complements the latter stream of literature by studying both the redemption and the sharing of incentives through social promotion. Specifically, we examine whether the shareability and the quantity (lack of scarcity) of incentives would influence consumers’ purchases and referrals. Prior work shows that the volume of a social promotion could serve as a popularity signal and increases the subsequent likelihood of consumer purchase and reduce the time of coupon redemption (Luo et al. 2014). In contrast, the intensity of social promotion from firms is also often considered to be a signal of low quality by consumers (Rao and Monroe 1989; Cao et al. 2018). Thus, for social promotions, multiple promotion codes could benefit consumers since it would enable them to share the promotion with their friends without sacrificing the opportunity of using the promotion for their own purchase. However, consumers may feel that incentives with multiple promotion codes are not exclusively designed for them (Barone and Roy 2010; Chandon et al. 2000). Considering that the overall implication of the volume of incentives is not clear in the case of social promotion, our study seeks to empirically examine this issue.

Moreover, our study also contributes to related research on the profitability of social promotions. Prior analytical work has suggested that social promotions are not profitable for businesses (Kumar and Rajan 2012; Edelman et al. 2016) because, compared to social information sharing (e.g., consumer word-of-mouth, referral programs), social promotions bear higher costs with the provided incentives (Trusov et al. 2009; Jing and Xie 2011). As a result, businesses such as restaurants that offer high-frequency services might recoup their social promotion costs and profit through repeated purchases over time. Yet, for businesses with low purchase frequencies, social promotions could be unprofitable, and the possibility of recovering the promotion costs depends on the volume of new customers attracted during the process and consumers’ probability of returning to make purchases (Kumar and Rajan 2012; Subramanian 2012). The absence of empirical evidence in prior research on the profitability of social promotions can be partially attributed to the unavailability of necessary data. Through a large-scale randomized field experiment and the analyses of proprietary data, our work is among the first studies to empirically examine the profitability of the different designs of social promotions in a real-world setting in both the short term and the long term.

## Theoretical Framework

In this section, we set up a stylized analytical model to consider the critical factors underlying the senders’ purchase and referral decisions in response to the different incentive designs: namely, one non-shareable promo code (Treatment 1), one shareable code (Treatment 2), and two promo codes with one non-shareable plus one shareable code (Treatment 3). Our model jointly characterizes a sender’s purchase and referral decisions, as the two are inherently interrelated with each other. The analytical model also allows us to derive a series of testable hypotheses on the different effects of incentive designs on consumers’ purchase and referral outcomes.

To being, we set up the model framework as follows: Consumer i derives utility from purchasing with and/or sharing a promotional incentive of a deal. Thus, the consumer’s overall utility $U$ consists of two parts: $U p$ and $U s .$ The utility of purchase $U p$ is derived when consumers use the incentive to purchase the deal. Let $U p _ { i } = x _ { i } ,$ , where $x _ { \mathrm { i } }$ is the consumer’s utility from redeeming the incentive.<sup>4</sup> It is important to note that the value $x _ { i }$ is consumer $i \mathit { \Omega } _ { \mathbf { \hat { s } } } ^ { \ }$ private information and cannot be directly observed by the platform. Meanwhile, consumer i may also share the promotional incentive with friend j and gain utility $U s _ { i } = F ( U p _ { j } )$ , where $U p _ { j } = x _ { j }$ represents the friend’s utility from using the incentive to purchase the deal, and $F ( U p _ { j } )$ captures the consumer’s value function of the friend’s utility gain. Such utility from sharing the promotional incentive with friend j represents an interpersonal incentive (Kornish and Li 2010) and is consistent with the theory of social image (Andreoni 1990, Khalil 2004; Ariely et al. 2009). That is, consumer i derives utility on sharing from the enhanced social image by demonstrating altruism to others, i.e., social image (Rotemberg 2014) and to oneself, i.e., self-image (Bénabou and Tirole 2011). Additionally, since social image is directly related to i’s own benefits, we can express sacrificing sender $i \mathbf { \ ' } _ { \mathbf { S } }$ utility in incentive sharing as a function $G \mathrm { o f } - U p _ { i }$ . Thus, $U s _ { i } { = } F ( U p _ { j } ) + G ( - U p _ { i } ) . ^ { 5 }$

Taken together, the two parts of the overall utility $U ,$ the utility of purchase $U p$ and the utility of sharing $U s ,$ are functions of a consumer’s self-preference for using the promotional incentive to purchase and the consumer’s social preference for sharing the incentive with a friend.<sup>6</sup> It is important to note that similar to $x _ { i } ,$ the value of the incentive to friend $j$ is private information and cannot be directly observed by the platform. However, consumer i has information on the friend’s preference and thus can observe $j ^ { \circ } \mathbf { s }$ value of $x _ { j } .$ This is consistent with the observation that consumers usually have better knowledge about their social connections and consequently enjoy an informational advantage as compared to the platform (Beaman and Magruder 2012). Therefore, the key question is: How can the platform design promotional incentives to leverage consumers’ private information and social motives, engaging them as both purchasers and sharers?

Within this framework, all consumers in the population can be characterized on this two-dimensional plane. This framework naturally incorporates the dual role of consumers as purchasers and sharers, allowing us to compare the distinctive effects of the different incentive designs on both consumers’ purchases and successful referrals. A consumer i faces four alternatives: purchase with the incentive, sharing the incentive, neither purchase nor share, or both purchase and share.

To summarize, we derive a stylized analytical model as the theoretical framework of our study and deliberate over each incentive design and its corresponding hypotheses. This enables us to examine the different incentive designs under a comprehensive framework and quantify the comparative effects of the different incentive designs within this framework. To test the hypotheses, we conducted three studies employing experiments in both field and online settings. In Study 1, we conducted a randomized field experiment in collaboration with a leading online deal platform in the U.S. In the field experiment, over 20,000 consumers were randomly assigned to the five test groups and we tracked and compared their purchases and successful referrals across the groups. In Study 2, we used an online experiment on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), which allowed us to replicate the effects of the incentive shareability as well as scarcity on senders’ purchases in the field experiment and directly examine the potential underlying mechanisms. In Study 3, we conducted another online experiment on MTurk that replicates the effects of incentive quantity (lack of scarcity) on senders’ successful referrals in the field experiment and also explores the plausible explanations.

## Field and Online Experiments

## Study 1: Randomized Field Experiment

In Study 1, we designed and implemented a randomized field experiment in collaboration with a leading online deal platform (hereafter referred to as our partner platform) to examine the causal impact of different incentive designs that vary in shareability and scarcity, on consumers’ own purchases and their successful referrals. The partner platform, similar to Groupon.com, offers a wide variety of limited-time deals at relatively high discount rates, including travel, events, dining, and products, and has attracted a substantial customer base in the market space. Users of the platform can directly purchase a deal through the deal page or share the deal with their social connections using the social sharing feature on the deal pages. Social sharing can occur either before or after purchase when users click the prominently displayed sharing buttons on the webpage to share the deals. Each social share action only includes one specific deal. Although a considerable number of social shares are made on the partner platform every day, only a small percentage of the shares lead to purchases.

## Experimental Design

Our field experiment employed a between-subjects design. In particular, we focused on the organic social sharing senders who had shared any deal with their social connections in the past 24 hours but did not end up purchasing the shared deal themselves.<sup>10</sup> We randomly assigned the eligible subjects (i.e., original deal senders) into one of the five experimental groups, as presented in Table 1 below. The experimental manipulation was performed at the sender level. The platform observed the senders’ own purchases as well as their successful referrals (i.e., others’ purchases attributed to the sender’s sharing of the deal).<sup>11</sup> Notably, we delivered unique and personalized promo code(s) for each sender in the treatment groups, which enabled us to accurately track both a sender’s purchase and any successful referrals.<sup>12</sup>

## Procedure

The experiment was implemented for a period of seven weeks.<sup>13</sup> Our treatments were designed as part of the partner platform’s email notification system. The treatment email samples, as reported by the partner’s firm’s marketing team and shown in Figure 4, were delivered to eligible senders once a day at the same time during the experiment. Each user on the platform was eligible to receive the treatment at most once during the test period. The randomization process ensured that the incentives in the email were completely orthogonal to the sender’s characteristics and past behavior, and any difference in the sender’s purchase and sharing behaviors were directly attributable to the differences in the received incentives.

After receiving the notification emails, depending on the experimental groups, the senders could take several different actions (as discussed in Figure 1-3). For example, the participant might completely ignore the incentive and not make any purchase. Alternatively, the senders could use the incentive to purchase the deal themselves. Further, the sender in the sharable code groups could pass on the incentive to another user. Given these possibilities, we focus on two key outcomes in the experiment: senders’ purchase likelihood and senders’ successful referrals.<sup>14</sup> The first outcome reflects the conversion of the senders, and the second outcome sheds light on whether the impact of the treatment extended beyond the targeted senders.

Following these analyses, we also estimated the profitability of the incentive designs both in terms of the current period during the experiment and from a customer lifetime value perspective. Since our experiment was conducted in a networked environment, we first discuss the potential spillover issues in our study following the standard approach in prior literature (Aral and Walker 2012; Bapna and Umyarov 2015). To begin with, social shares in the daily deal context are rare (Sun et al. 2021). Thus, given the randomized nature of our experiment, treatment spillover among senders is of little concern. One possible scenario of sender contamination is that a certain sender in one social share may also be the recipient in another social share. We therefore excluded those few senders in our sample (about 0.3% of the senders). In addition, some recipients may receive shares from multiple senders (either in a different or the same treatment group).

Therefore, further investigation into the net economic values of the treatments is needed to understand the bottom line of the incentive designs and to provide managerial insights for the platform. Specifically, we consider the outcome of overall purchases that can be attributed to each sender in the experiment. We first calculate a variable named short-term overall purchase volume, which we defined as the accumulation of a sender’s purchase and the sender’s successful referral at the sender level during our observation window of Study 1. This variable takes the value of 0 when neither a sender nor a recipient made a purchase, 1 when either a sender or a recipient made a purchase, and 2 when both made purchases. We then performed regression analyses on the overall purchase volume (Table 6 reports the results) As shown in Table 6, both one non-shareable promo code and one shareable promo code led to increases in the short-term volume of consumers’ overall purchases.

Comparing these coefficients to the average value of shortterm overall purchase in the simple reminder group and taking into account the 15% discount, we find a potential average net revenue increase of 9%<sup>17</sup> for the one nonshareable code group, and 5%<sup>18</sup> for the one sharable code group within the short-term period of Study 1. Table 7 presents the short-term net revenue calculation based on the regression coefficients in Table 6, which suggests that the incentive designs with one non-shareable promo code and one-shareable promo code are most profitable in terms of short-term net revenue. These numbers are economically significant and meaningful when one considers the extensive scale of the digital platform.<sup>19</sup> Additionally, the platform also makes profits based on commissions per sale, given that the one non-shareable code and the one sharable code groups receive more individual orders, the commission-based profit also adds to the overall profit advantage of these two groups.

Next, we consider the profitability of the incentive designs from a customer lifetime value’s (CLV) perspective. Although the one non-shareable promo code appears to be more profitable than the one sharable promo code in the short term, estimating the long-term profitability of the incentive designs requires an examination of the customer lifetime value (e.g., Venkatesan and Kumar 2004; Borle et al. 2008).

One key distinction between the one non-shareable code and the one shareable code is that the latter would lead to more conversions from new customers as opposed to existing customers. Thus, we sought to compare the profitability of the incentive designs in the long run, with a focus on the CLV of the promotions implemented in our field experiment. We extracted additional proprietary data from the firm and analyzed the purchase history of all users who had been on the platform in the five years prior to our experiment (\~410k users). We then estimated the value of any new customers in terms of average transactions as 11.2 in the long-term period of five years. Note that 11.2 should be a lower bound estimate of the CLV of each new user. Based on the above discussions, we present the CLV in terms of transactions in Table 8.<sup>20</sup> As shown by Table 8, the lifetime value of the one shareable code group is much higher that of any other group, suggesting that the long-term value of the sharing incentive design depends critically not just on immediate purchases from the existing customers but also the future value derived from newly acquired customers. To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first empirical evidence on the profitability of social promotions beyond theoretical prediction based on prior analytical work (Kumar and Rajan 2012; Subramanian 2012). Overall, the CLV analysis suggests that the one shareable code incentive design is optimal, as it brings in both short-term net revenue from senders and recipients (albeit lower than that of the nonshareable code group) and also attracts new customers who will generate a stream of future transactions for the platform.

By removing the incentive’s scarcity constraints in Treatment 2, the initial goal of Treatment 3 was to encourage consumers to not only purchase the deal using the non-shareable code but also drive successful referrals using the shareable code. However, a sender receiving multiple promo codes may also perceive the incentive as not being exclusive and would perhaps be therefore less likely to redeem it for purchase (Barone and Roy 2010; Dreze and Nunes 2008).<sup>21</sup> In addition, providing more promo codes may also influence consumers’ perceptions of the quality of the product as they may infer that the firm is trying to clear undesirable inventory (Nelson 1970, Cao et al. 2018).<sup>22</sup> Further, senders given two codes may be less likely to engage in social shares, as this may appear less altruistic, making sharing potentially less useful for building positive social image than it would be in the one shareable code context (Ariely et al. 2009, Rotemberg 2014).<sup>23</sup>

Bearing the above considerations in mind, we extend the analytical framework to include behavioral factors such as exclusivity and social motives (see Figure 6) in an attempt to provide plausible explanations for the divergence between our hypotheses and the empirical findings. While the incentive design with two promo codes lifts the scarcity constraint on consumers’ decisions (D<sub>i</sub>, S<sub>i</sub>), it may also negatively influence consumers’ perceptions about the exclusivity of the promotion and product quality, as well as their social motives to share the promotion, thus potentially affecting their decisions to purchase or share. As shown in Figure 6, the removal of the incentive scarcity in Treatment 3 introduces concerns regarding the lack of exclusivity and quality perception, leading to a cost of Upc on consumer purchase. Thus, consumers with $U p > 0$ and Up < Upc now would not make a purchase. Similarly, the lack of scarcity might reduce the signaling value of demonstrating altruism and enhancing one’s social image, resulting in a cost of Usc on consumer sharing of the incentive. Hence, consumers with $U s > 0$ and Us < Usc would not share the incentive because of social image concerns.

From a theoretical perspective, once the behavioral factors are taken into consideration, whether an incentive design with two promo codes will outperform one shareable promo code becomes an empirical question. The results in the field experiment juxtapose with the initial hypotheses on the effects of the two promo codes design and indicate that behavioral factors play an important role in driving both senders’ purchase and referral outcomes. As a result, the benefits of removing incentive scarcity are apparent, yet one must take into account the behavioral factors and carefully draft an optimal design of incentives in social promotion. Although the field experiment successfully established internal and external validity, Study 1 sheds limited light on the underlying mechanisms of the observed main relationships (Bapna et al. 2018). As such, we complement Study 1 with Studies 2 and 3 to examine the possible mechanisms using online experiments on MTurk.

## Study 2: Mediating Role of Perceived Exclusivity on Senders’ Purchase Goal

The primary goals of Study 2 are twofold. First, we aimed to investigate the plausible explanation underlying the effect of one non-shareable promo code on the senders’ purchase likelihood, while also testing the robustness of our findings in the field experiment. As noted earlier, changing either the shareability or the quantity of the promo codes in the incentive design may affect consumers’ perceptions of incentive exclusivity since the senders who received shareable or multiple incentives may feel that the promotion is not exclusively designed for them (Barone and Roy 2010; Dreze and Nunes 2008; Chandon et al. 2000). Moreover, we were interested in understanding whether the incentive shareability and quantity (i.e., lack of scarcity) would lead to variations in individuals’ perceived quality of the deal (Rao and Monroe 1989; Cao et al. 2018). Thus, in Study 2, we primarily explore the idea that an incentive design with one non-shareable promo code is more likely to be perceived as exclusive, compared to the designs with either one shareable promo code or two promo codes.

## Procedure

We conducted the Study 2 experiment on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk). This online experiment employed a between-subjects experimental design. We manipulated the different incentive designs, and measured perceived quality, perceived exclusivity, as well as the sender’s purchase intention in each scenario. The participants were randomly assigned to one of the three groups: the one non-shareable code group, the one shareable code group, and the two promo codes group. To simulate the scenario of the field experiment (i.e., Study 1), subjects were asked to visit Groupon.com and select a deal they hoped to share with a friend. To do so, the participants were required to copy and paste the link of the deal page in a text response question. After submitting the deal link, the participants entered into a scenario of receiving a post-sharing promotion that differed by experimental group. As a manipulation check, the participants were asked to point out the shareability and quantity of promo codes in their treatment. Specifically, we presented a promotion scenario to the subjects and then performed a manipulation check by asking them to point out the correct incentive design corresponding to the promotion message in a multiple-choice question format.<sup>24</sup>

Upon completing the manipulation check, the participants were then asked to report their (1) perceived quality signal of the deal, (2) perceived exclusivity of the incentive, and (3) intention to use the incentive to purchase the shared deal. We measured the perceived quality signal of the incentive using two items adapted from the related prior work on product quality (Sweeney and Soutar 2001; Dimoka et al. 2012). Additionally, perceived exclusivity was measured on an 11-point Likert scale (from 0 to 10), using three items adapted from Barone and Roy (2010). Further, we measured the purchase intention on a singleitem 11-point scale, consistent with Wilcox et al. (2009). At the end of the experiment, we implanted a screener question using an instructional manipulation check (IMC) (Oppenheimer et al. 2009; Paolacci et al. 2010) to screen out the inattentive respondents. Appendix B presents further details of Study 2, including a flow chart to demonstrate the experimental procedure, the measurement items, and an example of the IMC question.

## Results

A total of 150 requests were posted on MTurk, and 43 Turker hits were rejected and dropped from the responses because of invalid responses, such as failing to provide a valid Groupon weblink, failing the IMC screener question, or failing the manipulation check on identifying the correct incentive design in the promotion scenario. As a result, we dropped the invalid hits and obtained effective responses from 107 workers. The demographic measures of the remaining valid responses indicate that the participants had an average age of 34.85 with a gender composition of 35 females and 72 males. Around 74.77% of the participants reported having a bachelor’s degree or higher, around 72.90% of the participants reported a household income of \$30,000 or higher, and around 98.13% of the workers stated that they shop online monthly or more frequently. Our first question is whether the incentive designs that varied by shareability and quantity induced any significant variation in the perceived quality signal of the deal. Our results show that the three experimental groups do not show significant differences in either of the perceived quality signal measures.<sup>25</sup> As a result, we cannot conclude that the incentive designs led to differences in the perceived quality signal of the deal, which may not offer a candid explanation for the main effects of the incentive designs on senders’ purchase.

Our second and more important question concerns whether the senders’ perceived incentive exclusivity and purchase intentions vary across the experimental groups. Before further analysis, we examined the reliability and validity of exclusivity measurements. Cronbach’s alpha for perceived exclusivity items was 0.96, suggesting adequate internal reliability for the exclusivity construct (Rosenthal and Rosnow 1991). In addition, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to assess the convergent and discriminant validity of the exclusivity construct (Härdle and Simar 2007). The EFA result with varimax-rotation suggests that the items belong to one factor, with loading being greater than 0.92 on each item. Further testing on the average variance extracted (AVE) shows the AVE value to be greater than 0.89, thus validating the convergent validity of our measurement items (Fornell and Larcker 1981). As such, we averaged the values of the three items and generated the variable of perceived exclusivity.

To further investigate the role of perceived exclusivity in the relationship between the incentive designs and purchase intention, we performed additional mediation tests using a bias‐corrected bootstrapping procedure in

Stata.<sup>26</sup> Specifically, following previous literature (Madanoglu et al. 2016, Davis et al. 2019), we used the bootstrap command in tandem with the sgmediation package in Stata, employing perceived exclusivity as the mediator. Considering the fact that the experimental groups varied according to two key constructs, namely incentive shareability (i.e., one non-shareable vs. one shareable code) and quantity (or lack of scarcity, i.e., one non-shareable code vs. two codes),<sup>27</sup> we conducted a set of two mediation analyses, with the dependent variable being the purchase intention and the independent variables being shareability and quantity separately. The results of the mediation analyses are reported in Table 9. In the first set of the mediation analysis, the indirect effect of incentive shareability on purchase intention, via exclusivity, yielded a 95% confidence interval that did not contain zero (95% CI = [-2.009, -0.657]). This finding indicates that the participants perceived two promo codes as being less exclusive than one non-shareable code, resulting in reduced purchase intention. In sum, the mediation analyses show that the perceived exclusivity indeed mediates the effects of incentive shareability and quantity (lack of scarcity) on senders’ purchase intention, corroborating the results from our field experiment.

## Discussion: Study 2

The online experiment in Study 2 replicates and supplements the field experiment in Study 1 by providing direct evidence that perceived incentive exclusivity is a plausible mechanism underlying the main effects between one non-shareable promo code and the senders’ purchase outcomes. Specifically, Study 2 shows that the users perceive the incentive designs with one sharable promo code and two promo codes as being less exclusive than the design with one non-sharable code, indicating a lower intention to use the incentive to purchase. Meanwhile, Study 2 suggests that the incentive-induced quality signal might not be a potential mechanism for the observed main effects of the field experiment. Of course, one possible alternative explanation of the null difference here is that our sample only included users who had already chosen the deal, likely making their concerns about quality lower to begin with. Next, we turn to Study 3 to explore the possible mechanisms underlying the variation in senders’ successful referral between the one shareable code group and the two codes group.

## Study 3: Mediating Role of Social Motives on Senders’ Successful Referral

## Goal

The main purpose of Study 3 was to investigate the plausible explanation of the main effect of incentive quantity (or lack of scarcity, i.e., one shareable promo code vs. two promo codes) on the probability of senders’ successful referrals, while also examining the robustness of our findings in the field experiment. Considering the behavioral factors, we explore the idea of social motives, including perceived altruism and social image (Andreoni 1990; Ariely et al. 2009), as a plausible explanation for the observed effect of incentive quantity on the probability of the sender’s successful referral. Prior literature suggests that there exists a reinforcing relationship between individuals’ altruistic action and their interest in building a social image (Bénabou and Tirole 2006; Andreoni and Bernheim 2009). Therefore, in Study 3, we consider whether senders might experience a greater sense of altruism and a higher tendency to build social image when sharing the only promo code versus sharing one of two codes, which could subsequently play a significant role in the probability of successful referral.

## Procedure

The online experiment of Study 3 employed a betweensubjects design and was conducted on MTurk. Since one non-shareable promo code does not support further referral, Study 3 mainly compared two experimental groups: the one shareable promo code group and the two promo codes group (i.e., one non-shareable and the other shareable). Following Study 2, which simulates the scenario used in the field experiment, the subjects in this online experiment were asked to visit a deal page on Groupon.com that they hoped to share with a friend. To do this, participants were required to provide the name and the link of the deal. The participants then walked through the scenario of receiving a post-sharing promotion and completed a manipulation check on the scenario accordingly. Specifically, similar to the manipulation check in Study 2, the subjects were presented with a promotion scenario and then asked to identify the correct incentive design used in the promotion message by answering a multiple-choice question.

We then asked the participants to report (1) their social sharing tendency, (2) social motives behind sharing, and (3) the name of the friend with whom they intended to share the incentive, as well as their confidence that the friend would use the shared incentive. Note that before measuring the subjects’ social sharing intention, we inserted a filler task that asked participants to write down the name of the friend with whom they intended to share the deal. This filler question was designed to address the potential demand effect derived from hypothesis guessing. We measured social motives, namely perceived altruism and social image, with two items on an 11-point Likert scale adapted from Ryu and Feick (2007). Consistent with Study 2, we measured the sharing intentions on a single-item 11-point Likert scale (Wilcox et al. 2009). Similarly, the perceived confidence was also measured on an 11-point Likert scale. At the end of the experiment, we asked participants about the perceived realism of the scenario (Morales et al. 2017) and, like in Study 2, we also employed a screener question using an instructional manipulation check (IMC, Oppenheimer et al. 2009) to screen out inattentive respondents. We report further details of Study 3 in Appendix C, offering a flow chart of the experimental procedure and a table of measurement items.

## Discussion: Study 3

Study 3 directly measures and tests the potential role of perceived altruism and social image on the effects of incentive quantity (lack of scarcity) on senders’ successful referral outcomes. The results of Study 3 corroborate the predictions of our theoretical consideration on those behavioral factors. Specifically, through this online experiment, we find that consumers are less likely to feel that they are sacrificing their own benefits and less likely to inform friends about the sacrifice when sharing the incentive designed with two promo codes (vs. sharing only one code). Study 3 also provides consistent evidence supporting the field experiment regarding the likelihood of successful referral with one shareable promo code. Given lower social motives, our results indicate that users feel less confident that their referrals will be successful when sharing the incentive with two promo codes (vs. sharing one promo code). In summary, Study 3 supplements the randomized field experiment (Study 1) and the first online experiment (Study 2), by providing direct evidence that perceived altruism and social image may be plausible mechanisms underlying the main effect between incentive quantity (lack of scarcity) and senders’ successful referrals.

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Key Findings

Through a series of field and online experiments, this work investigates whether and how online platforms can employ incentive designs that vary in shareability and scarcity to engage with consumers across their duel-role of purchasers and sharers in social promotions. Our field experiment (Study 1) shows that the incentive design using one non-shareable promo code leads to increased purchase likelihood among senders. Additionally, we found that the incentive design with one shareable promo code results in increases in the senders’ successful referrals but not in senders’ self-purchases. Moreover, we observed that the incentive design with two promo codes is not effective in driving the senders’ purchases or further successful referrals. Following the field experiment, we conducted two online experiments (Studies 2 and 3) to replicate the field experiment’s findings and to explore potential underlying mechanisms. Study 2 suggests that the effectiveness of one non-shareable promo code on senders’ purchases might be caused by increases in senders’ utility through generating a feeling of exclusivity. Study 3 indicates that the effectiveness of one shareable promo code on senders’ successful referrals could be attributed to increases in senders’ utility by enhancing social motives (i.e., altruism and social image). Further economic analyses on the profitability of the incentive designs suggest that although the one nonshareable promo code design generates the highest shortterm net revenue, the incentive using one shareable promo code clearly stands out as the optimal option when we consider the lifetime value of the new customers brought in through the design employing one shareable code in social promotion.

## Implications for Research and Practice

Our study makes several important contributions to research. First of all, this work advances the related IS literature on IT-enabled social sharing. While prior research on social sharing has primarily focused on the sharing of information (Aral and Walker 2011; Bapna and Umyarov 2015; Hong et al. 2017; Sun and Taylor 2020), our research extends this literature to the phenomenon of sharing incentives. Incentive sharing is different from information sharing because incentives contain financial value and thus are not easily duplicable by consumers (Aydinli et al. 2014, Goldfarb and Tucker 2019). In this regard, from a consumer’s perspective, incentive sharing could impose a trade-off to the consumer between using the incentive for self-benefit and passing on the incentive for the benefit of others. Meanwhile, from a firm’s perspective, incentive sharing involves the strategic consideration of engaging the dual role of consumers as purchasers and sharers. Therefore, our study empirically examines the impacts and underlying mechanisms of different incentive designs in social sharing, varying by shareability and scarcity, on consumers’ purchase and referral outcomes. Note that IT-enabled information and incentive sharing are inherent IT phenomena (Ba et al. 2001; Hevner et al. 2004; Hong et al. 2017), and thus our study highlights the importance of considering the design of incentive sharing as an IS problem.

Our study also contributes to the literature on social promotions (e.g., Kumar and Rajan 2012; Subramanian 2012). Previous studies in this stream have shown inconclusive results on the effectiveness of incentive quantity (lack of scarcity) in social promotions. On the one hand, the quantity of social promotions could serve as a popularity cue to consumers and lead to increased sales (Luo et al. 2014). On the other hand, the volume of incentives in social promotion might signal low product quality and curtail consumer purchases (Rao and Monroe 1989; Cao et al. 2018). Adding to the prior findings, our study shows that the incentive design with increased promo code quantity (lack of scarcity) neither drives consumers’ purchases nor increases successful referrals, compared to the design using a single code. Our further investigation suggests that this is because the incentive with two promo codes was perceived as less exclusive than that offering one non-shareable code and fosters less social motive than the one shareable code. We believe that the different results on the economic implications of incentive quantity are rooted in the variation of the promotion scenarios. For example, Luo et al. (2014) consider the volume of incentives already purchased by other consumers rather than that merely offered by merchants (Cao et al. 2018). While Luo et al. (2014) and Cao et al. (2018) study consumers’ organic purchases on public social promotion sites, our work investigates consumers’ responses to private, targeted social promotion events. Furthermore, our findings highlight how firms can strategically engage their consumers as potential sharers to acquire new customers and increase profits in the long run. Our estimations show that the one non-shareable promo code group resulted in the highest net revenue in the short term, whereas the one shareable promo code group resulted in the highest lifetime value, bolstered by the new customers that the shareable incentive attracts. Thus, this work offers the first empirical evidence on the prior analytical work investigating the profitability of short-term and long-term sharing incentive design.

From a practical perspective, the series of experiments in this study shed light on the importance of considering behavioral factors such as perceived lack of exclusivity and social motives in incentive designs. Since providing two promo codes might not achieve the global optimal outcome, firms may have to face a choice between whether they should encourage consumers to purchase more by redeeming the one non-shareable code or whether they should suggest that consumers share the code with others. In terms of promotion profitability, we show that the incentive design with one shareable code brings in both short-run net revenue from senders purchases and long-term value from their successful referrals who will generate a stream of future transactions for the platform. Our results suggest that the lifetime value of an incentive design in social promotion critically depends on the immediate purchases from existing customers, the sharing motives of consumers as potential senders, and the future value of newly acquired customers via social sharing.

Our empirical findings are based on real-life business operations and thus are economically significant and meaningful when one considers the scale of IT-enabled social sharing (i.e., millions of shares per year in the collaborating firm). The insights of our study inform firms on how to design promotional incentives that accommodate the dual role of consumers as purchasers and sharers and leverage their social motives for social sharing. The shareable incentive design has also been adopted by the collaborating platform, impacting a large number of customers.

## Limitations and Future Directions

Our work is subject to several limitations, which also offer ample opportunities for future research. First, our study manipulates the shareability and scarcity of promotional incentives while keeping the discount level constant across treatments to achieve comparability of the experimental treatments and parsimony of the experimental design. However, the effects of the discount level on user responses might be nonlinear in nature (Lacetera et al. 2012; Cabral and Li 2015). Future work could consider exploring various levels of discounts and examine the variance among users’ responses. In the same vein, the effects of incentive quantity (i.e., one vs. two promo codes) are also likely to vary nonlinearly according to the number of promo codes. For instance, it would be interesting to investigate how social motives operate in social sharing without scarcity. Our field experiment focused on the organic social sharing of senders who had shared a deal with their social connections during the previous 24 hours but did not end up purchasing the shared deal themselves. Though the sample is managerially relevant, it would be interesting to examine the effect of promotional incentive design on a broad set of consumers. The qualitative pattern or direction of the findings might be similar but the magnitude of treatment effects for regular users without previous shares could be smaller.

Furthermore, we focus on senders’ successful referral as the outcome. Since we do not directly observe the intermediate social sharing process that occurred beyond the digital platform’s tracking system (e.g., private communication), the total number of shares and related outcomes was unobservable in our field experiment. Considering this limitation, future research could further extend this study and explore intermediate sharing processes and additional measures, such as the number of social shares per sender and the conversion rate of each referral. Moreover, the online experiments were conducted under a hypothetical scenario, making it challenging to rule out potential demand effects. Future research could attempt to address this issue by conducting these experiments in the field setting. In addition, it would be interesting to test an incentive design with one share-only promo code, which might elevate senders’ successful referrals. From a theoretical perspective, the share-only promo code would likely increase the sender’s successful referrals as compared to the control group, because the share-only code could motivate social customers who are willing to pass on the code to their friends. Meanwhile, the comparison between the share-only design and the two-codes design is less clear-cut, and the relative effectiveness of those two designs depends on the extent to which behavioral factors play a central role in social promotion.

Finally, targeting sharing traffic through incentive design could be complementary to other social interventions such as targeting influencers (Manchanda et al. 2008), network seeding (Hinz et al. 2011), viral product design (Aral and Walker 2011), viral content design (Berger and Milkman 2012), and referral programs (Schmitt et al. 2011), among other things. It would be valuable to examine how incentive designs complement or substitute for these innovative approaches. We hope that our study serves as a first step in that direction. Given the rapid advancement in firms’ capabilities to process large amounts of data and analyze real-time consumer behaviors, we envision that, in the near future, firms will be able to further optimize incentive designs and deliver them in an instantaneous and personalized fashion. Our work serves as a pioneering proof of concept of this impending development. It is our hope that future research can extend this study and further explore various incentive designs for different types of consumers, social networks, and research contexts.

We assess the validity of our randomization by conducting t-tests for mean comparisons across observable covariates, including sender characteristics such as the number of past purchases, total past spending, user tenure, and the number of past shares with the recipient, as well as deal characteristics like deal price and number of deal shares.<sup>31</sup> The results of the pairwise comparisons tests are reported in Table A1, which shows that there is no statistically significant difference between any pair of experimental groups in any aspect. The randomization check confirms that the pre-experiment purchase level for senders and the price of deals are comparable across all test groups. Further, we verify the balance with regard to deal category (i.e., a list of dummies for every major deal category in the experiment) and find no significant difference for all deal categories. To summarize, the well-balanced sample indicates that our randomization among the experimental groups is properly achieved.
