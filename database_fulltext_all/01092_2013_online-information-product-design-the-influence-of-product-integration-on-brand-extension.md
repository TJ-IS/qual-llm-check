# Online information product design: The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of product integration on brand extension

## a b s t r a c t

With users placing an increasing demand on cross-product integration in electronic markets, the success of new information products becomes increasingly dependent on its integration design with existing products. Consequently, many online vendors have been incorporating branding into information product designs. This approach re<sup>fl</sup>ects a critical marketing strategy called “brand extension.” In contrast to the popularity of this strategy by online vendors, however, there is little theoretical work or empirical evaluation in the information systems (IS) literature on the relationship between information product integration design under the same brand umbrella and consumers' usage of the newly introduced information products. Addressing this gap, this study investigates the antecedents of online brand extension evaluation with an emphasis on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of product integration. Based on the stimulus–organism–response paradigm and the categorization theory, this study proposes and validates a research model using a scenario-based experiment that involves a search engine and its extension to an e-commerce website and an online encyclopedia. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm that integration level in<sup>fl</sup>uences perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and perceived tie between focal and newly extended products. Perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and perceived tie positively impact users' evaluations of online brand extension, and consequently in<sup>fl</sup>uence users' intention to use the extended products. User expertise moderates the effect of product integration on perceived tie: the greater the users' expertise in focal product, the stronger perceived tie is affected by online product integration. This study contributes to both research and practice by advancing the overall understanding of exploiting online branding values and by providing insights into online information product design and promotion.

## 1. Introduction

The commercial success or failure of any product innovation does not rely solely on technological features, but often rests on <sup>fi</sup>nding the right combination of product design and marketing strategies [13,27,65]. Online information products are no exception. As the widespread acceptance of Internet has given rise to popular online brands such as eBay, Google, Amazon, and Yahoo, exploiting brand values to support product innovation has become of increasing concern to companies. In this regard, the design of new information products and their introduction in electronic markets are of particular interest, because a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of a brand's value comes from its contribution to launching new products [60].

Brand extension, described as the “use of established brand names to enter new product categories or classes” [1, p. 27], has become a subject of increasing interest to scholars in marketing and information systems disciplines because it represents an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient strategy for <sup>fi</sup>rms to leverage online brand value. Literature on this topic has focused on: (1) various conceptualizations of perceived <sup>fi</sup>t (usually de<sup>fi</sup>ned as shared associations between the extension and parent brand) (e.g. [49,61]), (2) communication strategies for brand extension success (e.g. [42,64,75]), and (3) individual-level differences (e.g. [2,34,43,79]). However, little is known about the product design elements for online brand extension. IS scholars are thus presented with ample opportunities to contribute unique knowledge to this research area.

Online information products refer to intangible information goods that can be used to satisfy Internet users' desires or needs. Compared with traditional physical products, there are at least three distinct characteristics for online information products. First, as intangible goods, electronic bits are the most important constituent of online information products. With increasingly transmission speed, online information products can be delivered within minutes or even seconds, resulting in a delivery scheme that is impossible for physical products [50]. This extends the traditional notion of product boundaries, and facilitates an information retrieval process that strengthens associations between extended and existing products [72]. Second, hyperlinks are another distinguishing feature for online information products that allows vendors to easily associate one product with another [67]. As these are based on stable and prede<sup>fi</sup>ned interfaces, the high connectivity of hyperlinks facilitates information sharing and ensures a sense of interoperability between products [72]. Third, the marginal cost of producing information products is generally negligible when compared with conventional physical products. Hence, online information products can be easily integrated and packaged for users [35,36]. These distinctions present IS scholars with both challenges and opportunities for investigating online product design.

Studying online brand extension builds upon and further contributes to innovative thinking in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld because it considers a behavioral mechanism that has been under-investigated. That is, if newly developed information products share the same brands with incumbent products, users may be more likely to adopt and use them. As a result, many online vendors have been trying to incorporate branding values into product design to promote product adoption. For example, building on its success in the search engine market, Google exploited its widely recognized brand to launch its email service, Gmail. To promote Gmail, Google added a hyperlink between the main page and Gmail, and incorporated search engine functionality into Gmail pages. This coupling allows Gmail users to perform searches without going to Google's home page, and facilitates visitors of Google's home page to check their email messages.

Despite the advanced practice of brand extension by online vendors, there is little theoretical work in the IS literature that considers the relationship between information product design and the cognitive and behavioral measures of users. Incisive research on product design can help online providers better understand the different factors contributing to successful product design, and the ways they can be used to maximize the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of exploiting branding values. The notion of IS/IT adoption and usage has played a central role in IS research [12,74]. Following a call by Rogers [56] that people should not view innovation in isolation, and that the adoption of one innovation may trigger the adoption of others, IS researchers have gradually paid attention to the relationship of usage behavior between different information technologies [40,63]. However, scant research has scrutinized the adoption and usage behavior between different information products from the product design perspective. Thus, studies in this area should complement the current understanding of adoption of multiple IS/IT products. In particular, we expect that the research presented here can shed light for a cross-boundary research stream that incorporates market value and product design into technology innovation research.

Our study identi<sup>fi</sup>es distinct elements of online brand extension and focuses on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online information product integration design. Product integration is the assembling of different products together to facilitate data/information sharing (such as information about user pro<sup>fi</sup>le) and to enhance the overall value to users through products' mutual cooperation [45,57]. In the context of product integration and brand extension, products can be categorized as a focal product and a newly extended product under promotion. Although studies have argued that the success of a new product is dependent on its integration with relevant extant products [18], the present study poses the following questions that have so far received little theoretical and empirical attention: (1) to what extent does information product integration in-<sup>fl</sup>uence users' acceptance of a newly extended information product sharing the same brand? (2)What is the underlying psychological process that explains the relationship between information product integration and online brand extension evaluation?

## 2. Theoretical background

The stimulus–organism–response (S–O–R) paradigm provides an overarching framework to explain how human responses are developed from environmental stimuli [41]. Based on the S–O–R paradigm, environmental cues act as stimuli that cause an individual's cognitive or affective reactions, which in turn affect his or her behavior [41]. Stimulus is conceptualized as an in<sup>fl</sup>uence that arouses the individual, affecting his or her internal organismic states [4,24]. In the context of a marketing environment, the stimulus can be any product design elements, such as product presentation contents and formats, which form an extrinsic attribute of a product [48]. As a design element of online information products, product integration works as an extrinsic attribute of the focal product and, thus, triggers an individual's cognitive and behavioral reactions.

## 2.1. Online information product integration as an environmental stimulus

Product integration has two facets of coupling: the nature of coupling and the extent of coupling. The former refers to whether the integration is achieved outside or inside of the focal product [44,66], while the latter refers to whether the integration is comprehensive with additional value-added functionalities or a minimal level of functional integration across two products [10,44]. Three different types of product integration were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed based on the two dimensions: value-added integration, add-on module integration, and data interface integration [45].

Value-added integration involves combining the focal product with a relevant product internally, and merging the data and the functions of the two products in a seamless fashion [28]. On the basis of the coupling between the two products, the integration offers additional product features. Because of internal coupling and the comprehensive sharing of functions, the boundary between the focal and the relevant product is obscured. This boundary becomes even more obscured in online environment because additional features can be delivered to users as soon as they are requested. For example, through the coupling of Yahoo! portal and Yahoo! mailbox, Yahoo! provides additional features that were previously unavailable. On the homepage for Yahoo.com, Yahoo! email users can see their number of new messages, subjects, and sender names without having to log into the inbox page.

Add-on module integration involves the integration of the focal product with a relevant product through an external module or component. The external module or component is not an independent product, but rather an add-on function (e.g. the Skype add-on for Internet Explorer), a pop up mini-site (e.g., the mini-site of Tencent QQ in China), or a pop up messenger (e.g. Real Messenger). For example, in order to promote <sup>fi</sup>lm.com through the RealPlayer software, RealNetworks uses the external module, Real Messenger, to integrate the RealPlayer and <sup>fi</sup>lm.com. While the integration is achieved externally, such an add-on module provides the requisite support for a comprehensive integration and sharing of data and functions between products [45].

Data interface integration involves the external integration of the focal product with a relevant product by de<sup>fi</sup>ning the technical interface to facilitate the transfer of data across the two products [57]. Such product interface speci<sup>fi</sup>cations provide only a minimal level of functional integration across the two products. Based on well-documented and prede<sup>fi</sup>ned interface, online vendors usually use hyperlinks to facilitate data sharing across focal and promoted products [72]. The integration of Google search engine and Google Calendar represents such an example. To promote Google Calendar, Google adds a hyperlink between Google's home page and Google Calendar to facilitate data exchange. Hence, Google search engine users can directly follow this linkage and visit Google Calendar. Except for simple connectivity between these two products, any changes made in Google's search engine do not require the company to make changes to Google calendar.

## 2.2. Categorization theory and online user's cognitive reactions

When an individual faces a stimulus, cognitive reactions usually occur [24]. Upon being presented with a newly extended product, one's natural cognitive reaction is to group the product into a given category [17,71]. Categorization is the process by which objects are recognized, differentiated, and understood. The use of categories allows people to structure and simplify their world so that they can function in complicated environments [47]. Categorization theory has been richly investigated through empirical research in marketing and information systems (e.g., [1,9,64]). Studied have found that a new object's match with an existing category in<sup>fl</sup>uences users' impression of that object, and therefore how they process it [25,62]. If the match proves successful (i.e., the new object is a member of an existing category), categorization processes are evoked which involve the abstraction of a stereotype of the category, and the transfer of that stereotype across all the members belonging to it [17,71]. As a result, the traits associated with that category are transferred to the new object [9]. In the brand extension context, if a newly extended product is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as belonging to a previously de<sup>fi</sup>ned category (i.e. parent brand), users' attitudes associated with the parent brand will be transferred to the extended product.

In contrast, if no match is established between the new object and an existing category, piecemeal processes are evoked [9], whereby the new object is treated as a separate, individual entity. Compared to categorization processing, piecemeal processing results in slower impression formation and more attention to speci<sup>fi</sup>c attributes of the new object [71]. As a result, the attitude toward the new object will be computed through a weighted combination of attributes [62]. In the context of brand extension evaluations, if a newly extended product does not belong to a previously de<sup>fi</sup>ned parent brand, the transfer of attitude from the parent brand to the new product will be weak. Hence, attitude toward extended product is “computed” only from speci<sup>fi</sup>c, unique attributes, rather than from pre-existing attributes of parent brand.

## 2.3. User's evaluations of extended information products

The responses in the S–O–R paradigm represent the processing outcomes [23,48], manifesting in many forms that range from conscious to unconscious, and from internal (i.e., invisible) to external (i.e. detectable) [29]. When it comes to online information product brand extension, the following responses may happen when an individual is exposed to an extended product: <sup>fi</sup>rst, s/he evaluates the product and forms an attitude toward it [80]; second, she develops usage intention toward it as a result of evaluative judgment; and <sup>fi</sup>- nally, she may be more likely to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the positive or negative usage behavior toward it after the intention has been developed. Scholars have become increasingly interested in investigating brand extension evaluations. Over time, researchers have progressed toward building a general model for antecedents of evaluations. For example, despite some mixed results, prior studies showed that the quality of the parent brand, and the <sup>fi</sup>t between the parent brand and extended product, are highly important brand extension factors [8]. However, little attention has been paid to understanding the speci<sup>fi</sup>c design elements for brand extension, especially in online contexts.

## 3. Hypotheses development

## 3.1. Independent and dependent variables

In this study, we examine the effectiveness of product integration types in online contexts: the value-added integration, the add-on module integration, and the data-interface integration. Two major dependent variables are used to measure users' cognitive reactions to the integration. The <sup>fi</sup>rst dependent variable, perceived <sup>fi</sup>t, is de-<sup>fi</sup>ned as the number of shared associations between the newly extended product and the parent brand [1,20]. The choice of this dependent variable is due to extensive research showing that perceived <sup>fi</sup>t is an important determinant of brand extension evaluations [8]. The second dependent variable, perceived tie, refers to the strength of observable interaction between different products [68]. This dependent variable is particularly applicable in the context of online brand extension where vendors can implement electronic linking features like hyperlinks to associate one information product with another. Furthermore, perceptions are key in<sup>fl</sup>uences on intended behaviors. Given that an important goal of product integration is to promote newly extended products, it is important to assess the effects of product integration on perceptual constructs that can potentially in<sup>fl</sup>uence users' intention to use the newly extended product.

## 3.2. Effects of product integration level on perceived tie and perceived fit

In the context of online brand extension, vendors can easily apply electronic features like hyperlinks to associate one information product with another. Electronic links represent business relationships among the linked products in the form of promotional agreement or strategic alliance [67]. As a salient cue, electronic links strengthen users' perception of a positive relation between the two. Therefore, perceived tie is an essential factor for the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of product integration on online brand extension. Considering the extent and nature of coupling, value-added integration is considered to have the highest integration level. Data interface integration has the lowest level due to its minimal level of functional integration, while add-on module integration falls in between.

When the level of integration is low (as is the case with data interface integration), the products share data through parameters or public interface [10,44,66] and the dependency between products is low. Hence, users are likely to perceive a low degree of tie between focal and newly extended products. Consequently, these products can be perceived as aggregates of individual products. When the level of integration between focal and newly extended products is high (as with value-added integration), the level of functional sharing becomes so salient that the focal product modi<sup>fi</sup>es or relies on the internal workings of newly extended product. This calls for extensive sharing of product and technical knowledge, as well as careful modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the focal and extended products to affect the internal integration without disrupting the overall product performance [10,54]. Faced with a high level of interaction between focal and newly extended products, online users will perceive a higher degree of tie. Hence, the following hypothesis can be made:

H1. The product integration level has a positive impact on the perceived tie between focal and newly extended product.

Internet is capable of delivering extended information products quickly. This capability facilitates information retrieval processes, and strengthens associations between an extended product and the parent brand. Compared to their of<sup>fl</sup>ine counterparts where rapid transmission speed through electronic means is impossible [50], the role of perceived <sup>fi</sup>t may be even more crucial in online brand extension contexts. According to existing literature, a parent brand and extended product “<sup>fi</sup>t” each other if both are perceived as members of the same category (e.g. [1,9]). Categories are not <sup>fi</sup>xed entities; rather, they are <sup>fl</sup>exible and context dependent, serving different discrete goals [17]. Product design can be used as an effective tool to help build perceptions of category membership [37].

Empirical studies have found that, through increased exposure and better understanding of an extended product, promotion can facilitate information retrieval processes and thus improve <sup>fi</sup>t perceptions [34,38]. Online product integration is a strategy to introduce a product by referring to its focal product, whose effectiveness is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by differences in the product integration level [14]. When integration level is high, the integration offers additional product features. Therefore, the available cues or stimuli for eliciting associations will increase, which result in more shared associations between extended product and its parent brand [38]. For example, the integration between Yahoo! portal and Yahoo! mailbox provides users with additional features of viewing new messages without logging-on the inbox page. Such may elicit associations of convenience, usefulness, and timeliness between the two, which in turn facilitate an increase in <sup>fi</sup>t perceptions.

When the integration level is low, the integration only facilitates the transfer of data across the two products. In this situation, users can only engage in understanding the newly extended product through the transfer of data [45]. Therefore, without additional product features, users are unlikely to have a better understanding of the newly extended product. With a minimal level of functional integration, users may have more dif<sup>fi</sup>culties eliciting associations between the newly extended product and the parent brand. Therefore, we propose:

H2. The product integration level has a positive impact on the perceived <sup>fi</sup>t between the parent brand and the newly extended product.

3.3. Impacts of perceived tie and perceived fit on evaluations of brand extensions

If users perceive a high-level tie between the focal and newly extended products, these two products will be perceived as constituents of a coherent single unit [63,67,68]. The new product will be more easily matched with the current category, and categorization processes will ensue. Consequently, the values associated with focal product (such as utilitarian, hedonic, and social values) are likely to be transferred to the newly extended product [8]. When the perceived tie is low, piecemeal processing will be evoked, and the products will be perceived as being an aggregate of individual members. Each product will be treated as a separate unit, and the transference of values from the focal product to the newly extended product will be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. This may increase uncertainty about the values of the new product. Users may even suspect the trustworthiness of the online <sup>fi</sup>rms that recommend the newly extended products. Empirical studies have generally supported the expectation that the success of a brand extension depends on the users' inferences about the performance of the newly extended product [11,42]. This leads us to the following:

H3. The perceived tie between the two involved products has a positive effect on attitude toward the extended product.

If users perceive a <sup>fi</sup>t between the parent brand and newly extended product, the number of shared associations will be high. The extension will be more easily matched with the original category, and categorization processes will occur. Therefore, perceptions would be transferred to the newly extended product. When the <sup>fi</sup>t is weak, piecemeal processing will be evoked, and the attitude will be “computed” from the unique, speci<sup>fi</sup>c attributes of the extended product. Users will consider information from personal, hands-on experiences, word of mouth, and other available sources. They will evaluate each piece of information separately, and through their own attribute-integration process, arrive at a <sup>fi</sup>nal judgment about the extended product [71]. Users may even question the competency of the <sup>fi</sup>rm to supply the extended product because of an inability to match the newly extended product with the image of the parent brand [20,49]. Thus, we expect a positive association between perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and attitude toward the extended product.

H4. The perceived <sup>fi</sup>t has a positive effect on attitude toward the extended product.

Attitude is a psychological tendency that is expressed by evaluating a particular target with some degree of favor or disfavor [19]. The particular target can be an object such as an information product, or behaviors toward an object, such as using an information product [80]. Attitude toward an object has a strong positive effect on attitude toward behaviors toward the object. Attitude toward behaviors has been regarded as an important predictor of information technology usage intention and behavior [22,33,74]. According to the theory of reasoned action, behavior is a function of attitude toward behaviors which re<sup>fl</sup>ects a combination of evaluative judgments and feelings toward performing a particular behavior [3]. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H5. Attitude toward extended product has a positive effect on intention to use the extended product.

## 3.4. The moderating role of user expertise

As an environmental stimulus, product integration represents a persuasion opportunity for vendors, and can in<sup>fl</sup>uence online users' perceptions and behaviors. However, users' judgments about this persuasion strategy may be moderated by their judgment ability. According to the elaboration likelihood model (ELM), individuals vary widely in their ability to elaborate on a stimulus' central merits, which in turn constrains how a given stimulus impacts an individuals' perception formation or change [51]. One key enabler of such variedness is individuals' ability to process detailed information in a given stimulus. Persuasion occurs via the central route when information processing is based upon critical thinking and the stimulus is given due consideration. Individuals scrutinize all available information relevant to the stimulus. When individuals lack the ability for critical thinking and due consideration to process the detailed information in the stimulus, they engage in peripheral processing, which relies solely on visible and simple cues, and triggers less cognitive effort than the central processing for judgment formation. Consequently, the individuals undertake less-thoughtful processing and process only part of the information, which results in rough observation and decision making.

Drawing on ELM related literature, user expertise is an individuals' ability to use focal product [7]. With expertise, users are more inclined to scrutinize new stimulus carefully and identify key acceptance-related arguments. Consequently, expert users have higher cue-detection ability regarding production integration, forming more accurate perceptions, and making more informed judgments through examining the integration cues. In contrast, novice users, with less knowledge and skill, may employ less cognitive effort in framing their tie and <sup>fi</sup>t perceptions [7]. Therefore, as a persuasion strategy, product integration may be less in<sup>fl</sup>uential on formation of perceived tie and <sup>fi</sup>t. This suggests that user's expertise of focal product may play a positive moderating role on the effects of product integration on perceived tie and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H6. User expertise with a focal product moderates the effect of product integration on perceived tie: the greater the expertise, the stronger the effect.

H7. User expertise with a focal product moderates the effect of product integration on perceived <sup>fi</sup>t: the greater the expertise, the stronger the effect.

## 4. Research method

We conducted a quasi-experiment to test the seven hypotheses as this approach allows us to manipulate key variables, and to exercise control over extraneous variables. We used a between-subject (data interface, add-on module, and value-added integration) factorial design. Product integration was the manipulated factor and randomly assigned to three groups. A total of 190 students from a public university in China participated in the experiment. Prior to the study, the subjects were informed that they would each receive \$5.00 as an incentive for their participation.

## 4.1. Manipulation

We operationalized information product integration by using a scenario-based method (e.g. [48,58,77]). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we manipulated three types of integration (data interface, add-on module, and value-added) using different scenarios. Following the call by Kling and Smith [34] to increase the generalizability of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, two different online information products, as opposed to a single product, were examined in this study. A search engine (Baidu) and two new products it was promoting (an e-commerce website and an online Chinese encyclopedia) were adopted for this study to yield a total of six different scenarios.

In the data interface integration scenarios, the vendor used one hyperlink to promote the new product (i.e. the e-commerce website or the encyclopedia). When logging on to the search engine, if users wanted to use the promoted products, they needed to click the hyperlink (see Appendix A for illustration). In the add-on module integration scenarios, there was a pop-up messenger to promote the new products. When users logged on the homepage of the search engine, a messenger popped up. If users wanted to use the promoted product, they needed to click the link that appeared in the messenger window. In the value-added integration scenarios, vendors supplied additional features based on the coupling between the focal and the extended products. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in the case of integration between the search engine and the e-commerce website, when users searched one item in the search engine, the relevant links about this item in the e-commerce website appeared as multiple search suggestions. Without visiting the homepage of the e-commerce website, users could <sup>fi</sup>nd the item from the e-commerce website directly. In the case of integration between the search engine and the online encyclopedia, when users queried one term in the search engine, the relevant links in the online encyclopedia also emerged as multiple search suggestions. Users, therefore, could <sup>fi</sup>nd the explanation about this concept from the online encyclopedia directly.

## 4.2. Procedure and task

Participants were told that instructions were provided online and that they should read these carefully, and complete the task independently. Because two product evaluation tasks were involved in the experiment, the order by which subjects examined products was randomized, so that half of the participants examined the search engine and e-commerce website integration <sup>fi</sup>rst, while the other half examined the search engine and online encyclopedia integration <sup>fi</sup>rst. As is commonly used in marketing research, after participants logged into the online experiment system, a cover story was provided to them. They were told that, following its great success in the Chinese search engine market, Baidu recently developed a new product (an e-commerce website or an online encyclopedia site) and decided to use its search engine to promote this new product. Participants were also told that their feedback about the new product and the relevant promotion strategy would be important to this vendor. For the e-commerce website integration case, participants were asked to act as a potential user to navigate through the search engine, <sup>fi</sup>nd and log into the e-commerce website, and then browse the digital camera selection. For the encyclopedia integration case, participants were told to act as a potential user to navigate through the search engine, log into the encyclopedia, and then <sup>fi</sup>nd the descriptions about the subprime mortgage crisis.

Next, the subjects were randomly assigned to one of the three treatment scenarios generated by our Web-based system, so that each respondent had an equal and independent chance of being put into any of them. Then, using the assumed task as the example, the subjects were presented with the introduction of the integration used for promotion. In order to make the scenario appear realistic, this introduction took the form of an animation, demonstrating the actions that users could perform on the website (e.g., entering the keywords, clicking the links and looking at products available). Every participant went through these same actions in each condition to ensure that they were familiar with every aspect of the interfaces. The experimental system logged the site visits made by subjects to ensure that they had actually read the manipulated condition. Finally, the subjects were asked to complete an online questionnaire, including items for manipulation checks and dependent variables. A total of 185 questionnaires were valid, yielding 370 (185× 2) observations for analysis.

## 4.3. Measurement

Constructs were operationalized using validated items from prior research, with minor changes in wordings to <sup>fi</sup>t the investigation context. The measures of attitude toward brand extensions (ATTE) were derived from Crites et al. [19] and Zhang et al. [80]. The measures for intention to use the extended product (INTE) were adopted from Wixom and Todd [76]. Perceived <sup>fi</sup>t was assessed using measures from Bottomley and Holden [8] and Volckner and Sattler [75]. For measuring perceived tie, we used the construct from Stewart [67,68]. We developed four items to measure the level of information product integration (INTGL) based on its de<sup>fi</sup>nitions from Nambisan [45] and Stevens et al. [66]. We tested subjects with 5 questions on the search engine usage (Baidu) and calculated the total number of correct answers as user expertise. All survey items are listed in Appendix B.

## 5.2. Evaluating the measurement model

Following Anderson and Gerbing [5], the measurement model was tested for all multi-item constructs to ensure the psychometric property of the instrument. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to test the convergent and discriminant validity. To assess the convergent validity, three tests were used: factor loading of items, composite reliability of constructs, and average variance extracted by constructs. We used the method proposed by Lastovicka and Thamodaran [39] to crosscheck discriminant validity. They suggested the use of the average variance extracted (AVE), which provides information about the amount of variance in items that are explained by the corresponding construct. For each construct, if the square root of its AVE is greater than its correlation with other constructs, discriminant validity is established [26]. Table 2 lists the results of discriminant validity testing. As both dependent and independent variables were collected from a single informant around the same time, we analyzed the severity of common method variance. First, following Podsakoff and Organ [52], we used Harman's one-factor test to examine the extent of the bias. The results of principle component analyses indicated that common method variance is not a problem because several factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed while no single factor accounted for almost all the variance. Second, following Podsakoff et al. [53], we included a common method factor in the PLS model. The indicators of this common factor included all the principle constructs' indicators. After calculating each indicator's variance substantively explained by the principal construct, we found that most of the method factor loadings were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Given the small magnitude and non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the method variance, we contended that common method bias is unlikely a serious concern in this study.

## 5.3. Testing the structural model

Following the establishment of the measurement model, the structural model was assessed. The proposed model was tested through structural equations modeling (SEM) with PLS Graph Version 3.0. Tests of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of all paths were performed using the bootstrap re-sampling procedure. Prior to estimating the structural models, we estimated interaction terms for product integration level and user expertise. First, in order to avoid computational errors by lowering the correlation between the product indicators and their individual components, we standardized and centered the indicators of each construct [16]. Then, we multiplied indicators of user expertise by indicators of product integration level. Finally, the products were used to estimate the interaction of user expertise with product integration level in the structural model [69].

Fig. 2 presents the results of the estimated standardized path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients. As shown in Fig. 2, most of the hypothesized paths in the research model were found statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. As predicted, product integration has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on perceived tie (0.53) and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t (0.44), providing support for H1 and H2. Results from a Scheffe post hoc test further revealed that: (1) the value-added integration is associated with a strong perceived tie and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t than the data interface integration and the add-on module integration; (2) the perceived tie and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t in the add-on module integration are not stronger than that of the data interface integration, thus providing partial support for H1 and H2.

Perceived <sup>fi</sup>t (0.46) and perceived tie (0.19) both have a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant impact on attitude toward the extended products, supporting H3 and H4. R squares show that perceived tie and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t explain about 34% of the variances in attitude toward the extended products. Additionally, attitude toward extended products has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the intention to use the extended products (0.67). Hence, our study found support for H5. As expected, user expertise has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the relationship between the product integration level and perceived tie (0.18). However, contrary to our expectations, user expertise is not found to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the association between product integration level and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t (0.04).

To further interpret the interaction effect between user expertise and product integration level, we coded product integration as a dichotomous variable and conducted a regression analysis based on the general linear model. Table 3 summarizes the model <sup>fi</sup>t and shows that for perceived tie, two signi<sup>fi</sup>cant main effects, namely product integration level and user expertise, and one signi<sup>fi</sup>cant two-way interaction effect between the two measurements exist (pb0.05). In contrast, only two main effects are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for perceived <sup>fi</sup>t. Post hoc analysis based on Scheffe test reveal that, for the strong expertise subgroup, value-added integration is associated with a higher level of perceived tie than the data interface integration and add-on module integration. Add-on module integration is also associated with a higher level of perceived tie than the data interface integration (see Table 4). However, for the weak expertise subgroup, value-added integration is not associated with a higher level of perceived tie than other integration forms. Add-on module is not associated with a higher level of perceived tie than data interface integration (see Table 5). These results support H6.

To increase the reliability of the results, a multi-group analysis for differences across products was conducted. Because the variances do not differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly across groups, T-tests were applied to assess the differences for each pair of path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients [15,77]. Results show that there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant path difference between the e-commerce website and the online encyclopedia samples (t values are between −0.51 and 0.71). This <sup>fi</sup>nding indicates that the results remain robust across different subsamples. We also tested the mediating effect of perceived tie and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t between product integration and brand extension evaluation following Baron and Kenny's [6] formal test for mediation. Results verify the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant existence of the mediating effect.

## 6. Discussions and conclusion

## 6.1. Discussions of findings

Research to date on brand extension has overlooked the design elements that are important factors for brand extension success. Although studies have found that perceived tie and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t play important roles in online brand extension evaluations (e.g. [64]), this study demonstrates that, as a design element, product integration can in<sup>fl</sup>uence perceived tie and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t, and consequently impact users' online brand extension evaluations. Since the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of perceived tie are relatively weaker than that of perceived <sup>fi</sup>t, we interpret this to mean that (1) perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and perceived tie have different effects, and that (2) perceived <sup>fi</sup>t is a primary mediator, and perceived tie is a secondary mediator in the impact of product integration on online brand extension evaluations.

Among the three product integration levels, perceived tie and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t in the add-on module integration are not higher than that of the data interface integration. Thus, H1 and H2 were partially supported. This may be due to the dual factor (presentation and function) manipulation of product integration. The data interface integration and add-on module integration share a similar interface design, except for the pop-up window and the hyperlinks. Although a pop-up window may easily draw the subjects' attention and present more content, they provide vendors with limited options to enhance the overall value to users. Therefore, the functional sharing between focal and promoted products is relatively low for add-on module integration. Faced with low interaction between products and limited cues for making associations, online users may have dif-<sup>fi</sup>culty perceiving the advantage of add-on module integration in product promotion. Thus, designs that make additional functions on the basis of coupling between focal and promoted products are a key driver for the success of product integration.

Contrary to our expectation, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd the moderating effect of user expertise on the relationship between product integration level and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t. Brand-speci<sup>fi</sup>c associations are perceived to be more diagnostic than other cues. When a user's knowledge level of the focal product is high, brand-speci<sup>fi</sup>c associations dominate the effects of perceived <sup>fi</sup>t [11]. Hence, experts' evaluations are based on the processing of brand-speci<sup>fi</sup>c associations. In contrast, novices' evaluations are based on perceptions of product category similarity [79]. Therefore, users' shifted focus may counteract the moderating role of user expertise on the relationship between product integration and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t, resulting in no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating effect of user expertise.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

There are several theoretical implications. First, in viewing product integration as one important design element, this study contributes to the existing literature by recognizing and developing formal conceptualizations of product design factors that uniquely pertain to online brand extension. Although the antecedents and process of brand extension evaluations have been explored in previous research, design elements have not been studied and operationalized, especially in the context of online brand extension. By examining the design variables together with user differences in product expertise, this study makes an innovative theoretical contribution by providing novel insights into the success of online brand extension.

By revealing the inherent linkages among product integration design, user cognitive processes and behavioral consequences, this study further advances our understanding of adoption intention between different information products from the product design perspective, especially if these products share the same brand. Although IS researchers have examined the usage intentions between different information products, few researchers have scrutinized these intentions from the product design perspective. This study shows that, as a strategic design element, product integration level positively impacts perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t between focal and newly extended products, and consequently in<sup>fl</sup>uence users' intention to use the extended products. This study suggests that the rich practice of online information product design can and should be applied in future IT acceptance and usage research.

Finally, this study enriches the literature by offering new insight into the stretchability (i.e. extension potential) of online brands [2,42,78]. As brand extension has become an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient strategy for brand value exploitation and new product introduction, the growth in online brand extension practices reveals the importance of identifying factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the stretchability of an online brand. Hence, the extent to which a given brand transfers successfully into other various categories has generated high levels of managerial and academic interest [21]. Existing literature on brand stretchability mainly explores consumer differences and their effects on perceptions of brand extension <sup>fi</sup>t (e.g. [2,79]). Our model details a process whereby comprehensive function sharing and additional product features offering can increase users' attitude toward the extension. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of few that have attempted to investigate how to enhance the stretchability of online brands from the product design perspective.

## 6.3. Practical implications

Our study also has important implications for business practice. First of all, this study con<sup>fi</sup>rms product integration as a valuable online information product design strategy. Brands have been widely considered to be the most important assets of a <sup>fi</sup>rm [32]. With the cost of introducing an entirely new brand often being prohibitively high, brand extensions represent a strategic tool for companies to exploit an online brand's value. However, brand extension does not necessarily mean that new products will <sup>fi</sup>nd success [21,27]. While companies are spending a substantial amount of resources in online promotion, an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient product integration approach represents an opportunity for <sup>fi</sup>rms to succeed in promoting their newly extended information products online [13]. This study validates three useful product integration approaches for <sup>fi</sup>rms to operate in various online situations.

Second, the integration of new information products with established products has become a source of market advantage and a strategic necessity. To ensure product competitiveness in dynamic and evolving electronic markets, it becomes imperative for Internet <sup>fi</sup>rms to integrate their products with as many different existing complementary products as possible [18]. With the rapid development of multimedia and Internet technologies, online information product designers have an increasing collection of tools at their disposal to offer additional product features. However, value-added integration calls for extensive sharing of product and technical knowledge, as well as careful modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the focal product. Therefore, the initial design and development of products should be <sup>fl</sup>exible enough so that <sup>fi</sup>rms can rapidly integrate their products with new products to respond to user demands and changing environments [46].

Finally, this research also provides a valuable perspective to further explore the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of product bundling, an important promotion strategy widely discussed in recent literature [31,73]. Product bundling is a marketing strategy for integrating two or more related products and selling them at any price [70]. The primary goal of product bundling is to enhance the perceived functionality of bundled products and provide value-added bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to users [55]. This is consistent with the de<sup>fi</sup>nition and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of value-added integration. According to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our research, product bundling can increase users' intention to use the newly extended product. Consequently, once vendors have gained a strong user base through one <sup>fl</sup>agship product, vendors can package newly extended products with the <sup>fl</sup>agship product. Through the product bundling strategy, multi-product vendors can facilitate user overlap and extend their leadership from one market to another.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research directions

First, this study investigates two products sharing the same brand. Consumers' intention to use the extended products may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by whether these products share the same brand. Although as many as eight out of ten new product introductions are brand extensions [59], this study may overestimate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of product integration on users' intention to use extended products. As a result, replication of this study in other types of contexts, such as focal and extended products that do not share the same brand, is necessary before the results can be generalized. Second, although the cognitive process of online users' evaluations explored in the study contributes to an innovative approach for practitioners to exploit online brand extension strategies, the investigation is not yet completed. With the fast development of multimedia and Internet technologies, newly emergent approaches for offering additional product features may be developed. Detailed presentations of each product integration type, which may cause various brand extension effects, should be further investigated following the S–O–R paradigm [30]. Finally, as is the case with recent research on privacy issues [77], a scenario-based approach is considered appropriate at the initial adoption stage of information products and services. Nevertheless, we believe that such an approach represents a simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation of the real online promotion context, which limits the generalizability of research results. A longitudinal <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, in which participants encounter more realistic experiences in online promotional environments, would provide more robust <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

3. Existing main product and new launched product have a business relationship with one another.

1. The strength of the dependency between products is high.
