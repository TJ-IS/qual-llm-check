# DO TECHNOLOGY RESOURCES INFLUENCE INCOME MOBILITY? THE ROLE OF REGIONAL AND CASTE SPILLOVERS FROM COMPUTER OWNERSHIP<sup>1</sup>

Che-Wei Liu W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University Tempe, AZ, U.S.A. {cheweiliu@asu.edu}

Sunil Mithas Muma College of Business, University of South Florida Tampa, FL U.S.A. {smithas@usf.edu}

Terence J. V. Saldanha Terry College of Business, University of Georgia Athens, GA U.S.A. {terence.saldanha@uga.edu}

How do technology resources such as computer ownership help create opportunity equality and facilitate income mobility? This is an important question because opportunity constraint, or the lack of opportunity equality, often manifests in low income mobility in many countries. This study addresses this question by examining the spillover effects of computer ownership of households in terms of household income mobility considering spatial proximity and social proximity. Drawing on knowledge spillovers theory, we hypothesize that households experience upward income mobility due to increased computer ownership among households in spatial proximity. Further, drawing on social capital theory, we hypothesize that households experience higher upward mobility due to increased computer ownership among households in the region that are socially proximate or in the same caste. Our empirical analysis of data from over 32,000 households across all states in India from two waves (2005 and 2011) of the Indian Human Development Survey supports our hypotheses. Our exploratory analyses suggest that households in regions with high social harmony experience higher upward income mobility from increased computer ownership among households in the region. We also found that increased computer ownership among households in the region helps households belonging to disadvantaged castes overcome low income mobility. Overall, the study shows how the returns to computer ownership in terms of household upward income mobility go beyond private returns, and spill over to other households in spatial proximity, particularly to those belonging to the same caste. A key implication for policymakers is to use twin levers of social harmony and technology resources to create opportunity equality to facilitate income mobility, instead of focusing solely on technology resources.

Keywords: Computer ownership, income mobility, caste, spillovers, social capital, India, spatial proximity, social proximity, equality of opportunity, opportunity constraint

## Introduction

Opportunity equality, which refers to the notion that an “individual’s economic (or welfare) prospects are independent of attributes such as race, gender and the economic class to which one is born” is a widely considered to be desirable for societies (OECD, 2023; Pignataro, 2012, p. 800). The lack of opportunity equality, often called opportunity constraint, presents a major problem for societies and economies because it implies that poor people are likely to remain poor regardless of their aspirations, potential, or effort. Opportunity constraint often manifests in low income mobility (Azam, 2022) in that it creates friction in climbing the income distribution ladder, relative to others (Chetty et al., 2020; Chetty et al., 2014b; Martinez et al., 2013).

This study examines one view of income mobility, i.e., intragenerational household income mobility, defined as the movement of a household from one income tier to another income tier over time, often considered a key metric of economic progress (Carter et al., 2014). Household income mobility dynamically gauges a household’s position change in the income distribution (Jäntti & Jenkins, 2015; Mishra, 2018) by tracking how the same household gets ahead, falls behind, or remains in the same place over time (Woolard & Klasen, 2005). In contrast to measures of income inequality (e.g., Gini coefficient) that provide static and incomplete views of income distribution at a specific point in time (Deininger & Squire, 1996), studying income mobility, rather than studying changes in absolute income, considers the changes in income to be important only when the change is large enough that it enables the income recipient (e.g., household) to cross a quintile boundary (Fields, 2000).

Many government programs seek to promote household income mobility through digital initiatives that provide technology resources such as computers or computer skills. However, it is not clear if a focus on technology resources alone is desirable due to competing arguments for how technology influences income mobility. On the one hand, technology can hurt income mobility by “reducing demand for low-skilled jobs and rewarding high-skilled jobs disproportionately” (Autor et al., 2003; WEForum, 2020, p. 12).

On the other hand, technology can facilitate income mobility by increasing individuals’ access to information regardless of their background or socioeconomic status (Friedman, 2005; Venkatesh et al., 2020). Though some studies offer useful insights on the private returns to computer use in terms of income ( Krueger, 1993; Pabilonia & Zoghi, 2005), we know little about how technology resources influence income mobility at the individual or household level. Therefore, we focus on spillovers from technology resources (i.e., computer ownership), defined as the external benefits from technology resources that accrue to parties other than the owners of the resources (Agarwal et al., 2010; Goolsbee & Klenow, 2002). We study whether spillovers from household computer ownership in India occur due to interactions among households in spatial proximity, i.e., closeness in geographic distance, and social proximity, i.e., similarity in characteristics, such as identity, religion, caste, or ethnicity (Agrawal et al., 2008).

Emerging economies such as India exhibit low income mobility (WEForum, 2020), which amplifies the importance of studying spillovers from computer ownership on income mobility in such countries. For example, a report by the World Economic Forum ranked India 76th among 82 countries on social mobility (WEForum, 2020). The report also suggests that it takes seven generations for those born in a low-income family in India to approach the mean income (See Figure 1 of the Report), and it may take nine generations in South Africa and Brazil, in contrast to Nordic countries such as Denmark, Norway, and Sweden, where it takes only two or three generations.

Further, in many emerging economies, social structures such as caste-based social stratification facilitate or impede social interactions by erecting institutional walls (Bapuji & Chrispal, 2020; Ranganathan et al., 2016), thereby hurting the mobility of lower castes and disadvantaged social classes (Azam, 2022; Chetty et al., 2020). For example, people of some castes in India are considered to be of low status based on birth, restricting social interactions (Desai & Kulkarni, 2008; Srinivas, 1996). Caste is distinct from the notion of class in developed countries, which is mostly based on wealth (Sivaramayya, 1996). Caste is a “closed” category in that caste status is an attribute determined by birth, whereas class is an “open” category in that the class status of an individual can change during their lifetime (Sivaramayya, 1996; Srinivas, 1996). The Indian caste system, based on the varna, stratifies society into four castes, each traditionally associated with different occupations: Brahmins (priests, teachers), Kshatriyas (rulers, warriors), Vaishyas (traders), and Shudras (artisans, manual laborers) (Desai & Kulkarni, 2008).<sup>2</sup>

Against this backdrop, we address our research questions: (1) How does computer ownership among other households in the region (spatial proximity) influence the focal household’s income mobility? (2) How does the influence of computer ownership among other households in the region on the focal household’s income mobility depend on whether the other households are from the same or different caste group (social proximity) as that of the focal household?

Drawing on knowledge spillovers (Goolsbee & Klenow, 2002) as our primary theoretical lens, we argue that with an increase in computer ownership among other households in the region, a household’s income mobility increases upwards due to knowledge spillovers between the focal household and computer-owning households in the region. Further, drawing on the lens of social capital (Portes & Landolt, 2000), we argue that caste as a social structure is a source of social capital that bounds the spillovers from household computer ownership by facilitating knowledge sharing (Portes, 1998) and social learning (Lee & Bell, 2013) within the same caste, and hindering knowledge spillovers across castes.<sup>3</sup>

We analyzed data on household income mobility, computer ownership, and caste from a nationally representative dataset of over 32,000 households across all states in India in 2005 and 2011 (Desai & Vanneman, 2016a; Desai et al., 2016b) and obtained two key findings. First, a household’s upward income mobility increases with an increase in computer ownership of other households in the region in spatial proximity. Second, an increase in computer ownership among other households in the region of the same caste or in social proximity to that of the focal household is positively associated with the focal household’s upward income mobility. In contrast, an increase in computer ownership among households of castes that are different from that of the focal household has no significant association with the focal household’s income mobility.

Our exploratory analyses in the spirit of computationally intensive theory building (Miranda et al., 2022) also suggest that households enjoy higher upward income mobility from increased computer ownership among other households in regions with high social harmony (i.e., regions where people generally get along well with each other). We find that while households receive spillovers from computer ownership among other households of the same caste regardless of social harmony, spillovers from computer ownership among households of other castes require high levels of social harmony. These results resonate with the arguments supporting our hypotheses. We also find that increased computer ownership among other households in the region helps households belonging to disadvantaged castes overcome low income mobility.

Our study makes at least two key contributions. First, we go beyond examining the private returns to computer ownership by viewing the influence of computer ownership on household income mobility through the primary lens of knowledge spillovers arising from spatial proximity. Second, leveraging the concept of social proximity, we contribute by using social capital as a theoretical lens that bounds spillovers. We also contribute to the digital divide literature by surfacing how geographic spillover effects from computer ownership bounded by social capital (i.e., caste) increase household income mobility, a digital divide outcome that has received less attention in the literature (Wei et al., 2011). Our findings imply that the evaluation of the efficacy of technology policies in providing opportunity equality via household computer ownership should incorporate spillovers from computer ownership. When framing policies to promote opportunity equality from technology resources, policymakers should consider social institutions such as caste and complement such policies with initiatives that promote knowledge spillovers and social harmony among the “computer haves” and “computer have-nots.”

Together these contributions extend prior work that does not consider spillover effects of the type that we study here. For example, our related work (Liu et al., 2024b), which leverages data on more than 20,000 individuals documents that the positive returns to digital skills are greater for individuals from the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes than for individuals from other castes, greater for women than for men, and even greater for women than for men among individuals from the lowest castes. To the extent that “technology, and its use in human interactions” shape human destiny and outcomes (Singhal & Singhal, 2022a, p. 4276), our work here responds to the call for research examining the important role of technology resources and digital platforms in providing better opportunities to those at the base of the social pyramid (Corbett & Narayanan, 2022; Plambeck & Ramdas, 2020; Prahalad, 2005; Prahalad & Krishnan, 2008; Sunar & Swaminathan, 2022; Tang, 2022; Corbett et al., 2024).

## Background and Theoretical Framework

## Background and Prior Literature

We briefly review the three literature streams that are most relevant to our study.<sup>4</sup> First, our work focuses on the intragenerational mobility of households over a period of time (Sørensen, 1975) and draws on the household income mobility literature, which emphasizes the importance of household income mobility as a socially desirable outcome that increases equality in long-term incomes (Jäntti & Jenkins, 2015; Shorrocks, 1978). Prior studies have identified determinants of household income mobility that often vary based on national contexts. Determinants common across countries include education (Bhide & Mehta, 2004; Namirembe-Kavuma & Bbaale, 2019; Rowe et al., 2014; Shi et al., 2010), the employment composition of household members (Buchinsky et al., 2003; Castro, 2011; Rowe et al., 2014; Woolard & Klasen, 2005), the demographic composition of households (Bhide & Mehta, 2004; Shi et al., 2010; Woolard & Klasen, 2005), and social groups (e.g., castes) (Azam, 2022; Ranganathan et al., 2016).

Prior research on intragenerational (Azam, 2022; Ranganathan et al., 2016) and intergenerational mobility (Chetty et al., 2020; Chetty et al., 2022a, 2022b) in various national contexts collectively highlight the importance of social capital in impacting income mobility. Social capital is reflected in membership in different social groups (e.g., racial or caste groups) (Azam, 2022; Chetty et al., 2020) and in the economic connectedness between individuals (Chetty et al., 2022a). Studies examining social groups have found that disadvantaged groups have lower income mobility because they have less social capital than other groups (Azam, 2022; Chetty et al., 2020). Despite these important insights, the income mobility literature is silent on how technology resources and spillovers from technology resources impact household income mobility but does recognize that data availability is a key hindrance to the study of income mobility because it entails tracking the same economic units over time (Martinez et al., 2013).

Second, we draw on the literature on the digital divide, which can be disaggregated into the digital access divide and the digital outcome divide (Wei et al., 2011). Whereas digital access divide studies use digital access (e.g., computer, internet access) as a dependent variable (Agarwal et al., 2009; Hsieh et al., 2008), digital outcome divide studies examine outcomes of the digital divide (Leong et al., 2016; Venkatesh & Sykes, 2013).

Two types of studies in this literature are particularly relevant to our work. The first type concerns the impacts of IT on poverty, although our study is not concerned with poverty per se but with a household’s opportunity to move out of poverty. Studies of this type suggest that IT empowers individuals in rural areas to improve their standard of living (Jha et al., 2016) by aiding their access to markets (Leong et al., 2016) and financial services (Kendall et al., 2011), and facilitating earnings growth (DiMaggio & Bonikowski, 2008). The second type of studies in this literature concerns IT and social capital. Such studies, largely focusing on the digital access divide, suggest that factors such as social capital (Hsieh et al., 2011); utilitarian, hedonic, social outcomes; and demographic characteristics (Brown & Venkatesh, 2005; Venkatesh & Brown, 2001) influence computer adoption. Our work is most closely related to the digital outcomes divide stream but is distinguished from it because we study how spillovers from computer ownership bounded by social capital influence household income mobility.

Third, we draw on the IT spillover literature, which suggests that IT generates externalities that arise from nonadopters’ connections with adopters. Studies in this literature stream suggest that IT investment by one entity (e.g., country, firm, or individual) increases productivity or other economic outcomes (Kim et al., 2021; Moshiri, 2016; Tambe & Hitt, 2013) of other entities. This idea is consistent with the concept of spillovers from technology resources (e.g., computer ownership) (Agarwal et al., 2010; Goolsbee & Klenow, 2002).

A general theme in the IT spillover literature is that spillovers arise when knowledge is transferred through some form of interaction between entities that possess technology (or make technology investments) and other recipients. Such knowledge transfers are generated by interactions due to spatial proximity (Goolsbee & Klenow, 2002), supply chain interactions (Cheng & Nault, 2007), job hopping (Tambe & Hitt, 2013), or international trade (Kim et al., 2021). Drawing on mechanisms discussed in the IT spillover literature, we argue that spillovers occur due to knowledge shared via interactions between households with and without technology resources in spatial and social proximity.

## Hypotheses Development

We focus on the spillover effect of computer ownership and the role of caste, i.e., we focus on the benefits of technology resources such as computer ownership beyond the private returns to focal households. We posit that households gain upward income mobility due to an increase in computer ownership among other households in the region (spatial proximity) via knowledge spillovers. Further, we posit that because knowledge spillovers from computer ownership are bound by social capital, households gain upward income mobility due to an increase in computer ownership among other households in the region that are of the same caste group (social proximity) as that of the focal household.

## How Spillovers Arise from Spatial Proximity

We argue that households gain higher upward income mobility when there is an increase in computer ownership among other households in the region, for two reasons. First, when more households in the region own a computer, the focal household’s members can learn about digital knowledge and digital skills by interacting with members of other households that own a computer (Goolsbee & Klenow, 2002). Thus, spatial proximity fosters knowledge accumulation via the sharing of knowledge and skills through formal and informal interactions that generate positive externalities (Moretti, 2004). Computer ownership creates positive spillovers because spatial proximity fosters knowledge sharing (Agrawal et al., 2008) and social learning (Agarwal et al., 2009; Munshi, 2004), thereby facilitating knowledge spillovers. Knowledge spillovers from members of computer-owning households in the region help members of the focal household identify and exploit income opportunities, consistent with the idea that increasing the aggregate stock of human capability in a region leads to positive effects beyond direct effects on individuals (Moretti, 2004). In turn, the enhanced knowledge and digital literacy obtained via knowledge spillovers increase the wages of the focal household member(s) and help the focal household move up the income distribution ladder relative to other households.

Second, for households that already possess a computer, increased computer ownership among other households in the region results in learning that occurs via shared computer knowledge between the focal household and other households (Goolsbee & Klenow, 2002) and indirectly affects earnings. Through frequent and personal contact facilitated by spatial proximity, household members can receive information (Kalnins & Chung, 2006) that can be leveraged by the focal household to advance its economic standing relative to other households. For example, members of the focal household can exchange information about job opportunities with members of other households in the region, thereby getting more information and chances to increase their own income. Thus, computer ownership affords increased income opportunities not only to computer owners but also to other households in the region (Bresnahan & Trajtenberg, 1995). Moreover, shared information from computer ownership can increase incomes for households that work in similar industries. For instance, households in agricultural industries can benefit from reliable price information attained from computers, potentially allowing them to increase the price of their crops (Goyal, 2010).

In sum, an increase in computer ownership among other households in a region fosters the development of human capital for the focal household, facilitates social learning, and increases shared computer knowledge due to knowledge spillovers from other households in spatial proximity. In turn, the increased accumulation of human capital helps the focal household enhance its earning capacity (Manda et al., 2002) and move up the income hierarchy. Based on the aforementioned arguments, we propose that the focal household benefits in terms of upward income mobility when the proportion of other households in the region that own computers increases.

H1 (spillovers from spatial proximity): An increase in the proportion of other households in the region that own computers has a positive association with the focal household’s upward income mobility.

## How Social Capital Bounds the Spillovers from Spatial Proximity

We suggest that spillovers from spatial proximity are more pronounced when households are socially proximate (i.e., of the same caste or religious group). Specifically, we argue that the effect of computer ownership among other households in a region on the focal household’s income mobility depends on whether the other households are from the same caste or religious group (i.e., socially proximate) as that of the focal household. We build our arguments by theorizing that spillovers arising from spatial proximity are bound by social capital. We draw on social capital theory (Portes, 1998; Portes & Landolt, 2000), which views social capital as the “ability to obtain resources through networks or other social structures”, and to leverage social capital to increase “economic benefits” (Portes & Landolt, 2000, p. 534).

Social capital theory is an apt theoretical lens to explain how social capital bounds spillovers from computer ownership. Social structures such as caste are a significant source of social capital (Kalnins & Chung, 2006; Portes, 1998). The word caste derives from the Portuguese word casta, meaning breed or race, and caste has long existed in India (Deshpande, 2000). The caste system is a form of social stratification system that has undergone significant changes since India’s independence but still plays a significant role in modern Indian society (Agrawal, 2014; Deshpande, 2011). In India, “there is an increasing tendency to view caste as a form of ethnicity in which castes compete with each other for power and proudly brandish their own narratives of origin” (Desai & Dubey, 2012, p. 7). The caste stratification in India has also influenced religious or ethnic groups such as Muslims, Christians, and Sikhs (Desai & Kulkarni, 2008). Caste has significant social implications that restrict social and civil interactions (Desai & Kulkarni, 2008; Srinivas, 1996). On the one hand, as a social structure, caste erects institutional walls in society that disrupt social interactions and impede knowledge flows across social walls (i.e., across castes). On the other hand, caste facilitates the development of social capital and encourages social interactions and knowledge flows within social walls (i.e., within the same caste). Thus, the social capital lens suggests that caste as a source of social capital bounds the spillovers arising from interactions and knowledge flows within institutional walls erected by caste.

As argued previously, social interactions and knowledge spillovers are key underlying mechanisms that explain the influence of computer ownership among other households in the region on the focal household’s income mobility. Building on this argument and drawing on social capital theory (Portes, 1998), we suggest that geographic spillovers from computer ownership are bound by social capital, i.e., are stronger within institutional walls erected by caste. In other words, among households living close to each other, social capital derived from shared caste groups facilitates greater knowledge spillovers from computer ownership.

We posit that because caste facilitates knowledge spillovers within institutional walls enclosing the same caste and inhibits knowledge spillovers across institutional walls between different castes, a household will experience higher income mobility due to computer ownership among other households of the same caste for at least two reasons. First, caste can be regarded as bounded solidarity (with members of the same caste or religious group) and is thus a significant source of social capital (Kalnins & Chung, 2006). Bounded solidarity refers to “granting resources to others out of solidarity with members of the same territorial, ethnic or religious community” (Portes & Landolt, 2000, p. 533). For example, bounded solidarity derived from ethnicity affects entrepreneurs’ survival among immigrants (Kalnins & Chung, 2006) and technology transfer among scientific and entrepreneurial communities (Kerr & Mandorff, 2023).

Social capital derived from caste increases knowledge spillovers among households in a region because social interactions within the same caste are generally more frequent and influential than social interactions across different castes (Desai & Kulkarni, 2008). This notion that social capital facilitates knowledge spillovers among households is similar to the idea that social capital positively influences knowledge sharing in electronic networks (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Thus, caste as a social stratification system erects institutional walls because it impedes social interactions among members of different castes, whereas it facilitates interactions among members of the same caste (Bapuji & Chrispal, 2020). This argument builds on the idea that individuals are more likely to share knowledge with others who are similar to themselves (Dahlin et al., 2005). Hence, members of households in a region that are of the same caste are more likely to intermingle with each other and share information and knowledge obtained from computers than are members of households belonging to different castes.

Second, social learning, or the process of information transfer from one individual to another, is strengthened by social capital, such as the propensity of neighbors to trust or communicate with each other (Lee & Bell, 2013). Since individuals are more likely to learn from others who are similar to themselves (Dahlin et al., 2005), trust and reciprocity among members of the same caste (socially proximate) can facilitate social learning to a greater extent, compared to social learning among members of different castes (Agrawal et al., 2008). Due to “strong social ties within castes and the weak social interactions between castes” (Munshi, 2019, p. 785) and because “information from socially proximate caste peers is viewed as more credible” (Debnath & Jain, 2020, p. 467), household members are likely to be more strongly influenced by social interactions with members of households of the same caste than by social interactions with members of households belonging different castes. Because caste “represents highly salient communities of trust, exchange, and information sharing” (Debnath & Jain, 2020, p. 465; Munshi, 2019), social interactions, trust, and social ties among households of the same caste are likely to more strongly influence the social learning process (Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998) and thus strengthen the knowledge spillovers from the computer ownership of other households to the focal household.

In sum, we propose that social capital derived from caste bounds the spatial spillovers from computer ownership by strengthening knowledge spillovers (Desai & Dubey, 2012) and social learning because neighbors of the same caste are likely to have a stronger influence than those of different castes (Gille, 2012) due to greater trust and reciprocity (Debnath & Jain, 2020; Lee & Bell, 2013). For these reasons, we posit that a common caste or religious background helps the focal household receive the benefits of social capital manifested in increased knowledge spillovers and social learning from computer ownership among socially proximate households in the region. These mechanisms help the focal household obtain resources via networks and thus reap the economic benefit of increased income mobility relative to other households. In contrast, we propose that households experience weak to no influence from computer ownership among households of different caste groups due to the weakened social capital across the institutional walls erected by caste.

## Method

## Data

We leveraged archival data from households in India that were tracked in 2005 and 2011, with details on caste, computer ownership, and income mobility. The caste-based social stratification system is an “all-India phenomenon of groups that form a hierarchy” (Munshi, 2019; Srinivas, 1995, p. 3) that has persisted for generations and continues to play a significant role in modern Indian society (Agrawal, 2014; Bapuji & Chrispal, 2020; Deshpande, 2011).

India is also characterized by low income mobility and relatively low levels of computer literacy, further magnifying the importance of studying the impact of computer ownership on household income mobility in India (WEForum, 2020). For example, according to estimates, computer literacy is almost nonexistent for over 90% of India’s population (DefIndia, 2019). In 2016, only 12.96% of households in India owned a computer, with similarly low numbers in Pakistan (15.90%), Bangladesh (6.88%), Sri Lanka (17.77%), and Nepal (8.2%) (WorldBank, 2020).

We used two waves of a nationally representative survey from India, using the latest versions of data published in 2016 from the two waves of the Indian Human Development Survey (IHDS) from 2005 and 2011. These surveys were conducted by the National Council of Applied Economic Research (New Delhi) in collaboration with University of Maryland in 2004- 2005 (Desai et al., 2016) and 2011-2012 (Desai & Vanneman, 2016). IHDS surveys are a reliable data source and have been used in studies in management (Jaikumar et al., 2018), operations management (Liu et al., 2024b), and economics (Corno et al., 2020).

The IHDS surveys used a stratified multistage sampling procedure, resulting in a nationally representative sample covering households in all Indian states and union territories (except Andaman and Nicobar, and the Lakshadweep Islands, which have very small populations) (Desai et al., 2016). Respondents in the IHDS surveys were typically the household head or a knowledgeable person who was well-informed about the household’s economic condition, and the response rate of IHDS surveys was as high as 92% (Sonalde et al., 2010).

For each wave, the surveys covered 1,503 villages and 971 urban neighborhoods across India. The IHDS 2005 survey covered 26,734 households and 14,820 households in rural and urban areas, respectively, while the IHDS 2011 survey covered 27,579 households and 14,573 households in rural and urban areas, respectively.

Approximately 83% of the households surveyed in 2004-2005 were resurveyed in 2011-2012, enabling us to calculate income mobility for each household. This small amount of attrition does not raise major concerns about external validity for two reasons. First, the IHDS surveys are nationally representative (IHDS, 2021). Although the IHDS surveys did not provide a reason for why some households were dropped, some attrition is expected in large-scale surveys such as the IHDS surveys.

Second, the small amount of attrition observed in our study is similar to that observed in published studies that conduct surveys of households across time (e.g., Woolard & Klasen, 2005), where it is expected that some households will be missing in some years. For example, in a study of income mobility of households in Africa across two periods (1993, 1998), “85 percent of Africans in rural areas and 90 percent of Africans in urban areas who participated in 1993 could be re-interviewed in 1998” (Woolard & Klasen, 2005, p. 870). These numbers are very similar to the 83% overlap across the years in our study. As Woolard and Klasen (2005, p. 870) note, this attrition rate is “very low.” Further, the attrition rate in our data (IHDS data) is less than that reported in other studies. For instance, Carter et al. (2014, p. 8) stated that “only 63 percent of the original sample were interviewed in the final wave” (from 2002 to 2010), and Rowe et al. (2014, p. 12) matched “approximately sixty percent of all observations” in their time window (from 2001 to 2007). Moreover, attrition is a practical reality that researchers face when conducting studies using data collected from large-scale national surveys across time. As prior research (Woolard & Klasen, 2005, p. 869) notes: “the relatively few studies on income mobility in developing countries … is partly due to the paucity of reliable panel datasets” in developing countries such as India (Ranganathan et al., 2016).

## Variables

We constructed our main measure of income mobility using transition matrices over the two time points (2005 and 2011), consistent with prior work on income mobility (Jäntti & Jenkins, 2015; Mishra, 2018; Namirembe-Kavuma & Bbaale, 2019; Rowe et al., 2014; Woolard & Klasen, 2005). We divided households’ incomes into m groups (e.g., quintiles) in each period. Let P be a matrix of m × m transitions, and $P _ { i j }$ be the percentage of households whose income class falls in class i in the first period and class j in the second period. In the transition matrix, rows represent income classes in the first period and columns represent income classes in the second period. Following prior research (Mishra, 2018; Woolard & Klasen, 2005), we categorized households into a quintile (m = 5) such that households in a given year were sorted from highest to lowest, and each household was categorized into one of five categories.<sup>7</sup>

Similar to prior research (Rowe et al., 2014), based on the transition matrix, we computed the dependent variable, income mobility (Mobility). To categorize households into a quintile, we first calculated Household Income, which was derived from over fifty questions about agricultural topics, nonagricultural topics, pensions, and government benefits. Following prior research (Lukiyanova & Oshchepkov, 2012; Roberts, 2001; Woolard & Klasen, 2005), we used the adjusted household income, Adult Equivalent Income, to calculate our key dependent variable:

$$
\text { Adult   Equivalent   Income } = \frac {\text { Household   Income }}{(\text { Adults } + 0 . 5 \times \text { Children }) ^ {0 . 9}}\tag{2}
$$

where Adults is the number of adults in the household older than 18 years of age, Children is the number of children aged below 18 years. Using Adult Equivalent Income provides “a measure of ‘living standard’ that is comparable across households with differing compositions” (Creedy & Sleeman, 2005, p. 51). We followed Woolard and Klasen (2005) and set Children’s weight as 0.5 and used a scaling parameter of 0.9 to reflect modest economies of scale.<sup>9</sup> We log-transformed the Adult Equivalent Income variable to reduce skewness.

For computer ownership, we used data from the survey question that asked respondents whether their household owned a computer. We computed the change in computer ownership (∆CO) which meant a household had no computer in 2005 and owned one in 2011. The survey in 2005 asked participants whether they owned a computer or not, and the survey in 2011 combined computers and laptops into a single variable. Considering technology development in the 2005- 2011 period, the word “computer” in the survey could only represent a personal computer or a laptop. During the 2005- 2011 time frame, tablets and mobile phones with computing capability (e.g., smartphones) were not widely prevalent in India (Harjani, 2014).

To test H1, we calculated the proportion of households that owned computers at the district level (a district is similar to a U.S. county). Theoretically, the number of households in the chosen geographical unit of analysis should have been large enough for us to be able to detect spillover effects. For the spillover mechanisms underlying our hypotheses (e.g., learning, knowledge spillovers) to cause an increase in income mobility in a household, there would need to be a sufficiently high number of households in the geographical region.

The smallest geographical unit in the IHDS surveys is the primary sampling unit (PSU), which represents a village or neighborhood. A district is one level of aggregation above the PSU level. Our use of district as the geographical unit of analysis is consistent with prior research. For instance, Munshi (2004), an India-based study, used district as the geographical unit. Similarly, Agarwal et al. (2009) and Goolsbee and Klenow (2002), two U.S.-based studies that examined IT spillovers, used the metropolitan statistical area and city, respectively, as the geographical unit. A region larger than a district, such as a state, would be too large an area because it is less likely that social interactions among households will occur with other households in the same state, since households may be widely dispersed across large areas. This is especially true since many states (e.g., Maharashtra) in India are very large in terms of area. In all our models, we winsorized the district-level variables at the 5th and 95th percentiles to avoid extreme values. Although the number of households in a PSU may be too low to detect spillovers, our robustness analysis at the PSU level yielded similar results. Districts in India are of a large enough size; with 373 districts, the average number of survey participants per district was approximately 86, which is large enough to detect spillover effects.

Consistent with prior studies that have examined spillover effects in various contexts (e.g., Goolsbee & Klenow, 2002), we computed district-level computer ownership (DISTCO) as follows: <sup>10</sup>

$$
D I S T C O = \frac {\text { Number   of   households   owning   a   computer }}{\text { Number   of   households   in   a   district }}\tag{3}
$$

To test H2, we computed the proportion of households that owned computers at the district level based on the households’ caste and religion. Specifically, we computed the proportion of households in the focal household’s district (excluding the focal household) that owned a computer and were in the same caste or religious group as the focal household. We also computed the proportion of households in the focal household’s district that owned computers and were in other castes or religious groups, i.e., different from the caste or religious group of the focal household.

According to the IHDS survey, a household belongs to one of the following castes or religious groups: Brahmin; Forward Caste; Other Backward Classes (OBC); Dalit; Adivasi; Muslim; Christian, Sikh, and Jain. This categorization of caste-based groups, which also incorporates religion, is consistent with the recognition in prior academic literature that “caste and religion form major axes of social stratification in contemporary India” (Desai & Kulkarni, 2008, p. 247; Hasan, 2019) and consistent with our theoretical arguments that social interactions are stronger among the same groups than among different groups.

Our categorization of caste groups is also consistent with prior studies that have used IHDS datasets and relied on castes in similar ways (Desai & Dubey, 2012; Ranganathan et al., 2016; Thorat et al., 2017). For example, as explained by Thorat et al. (2017, p. 416):

We divide all households into four groups, Adivasis (Scheduled Tribes), Dalits (Scheduled Castes), OBCs (Other Backward Classes) and Forward Castes (all others). We use this fourfold classification for both Hindu and non-Hindu households because in other analyses not reported here we find that the differences between self-reported caste groups among Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, and others largely parallel those among Hindus. <sup>11</sup>

For each household, we calculated the following variables to represent district-level computer ownership in the same group (DISTCO (SG)), and district-level computer ownership in other groups (DISTCO (OG)):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { DISTCO(SG) } \\ = \frac {\text { Number   of   households   owning   a   computer   in   a   district   in   the   same   group   -   self }}{\text { Number   of   households   in   a   district   in   the   same   group   -   1 }} \end{array} \tag {4}\tag{4}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {DISTCO (OG)} \\ = \frac {\text {Number of households owning a computer in a district in the other groups}}{\text {Number of households in a district in the other groups}} \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

This measure combines Brahmins with all Forward Castes. Results using both these alternate ways of categorizing castes were substantively unchanged, thereby adding robustness to our findings. According to the IHDS User Guide (p. 25), “India has a vast array of jatis (castes) and religions. … it is useful to collapse religion and caste into a single composite variable.” We recognize that the operationalization of caste may differ across studies depending on the research question and focus (Liu et al., 2024). Before discussing the results for our hypotheses, we note that in Column 1 of Table 2, the coefficient of change of computer ownership (∆CO) is positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 8 7 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ Consistent with the idea of private returns from computer ownership, this suggests that a positive change in the household’s own computer ownership is associated with upward income mobility of the focal household. ## Causal Inference and Accounting for Endogeneity

We took several steps to enhance the interpretations of causality and alleviate concerns due to endogeneity (Mithas et al., 2022c), even though we saw significant value in rigorous descriptive analyses for advancing science. As an example, Chetty et al. (2014a, p. 1554) explicitly noted that their “descriptive analysis does not identify the causal mechanisms that determine upward mobility.” Yet their work paved the way to “facilitate research on such mechanisms” and their subsequent work (e.g., Chetty et al., 2022a, 2022b) made some progress in that regard. Relatedly, it is doubtful whether any one paper can ever “prove” causality by itself, as noted elsewhere (Mithas et al., 2022a, 2022c).

We estimated a two-stage least squares (2SLS) model to address potential concerns due to omitted variables, self-selection, or simultaneity (i.e., reverse causality). We considered household computer ownership and household computer ownership in the district to be endogenous by leveraging two instrument variables based on the wedding-gift culture in India.

The first instrument variable (WeddingGift) instrumented for the change in computer ownership of the focal household and captured whether a computer or laptop is sometimes or usually given as a wedding gift in the focal household’s community. The IHDS survey asked: “Generally in your community for a family like yours, what are the kind of things that are given as gift at the time of the daughter's marriage?”<sup>16</sup> We computed a binary variable (WeddingGift), which is defined as 1 if the respondent indicated that a computer or laptop is sometimes or usually given as a wedding gift in the respondent’s community, and 0 otherwise. WeddingGift is a valid instrument and satisfies the relevance and exclusion conditions (Greene, 2003) for two reasons. First, because marriage practices play a critical role in the Indian culture, households that live in a community that has a practice of giving a computer as a marriage gift are more likely to adopt computers since social norms generally make people conform to the local culture (Gerber & Rogers, 2009; Schultz et al., 2007). Hence, WeddingGift is likely correlated with household computer ownership, the potentially endogenous variable. Second, the mere practice of giving a computer as a wedding gift in a community is unlikely to directly influence the focal household’s income mobility, except via its influence on the focal household’s computer ownership.

The second instrument variable instrumented for computer ownership at the district level and is an aggregate measure of WeddingGift at the district level computed as the proportion of households in the district that indicated that there was a practice of giving a computer as a wedding gift in their community. The F-statistic (25.70) for the joint significance of the instrumental variables is well above the Stock-Yogo critical values of weak instruments at the 10% maximal IV size (7.03) (Stock & Yogo, 2005).

The coefficient of WeddingGift was positive and significant (p < 0.01) in the first-stage equation for the change in computer ownership (∆CO) (not shown for brevity). The coefficient of proportion of computer as a wedding gift in the district was positive and significant (p < 0.01) in the first stage for the change in computer ownership in the district (∆DISTCO). These positive and significant coefficients in the first-stage equation lend empirical validity to the use of WeddingGift and proportion of households indicating the practice of giving a computer as a wedding gift in the district as instruments for computer ownership at the household and district level, respectively. Column 4 of Table 2 shows the 2SLS results. The coefficients of change of computer ownership (∆CO) (β = 1.677, p < 0.01) and change of computer ownership in the district (∆DISTCO) (β = 1.148, p < 0.05) are positive and significant.

We considered and used two strategies to address the endogeneity of computer adoption by focal households due to self-selection: propensity score matching to address selection due to observables, and Heckman’s (1979) model to address selection due to unobservables.<sup>17</sup>

Our propensity score approach matched households based on computer ownership in the base year (2005), whereas the second approach matched households based on the change in computer ownership from 2005 to 2011; both the matching approaches yielded results broadly similar to the main findings (Appendix Table A2, Columns 1 and 2 respectively) (Mithas & Krishnan, 2009).

Turning to the Heckman (1979) approach, we used the twostep procedure, wherein the first stage is a probit model of change in computer ownership (as the dependent variable) on the variables expected to influence household adoption of computers. We then calculated the inverse Mill’s ratio, referred to as λ, using the estimates obtained from the first stage. Following prior studies in information systems (Huang et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2014; Saldanha et al., 2020; Saldanha et al., 2022; Whitaker et al., 2019), we conducted a sensitivity analysis to quantify the robustness of our findings to further rule out concerns about omitted variables. We calculated the impact threshold of the confounding variable (Frank, 2000) and assessed how strongly an omitted variable needs to be correlated with the dependent and independent variables to nullify the results. We applied Frank et al.’s (2013) method and concluded that it 56.43% of the sample (18,154 observations) would need to be replaced with cases for which the effect is 0 to invalidate the spillover effects. This high percentage suggests that our results were not driven by omitted confounding variables.

Overall, the above robustness tests largely assuage endogeneity concerns arising from omitted variables, selection, and reverse causality, thereby enhancing the causal interpretations of our findings.

## Validation and Refinement of Underlying Mechanisms

To validate the spillover mechanisms identified and refine our insights, we engaged abductively with our data and source literatures to refine our theoretical contribution (Miranda et al., 2022). First, to develop H1, we argued that spillovers from computer ownership of other households in the region arise due to social interactions and knowledge spillovers.

Our exploratory analysis of this mechanism examined whether the effect of the change in computer ownership in the district on household income mobility was stronger in districts where people get along well with each other (i.e., regions with greater harmony) than in districts with conflicts. We used a proxy for social harmony (Harmony) in a district by using an IHDS survey question that asked respondents to indicate whether people in their village or neighborhood generally get along with each other well or are often in conflict. On average, 54.9% of households indicated that their villages or neighborhoods got along well. We coded the Harmony variable as 1 if participants responded that people in their village or neighborhood “get along well,” and 0 otherwise. We then aggregated this variable to the district level by calculating the average of this variable in the district as a proxy to represent the level of social harmony in the district. Thus, following aggregation, Harmony became a district-level proxy for social harmony in the district.

We found support for this mechanism because the interaction of the change in computer ownership in a district (∆DISTCO) with Harmony is positive and significant (Table 3, Column 1 $, \beta = 3 . 4 6 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , implying that the effect of increased computer ownership in the region on household income mobility is greater in regions with more harmony.

Figure 1 depicts these results graphically, showing that the marginal effect of a 1% increase in computer ownership in a district (∆DISTCO) led to a 0.22% increase in the focal household’s upward mobility in a district where Harmony ranked at the 40th percentile, whereas the marginal effect was 0.37% in a district where Harmony ranked at 60th percentile.

Second, to develop H2, we argue that due to “strong social ties within castes and the weak social interactions between castes” (Munshi, 2019, p. 785), household members are more strongly influenced by social interactions with members of households of the same caste. Hence, we conducted an analysis to explore whether social harmony affects the impact of caste on mobility.

We found that ∆DISTCO(SG) × Harmony is nonsignificant (Table 3, Column 2, p > 0.10), suggesting that households enjoy spillovers from computer ownership among households of the same caste even if social harmony is low, i.e., regardless of social harmony. In contrast, while ∆DISTCO(OG) is nonsignificant (p > 0.10), ∆DISTCO(OG) × Harmony is positive and significant (Table 3, Column 2, β = 3.105, p < 0.01), and ∆DISTCO(OG) and ∆DISTCO(OG) × Harmony are jointly significant (chi-square = 14.73, p < 0.01). This finding suggests that to enjoy spillovers from computer ownership among households of other caste groups, there is a need for high social harmony.

Third, we conducted exploratory analysis of the underlying logic for the caste-related hypothesis (H2). Our key arguments for H2 were that (1) social interactions within institutional walls erected by caste are more frequent and influential than social interactions across different castes (Bapuji & Chrispal, 2020; Desai & Kulkarni, 2008) and (2) social learning is stronger among households of the same caste due to stronger social ties (Munshi, 2019) and greater trust and reciprocity among members of the same caste (Agrawal et al., 2008).

Consistent with these arguments, we expect that if the focal household’s district has many other households of the same caste as the focal household, then the focal household would have more influential and frequent social interactions and therefore enjoy stronger geographic spillovers of knowledge due to social learning. Hence, we expected that an increase in computer ownership in a district would have a stronger positive geographic spillover effect on households whose district had many other households of the same caste as the focal household. In contrast, we expected that households in districts that had fewer households of the same caste as the focal household would experience weaker geographic spillovers from an increase in computer ownership in the district because such households would be likely to have less frequent and less influential social interactions and weaker spillover of knowledge from social learning (Agrawal et al., 2008; Bapuji & Chrispal, 2020; Desai & Kulkarni, 2008; Munshi, 2019).

As an exploratory analysis of this mechanism, we used a binary variable (CasteBelongLargestGroup), defined as 1 if the focal household’s caste was the same as the most common caste or religious group in the district (i.e., same as the caste of the highest proportion of households in the district), and 0 if focal household’s caste was different from the most common caste in the district. As discussed above, based on our arguments for H2, an increase in computer ownership in a district (i.e., regional spillover) would be expected to have a stronger positive effect on households whose district had many other households of the same caste as the focal household.

Column 1 of Table A4 shows a positive and marginally significant $( \beta = 0 . 7 1 7 , p < 0 . 1 0 )$ interaction of the geographic spillover (∆DISTCO) with CasteBelongLargestGroup, suggesting that the geographic spillover from computer ownership was stronger for households that were of the same caste as the most common caste in their district. This is consistent with our arguments for how caste bounds the spillovers from computer ownership. We conducted another analysis by creating a binary variable (HighCasteDensity), defined as 1 if the focal household’s caste was a caste with high density in the district, i.e., a caste with higher occurrence than the mean proportion of all castes in the household’s district, and 0 otherwise. We tested the interaction of HighCasteDensity with ∆DISTCO. The results, shown in Table A4, Column 2, suggest that households whose caste had high density (HighCasteDensity) in the district enjoyed a stronger spillover effect (p < 0.10). These results are similar to the findings of the analysis using CasteBelongLargestGroup and are consistent with our logic for H2.<sup>18</sup>

Finally, we explored whether disadvantaged caste groups enjoyed stronger spillover effects than non-disadvantaged caste groups. We categorized households into disadvantaged caste (Dalit, Adivasi, and OBC) group and non-disadvantaged caste (Brahmin, and Forward Caste) groups because Brahmins and other Forward Castes are the traditional decision makers due to land ownership, while Dalits (Scheduled Castes) more often work as landless laborers, and Adivasis are typically the poorest of the poor (Thorat et al., 2017).

For this analysis, we restricted our analysis to Hindus only (since the other religions examined do not use caste categorizations; in other words, we dropped Muslim, Christian, Sikh, and Jain households from this analysis), consistent with prior research using IHDS data (Deshpande & Ramachandran, 2017). The results are shown in Column 3 of Table A4. The interaction of ∆DISTCO with DisadvantagedCaste is positive and significant (β = 1.000, p < 0.1), suggesting that the geographic spillover effect was stronger for households belonging to disadvantaged caste groups than for households belonging to nondisadvantaged caste groups. DisadvantagedCaste has a negative and significant coefficient (β = -0.239, p < 0.01), consistent with prior research findings that lower castes or disadvantaged social classes have lower mobility (Azam, 2022; Chetty et al., 2020; Ranganathan et al., 2016). Combined with the positive and significant interaction term, this suggests that although households of disadvantaged caste groups had lower income mobility, the geographic spillover effect of computer ownership helped these households overcome their lower income mobility.

## Ruling out Alternative Explanations for the Spillover Effects

We conducted further analyses to rule out alternate explanations for the spillover effects. One potential alternative explanation may be that caste is simply a proxy for geographical proximity. We did not consider this alternative explanation to pose a serious challenge in our setting because we did not view geography and caste as two exclusive and competing alternative explanations. Instead, our argument is that interactions among households of same-caste groups are stronger than interactions across other-caste groups within the same geography (i.e., H2 disaggregates the effects for the same and other castes within a particular region).

Also, caste and geographical proximity are conceptually distinct phenomena: the former is based on birth, whereas the latter is based on distance. Indeed, we found no evidence in prior literature that suggests that caste is a perfect proxy for geographical proximity in India. It is not uncommon for people of the same caste or groups to live in geographic proximity in India and even in the same neighborhoods. Especially given the diffusion of technology and urbanization, people of different castes often live in close geographic proximity to each other in many districts or villages, barring a few small areas where Adivasis may be the only population group. (e.g., akin to areas in the U.S. that are wholly populated by, for example, Amish people or Native American tribes).

Nonetheless, we conducted additional analyses that support the view that our social proximity results cannot be attributed to geographic proximity. First, if caste were simply a proxy for geographical proximity, when we narrowed our analyses to a more granular geographical region (i.e., village or neighborhood) we should have observed a positive and significant spillover effect from increased computer ownership among households of the same caste as well as among households other caste groups because they live close to each other. Therefore, we conducted analysis at the PSU (village or neighborhood) level, which is a more granular level than the district level. The PSU level results, shown in Column 1 of Appendix Table A5, indicate a positive and significant spillover effect from the same caste, but not from other castes. Because a PSU is small enough, the nonsignificant spillovers from households of other castes suggest that just being close to other households in the PSU does not explain income mobility.

Next, we conducted analyses using information on village size by using households in villages smaller than 500 acres. In very small villages, most households can visit other households within 30 minutes, and most households in such villages are in walking distance of each other. The rationale for this analysis is that if caste is irrelevant and is just a proxy for geographical proximity, we should find positive and significant spillovers from households of the same caste as the focal household as well as from those of different castes. However, the results in Appendix Table A5, Column 2 show that even when considering very small villages, computer ownership among households of the same caste as the focal household only had a positive and significant effect on household income mobility, whereas computer ownership among households of other castes had no significant effect. The results were robust even when we limited the area to an even smaller region (e.g., 300 acres, results are omitted for brevity). Thus, these results, using a very limited sample and a very small geographical region, address concerns that caste is just a proxy for geographical proximity.

To further assuage the concern that caste may be a proxy for geographical proximity, we conducted an analysis that excluded districts with a high proportion of households belonging to the same castes, i.e., districts where one caste dominated the sample in the district. We used a threshold of 0.8, i.e., we excluded districts where more than 80% of households were from the same caste. (Results were robust even using a threshold of 0.5). This ensured that we had enough variation in castes in each district and enabled us to tease out the effect due to geographic spillovers from the effect due to caste (because we removed districts with one dominant caste, thus avoiding the potential conflation of caste and geographic proximity). The results, shown in Table A5, Column 3, are consistent.

Thus, even under the assumption of caste-based agglomeration (i.e., assuming that caste is a proxy for geographic proximity), dropping districts with agglomerations of castes yielded substantively unchanged results. Indeed, if caste were just a proxy for geographical proximity, we would have expected the statistical significance of the spillover effects to disappear or at least expect the magnitude of the spillover effect to be significantly reduced once we dropped districts with a large proportion of households from the same caste.<sup>19</sup> Thus, even under the assumption of caste-based agglomeration, the results of our hypotheses held, thereby mitigating any concern that caste is a proxy for geographical proximity.

## Discussion

## Findings

This study has two key findings. First, we found that a household’s upward income mobility increased with an increase in computer ownership among other households in the region. This finding points to the salient role of knowledge spillover effects of computer ownership arising from spatial proximity, plausibly resulting from social interactions among households in the same region.

Second, we found that increased computer ownership among other households in the region had a stronger positive association with the upward income mobility of the focal household when the other households were of the same caste as the focal household. Given the nature of caste as a social stratification system in India and other countries, this finding resonates with our argument that the social capital derived from membership in the same caste strengthens knowledge spillovers, thereby bounding the spillovers from computer ownership arising from spatial proximity.

Our exploratory analyses shed light on mechanisms underlying our hypotheses. First, we found that the effect of increased computer ownership in the region on household income mobility was greater in regions with greater social harmony, implying that knowledge spillovers from computer ownership are amplified when people in the region get along well with each other.

Second, we found that households enjoyed spillovers from computer ownership among households of the same caste, regardless of social harmony. In contrast, experiencing spillovers from computer ownership among households of other caste groups requires high levels of social harmony. This finding resonates with our argument that household members are more strongly influenced by social interactions with members of other households of the same caste (vs. other castes).

Third, we found that households of the same caste as the most common caste in the household’s district experienced higher income mobility from increased computer ownership among households in the district than households belonging to a different caste than the most common caste in the district. This finding aligns with our theoretical argument that social capital derived from caste bounds knowledge spillovers from computer ownership. Finally, our post hoc analysis suggests that although disadvantaged castes have lower income mobility, spillover effects from computer ownership help disadvantaged castes overcome low income mobility.

## Contributions and Research Implications

Our study offers two main contributions with important research implications. First, we show that households experience higher income mobility from the computer ownership of other households in the region. We thus contribute by going beyond the private returns to computer ownership and viewing the effect of computer ownership on household income mobility through the lens of knowledge spillovers via social interactions (Gille, 2012; Heckman, 2000). A key theoretical implication for the digital divide conversation is that although it may not always be possible for households to directly acquire technology resources, households benefit from increased technology resources among other households in their region via knowledge spillovers from spatial proximity. This is an important theoretical contribution since most prior or related studies have focused on the private returns to computer use or digital skills (Krueger, 1993; Liu et al., 2024b; Miller & Mulvey, 1997; Pabilonia & Zoghi, 2005), for the same individuals.

Second, we contribute by showing how social structures such as caste matter for understanding how computer ownership in a region influences household income mobility. Our study underscores that the spillover benefits of computer ownership are bound by key social institutions such as caste. Although caste as a social institution has been entrenched for generations in many emerging economies, particularly in Southeast Asia, other social institutions (e.g., those based on minority groups) are prevalent, even in developed countries. There is limited research at the intersection of IT and caste or social institutions. Our study documents the salience of caste as a source of social capital that bounds how knowledge spillovers due to social (caste) proximity occur from computer ownership among other households to the focal household. Thus, we introduce the perspective of caste as a source of social capital to the scholarly discourse on IT and income mobility.

We highlight the potential of the caste perspective for informing future research on technology resources and household income mobility and inform research on IT and social institutions like caste more broadly. The insights from our study of spillovers among households bounded by social capital (represented by caste) complement prior studies focusing on IT spillovers at the levels of the firm (Saxenian, 1996; Tambe & Hitt, 2013), the industry (Cheng & Nault, 2007), country (Kerr & Mandorff, 2023), and the individual (Goolsbee & Klenow, 2002). A key implication for future research would be to assess how the spillovers from computer ownership are impacted by other factors or types of social strata beyond caste, such as those defined by immigrants or racial minorities (Hasan & Kumar, 2019). Although we found positive spillover effects from the same caste group and insignificant spillover effects from other caste groups, future studies might explore whether other caste groups might introduce negative spillover effects of computer ownership due to competition among different castes. Future work could study other boundary conditions that enhance or impede spillovers from computer ownership or technology resources more broadly.

Viewing our results of geographic spillover and caste in combination, a key theoretical insight is that the geographic region provides households with advantages from computer ownership among other households, particularly within institutionalized social categories, i.e., within institutional walls erected by social institutions such as caste. These findings contribute to the social capital theoretical perspective (Portes, 1998) by revealing how computer ownership impacts household income mobility through knowledge spillovers arising from spatial proximity in regions bounded by social capital derived from social proximity (e.g., caste relationships).

Although not a key contribution, our study shows that computer ownership increases household upward income mobility, an important digital divide outcome that has received less attention in extant literature (Wei et al., 2011). Our study of computer ownership’s effect on income mobility complements prior studies focusing on absolute individual wage changes. Our study thus contributes to the literature on computer literacy and the digital outcomes divide (Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015; Venkatesh & Sykes, 2013) and responds to calls for “research on the effects of a lack of a home computing environment” on the digital outcomes divide (Wei et al., 2011, p. 171). More generally, our study enriches the understanding of household income mobility as it relates to IT, thus complementing the income mobility literature that considers human capital, socioeconomic status, and labor market outcomes (Buchinsky et al., 2003; Cuesta et al., 2011; Dartanto & Nurkholis, 2013).

## Implications for Practice

Our findings have implications for understanding the social impacts of public policies related to IT. Our study reveals how computer ownership, which can be viewed as a digital access divide, affects the digital outcome divide of households in terms of income mobility. We offer policymakers a better understanding of the positive spillover effects of technology resources such as computer ownership on income mobility.

Our findings are informative for policymaking, particularly in emerging economies such as India, China, and Brazil that seek to enhance opportunity equality and the economic progress of households through computerization. From a practice perspective, understanding and documenting the spillovers from technology resources provide a more complete assessment of the efficacy of government digital initiatives (e.g., the Digital India initiative; see DigitalIndia, 2020; Sharma, 2023) to improve income mobility.

Our findings imply that households benefit from computer ownership among other households in the region due to spatial proximity. Our theorizing suggests that social interactions and knowledge spillovers are key reasons for the effect of computer ownership on the upward income mobility of other households. A key policy implication is that the benefits from technology resources are enhanced by initiatives that promote regional social harmony and knowledge spillovers among “computer haves” and “computer have-nots” in a region. The lens of knowledge spillovers from computer ownership and technology resources more broadly provides novel implications for IT policymaking, suggesting that IT policies should evaluate not only private returns but also the social spillovers of computer ownership.

Finally, a key implication is that policymakers need to consider social institutions since social capital derived from social proximity based on social structures such as caste plays a crucial role in determining whether households receive spillovers and thereby improved opportunity equality and economic uplift from computer ownership in their region. To reap the social welfare of the dissemination of knowledge spillovers from technology resources (e.g., computer ownership) in a region, policymakers should pay attention to reducing the cost of information exchange and removing obstacles to interactions among individuals who share a common identity, based, for example, on caste or ethnic or social identity.

Although the caste system is associated with myriad ills (Deshpande, 2011; Gupta, 1992, 2000), in our study, we observe it as a form of social capital that enables members of castes, especially disadvantaged castes, to receive spillovers from technology resources held by other geographically proximate members of their caste. More generally, a key message for policymakers aiming to foster opportunity equality from technology resources is that they should invest in and focus on creating social harmony as a lever for increasing income mobility instead of solely focusing on technology resources.

## Limitations, Future Research, and Conclusion

Like all empirical work, our study has limitations that can serve as starting points for future research. First, although our observation window of seven years (2005-2011) is comparable with similar windows examined in prior studies (Lukiyanova & Oshchepkov, 2012; Woolard & Klasen, 2005), future research using longer time windows could yield additional insights. Second, since our study is limited to one country, future studies should assess the generalizability of our results using other countries with social structures similar to caste.

Third, despite our many analyses that mitigate concerns of reverse causality and endogeneity, our findings “balancing rigor and reasonableness toward theory construction” should be considered in the spirit of “computationallyintensive theory construction” (Miranda et al., 2022, p. xii). As such, our findings should be considered as plausible and tentative, subject to “corroboration pending account” (Goldthorpe, 2001, p. 15) in further studies. Finally, although our categorization of caste follows prior studies that use similar categorizations (Desai & Dubey, 2012; Ranganathan et al., 2016; Thorat et al., 2017), the lack of more granular data on caste precluded us from categorizing caste in a more granular way. Future research could use more granular data on caste or jatis if and when such data becomes available (Chaurasia, 2023).

Our study suggests avenues for future research. First, although we grounded our hypotheses in a theoretical lens of knowledge spillovers bounded by social capital, future research could explore other mechanisms, perhaps using case-study or ethnographic approaches, to understand how the effect of computer ownership in a region occurs via social or within-caste interactions.

Second, future work could study how other technology resources, e.g., smartphones or specific IT applications such as social media, affect income mobility and other outcomes at the individual level, such as trading behavior and the performance of investors (e.g., Liu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024a; Pan et al., 2023). Likewise, given the hype associated with artificial intelligence, large language models, ChatGPT and similar technologies (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Mithas et al., 2022b), there is a need to assess how the deployment and use of such technologies may affect individual or household outcomes such as income and income mobility as they interact with old institutions such as caste. Such studies could illuminate how forces of digitalization shape phenomena such as Sanskritization and Westernization that have been at play in shaping human aspirations and societal changes for decades (Srinivas, 1956).

Third, future work could study factors beyond caste, such as ethnicity or individuals’ social ties, to explore how computer ownership influences income mobility. A related opportunity would be to study how nontechnology interventions, such as affirmative action policies (also called reservations or quotas), and technology ownership or skills influence educational or income mobility—perhaps drawing on work studying the effect of reserving seats for minorities and female students in college admissions on attendance among the targeted group (Bagde et al., 2016; Chen & Mithas, 2021). Another promising avenue would be to explore the effect of computerization, spillover effects, and caste on outcomes beyond income mobility (e.g., health outcomes and mortality), which could have implications for social welfare and policy.

To conclude, using nationally representative data from India, our findings reveal that household income mobility increases due to computer ownership among other households in spatial proximity, especially when households are socially proximate to other households of the same caste in the region. The novel insights from this study, using the lens of spillovers bounded by social capital derived from caste, are especially important in an age when many emerging economies with entrenched social institutions such as caste are seeking to improve opportunity equality and uplift their societies economically by expanding access to technology resources such as computers, artificial intelligence, and digital skills.
