# THE ROLE OF VENTURE CAPITAL IN THE FORMATION OF A NEW TECHNOLOGICAL ECOSYSTEM: EVIDENCE FROM THE CLOUD<sup>1</sup>

Dan Breznitz Munk School of Global Affairs, University of Toronto, 1 Devonshire Place, Toronto, Ontario M5S 3K7 CANADA {dan.breznitz@utoronto.ca}

Chris Forman Dyson School of Applied Economics and Management, Cornell University, 137 Reservoir Avenue, Ithaca, NY 14853 U.S.A. {chris.forman@cornell.edu}

Wen Wen McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin, 2110 Speedway Stop B6500, Austin, TX 78712 U.S.A. {Wen.Wen@mccombs.utexas.edu}

The diffusion of new information technology (IT) requires the development of an ecosystem of hardware and software producers, users, and other firms. It is widely believed that venture capitalists (VCs) can play a role in the development of such ecosystems. However, empirical research to elaborate on these points is lacking. Focusing on the recent rapid rise of one platform, cloud computing, we assemble a unique dataset to look at the period just before wide acceptance of the cloud. We find evidence of complementarity between VC financing and the introduction of new products offered over the cloud. Moreover, the complementarity effects are significantly stronger for firms backed by VCs that had rich experience in the IT industry and are significantly weaker for firms that had prior experience developing traditional client/server products. These results provide evidence that supports a role for VC financing in the creation of new technological ecosystems.

Keywords: Cloud computing, complementarity, platform ecosystem, product introductions, venture capital

## Introduction

A central theme in information systems (IS) research is the exploration of the diffusion and valuation creation of new forms of information technology (IT). However, the IS field traditionally focuses on the implications of IT investment for well-defined organizational units, such as groups, organizations, and markets (Sidorova et al. 2008; Tilson et al. 2010).

There is an increasing realization that this focus on the impact of digital infrastructures may be inappropriate in an environment of increasing digital convergence (Tilson et al. 2010). In particular, the diffusion of new digital infrastructures and the creation of economic value from them is often accompanied by innovation on the part of a technological ecosystem of hardware and software producers, users, and other firms that facilitate the adaptation of technological innovations into valuable uses (Greenstein 2010).

A move to a new technological trajectory is a paradigmatic shift in how core technologies are used. For such shifts to occur, it is not enough for the technology to be developed or even for a lead company to develop a product; an entire range of products and functions at the system level have to emerge in parallel (or at least in a sufficiently condensed period) so that companies can switch to the new technology (Rosenberg 1996). In this paper, we study whether one specific set of institutional actors can help to stimulate and facilitate the creation of new technological trajectories: venture capitalists (VCs). VCs are increasingly seen as playing a critical role in influencing the rate of success, internal functions, and specific tasks in new businesses growth.<sup>2</sup> However, despite the important role of VCs in facilitating the development of new industries and technologies (Kortum and Lerner 2000), and the widely held belief in both academia and industry that VCs active involvement in all aspects of their invested companies is what makes them different from other private equity investments (Hellman and Puri 2000, 2002), the reality is that we have very little systematic empirical evidence on the role that VCs play in influencing technological trajectories and the resulting effects on the diffusion of new digital infrastructures.

As a first step in advancing our knowledge in this key area, this paper focuses on the role of VCs in setting new technological trajectories, which, in the case of IT products, usually takes the form of new platform development. We define IT platforms as products, services, or technologies on which others build complements. Our aim in this paper is to establish that, in their rational pursuit of profit maximization, VCs act as important coordination and instigation agents for new IT platforms.

Our research offers several contributions to the literature. First, our research furthers understanding of how ITproducing firms transition to a new platform. Decisions to deploy software over a new platform involve both a set of technological investments required to adapt new general purpose IT to specific requirements and a set of organizational and business model changes. The challenges in this type of innovation are well known (e.g., Bresnahan and Greenstein 1996; David 1990; King et al. 1994). However, much of the research in the IS literature focuses on the technological transitions of IT-using firms (e.g., Attewell 1992; Bresnahan et al. 2002; Bresnahan and Greenstein 1996). As noted above, we know relatively little about the role of IT-producing firms in the ecosystem in facilitating the transition to a new technological platform. In the absence of this knowledge, we are less likely to know when and where new platforms will arise.

Our research is related to studies that investigate the impact of VC financing on innovation output and new firm formation (Kortum and Lerner 2000; Nanda and Rhodes-Kropf 2013; Samila and Sorenson 2010). Further, our main hypothesis— VCs facilitate the transition to new technological trajectories—is motivated by prior research on the investment behavior of VCs and the value-adding services that they provide (Hellmann and Puri 2000, 2002; Hsu 2006) and that investigates the types of firms that VCs include in their portfolios (Chemmanur et al. 2011; Mann and Sager 2007; Puri and Zarutskie 2012). However, none of these studies addresses the impact of VCs on the formation of new technological trajectories.

## Overview of Research Setting and Empirical Approach

The VC business model specifically seeks “big-ticket” financial exits that will not only cover the losses of their other investments but also generate enough payout to offer high rates of return to their own investors. The main way to achieve these kinds of exits is by betting early on new technologies in the hope that their invested companies will become the next Oracle, Apple, or Salesforce.com. By aggressively investing in new, unproven technological platforms, VCs ensure that capital is channeled to new businesses that either offer these technologies or offer products that utilize these technologies. As a consequence, on the macro level, a strong signal is sent to would-be entrepreneurs to rapidly develop these technologies and to prospective customers and financial markets that enough products are developed and a vibrant ecosystem is formed.<sup>3</sup>

We examine the salience of these arguments by developing and testing hypotheses within the context of the recent rapid rise of a new computing platform, cloud computing. We focus on a particular use of cloud computing, namely, as a platform to offer software-as-a-service (SaaS), which refers to software that is delivered over networks and managed by a central provider.<sup>4</sup> Our empirical investigation focuses on the period before the widespread acceptance of cloud computing and, hence, is based on a 1999–2009 sample of start-up firms that offer enterprise software. This was one of the first crucial areas to use cloud computing and includes such wellknown firms as Salesforce.com. A further advantage of our sampling strategy is that enterprise software firms had a clear decision to make as to whether to offer products “over the cloud” or through a traditional client/server (C/S) platform, a key requirement of our research design.<sup>5</sup>

VCs can facilitate the transition to new platforms through multiple means. First, through treatment: that is, the direct influence VCs may have on the technology decisions of their portfolio firms. Second, VCs can have that impact through selection: that is, both signaling to would-be entrepreneurs that if they wish to maximize their chances for securing VC investment they should focus their efforts on the new platform and selecting the best team out of the population of new startups focusing on the new platform. Given the goals of our study—to identify whether VCs play a role in the transition to new platforms—it is not essential for us to identify whether VCs have this impact through treatment or selection. Since either mechanism could give rise to this outcome, it is sufficient for us to show that either or both may be at work.

Our empirical approach is to study the micro-level decisions of VCs and their portfolio firms. These have important implications at the macro (i.e., systemic) level that interest us. To do this, we look for evidence as to whether VC financing is complementary to a start-up firm’s decision to offer cloud products. We define complementarities to exist when the combined effect of two or more decisions is greater than the sum of their individual effects. In our setting, this means that the value of offering products over the cloud is higher in the presence of VC financing and vice versa. Our model does not specify the particular mechanism through which VC financing and cloud products may be complementary. For example, one such channel is that VCs may facilitate the commercialization of cloud products by start-ups. Another is that, because of pressure from exit channels, VCs disproportionately invest in start-ups with a propensity to offer cloud products. Either mechanism could generate complementarity and support our hypothesis that VCs facilitate the transition to a new platform. It is unclear ex ante whether these types of complementarities will exist. For example, although VCs might be well equipped to handle risk, especially technological risk, they may prefer lower uncertainty by funding risky ventures that use existing technological infrastructure, because the necessary ecosystem already exists.

Historically, two approaches have been used to test for complementarity (Brynjolfsson and Milgrom 2013). The first is to measure explicitly the performance implications of these decisions to empirically test the effects of these decisions directly (e.g., Bresnahan et al. 2002; Ichniowski et al. 1997; Tambe et al. 2012). The second is to use the revealed preference implications of these performance differences to study clustering or correlation among practices (e.g., Arora 1996; Arora and Gambardella 1990; Cassiman and Veugelers 2006). Because of the difficulty of empirically identifying the performance implications of choices in our setting, we use the second approach. We follow recent research and use discretechoice models to identify complementarities (Arora et al. 2010; Fares 2014; Gentzkow 2007; Miravete and Pernias 2010). We develop a model of a firm’s simultaneous decisions to offer products over the cloud and to be financed by VCs. In the baseline model, firms decide whether to offer products over the cloud, receive VC financing, or a combination of the two. Complementarities are identified using a parameter that measures the additional payoff after both decisions are made.

We find that additional payoff accrues from offering cloud products and receiving VC financing. Based on the estimates of our model, the predicted probability of receiving VC financing and offering cloud products is 7.3%. In contrast, the counterfactual estimate of this probability in the absence of complementarities is 6.8%. That is, the likelihood of engaging in both activities is 0.5 percentage points (or 7.4%) higher. This represents a significant difference in model outcomes.

We check the robustness of our results to different assumptions about the distribution of error terms in our model, as well as a different identification strategy that relies on panel data. In addition, we employ a targeted qualitative strategy by conducting interviews with representatives from companies in our dataset and with leading VCs to help us gain deeper knowledge of their operations. This ensures that our interpretation of the results fits with their understanding of the VC industry’s investment–investee dynamics.

Our examination of micro-level data on the financing of individual start-ups allows us to extend our findings in two important ways. In particular, following the established literature (Bottazzi et al. 2008; Gompers et al. 2009), we examine the hypothesis that VCs with prior experience in the IT industry are more effective at performing the functions described above. We find that complementarity between VC financing and cloud offerings increases when the funding VC has greater experience with funding IT ventures. Second, to assess how treatment and selection might influence our results, we turn our attention to heterogeneity in results based upon the experience of the start-up. In particular, we investigate differences in our results based upon experience related to the prior technology. We find that the value of complementarities is lower for firms with prior experience in the legacy technology, measured by the number of C/S products. Our empirical results show that, in the presence of complementarity, the predicted probability of VC financing and cloud offerings is 7.9% when C/S product experience is low, but falls to 7.1% when C/S product experience is high.

## Theoretical Motivation

## Challenges in Developing Products on New Technological Platforms

The literature on IS and economics has made great progress in delineating how investment in IT and associated business process innovation contribute to productivity growth at the establishment (Bresnahan and Greenstein 1996; Mukhopadhyay et al. 1995), firm (Bresnahan et al. 2002), industry (Kundisch et al. 2014), and macroeconomic (Jorgenson et al. 2005) levels.<sup>6</sup> A key focus of this literature is the adjustments that firms must make to adapt general purpose IT to productive uses at firms. These are important matters; however, the digital infrastructure and applications adapted by firms do not spring fully formed from new technological innovations. In other words, it is widely realized that a “technology push” model, in which new technological innovations give rise to a search for applications of that technology, is incomplete (Kim 1997).

For example, a major focus of the IS literature has been the complementary organizational adjustments necessary to obtain productive value from enterprise software investments, such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems. However, ERP systems such as SAP R/3 did not arise automatically from technological developments in mainframe and C/S computing and relational databases. As emphasized in the IS literature, ERP products have evolved since the 1970s, partly because of cooperation between enterprise software firms and users engaged in business process innovation.

For a new technological platform to emerge, a range of products and functions have to be developed in parallel for companies to switch to the new technology. For example, it was not enough for the personal computer (PC) to be invented and for significant competition to develop among various makers of PCs.<sup>7</sup> Only after an entire ecosystem of software, services, data communication, and skills evolved around the specific Windows-Intel (also known as Wintel) platform did companies shift their computing paradigm from mainframe, mini, and workstation computing to the PC and its clientserver architecture.<sup>8</sup>

The formation of a new technological platform can arise from the actions of both established firms and new start-ups. Established firms play an important role in the development of new technologies. However, such firms may also have incentives to invest in existing technologies rather than new ideas, either because of fears of cannibalizing existing products or because new technologies may require new capabilities or combinations of capabilities that are not held by incumbents (e.g., Christensen 1997; Henderson and Clark 1990). In the market for cloud computing software, early entrants were often start-ups, such as Salesforce.com (e.g., Economist, 2003; Yoffie and Wagonfeld 2006). Although incumbent firms that have offered on-premises enterprise software, such as SAP and Oracle, have transitioned to the cloud, some evidence indicates that these firms have faced challenges in adapting their business models and capabilities to the new environment (Smith 2015; Thompson 2016). Given the important role that start-up firms often play in identifying and commercializing new technologies, specifically in the market for cloud computing, we focus in this paper on the actions of entrepreneurs in response to a new technological opportunity.

Entrepreneurs seeking to develop products for new IT platforms face significant uncertainty (Rosenberg 1996). Some types of uncertainty, such as the expected size of the installed base of users for products characterized by network effects, have been studied in the IS and economics literature.<sup>9</sup> A related source of uncertainty in forming a new IT platform arises from coordinated instigation. Enough actors need to be reassured that other actors are working on other necessary aspects of the technological-business problem that when they come up with their specific solution, it will be viable on both the technological and the business front. Otherwise, it would be irrational for firms to invest. More broadly, a variety of factors make it difficult for firms to forecast market outcomes, such as uncertain technology, the presence of unexpected complements, and unknown user valuation (Greenstein 2010; Rosenberg 1996).

In some fields, public or public–private solutions exist that reduce these uncertainties and frictions. A widely cited example of such solutions is the Semiconductors Technology Roadmap, which ensured widespread agreement on goals, standards, and time frames and stimulated investment from a wide range of actors around the globe to achieve its stated goals of technological development (Brown and Linden 2009). Large firms can sometimes play a similar role, as their importance allows them to set standards and manage the transition to new platforms; for example, Intel has entered complementary markets with the explicit purpose of generating new markets and expanding existing markets for its microprocessors (Gawer and Henderson 2007). However, these types of institutions do not always exist. Further, in an environment of substantial economic and technological uncertainty, players such as large firms, industry institutions, and governments may face challenges in correctly predicting the direction of technological change (Rosenberg 1996).

In the absence of coordinating institutions and in environments of significant uncertainty, economic experiments can help market participants to understand how opportunities enabled by the new platform can be translated into feasible business models (e.g., Rosenberg 1992). These experiments are costly and have an uncertain outcome. Start-up firms need to develop new technical components or change old designs completely, because the ways in which core technologies are used may be completely different from those on the old platform. Further, the market for products on a new platform is uncertain, and firms must experiment with new business models and methods of monetizing their investment.

These types of experiments have a history in corporate computing. For example, the transition from mainframe to C/S computing was accompanied by a change in platform market structure in which one firm (IBM) provided both hardware and software to another platform structure with divided technical leadership (Bresnahan and Greenstein 1999) in which heterogeneous firms were responsible for providing different parts of the platform. This change in environment required a change in business models, from one in which hardware and software were bundled to one where independent software vendors were forced to find new ways to monetize their investment. One common model that emerged was one in which software vendors charged a licensing fee plus an annual maintenance fee.

In a more recent example, consider the recent shift from the C/S computing paradigm to cloud computing: in order to launch an SaaS product on the market, the start-up firms not only had to identify new business models and sort out licensing and legal issues but also had to expend tremendous energy in reorganizing the entire organization (e.g., getting rid of the technical teams, which used to provide on-site services under the traditional C/S model) and spend a great deal to promote the new technology on the market. For instance, Salesforce.com spends roughly 50% of its yearly revenues on marketing to promote its on-demand customer relationship management (CRM) product.

These discussions of the uncertainty faced by start-up firms when developing new products on an unproven technological platform are by no means comprehensive, but they do cover some common problems. Interestingly, many of these problems can be addressed by VCs. Our next section, therefore, discusses the role of VCs in addressing these challenges.

## The Role of VCs in Complementing a Firm’s Product Development on New Technological Platforms

VC funds are usually organized as partnerships between general partners (GPs) and limited partners (LPs). GPs are the individuals who make the investment choices, impose active management, and are involved in the companies in which the fund invests. They are the ones whose profession is being a venture capitalist, so we refer to them as the VCs. LPs are institutional investors or wealthy individuals, who passively invest in a VC fund (usually for 10 years).

In order to understand VCs’ investment behavior, it is important to grasp how VCs make their money. VCs have two sources of revenue. First, every year the funds take a management fee, usually 2–3% of the fund’s total investment capital. Although this can be lucrative for the VCs, the more significant payout is the second source: the gains from a financial exit, either by initial public offering (IPO) or by being acquired by another company (M&A). At that point, the VCs distribute the gains (if any) between themselves and the LPs, with VCs retaining around 20% of the profits. In order for the VCs to be able to secure investment for their next fund, they need to show current and prospective LPs that, after taking all costs and risks into account, their fund’s rate of return on investment (ROI) is more attractive than that of other investments.

Accordingly, VCs aim to invest in firms that, they predict, will achieve a financial exit two orders of magnitude above the firm’s valuation at the time of that investment within three to five years. These constraints lead even the best VCs to refer to a rule of thumb known as “one in ten.” Under this rule, a VC needs at least one in ten of its investments to be a “home run”—an investment that generates not only enough profits to cover the other nine but also enough to ensure an ROI to the LPs high enough to justify follow-up investment in the next fund.

One of the investment strategies with the highest probability of achieving this kind of stratospheric returns in such a short time frame is betting on companies that aim to secure semimonopoly status by taking advantage of new business opportunities. For companies producing IT products and services, this usually means products for new technological platforms. In short, VCs who invest in the IT industry are constantly searching for the next technological platform in which to invest. As a prominent Silicon Valley VC we interviewed said:

We at [name of fund] have made specific investments so we are allowed into the best labs in Stanford and Berkeley and employ enough researchers as consultants to be able to know of new technological trends as soon as they appear. We find this very worthwhile, leading, over the years, to some of our best “hits.” Many times, we actively encourage teams, or graduates of these labs, to form companies around ideas we see as valuable so we can invest in them.

VC funds may also have a specific focal area of interest, for example, in a new technological area, such as cloud computing, and so may play a similar role in screening companies that may fit particularly well with the goals of the fund. Similarly, a few interviewees told us that some funds have specific initiatives aimed at specific technologies. Sometimes, these are posted directly on a VC’s website; for example, in 2010 Kleiner Perkins Caufield & Byers launched a \$250 million fund specifically targeting the social web (Arrington 2010).

To be successful at this strategy, VCs must have capabilities that enable them to increase the probability of success when investing in companies experimenting in both new technologies and new business models. VCs can facilitate the commercialization of new technological opportunities by playing the role of an information intermediary for the firms in which they are invested (Hsu 2006). Because of their strong network of information and contacts, VCs provide access to private information and reduce search costs for their portfolio firms. They might also be more aware of potential opportunities and threats than internal directors of the firms in which they invest (Gans et al. 2002; Hsu 2006).

In our setting, this intermediation function has two implications. First, by reducing some sources of uncertainty, VCs can significantly help their portfolio firms efficiently learn and develop new products that fit with the new platform. Additionally, when their portfolio firms need to negotiate with external actors about missing components, VCs can act as bargaining intermediaries to facilitate transactions among market participants, such as licensing of technology protected by intellectual property agreements (Gans et al. 2002).

Second, VCs also act as intermediaries between the firms and the financial markets and, hence, can influence firms to make technological decisions in directions they believe will lead to a higher-value financial exit. This was echoed in our interviews; for example, the founder and CEO of a successful ERP company, reflecting on his decision to move to the cloud, stated:

Good VCs do not invest without knowing where the exit would come from, so they will constantly think about what will help them get good valuation in terms of future exists—which means that they are thinking about what new technologies will be hot. In effect the VC–investment-bankers connection is much stronger and intimate than the VC–portfolio company. In our case, the push for us to move to cloud started with the investment bank XXX, they said to the VC that what would be selling two years from now are cloud-based companies and that if we develop cloud-based instead of C/S products, the valuation will be higher. This is the reason why we choose [the] cloud.

This information intermediation role can help start-up firms develop into established companies. VCs also play an important role in building the internal organization of firms, formulating human resource policies, and hiring the vice president of sales and marketing (Hellmann and Puri 2002). We argue that all these extra-financial value-added services could effectively assist the firms in sorting through all the issues discussed above related to commercializing the products based on a new platform.

VCs may also be able to help start-ups producing for a new platform with their unique financing needs. Because of limited experience with the new technology and limited monitoring capabilities, traditional external funding sources, such as banks, may have a difficult time evaluating the ongoing risk of investing in a new platform (Ueda 2004). VCs, because of their active role on the boards of companies, may be better able to evaluate the risks and potential returns of such investments (Dushnitsky 2010; Puri and Zarutskie 2012; Winton and Yerramilli 2008). As we discuss in further detail below, these concerns are especially important for cloud computing ventures, for which cash flows from new sales are more likely than traditional IT firms to be back-loaded.

This discussion suggests that the business model of VCs encourages them to invest specifically in situations with higher technological risk and associated business model risk. These situations arise particularly for firms that seek to produce for new technological platforms. VCs also help their portfolio firms to overcome the uncertainties of developing and bringing new platform technologies to the market by successfully managing and navigating series of economic experiments to figure out the best business model and scale it up rapidly. This approach to investing provides us with testable implications. Namely, we expect to find complementarities between VC financing and cloud business models. Complementarity in our setting means that the joint payoff of launching products on a new platform and funding a firm through venture capital will be greater than the payoff from performing these options separately.<sup>10</sup> These complementarities can arise through the desire for VCs to identify financing opportunities that align with their business model or through their specific capabilities in helping such firms bring new products to market.

The presence of such complementarities can have broader implications for the transition to new technological platforms. One, as noted above, is that by facilitating the economic experiments of their portfolio firms, VCs will directly contribute to the shift to the new platform. Further, when VCs pour investment into new platform technologies, would-be entrepreneurs become aware that they have to focus on the new technology if they want to secure VC funding. In short, VCs act as a catalyst to the shift to a new technological platform by aggressively selecting the best firms, pushing them into the new technologies, and betting on them early. This continues until either the new technology becomes the new paradigm or it clearly fails to do so; in both cases, the VCs then start to look for new firms in which to invest.

## Prior Experience and the Value of Complementarities

The ex ante experience of VCs and their portfolio firms has been an important focus of research on the consequences of VC investment (Bottazzi et al. 2008; Dimov and Shepherd 2005; Zarutskie 2010). In this section, we describe how such variations in experience can influence the complementarity between VC financing and the development of new platform products.

Both theory and some findings (Bottazzi et al. 2008; Gompers et al. 2009) suggest that the more experienced the VCs are as technologists and entrepreneurs, the better they are at performing the functions described above, including selecting the best ideas and firms, mitigating the costs involved in new product development, and providing active management guidance. As a result, we expect that the complementarity between VC financing and launching a product on a new platform will be stronger when VCs have gained rich experience in the focal industry.

However, experience can lead to lower complementarities in cases where start-ups already have experience in developing products on the established platform. The informational benefits of VCs may be lower for such firms, leading to weaker complementarity. This might occur for several reasons. First, to the extent that such firms may already have significant experience tied to developing products for the old platform, they may reap lower net benefits from developing products for the new platform. This will be particularly true in environments where lessons learned on one platform may not be easily transferred to the new environment (e.g., Davis et al. 2014). Further, some prior literature argues that firms’ existing capabilities make it more difficult for them to adapt to new circumstances (e.g., Nelson and Winter 1982; Teece et al. 1997; Tripsas and Gavetti 2000). In our environment, prior experience with the old platform could create inertia that is difficult to overcome, even with access to new information from the VC. Consequently, we expect that the complementarity between VC financing and launching a product on a new platform will be weaker when the portfolio firm has gained rich experience in the prior technology.

## Institutional Setting: Cloud Computing

To investigate this hypothesized influence of VCs, we look at the early stages of a new paradigmatic technological shift. Those shifts happen during the early stages of diffusion of a new paradigm of computing that revolutionizes the ways in which information systems are used, before the emergence of an ecosystem of complementary institutions and services. The early stages of cloud computing, before it became clear that the new paradigm was taking hold, supplies a rich case study of complementarity between new technological platforms and VC financing under a yet-to-be-proven paradigm.

Significant confusion remains about what constitutes cloud computing. We define cloud computing following the definition of Kushida et al. (2010, p. 2):

Cloud Computing provides on-demand network access to a computing environment and computing resources delivered as services. There is elasticity in the resource provision for users, which is allocated dynamically within providers’ data centers. Payment schemes are typically pay-as-you-go models.<sup>11</sup>

Importantly for our purposes, cloud computing defines both a technical architecture and a business model. The computing resources that are delivered as services can be applications, platforms, or infrastructure. In this paper, because we focus on enterprise software, we concentrate on software applications that are delivered as services (software as a service). Again using the definition of Kushida et al. (2010, p. 5),

Software as a Service (SaaS) refers to software that is delivered or accessed over networks. Software is managed centrally by the SaaS provider, relieving the IT managers of users from dealing with incremental upgrades and security patches to end-user PCs.

Enterprise software consolidates the information needs of a firm’s departments into a single, integrated software program that operates on a shared database. Enterprise software is used to run the back-office operations of a firm and includes enterprise resource planning (ERP) and customer relationship management (CRM) software as well as other applications, so it was rarely sold as packaged software to be run exclusively on a desktop or other client.<sup>12</sup> We focus on enterprise software to reduce the extent of unobserved heterogeneity in our sample. Further, some of the most popular early providers of cloud application services operated using enterprise software, such as Salesforce.com. Last, in contrast to producers of many consumer applications created specifically to offer services via the Internet (e.g., some Google applications), because many enterprise software firms are operating on client data, they faced a clear choice of business models between C/S and the cloud.<sup>13</sup>

Producers of enterprise software during our sample period had a choice of offering C/S software or SaaS. C/S software is delivered as a product to the customer’s premises to reside within the customer’s IT infrastructure. Using this option, customers usually purchase a perpetual software license upfront, with maintenance fees and the potential for periodic upgrades (usually associated with a new licensing fee). Because it is offered as a product and deployed on the client’s system, the client also has the opportunity to customize the software. In contrast, using the SaaS model, the software is hosted by the provider and delivered over the internet using a standard web browser. In contrast to C/S software, for which payment is a large up-front capital expense/gain, SaaS is a pay-as-you-go model in which the client leases use of the software service, generating a constant reliable revenue stream for the provider. Although initial customization is limited under SaaS, the existence of only one primary location for the software makes it easier for the firm to update it regularly (e.g., every several weeks).

The differences in the business model may create unique challenges for cloud firms compared to those that offer products for a C/S infrastructure. First, the cash-flow pattern is significantly different in the two business models: although the cash flow from new customers under C/S is front-loaded as they pay for new perpetual licenses, the cash flow to the provider of new SaaS licenses is spread out over time. Indeed, from a financial company life-cycle point of view, the large initial capital transfer from the customer in the C/S model can be seen as the capital-rich customers financing the final development of the capital-poor start-ups, while the cloud model seems to assume that the capital-poor start-ups partly finance the purchasing decisions of capital-rich customers. This also lengthens the period required for new cloud-based ERP start-ups to reach cash-positive revenue flows. Although a new SaaS product could generate significant growth in new customers, growth in realized revenues and cash flow from those customers come more slowly (Burgelman and Manoharan 2009; Burgelman and Schifrin 2014; Coates et al. 2008).

Even more challenging, early companies that offered cloud products were forced to experiment with new revenue models—such as deciding whether to price by the number of users or by how much the application is used (Burgelman and Manoharan 2009)—under conditions in which relatively little data was available for informing decisions. The issues of new cash-flow patterns, combined with additional expenses (described below), have the potential to create cash-flow problems for cloud providers, giving their VC investors more power to influence behavior.

Software developed for the cloud differs in many respects from that developed for a C/S application. First, delivering software services favored an approach to developing reusable software components, rather than a single monolithic application (Coates et al. 2008). Such an outlook may require different application developers (who may be in shorter supply) and a different structure for the development group than is the case under traditional C/S (e.g., Moore 2006). Second, as noted earlier, a cloud environment offers the opportunity for more frequent product releases (e.g., Coates et al. 2008). Third, customers for C/S and cloud software are likely to differ significantly. Customers for C/S are likely to be large enterprises that prefer full-featured software, which they may be able to customize; in contrast, clients for cloud software, on average, are smaller and may be satisfied with less flexibility and potential for customization. These differing requirements can increase the costs and put strains on the cash flow for firms that have developed C/S software but are entering the market for cloud software. They increase the technical uncertainty of whether a new product can be delivered. They also increase uncertainty related to whether a market exists for the product.

Because the cloud represents a new delivery platform, vendors seeking to deliver software services in this way have to design new models of customer engagement. Traditional on-premises C/S software often involves sales through account executives who played an active, consultative role with customers, seeking to show how the system could best be employed to meet the client’s needs. This type of hightouch sales often means significant sales delivery costs and high commissions, which are justified, given the potentially high revenues associated with sales to on-premises customers. In contrast, because revenues for a SaaS sale are significantly lower and involve less consultation, the role for sales forces becomes different and requires a different compensation structure (Petty 2008). The diffusion of cloud computing means that new models for developing, training, and compensating sales forces need to be developed. Where a sales force for C/S already exists, a natural tension exists among compensation, training, and incentives across the two channels that is often only unwound over time (e.g., Coates et al. 2008). As prior research has shown that VCs have a significant influence in the realms of HR and business model development, this period of experimentation tilts the power toward them in their relationship with their portfolio firms.

## Empirical Framework

We argue that VC financing is complementary to the introduction of new products offered over the cloud—that is, the returns to offering cloud products will be greater in the presence of VCs and vice versa (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Milgrom 2013). Two classes of statistical tests have been used to detect complementarities (Brynjolfsson and Milgrom 2013): (1) correlations in the adoption of practices and (2) evidence of systematic performance differences, such as productivity. Because we have limited information on inputs and outputs of the start-ups in our data, we are unable to study the performance implications of VC financing and the decision to offer cloud products and, instead, study correlations in the decisions to adopt VC and cloud delivery.<sup>14</sup>

Identification of complementarities through the measurement of the clustering of practices has a long history. Arora and Gambardella (1990) provided the first formal test using this method, and it was further analyzed by Holmstrom and Milgrom (1994). Because it does not require performance metrics, some authors have argued that it may be the most common approach for testing for complementarities (Brynjolfsson and Milgrom 2013). Although the performance approach is perhaps best known in the IS literature, several papers have utilized both approaches (e.g., Bresnahan et al. 1997; Tambe et al. 2012). As noted by Brynjolfsson and Milgrom (2013), the correlation test will have the most power when managers are aware of the relevant complementarities and are able to act on them, while the performance test will have greater power when practices are randomly distributed.

Arora and Gambardella provided a well-known test that formally laid out the identification conditions for the correlation approach.<sup>15</sup> This test, which has been widely computed, showed that two strategies are complementary if they are correlated conditional on firm and market features that might influence their use.<sup>16</sup> However, this approach assumes that decisions are continuous, and it is not possible to extend this approach to situations in which the observable choices are binary, rather than continuous (Miravete and Pernías 2010). Given that we study the binary incidence of offering cloud products and VC financing, we instead follow recent work that has shown that a structural estimate of complementarity can be recovered when strategies are binary, and a multinomial probit model is used (Arora et al. 2010; Fares 2014; Gentzkow 2007).<sup>17</sup>

Each firm i associates a payoff $\boldsymbol { V } _ { i } ^ { k }$ with a choice k. The firm’s strategies include whether to choose VC financing (VC) and to offer cloud-based products (Cloud). VC = 1 if the firm chooses VC financing, and is 0 otherwise; similarly, Cloud = 1 if a firm offers products over the cloud, and is 0 otherwise. The firm can make four choices: k = 0 when the firm has neither VC financing nor cloud-based products $( V C = 0 , C l o u d = 0 ) ; k = 1$ when the firm has cloud products only $( V C = 0 , C l o u d = 1 ) ; k = 2$ when the firm has received VC financing only $( V C = 1 , C l o u d = 0 ) ;$ and k = 3 when the firm has both VC financing and cloud products $( V C = 1$ Cloud = 1).

It is worth noting that in a typical discrete-choice model, decisions are made unilaterally by a single firm. However, in our setting, as in others that have attempted to quantify complementarities (e.g., Arora and Gambardella 1990; Cassiman and Veugelers 2006), the strategies reflect a joint decision by multiple firms. In particular, VC investment is a mutual decision that requires the consent of both the investor and the portfolio company (Sørenson 2007). In our model, the payoff can be viewed as the combined payoffs of the start-up and its investors.

Each choice has a payoff that is composed of elements that are observable and unobservable to the econometrician. We denote the observable payoffs associated with Cloud as $\mu _ { i } ^ { 1 }$ and the observable payoffs associated with $V C \mathrm { a s } \mu _ { i } ^ { 2 }$ . These are a function of firm characteristics. The additional payoff from using both strategies in conjunction is denoted by θ. Thus, the payoff from the choice k can be represented as

$$
\begin{array}{c} V _ {i} ^ {k} = (\mu_ {i} ^ {1} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {1}) C l o u d _ {i} + (\mu_ {i} ^ {2} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {2}) V C _ {i} + \\ (\theta + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {3}) C l o u d _ {i} \times V C _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {0} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $\varepsilon _ { i } ^ { k } ( k { = } 0 , 1 , 2 , 3 )$ denotes the unobserved characteristics associated with decision k for firm i. The model requires some distributional assumptions about the $\varepsilon _ { i } ^ { k } .$ Prior work that has demonstrated how complementarities can be identified using choice models has assumed that the error components are distributed multivariate normal, that is, ε N(0, Σ) (Fares 2014; Gentzkow 2007; Miravete and Pernias 2006).

Putting it into a more specific form, a firm i’s payoff can be written as follows, based on whether it has received VC funding and offered cloud products:

$$
\begin{array}{c} V _ {i} ^ {0} = \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {0} \\ V _ {i} ^ {1} = \mu_ {i} ^ {1} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {1} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {0} \\ V _ {i} ^ {2} = \mu_ {i} ^ {2} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {2} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {0} \\ V _ {i} ^ {3} = \theta + \mu_ {i} ^ {1} + \mu_ {i} ^ {2} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {0} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {1} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {2} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {3} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Complementarity exists when the value of two strategies together is greater than their value separately (i.e., $V ^ { 3 } + \bar { V } ^ { 0 } >$ $V ^ { \bar { 1 } } + V ^ { 2 } )$ . Thus, complementarity exists when $V ^ { 3 } + V ^ { 0 } - V ^ { 1 }$ $- \ V ^ { 2 } = \theta > 0$

If the error terms are distributed multivariate normal, then model (2) takes the form of a multinomial probit. Fares (2014) and Gentzkow (2007) provide a comprehensive discussion of identification in this model using the multinomial probit. The key challenge is to identify θ from the covariance parameters: observing a high probability of offering cloud products and VC financing could be attributable to complementarity or to a high covariance between the two choices. One solution is to use an exclusion restriction: a variable that influences the likelihood of choosing VC financing, but not cloud products.

We use the presence of a high number of non-IT M&A (mergers and acquisitions) transactions that had previously received VC funding in a start-up’s home state (denoted as non-IT M&A) as an exclusion restriction. The logic of this exclusion restriction follows that of prior work that examines the relationship between venture capital activity and economic outcomes at the regional level. This literature uses as an exclusion restriction for VC activity variables that shift limited partner (LP) returns but do not influence other economic outcomes, such as employment or inventive activity (e.g., Samila and Sorenson 2010, 2011). The general intuition behind why such variables will shift VC activity is that (1) LP returns are positively correlated with their future investments in venture capital; (2) LPs exhibit a home bias when choosing venture capital funds; and (3) GPs exhibit a home bias when deciding on portfolio investments (Samila and Sorenson 2010). In our setting, we similarly seek to identify factors that will shift local LP returns, which will increase the supply of capital to geographically proximate VC firms and so increase the likelihood that geographically proximate start-ups will receive VC funding.

Successful exits of VC portfolio firms from prior cycles of investments influence LP returns in the focal period (Kolev 2013). As noted above, successful exits of portfolio firms usually involve IPOs and M&As. During the sample period used for our baseline model, weakness in IPO markets curtailed the use of IPOs as an exit method, so we focus on M&As of VC portfolio firms as our measure of successful exits. That is, the logic of the instrument is that the greater the number of M&As from prior portfolio investments, the greater the returns to LPs. Given that LPs invest a relatively fixed proportion of their investment portfolio in VC funds (Samila and Sorenson 2010), this increases the supply of LP financing into VC funds. We use the number of M&As from VC-backed portfolio firms outside the IT industry in the startup’s home state in the previous two years as our exclusion restriction. We argue this is a valid exclusion restriction because M&A transactions in industries other than IT are unlikely to be directly related to a firm’s decision to offer products over the cloud. However, M&As in one industry have been shown to shape the funding availability for investments in another industry (e.g., Townsend 2015).

As an exclusion restriction, non-IT M&A will affect $\mu _ { i } ^ { 2 }$ (the mean payoff associated with receiving VC financing) but not $\mu _ { i } ^ { 1 }$ (the mean payoff associated with offering products over the cloud). If high $\mathrm { P r } ( V _ { i } ^ { 3 } )$ is caused by the complementarity $\theta ,$ then a change of non-IT M&A will change the likelihood that $V C _ { i } = 1$ and Cloud =1; however, if high $\mathrm { P r } ( V _ { i } ^ { 3 } )$ is caused by the high covariance in firm preferences on the two choices (e.g., firms with a good track record may just prefer to receive funding from VC, and they may also have a higher preference for offering cloud products), then a change in non-IT M&A will only change the likelihood that $V C _ { i } = 1$ but will not affect the likelihood that $C l o u d _ { i } = 1 .$ 18

The model above uses cross-sectional variance and an exclusion restriction to obtain identification. Another approach is to use panel data. Gentzkow (2007) describes the use of panel data to gain additional moments to obtain identification in the model above. However, in our setting, whether a firm offers cloud products and whether it receives VC financing are absorbing states: after a firm makes these decisions, it is unlikely to retreat from them while it remains in business. This makes it difficult to employ a panel-data version of our baseline model. Therefore, our baseline model is estimated using a sample of cross-sectional data—we look at firms’ choices of cloud products and VC financing by a certain point in time. As a robustness check that requires different assumptions for identification, we follow the approach of Novak and Stern (2009) and estimate a fixed-effects linear probability model using panel data. In particular, we regress the decision to offer cloud products on VC financing and a set of controls. This alternative model is described in further detail below.

## Data

We create a sample of start-up firms that offer enterprise software products. As noted above, estimation of the model described in specification (2) uses cross-sectional data. We choose 2009 as our estimation sample, as it is the year when the cloud became widely accepted as the new up-and-coming computing platform, and thus every start-up should have been exposed to this idea by this time.

Although our primary estimation sample is based on data from 2009, we collect data on the activities of enterprise software start-up activities beginning in 1999 (the first year when the concept of cloud computing formed and leading cloud computing start-ups such as Salesforce.com emerged) to 2009 for several reasons. First, to determine whether a firm had offered cloud products by 2009, we need to obtain its product introduction history for this entire period to ascertain when it made the shift to the new platform. Second, as noted above, we use a panel-data fixed-effects model for robustness. Last, to characterize the shift to cloud computing, we provide descriptive evidence on how firms gradually transitioned to the cloud computing platform during the early years of its diffusion.

The sample firms come from the National Establishment Time Series (NETS) Database, which includes 100,000 U.S.-based firms that indicate they are in SIC 7372. NETS provides longitudinal information over 1990–2009 on sales, employment, location, and other basic variables. However, NETS only has information on firms’ SIC, and no subclass in SIC 7372 indicates whether a firm offers enterprise software. Because we require a dataset of enterprise software firms, we merge the NETS data with the CorpTech Directory of Technology Companies (hereafter, CorpTech).<sup>19</sup>

The CorpTech data include detailed information on the product categories in which firms are active. For example, the product codes SOF-MA and SOF-WD indicate the product categories for software related to “manufacturing” and “warehousing and distribution,” respectively. Based on the product categories defined by CorpTech, we select the firms that produce enterprise applications, such as those for accounting, business planning, banking, financial analysis/ management, manufacturing, sales and marketing, warehousing, and distribution. In order to focus only on start-up firms, we restrict the sample to firms founded after 1990 that have fewer than 1,000 employees and less than \$500 million in annual sales.<sup>20</sup>

## Product Introduction Data

Following prior literature (Fosfuri et al. 2008), our product introduction data come from the press releases in the PROMT database. After retrieving all press releases by each of our sample firms from 1999 to 2009, we employ a combination of automatic search and manual reading to determine if an article is about a new product introduction.

To determine whether a firm introduces a new product based on the cloud computing platform, we first derive a set of keywords related to the cloud in the SaaS category, on the basis of the definition of cloud computing by Kushida et al. (2010). These keywords not only include more generic ones, such as cloud, cloud computing, and SaaS, but also include phrases describing the features of a cloud computing business model, such as on-demand, pay-as-you-go, and subscription based.<sup>21</sup> Then, we use a text-mining tool and search for these keywords in the new product introduction articles. We further manually read all search results to eliminate false positives and ensure that we have correctly identified cloud product introductions.

As noted earlier, in addition to the new products offered over the cloud, we are also interested in a firm’s experience in producing traditional C/S software products, measured by the total number of new C/S products. For a new software product introduction identified from the PROMT database, we consider it as a C/S product introduction as long as it does not include any keywords related to cloud as described above. We acknowledge that because we do not possess press release data prior to 1999, we will miss products introduced before then, which will result in a systematic undercounting of the total number of C/S products. Nevertheless, this is unlikely to significantly influence the estimates of our baseline model, which is based on the 2009 sample.

## Venture Capital Data

Our second important data source is the VentureXpert database by Thomson Financial Corporation (e.g., Chemmanur et al. 2011; Tian 2011). VentureXpert provides detailed, roundby-round information for firms in which VCs invest. By matching our sample firms with firms in VentureXpert, we could determine whether and when a firm received VC financing. Our approach of considering VC financing as an absorbing state is consistent with the existing literature that looks at similar settings (e.g., Hsu 2006).

We hypothesize that the complementarity is higher when a VC-backed firm’s lead VC has rich IT experience. Our measure for a lead VC’s IT experience is also based on the VentureXpert database. The variable is computed in three steps. First, to identify the lead VC, we follow the existing literature (e.g., Tian 2011) and consider the VC that provided the largest (highest dollar value) investment across all rounds and that participated in the first round of funding as the lead VC. Second, we extract all VCs’ funding activities in the IT industry from VentureXpert; for each VC, we count the number of IT firms in which the VC invested as the first-round investor in five years before 1999.<sup>22</sup> Third, we match each of the lead VCs identified in the first step with their pre-sample funding activities identified in the second step. For firms backed by multiple lead VCs, we use average experience.

## Control Variables

Baseline controls. A large body of literature looks at the relationship between firm size and innovation (e.g., Cohen and Klepper 1996a, 1996b; Scherer 1965). Therefore, to control for size-related effects that could influence both VC funding and the decision to launch cloud products, we include yearly sales (denoted as Sales) as one of the baseline controls.<sup>23</sup> Sales data are directly obtained from the NETS database.

Prior work suggests that when a firm invests heavily in its marketing efforts, brands, and distribution channels, it is less likely to introduce new products because of a fear of eroding profits in existing business (Fosfuri et al. 2008). A firm’s downstream capabilities are also likely to correlate with the VC funding decision (e.g., Block et al. 2014). To control for this effect, following prior literature (Huang et al. 2013), we extract trademark data from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) and use the total number of live trademarks as a control.

Extended Controls. We incorporate additional control variables in robustness checks. First, prior literature suggests that VCs tend to fund firms with demonstrated innovation capabilities (Conti et al. 2013; Mann and Sager 2007), and meanwhile, a highly innovative firm will also be more likely to follow new technology paradigms. Hence, we control for a firm’s innovation capabilities by using patent stock (e.g., Kortum and Lerner 2000).<sup>24</sup> Second, firm age is also an important factor that affects both a firm’s financing opportunity and its product strategies. Third, certain locations may be especially attractive to VCs and start-ups, and they may also be important hubs for new technologies. Thus, we create a dummy that equals 1 if a start-up is located in California, Texas, or Massachusetts.

## Empirical Results

Our empirical analysis proceeds in several steps. First, we provide some descriptive evidence on firms’ transition to the cloud platform over time and the differences between VCbacked and non-VC-backed firms. Second, we investigate the complementarity between VC financing and offering cloud products based on the empirical framework mentioned earlier; we also examine whether the complementarity is stronger when a firm is backed by an experienced VC or when a firm is not locked into the old platform technology. Third, we test the robustness of the results using alternative empirical models. Fourth, we demonstrate the unique role that VCs play in helping start-ups overcome financing constraints when they invest in new technological platforms.

## Descriptive Evidence and Summary Statistics

We first show some macro-level evidence on how our sample firms shifted to the cloud computing platform from 1999 to 2009, a crucial period when the firms had to make a clear decision on whether to offer products “over the cloud” or on a traditional C/S platform. According to the product introduction articles we collected, we could determine exactly when a firm launched its first product over the cloud. Figure 1 presents a time-series chart of the cumulative percentage of sample firms that launched cloud products, for VC-backed versus non-VC-backed firms. Overall, while firms rarely entered into the cloud computing business before 2002, a significant rise occurred from 2003 to 2006; however, the rate of increase slows after 2006. Moreover, the percentage and speed of cloud product launches between VC-backed and non-VC-backed firms differ significantly.

We next turn to the summary statistics of the key variables used in our analyses. As noted above, our baseline empirical model seeks to identify complementarities by studying firms choices of cloud products and VC financing by 2009. Table 1 reports the summary statistics using this 2009 sample and then compares VC-backed and non-VC-backed firms.

Among the 231 firms in this sample, 52 firms (23%) received VC funding, and 31 firms (13%) had introduced cloud products by 2009. VC-backed firms are more likely to offer cloud products and have greater experience with C/S products; they are also associated with more annual sales and more claimsweighted patents. ## When Is Complementarity Stronger?

We next investigate whether complementarities are stronger when a firm is backed by a VC that has rich experience in the IT industry. The decision for a particular start-up to be funded by a particular VC is the outcome of a matching model (Sørenson 2007). As such, the outcome of being financed by an experienced or inexperienced VC is endogenous and should be represented explicitly as such in our choice model.

To represent the choice of VC experience, in our models we dichotomize VC experience and represent funding from VCs with rich IT experience and those with little IT experience as separate choices.<sup>26</sup> We compute the complementarity between offering cloud and VC financing for the former group as well as the complementarity for the latter group. Specifically, in this case, a firm faces six choices: k = 0 when the firm has neither VC financing nor cloud-based products; $k = 1$ when the firm has cloud products only $( V C = 0$ Cloud = 1); k = 2 when the firm is backed by VCs with little IT experience only (VC\_L = 1, Cloud = 0); k = 3 when the firm is backed by VCs with rich IT experience only (VC\_R = 1, Cloud = 0); k = 4 when the firm both offers cloud products and is backed by VCs with little IT experience (VC\_L = 1, Cloud = 1); and k = 5 when the firm both offers cloud products and is backed by VCs with rich IT experience (VC\_R = 1, Cloud = 1). We denote the observable payoffs associated with Cloud as $\mu _ { i } ^ { 1 }$ , the payoffs of VC\_L as $\mu _ { i } ^ { 2 } .$ , and the payoffs of VC\_R as $\mu _ { i } ^ { 3 } .$ We also denote the additional payoffs (i.e., the complementarity effect) associated with choice k = 4 as $\theta _ { 1 } ;$ similarly, we denote the additional payoffs associated with choice $k = 5$ as $\theta _ { 2 } .$ . Thus, the payoff from the choice k for firm i is

$$
\begin{array}{c} V _ {i} ^ {k} = (\mu_ {i} ^ {1} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {1}) C l o u d _ {i} + (\mu_ {i} ^ {2} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {2}) V C \_ L _ {i} + \\ (\mu_ {i} ^ {3} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {3}) V C \_ R _ {i} + (\theta_ {1} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {4}) C l o u d _ {i} \times \\ V C \_ L _ {i} + (\theta_ {2} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {5}) C l o u d \times V C \_ R _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i} ^ {0} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

If the complementarity is stronger for firms backed by VCs with rich IT experience, we would expect $\theta _ { 1 } < \theta _ { 2 } .$ As in specification (3), we assume $\mu ^ { 1 }$ to be a function of Sales and Trademarks and assume $\mu ^ { 2 }$ and $\mu ^ { 3 }$ to be a function of Sales, Trademarks, and non-IT M&A. We use the $5 0 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ percentile of the VC Experience variable (which equals 6) to define rich versus little IT experience. That is, if a VC-backed firm is backed by a VC who funded more than six IT companies in the prior five years, this represents a choice of VC funding with rich IT experience; otherwise, there is a choice of funding by a VC with little IT experience.

## Robustness Tests: Multinomial Logit

A challenge with the multinomial probit (MNP) is that it is both computationally demanding and requires estimation of a rich covariance matrix. For our baseline model with four choices, the MNP probit requires us to estimate 17 parameters; our model that incorporates VC experience in the choice structure includes six choices, and thus it requires estimation of 34 parameters. Researchers must frequently normalize some parameters in the covariance matrix for identification, and even in situations where the model is formally identified, it may be challenging to estimate the complete set of parameters, and sometimes further normalizations of the covariance matrix are used (Keane 1992; Train 2009).

As a robustness check, we have reestimated our model using a multinomial logit (MNL). Although it has more restrictive substitution patterns, estimation of the MNL allows us to verify our results in a setting in which identification of the model parameters is less demanding. This is valuable, given the comparatively small size of our dataset. Second, it allows us to add the set of extended controls to the model. To conserve space, these results are available in Appendix B, Tables B1 to B3. All the results are qualitatively consistent with the results from the MNP models.

## Robustness Tests: Panel-Data Model

We next turn to an alternative strategy that identifies complementarities by regressing one potential complementary variable on another. If we base the estimation on a crosssectional sample, in general it will not provide a structural estimate of complementarity; among other things, unobserved shocks may influence both choice variables, leading to biased estimates of complementarity (Arora 1996; Brynjolfsson and Milgrom 2013). One alternative approach is to rely on within-firm variation to remove unobservables that influence both decisions, regressing one decision on another and using fixed effects to remove time-invariant factors that potentially influence both decisions. We closely follow the approach of Novak and Stern (2009) and estimate a fixed-effects linear probability model,<sup>27</sup> regressing the decision to offer cloud products on VC and a set of controls. Therefore, we run this panel-data model using the data on firms’ activities from 1999 to 2009, which comprises 263 firms with a total of 2,212 observations.<sup>28</sup>

The baseline specification is

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { Initial   cloud   product   launch } _ {i t} = \beta V C _ {i t} + \\ \gamma Z _ {i t} + \mu_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

We consider the decision to offer cloud products an absorbing state, so our model focuses on the initial decision to offer cloud products. Initial cloud product $l a u n c h _ { i t }$ is a binary variable that equals 1 if a firm i launches its first cloud product in year t and 0 otherwise. As a result, we dropped all observations for a firm after it introduced its first cloud product. $V C _ { i t }$ is also a binary variable, which equals 1 if a firm i has received VC financing by year t and 0 otherwise. The paramete $\cdot \beta$ captures the complementarity, and we expect $\beta > 0 , Z _ { i t }$ is a set of control variables that vary by firm and by year and has the potential to influence the firm’s decision to launch a cloud product as well as its VC financing status.<sup>29</sup> $\mu _ { i }$ represents time-invariant unobserved firm heterogeneity, and $\tau _ { t }$ is the full set of time dummies that control for general time trends that may be correlated with both VC financing and offering cloud.

Column (1) of Table 5 reports the results based on specification (6). The estimated coefficient for VC suggests that receiving VC financing is associated with a 9.2-percentagepoint increase in the likelihood of launching a cloud product.<sup>30</sup> This positive correlation between these two variables supports our view on the complementarity between VC and offering cloud products.

We next investigate the circumstances under which complementarity is stronger or weaker. The results in columns (2) and (3) of Table 5 show that complementarity varies with VC and start-up firm experience when these variables are entered in the model separately, and column (4) includes both variables together.

For example, in column (4), the estimated marginal effects suggest that when VC experience and C/S product experience are at their mean value, VC financing is associated with a 7.8- percentage-point increase in the likelihood of launching a cloud product. When a firm is backed by VCs with IT experience at the 10<sup>th</sup> percentile, no economically or statistically significant relationship exists between VC financing and the likelihood of launching cloud products. In contrast, for a firm backed by a VC with IT experience at the 90<sup>th</sup> percentile, VC financing is associated with a statistically significant 8.8- percentage-point increase in the likelihood of launching cloud products. Although the results are consistent with the view that the marginal effect of VC funding on the likelihood of launching cloud products will be greater for VCs with greater IT experience, the statistical significance has a p-value of only 0.11. Similarly, for firms with C/S product experience at the 10<sup>th</sup> percentile, VC is associated with an 8.6-percentage-point increase in the likelihood of launching cloud products, but this relationship is significantly weaker for firms with C/S product experience at the 90<sup>th</sup> percentile.

## Exploring the Interaction between VCs and Start-Ups during the Post-Investment Stage

In this section, we explore one potential reason that VC financing and the offering of cloud products may be complementary. As emphasized earlier, VC funding may provide access to capital that may not be available elsewhere. This role may be especially crucial for start-ups that offer cloud products. In contrast to C/S firms that receive licensing revenues upfront when new products are sold, cloud firms that deliver services receive revenue only over time because of pay-asyou-go customer relationships. This both reduces realized revenue in the early years of a start-up’s life and increases uncertainty about the total revenue stream from any single customer. Motivated by these observations, in this section we seek to explore the financing role played by VCs in greater detail. We note that this is only one way in which VC financing may be complementary to the offering of cloud products. As we noted above, there may be others. We leave the exploration of these alternative mechanisms for future research.

The nature of analyses in this section of the paper is different from that in the other sections. Rather than examining the complementarity between VC funding and cloud products, in this section, we examine the intensity with which start-ups receive financing among those already receiving VC funding. That is, we explore whether VC-backed cloud firms receive funding in larger amounts and more rapidly than VC-backed firms that use other business models.

The purpose of this section is to explore in further detail how VCs help start-ups overcome the challenges associated with producing for a new platform. However, these results should be interpreted with care. In an ideal world, we would observe the rate of cash-flow burn for firms that transition to producing cloud products and compare that to the cash flow of the entire population of firms that do not offer cloud products. Unfortunately, we do not observe the rate of cash-flow burn. Instead, we compare the size of follow-on financing rounds for cloud and non-cloud firms, among those that are already financed by VCs. In addition to the fact that this is a selected sample, follow-on financing rounds depend upon the demand for new financing as well as $\mathrm { V C s } ^ { \mathrm { } }$ willingness to supply funding.

Our empirical investigation is based on a subsample of 74 VC-backed firms, from the period after they received the initial round of funding until 2009 or the year that they exited. Our focus on the intensity instead of the incidence of receiving follow-on financing is motivated by the consideration that VCs using financing in stages to reduce the potential agency costs will have more funding rounds, with lower amounts per round (Tian 2011).<sup>31</sup> If cloud firms consume funds more quickly but do not necessarily impose high risks on VCs, we should observe greater follow-on funding for firms that offer cloud products, especially ones that offer both cloud and C/S products.

Because the distribution of the follow-on fund size for a firm in a year is highly skewed (i.e., with a mean value of \$2.367 million, although around 75% of the observations equal 0), directly using it as the dependent variable with a linear model would violate the normality assumption on the error term. With some limitations, it provides evidence that cloud firms may require financing in larger amounts than other VC-backed firms and indicates one reason that we may observe complementarity between VC financing and the offering of cloud products.

## Conclusion

This paper takes a first step toward closing an important gap in understanding the role that third-party organizations play in the evolution of IT platforms. Focusing on VCs, we elaborated on their role in technology development and shifts in technological trajectories. To do so, we build on the current literature to offer a more nuanced understanding of the role that VCs can play in the creation of new technological platforms.

We tested for complementarities between investing in new— as-yet-unproven—computing platforms by start-ups and VC investment. Focusing our analysis on the period before cloud computing gained acceptance, our results show that VC financing and the decision to offer cloud products are complementary. This result is even stronger when the VC has more experience in related technologies and when the start-up does not already have significant experience in the old C/S technology. Last, we demonstrate that VC-backed cloud product firms are associated with more intensive financing than other VC-backed software firms.

We argue that these complementarities occur because VCs play coordination and instigation roles in the diffusion of new platforms. In this way, VCs help to ensure shifts in the ways in which core computing technologies are used. In their quest for high-value financial exits, VCs invest significant resources in new and unproven technological platforms. Further, VCs ensure that capital, as well as other necessary resources, are channeled to new businesses that either offer these platforms or offer products that utilize them. As a consequence, enough products are developed and a vibrant ecosystem is formed to make these new platforms viable. If VCs invest more near their geographic location (e.g., Florida and Kenney 1988a, 1988b; Samila and Sorenson 2010), this suggests that the creation of new computing platforms—and associated downstream technologies—will occur where the VCs are.

These results have a clear set of implications for policy makers. First and foremost, with so many policy makers around the world contemplating and implementing VC policies—that is, policies aiming to spur the creation of VC industries in their jurisdiction—it is important to make a distinction between the missing capital pool problem and the missing expertise problem. Most VC policies focus on the market failure problem of insufficient VC investment: not enough capital is being channeled to new technology startups. Accordingly, they aim to solve this problem by lowering the risk of investment through direct monetary transfer (such as matching grants) or indirect subsidies (such as tax incentives). While these programs can indeed attract investment, they do not solve the issue of expertise. The findings in our paper indicate that there are clear differences between VCs that have prior expertise and those that do not. Accordingly, particular attention should be given to the issue of experience and connections when VC policies seek to spur high-quality novel product development in the IT industry. Policy goals should explicitly include the recruitment of veteran entrepreneurs as VCs, the recruitment of highly experienced VCs to the area, and the building of connections between the VC industry and sources of cutting-edge technological knowledge such as universities.

We will be the first to admit that this is not an easy task. Indeed, experienced VCs are a rare resource even in areas such as Silicon Valley, and they are not easily moved. Nonetheless, there are several factors that can encourage experienced VCs to invest in new regions. First, in today’s global economy, the best VCs are always on the lookout for good opportunities to have a toehold in new and upcoming technology regions. Second, again due to increased globalization, there are a growing number of successful experienced diaspora entrepreneurs who have either made the career transition, or are in the process of making the transition, to becoming VCs. Such entrepreneurs are often looking for ways to reconnect to their home region (be it Iowa, Guazhou, or Bangalore). Successful VC policy should be shaped by an awareness of these behaviors by, for example, providing information to experienced entrepreneurs about investment opportunities in their home region.

A case that illuminates this strategy is Israel. The celebrated “startup nation” had more companies listed in 2012 on the Nasdaq than any country outside the United States except for China (Lazaroff 2012). It is interesting to note here that the Israeli Yozma initiative, which is widely recognized as the most successful VC policy attempt to this day, tried to solve both the expertise and capital pool issues. This approach was shaped by the failure of an earlier attempt—Inbal—that treated the lack of VC investment purely as a financial markets failure (Breznitz 2007). A crucial condition for any investor who sought Yozma funding was the demand that the at least one of the private investors would be a high quality foreign VCs with strong connections to the American financial markets (Avnimelech and Teubal 2006).

Despite these conditions, VCs agreed to invest in Yozma, in part for some of the reasons discussed above. First, it appeared that startups in Israel offered many promising investment opportunities and the matching sums asked for by Yozma (the total sum of each fund including the \$8 million from Yozma was \$20 million) were low enough to make the risk look negliable. Further, by then both Silicon Valley and Boston had a large number of successful Israeli entrepreneurs who had been recruited as VCs. Indeed, in the late 1990s, after the success of Yozma proved the potential of Israel for high-tech investment, leading Silicon Valley firms recruited Israeli entrepreneurs who successfully led at least one company to a financial exit. After training these enterpreneurs as VCs, these funds later sent them to Israel to open an Israeli subsidary of the main fund. Partly as a result, over 95% of all capital invested in Israeli start-ups in the last two decades is foreign.

Accordingly, innovation policies, especially those aimed at a specific geographic locale (the currently fashionable cluster policies are an example), should include a set of policies that encourage interaction and the exchange of best practices among VCs, research centers, and prospective entrepreneurs. Localities that wish to play a part in new technological transformation and do not have local, high-status, experienced VCs should devise ways of ensuring that finance is available to local entrepreneurs who are ahead of the technology acceptance curve. Here, there might be a need for a more direct form of government financing, at least as a bridging solution, until private investors manage to gain expertise in technology investment.

Our results also provide insights for entrepreneurs. They provide another perspective on the potential benefits of locating in regions where complementary inputs, such as venture financing, are available. In particular, our results highlight the importance of the nature and experience of the financing source for entrepreneurs working on products and inventions related to new technological trajectories. Consequently, an entrepreneur’s choice of location should be shaped by their company’s product strategy. If the company’s founders wish to develop products in a new, as of yet unproven, computing platform they should locate themselves and their companies in locales with high-quality, experienced VCs. However, if their business plan relies specifically on developing products on already proven platforms, it might very well be that they should look for other sources of funding, such as corporate venture capital, angles, and lower ranked VCs. An interesting venue for future research on this front is to investigate whether start-ups focusing on older platform technologies achieve greater success if they are located away from the main hubs of experienced VCs.

We view this paper as only a first step in empirical investigation on these issues. Much more needs to be done if we are to gain a better understanding of the systemic role of VCs in ushering in new technological trajectories. A first avenue for future research is to look at multiple paradigmatic shifts in computing platforms, which would allow not only a more nuanced understanding but comparison between such shifts that promise to yield a deeper understanding of contextual conditions for the VCs’ role. A second, related, avenue for future research is merging this analysis with economic geography, especially looking at the resurgence of particular locales as dominant regions for new technologies, as well as the ability of others, such as Silicon Valley, to ensure their continued dominance throughout several paradigmatic technological shifts, even as others, such as Boston, stumble. In a similar way, research that aims to compare the rise of new technologies in which VCs do not play such an important role with those that do, for example a comparison of energy with ICT, can yield insights into the limitations of VCs as a coordination and instigation actor in the sponsoring of new technological ecosystems. Finally, our understanding of the impact that VC’s customers—that is, the organizations that either buy portfolio companies or are necessary for taking them public—have on the VCs’ investment and portfolio companies’ management decisions is significantly underdeveloped. Our interviews with both firm founders and VCs suggest that this is a rich avenue for future research.

The role of third-party organizations in the deployment of new IT systems is by now widely recognized. Nonetheless research on the role that such organizations play in the transformation of IT platforms has been thin on the ground. We hope that our paper will inspire further efforts to fill this important gap.

## Complementarity Analysis Using Panel Data with Instrumental Variables

In this appendix, we describe in detail how we test for complementarity using instrumental variables in conjunction with our panel-data fixedeffects approach. We do not emphasize this result for several reasons. First, instrumenting for only one decision using this approach may not provide consistent parameter estimates (Arora 1996). Second, given our use of within-firm variation in this model, we must rely on a different set of exclusion restrictions from those in our baseline approach.

Our first step is to identify variables that are likely correlated with a firm’s likelihood to receive VC but is uncorrelated with a firm’s new product development strategy. As in our baseline multinomial probit model, we seek to identify exclusion restrictions that influence the supply of local VC funding but are unlikely to influence a firm’s product development strategy. Because we use within-firm variance for identification in this section, we adapt and augment our exclusion restriction from the baseline analysis

The sources of our first two instrumental variables are based upon successful exits from local VC-backed investments. The logic is similar to that described for our baseline exclusion restriction: successful exits from prior rounds of VC investments will increase the returns to local limited partners (LPs). Because LPs invest in local VCs and VCs also invest locally, this will increase the likelihood of VC funding to a startup, all things being equal. First, as in our baseline analysis, we use the number of M&As (mergers & acquisitions) from VC-backed non-IT firms in the start-up’s home state in the previous two years. Our second variable is the total dollar value of VC-backed initial public offerings (IPOs) in all non-IT industries in the start-up’s home state in the previous two years. We use the value of VC-backed IPOs rather than the number because it is more closely correlated with returns; however, we have experimented with using the number of IPOs as a robustness check, and the results are qualitatively similar.

Our third variable is motivated by prior research in the VC literature that has used the number of local limited partners as a source of variation that will influence the likelihood and extent of VC funding that firms will receive (Chemmanur et al. 2011; Samila and Sorenson 2010, 2011). Specifically, we use the number of limited partners that invested in VC funds during the prior five years (excluding the focal year) and are located in the same state. Again, the number of limited partners is correlated with the likelihood that a firm will receive VC funding but is unlikely to be correlated with its product strategy, as LPs usually do not directly interact with portfolio companies.

We use a dummy measure for these three variables (i.e., it equals 1 if it is above the 50<sup>th</sup> percentile and 0 otherwise) to incorporate potential nonlinearities of their effects on VC funding. Further, because the number and incidence of VC deals changes during our sample, we interact each of them with a linear time trend (denoted High M&As × time trend, High IPOs × time trend, and High limited partners × time trend).

Following prior literature (Angrist and Pischke 2009), our second step is to employ a probit model to predict the likelihood that a firm will receive VC funding using these three variables as the predictors. The results are reported in column (1) in Table C1. The next step is to use the predicted likelihood of receiving VC funding from this probit model in column (1) as the instrument in the second-stage regression. Using nonlinear fitted values of instruments in this way has been shown to have greater efficiency than a traditional linear first stage for binary endogenous variables but still provides consistent estimates (Angrist 2001; Newey 1990).

The results from the first- and second-stage regression are included in columns (2) and (3) in Table C1 respectively. As expected, in the first stage, the predicted value of the likelihood of receiving VC financing based on the above three factors is strongly correlated with a firm’s true VC funding status. The F-statistic is 15.94, above the commonly used threshold of 10. The results from the second stage show that the sign of the coefficient of VC is consistent with the baseline fixed-effects model result, although the magnitude and standard error are somewhat higher.
