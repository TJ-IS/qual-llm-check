# Exploring the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of <sup>fl</sup>ow and psychological ownership on security education, training and awareness e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and security compliance

Chul Woo Yoo<sup>a,⁎</sup>, G. Lawrence Sanders<sup>b</sup>, Robert P. Cerveny<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Technology and Operations Management, College of Business, Florida Atlantic University, 777 Glades Road, Boca Raton, FL 33431, United States

<sup>b</sup> Department of Management Science and Systems, School of Management, University at Bufalo, Jacobs Management Center Bufalo, NY 14260, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Security compliance Flow Self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy Psychological ownership Security training Education and awareness e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness

## A B S T R A C T

The purpose of this study is to investigate the impact of <sup>fl</sup>ow and psychological ownership on security education, training, and awareness (SETA) e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness, self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and security compliance intention. The important role of experiencing <sup>fl</sup>ow in SETA is presented as focal antecedents of psychological ownership, self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness, and security compliance intention. To achieve these goals, we propose a theoretical framework and analyze survey data to test the hypotheses. Flow components in SETA are extended to include challenge, feedback, autonomy, immersion, and social interaction. The results illustrate that experiencing <sup>fl</sup>ow in SETA shows signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships with SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and psychological ownership, which in turn positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence security compliance intention. Appropriate theoretical contributions and managerial im plications are also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Numerous surveys and research illustrate information security as a very important managerial concern [1–3]. In response, organizations have consistently increased their budgets for information security without satisfactorily addressing the issue [4,5]. Many information security studies describe employee noncompliance with information security policies, insecure user practices and security threat unawareness as main barriers to organizational information assurance [6]. In an attempt to o<sup>f</sup>set such breaches, organizations invest in and conduct employee security education training and awareness (SETA) programs [7,8]. SETA is regarded as one of the most important explicit methodologies that guide employees to achieve the security goals in the workplace [9]. SETA can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an educational program that is designed to reduce security breaches that occur through a lack of em ployee security awareness.

In information security literature, diverse theoretical approaches, such as deterrence theory [6,10], control theory [11], protection motivation theory [12,13], theory of planned behavior [14], institutional theory [15,16], health belief theory [17], criminology theories [5,18], etc., have been deployed to examine the antecedents of employees compliance or deviant behaviors. However, only a handful of studies have examined the role of SETA in line with security compliance. For example, D'Arcy et al. [7] investigated the relationship between SETA and IS misuse through the lens of deterrence. Puhakainen and Siponen [8] demonstrated that SETA should use contents and methods that motivate the learners to the systematic cognitive processing of information. Posey et al. [19] examined the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of SETA frequency on protective motivation factors. These studies provide valid contributions to the literature on information security by showing that SETA increases employees' awareness regarding penalties for non compliance, security threats, security e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, etc. However, these studies did not consider how employees' experience during the SETA participation in<sup>fl</sup>uences SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and eventually nurtures employees' security behaviors. Considering the e<sup>f</sup>ort, time and cost organizations spend on the SETA, examining the antecedents of SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and security behaviors have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for maintaining business continuity.

Numerous studies have reported that security behaviors, including SETA participation and security compliance, are often regarded as an impediment to regular work activities by employees [14,20]. Neglecting this con<sup>fl</sup>ict can lead to employees' unwilling and unenthusiastic security behavior, and is not an appropriate strategy for maximizing e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. Instead, recent security environments require employees to be more engaged in SETA and to display ownership over information security in the workplace [21], beyond simply checking in periodically with a few mouse clicks to increase the SETA attendance rate. However, users and managers are unsure as to what types of psychological experience/state in<sup>fl</sup>uence the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of SETA and employees' intention to comply with organizational security policies. Therefore, a key question becomes what factor allows SETA to be ef fective and motivate employees to be more willing to comply with security policies? For this research question, we put SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness in the center of our research agenda, and examine the key SETA experiences, and their e<sup>f</sup>ect on the employees' psychological state and em ployees' intentions toward security compliance. To our best knowledge, this question has not been answered in the previous literature of in formation security.

To explore the focal research question, we approached the issue by focusing on the role of flow (here de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a psychological state of being fully immersed in an activity [22]) and psychological ownership (here de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the internalization of ownership of the object [23]). These two theories are strongly related to the discussion above, but have not been well explored in the domain of information security [24]. We consider that using these two theories is appropriate to the context of this study because both help explain employees' immersive engagement and address the outcomes of that engagement. And the body of these two theory streams provides relevant insights for examining our focal research question. The present study proposes a conceptual framework derived from previous literature that discusses the theoretical underpinnings of both <sup>fl</sup>ow and psychological ownership. Since this study views the role of SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness as a central premise, we do not attempt to revisit the exhaustive list of the variables (e.g., deterrence, normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence, threats, etc.) often used to anticipate security compliance in the previous studies, in formulating the focal hypotheses. In order to empirically investigate employees' <sup>fl</sup>ow in SETA and psychological ownership, a survey instrument was designed and used to collect data from organizations that adopted <sup>fl</sup>ow elements in the SETA.

This study o<sup>f</sup>ers two main contributions to the information security literature. First, this study examines how SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and security compliance intention can be enhanced through the lens of <sup>fl</sup>ow. Five components that form users' <sup>fl</sup>ow were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and investigated. The results indicate that <sup>fl</sup>ow signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uences users to develop psychological ownership and enhances SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. Secondly, the role of psychological ownership in facilitating SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectivenes and security compliance intention is also investigated. By themselves, well-elaborated security policies do not automatically motivate employees to comply with organizational mandates. This study, however, demonstrates that psychological ownership signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhances SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and individual employees' willingness to participate in security compliance.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows: the next section reviews previous studies on psychological ownership theory and <sup>fl</sup>ow theory. This review provides the theoretical support for the hypotheses tested in this study. The measurement instrument used to test the hy potheses is then described, and the resulting data gathered by application of the instrument is used to test a structural equation model based on the theoretical framework. Finally, the paper ends with dis cussions on empirical results and accompanying conclusions.

## 2. Psychological ownership theory

In the management literature, psychological ownership has been examined as an important factor that leads to employees' positive be haviors, such as performance, commitment, and ethical behavior [25]. Psychological ownership is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the state in which individuals feel as though the target of ownership is theirs (i.e., “It is mine.”) [26]. A sense of ownership can be perceived with respect to nonphysical targets, such as thoughts or brands, and even to the informational assets of an organization. Researchers have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a variety of constructs, including important motives, key experiences, and the consequences of psychological ownership [23,26,27]. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide an important baseline to illustrate how <sup>fl</sup>ow increases psychological ownership, and how psychological ownership positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and security compliance.

Firstly, previous literature illustrates that having the experience of engaging in the target implies that an individual invests not only time and physical e<sup>f</sup>ort, but also mental energy toward the target object [25]. The engagement of an individual's self into objects causes the self to become one with the object and to develop feelings of ownership toward that object [23]. For example, Moon et al. [28] discussed that the immersive experience is one of the most critical precedents of psychological ownership toward game avatars in the context of massively multiplayer online role-playing games. Rudmin and Berry [29] also suggested that through the process of association, an individual integrates the objects into an artifact of possession. In the process, the individual becomes attached to and has feelings of belongingness with the object [30]. The engagement of the self, allows an individual to see their re<sup>fl</sup>ection in the target and feel their own e<sup>f</sup>ort in its existence.

Secondly, the previous literature indicates that employees with psychological ownership show positive job performance [31]. Van Dyne and Pierce [32] argued that when individuals have possessive feelings, they proactively put forth e<sup>f</sup>orts to control, enhance, and protect the object of ownership. It has been illustrated that when employees have a sense of psychological ownership toward the organization, it triggers active participation from employees, and leads to high levels of job performance [33]. Psychological ownership literature also demonstrated that increasing job performance by having psychological ownership explains variance beyond that provided by commitment and satisfaction [32].

Lastly, previous literature on psychological ownership shows that having psychological ownership increases ethical and responsible behaviors [23]. For example, it has been demonstrated that employees with strong psychological ownership do not tend to display such behaviors as stealing/damaging the organization's property, intentional errors in work, or cyber loa<sup>fi</sup>ng [34]. Van Dyne and Pierce [32] argued that when individuals feel ownership toward a social entity (e.g., fa mily, group, organization, or nation), they are likely to engage in ethical behaviors toward that entity. In other words, when an individual's sense of oneness is closely linked to the target, it enhances the person's self-identity and increases the sense of responsibility and ethics [27]. In a similar vein, Anderson and Agarwal [24] showed that psychological ownership toward the Internet and home computers initiate protective security behaviors.

Based on the above discussion, psychological ownership theory has important implications which are relevant for this study. First, previous studies show that having strong engagement lead to psychological ownership. Flow, one of the core variables which will be discussed later, is achieved when individuals focus on certain actions. And the focal activity or task of SETA is learning about securing the organization and its informational assets. Therefore, the immersive experience of engaging in SETA can lead to employees acquiring psychological ownership toward the object. Second, studies indicate that having psychological ownership leads to good performance on a given job. Considering that e<sup>f</sup>ectively completing a SETA program is a job given to employees, employees with psychological ownership are expected to complete this job successfully. Third, the literature illustrates the role of psychological ownership in motivating ethical and responsible behaviors. Security compliance cannot be fully achieved by an employee's passive attention. Even under the mandatory context, employees' perception regarding security compliance as extra work or an impediment might result in negative rami<sup>fi</sup>cations [20]. Therefore, we consider that psychological ownership can explain security compliance intention beyond that provided by previous studies. Based on the discussion above, we consider psychological ownership as an appropriate conceptual framework for the overarching theory in the study.

## 3. Flow theory

Flow, or immersive-flow, refers to a highly focused psychological state where an individual is completely absorbed and engaged in an activity [35]. Flow is so named because people described their “<sup>fl</sup>ow” experiences using the metaphor of a water current carrying them along in the study [22]. According to the literature, when individuals become immersed in a given activity, they are intrinsically motivated to actively engage in that activity and concomitantly feel a strong sense of control over the environment [36]. Since <sup>fl</sup>ow is intrinsically rewarding, individuals seek to replicate <sup>fl</sup>ow experiences. This introduces a selective mechanism into psychological functioning that fosters engagement [37]; it is often highlighted and manifested in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of education, information technology, music, sports, and gaming [38–40].

Previous studies have illustrated that a strong relationship between <sup>fl</sup>ow and learning exists [41]. For example, by examining the employee training of IT companies, Ho and Kuo [36] indicated that feeling in control, focusing attention on an activity, having an intrinsic interest in the activity and feeling curiosity concerning the activity are important components that enable <sup>fl</sup>ow, and <sup>fl</sup>ow positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences learning outcomes. Fu et al. [42] illustrated that the <sup>fl</sup>ow experience provides suitable stimulant that helps learners to externalize their internal knowledge through interaction with the stimulant, and consequently improves learning results. And they pointed out several important <sup>fl</sup>ow aspects, including clear goals, autonomy, feedback, and concentration. Previous studies have also shown that suitable media types and sense of control enable the individual's mind to focus on the desired performance as a singular action, stretching one's skills and leading to <sup>fl</sup>ow, which consequently leads to e<sup>f</sup>ective communication and learning [43,44]. Overall, the literature demonstrates that <sup>fl</sup>ow stimulated by relevant factors increases learning e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness.

The literature also illustrates continuing engagement in the object as an important outcome of <sup>fl</sup>ow [45]. By promoting intrinsic motivation, <sup>fl</sup>ow sets the foundation for continuing engagement and subsequent activity [46]. Researchers also argued that it provides the basis for becoming engaged with an object for its own sake [47]. In the information systems literature, Agarwal and Karahanna [38] examined how individuals experience cognitive absorption, a variable deeply rooted in the <sup>fl</sup>ow theory, and how this experience positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence information technology (IT) usages. They illustrated that temporal dissociation, focused immersion, heightened enjoyment, control and curiosity allow individuals to be absorbed when using IT, and even motivate individuals use the IT continuously. Lowry et al. [48] showed that in the gaming context, gamers' immersive experiences are strongly related to enjoyment and control, and helps them to invest more time in the game as well as to continuously use it. Previous literature has consistently presented that <sup>fl</sup>ow stimulates internal motives to make more e<sup>f</sup>ort and continue the engagement to the object.

Based on the discussion above, we posit that including the concept of <sup>fl</sup>ow in the study is meritorious, for two reasons. First, previous studies indirectly support that <sup>fl</sup>ow can increase SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. Considering that more and more organizations are attempting to in corporate enjoyment and immersiveness in SETA design [49], exploring <sup>fl</sup>ow re<sup>fl</sup>ects a recent trend in the area of information security. Second, the <sup>fl</sup>ow experience is related to continuing engagement, which is one of the key experiences that lead to psychological ownership. Hence, we consider that the <sup>fl</sup>ow experience during SETA can help employees to have psychological ownership.

## 4. Theoretical model based on psychological ownership and <sup>fl</sup>ow theory

Based on the discussion of <sup>fl</sup>ow and psychological ownership theories, Fig. 1 presents a conceptual model of drivers of SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and employees' security compliance intention. This conceptual model is used to test cross-sectional associations encompassing <sup>fl</sup>ow during SETA participation, psychological ownership, self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and security compliance intention. Detailed theoretical support for these relationships and discussion of individual components that construct <sup>fl</sup>ow are provided in the following subsections.

## 4.1. From flow to psychological ownership

In this study, <sup>fl</sup>ow is used as a second-order construct that consists of <sup>fi</sup>ve di<sup>f</sup>erent sub-dimensions. The immersion experience is often placed in the center of <sup>fl</sup>ow; however, other studies used full autonomy over the activity [50], facing proper challenges [51], and receiving exciting feedbacks [42] as measures of <sup>fl</sup>ow. Also, the education literature empirically supports social interaction [42] as an important aspect of <sup>fl</sup>ow in that it promotes learners' enjoyment and internal motivation thus resulting in <sup>fl</sup>ow [52]. Hou [53] also argues that social interaction relates to learning-mastery, resulting in strong engagement, promoting problem-solving and information sharing. This in<sup>fl</sup>uence is ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed when the educational application involves a computer-mediated environment [54]. We consider these <sup>fi</sup>ndings to be directly applicable to our SETA-<sup>fl</sup>ow study because the context of the study deals with employee training. Based on arguments related to the core components of <sup>fl</sup>ow from prior literature and SETA environments, this paper examines <sup>fi</sup>ve factors in exploring the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of <sup>fl</sup>ow components on psychological ownership. These <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions are feedback, challenge, autonomy, immersion and social interaction. We discuss them each below, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally as they relate to psychological ownership.

Challenge refers to the extent to which an individual feels a level of di<sup>fi</sup>culty that <sup>fi</sup>ts their particular skill level. Di<sup>fi</sup>culty behaves as a twoedged sword. Previous studies have shown that if the challenges are greater than the learner's current knowledge or skills, anxiety may result. However, if the challenges do not meet the learner's knowledge or skills, apathy may occur [55]. In the context of SETA, this argument is applicable because knowledge of security has various levels of di<sup>fi</sup>- culty. To understand security issues, learners must have appropriate background knowledge. On the other hand, learners with security experience may become distracted or bored if they are forced to repeat easy activities designed to improve the knowledge level of beginners. Having engaging experiences in the target area is a core component that provokes psychological ownership [23]. If learners have di<sup>fi</sup>culty completing a SETA program, this disrupts this experience. Hence, we propose that appropriate challenges keep learners engaged with the task, and in turn, facilitate psychological ownership.

Feedback in this study refers to the extent to which an individual feels that SETA extends noti<sup>fi</sup>cations to them about their progress. Such noti<sup>fi</sup>cations are often delivered in the form of reward, award, and message. For example, when an employee <sup>fi</sup>nishes one question or one chapter of education, she may receive points or other noti<sup>fi</sup>cations [56]. Through these noti<sup>fi</sup>cations, users can con<sup>fi</sup>rm their learning status in the SETA. Feedback is one of the most popular <sup>fl</sup>ow elements adopted because positive feedback encourages mastery of tasks [57]. And this feedback process promotes individuals to integrate the object into an artifact of possession, namely, psychological ownership [29]. Therefore, it can be hypothesized that good SETA feedback facilitates users to develop a sense of psychological ownership.

Autonomy refers to the control a user perceives over SETA. If learners <sup>fi</sup>nd it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand the procedures of SETA or to control SETA, they will not be fully involved with the experience that it provides. Perceived control over the subject has been investigated as a critical factor in forming psychological ownership [58]. Furby [59] argued that the greater the amount of concern or control individuals have over certain objects, the more the objects will be experienced psychologically as part of the self, i.e., psychological ownership. Hence, it is expected that well-developed autonomy in SETA will make it more likely that an employee will both facilitate <sup>fl</sup>ow and lead to psychological ownership.

Immersion refers to a state of mind experienced by people who are totally involved in a certain activity. Immersion has been investigated as one of the most representative experiences of <sup>fl</sup>ow [35]. High immersion is often derived from strong enjoyment and leads to high selfinvestment in the object in terms of time and energy. Prior studies also present that enjoyment and self-investment are critical factors in making sense of psychological ownership [28,58]. We can postulate that if employees perceive more enjoyment and invest more time and e<sup>f</sup>ort by experiencing <sup>fl</sup>ow during SETA, they might have stronger psychological ownership over informational assets and security in the workplace. Based on these arguments we assume that it is highly likely that engaged users have a high level of psychological ownership while experiencing <sup>fl</sup>ow.

Social interaction refers to the extent to which learners interact socially during SETA. One of the core antecedents of psychological ownership in an organizational setting is interacting with other employees [60]. Building a communication channel that allows members to ask for help with problems is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant foundation of a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment to organizations [61]. Other research has also shown that interactions among members, and the technology that allows that interaction to happen, are essential in forming a<sup>f</sup>ective emotions that can lead to psychological ownership [23]. SETA, with <sup>fl</sup>ow, can promote this social interaction among users through instant messaging, bulletin boards, etc. SETA a<sup>f</sup>ords employees with an opportunity to develop a sense of closeness with an organization and other coworkers, as well as psychological ownership toward the informational assets of an organization.

Richins [62] pointed out that possessions are valued for enjoyment, along with interpersonal and identity reasons. We argue that experi encing <sup>fl</sup>ow during SETA satis<sup>fi</sup>es these motives to have psychological ownership. Overall, previous literature directly or indirectly supports that each dimension of <sup>fl</sup>ow strongly relates to psychological owner ship. Based on the previous arguments, we posit:

H1. Flow has a positive impact on psychological ownership.

## 4.2. From flow to SETA efectiveness

Flow correlates with high performance [63] and is critical to and ampli<sup>fi</sup>es learning [42]. In this study, SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness refers to the extent to which trainees feel they achieve the goal of SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectively. The goal of SETA lies in promoting employees' awareness of security policy, security practice and other security issues. Learners who report experiencing <sup>fl</sup>ow are more likely to further develop their skills [64]. Flow was found to be a strong motivator for developing personal skills and enhancing individual performance.

Considering that SETA involves learning that occurs in the workplace, a positive relationship between <sup>fl</sup>ow and SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness can be expected. In particular, balancing challenge provides appropriate cognitive loading to SETA learners [44]. Feedback helps learners keep up with their curriculum and have a sense of achievement [63]. Autonomy over SETA enables learners to control the learning pace [36]. Immersion strengthens the internal motivation to stay focused on the program and not be distracted by other activities [36]. Lastly, social interaction helps learners communicate and collaborate with others [42]. Consequently, we argue that these experiences help learners to learn e<sup>f</sup>ectively about threat awareness, secure user practices, and security policy through SETA. Therefore, we posit:

H2. Flow has a positive impact on SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness.

## 4.3. From psychological ownership to SETA efectiveness and security compliance intention

E<sup>f</sup>ective SETA is developed through trainees' attitude toward the training as well as through the pedagogic quality of SETA. Pierce et al. [26] asserted that psychological ownership is positively associated with a desire to maintain and protect, and it provokes a sense of identity that results in an enhanced sense of responsibility for the outcome. Feelings of psychological ownership engender a sense of responsibility to invest time and energy [65]. When an employee's sense of self is closely connected to the information security, as in the case of psychological ownership, a desire to maintain and protect the identity results in enhanced responsible participation in SETA. Also, since SETA is one of the most important methods to promote the security of the organization, investments in time and e<sup>f</sup>ort in SETA could be easily encouraged, consequently making SETA more e<sup>f</sup>ective. It has been reported that these feelings of possession often lead to positive work outcomes in the organization [27]. And since completing SETA is one of the tasks given to employees, we can assume that strong psychological ownership can promote individual employees' successful completion of SETA. Thus, we propose:

H3. Psychological ownership has a positive impact on SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness.

The literature on psychological ownership has demonstrated that individuals who have psychological ownership toward certain objects often identify themselves in the object [30], and show not only a<sup>f</sup>ect, but also improved responsible and ethical behavior toward the object [66]. For example, Van Dyne and Pierce [32] described results that show positive links among psychological ownership of the organization, employee attitudes, and work behavior. Avey et al. [67] showed that psychological ownership has a negative association with workplace deviance. O'Reilly and Chatman [68] argued that psychological attachment may be predicated on compliance (instrumental involvement for speci<sup>fi</sup>c extrinsic rewards), identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (involvement based on a desire for a<sup>fi</sup>liation), and internalization.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be applied to the present study's context. It can be argued that having feelings of psychological ownership toward the informational assets of an organization allows employees to identify themselves in the organization, to be aware of the importance of pro tecting the informational assets of an organization from security threats, and to feel a responsibility for the future of information security. This psychological state strongly pushes employees toward security compliance. Psychological ownership is based on internal moti vation [48]. It can be argued that this reduces an employees' resistance or lack of active involvement with security compliance. Thus, we posit: H4. Psychological ownership has a positive impact on security compliance intention.

## 4.4. From SETA efectiveness to self-eficacy

Security researchers and practitioners have long proclaimed the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of SETA programs because SETA increases employees' awareness and self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy for security practices [7,69]. Likewise, it has been argued that the most e<sup>f</sup>ective way to ensure the practicability of a security policy in an organization is to make employees understand the policies and accept the necessary precautions for protecting the organization [70,71]. SETA programs address this by focusing on providing users with general knowledge of the information security environment, along with the skills necessary to perform any required security procedures [71,72].

Herath and Rao [70] found that security training works as an im portant resource that positively promotes employees' security e<sup>fi</sup>cacy. Tannenbaum et al. [73] illustrated that the sense of ful<sup>fi</sup>llment about the training positively nurtures self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy. Based on <sup>fi</sup>ndings from previous studies, we can assume that when employees perceive SETA as e<sup>f</sup>ective, they can believe they have the necessary knowledge and skills to handle security issues in the organization. It is reasonable that people who are trained e<sup>f</sup>ectively are better equipped with knowledge and skills on security guidelines and countermeasures. Hence, we suggest:

H5. SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness has a positive impact on self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy.

## 4.5. From self-eficacy to security compliance intention

Previous studies illustrate that individuals generally avoid tasks where self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy is low, and cannot persist longer in those e<sup>f</sup>orts [74]. Low self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy can lead individuals to believe tasks to be harder than they are. This often results in poor task planning and increased stress. The relationship between self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy and security compliance has been well examined in the security literature [70]. Numerous studies show that self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy with taking the necessary security precautions directly in<sup>fl</sup>uences security behaviors, e.g., security compliance [12,75]. Considering information security is often regarded as a burden to employees, and continuously changing threat environments require them to be alert, employees with high self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy on security are inclined to exhibit con<sup>fi</sup>dence and the intention to comply with security policy.

H6. Self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy has a positive impact on security compliance intention.

## 5. Method

## 5.1. Measurement development

Measurement items for the constructs were developed based on an extensive literature review on psychological ownership, <sup>fl</sup>ow, and information security. When available, existing scales were utilized. When no existing scale was found for a given construct, items were adapted from the most closely related scale. Items for psychological ownership were derived from Lee and Chen [58] and Pierce et al. [26]. We operationalized psychological ownership to capture the degree to which respondents perceive the informational assets as theirs. The items for constructs of <sup>fl</sup>ow in SETA were adapted and based on Fu et al.'s [42] work. Items for self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy and security compliance intention were drawn from the work of Bulgurcu et al. [14]. Items for SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness were adapted from Ho and Kuo's [36] work.

Instrument items were developed using the standard instrument development processes [76]. The content validity of all instrument items were examined by four experts familiar with information security and instrument development (one IS faculty member, one police ad ministration faculty member, and two IS doctoral students). The wording, contents, item order, and format of the items in the questionnaire were tested and articulated based on suggestions from these experts.

After measurement item development, the items were translated into a Korean version of the survey for use at a data collection site in South Korea. Since there are language-oriented di<sup>f</sup>erences such as nuance and interpretation, close attention was given to the translation by employing the backward translation technique. Further, three bilingual speakers assisted in the process to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that no semantic discrepancies existed. Moreover, to check for item clarity, reliability and construct validity, a pilot test was conducted using 21 graduate students in South Korea. Reliability assessment was done using Cronbach's Alpha, and construct validity support was determined in part by computing the average variance extracted AVE and evaluating cross-loadings. Each item was formatted into a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point Likert-type scale. The instrument items employed for the main survey are listed in Appendix A.

## 5.2. Data collection

Data was collected in South Korea. We distributed the questionnaires to 31 sister organizations of a law reinforcement agency in the southwest region of South Korea. They are not the same organization. The main tasks of the targeted organizations involve enforcing the law, protecting the property, preserving the order, and so on. Information security in the organizations is considered one of the most important organizational policies. ## 7.1. Theoretical contribution

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study make contributions to the literature of information security in three ways. First, the study found that <sup>fl</sup>ow in SETA provides a positive impact on information security in an organization. To the best of our knowledge, no other study has investigated these variables in the context of information security. As previous studies have argued, the failure of an organization to have employees comply with information security policy is regarded as a key threat to the organization and business continuity [6,88]. Diverse approaches, such as enhancing the awareness regarding the risk of security violations, developing a strong security orientation, focusing on peer pressure and other strategies have been used successfully in academia [7]. This study demonstrated that helping employees experience <sup>fl</sup>ow in SETA could be critical since it could enhance the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of SETA, self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and security compliance intention. Feedback, challenge, autonomy, immersion, and social interaction were found in this study as critical factors allowing <sup>fl</sup>ow. This is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to <sup>fi</sup>nd an important link on the impact of <sup>fl</sup>ow in SETA and SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and psychological ownership.

Secondly, the relationship between psychological ownership, SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and security compliance intention was examined. No previous studies attempted to examine these links in the literature. In the study, psychological ownership was adopted as a critical theoretical lens promoting training performance and explaining ethical and responsible behavior. According to prior security compliance research, employees' protective motivation is a critical step that initiates the security behavior [12]. This study indicated that psychological ownership positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness, and boosts ethical and responsible motivation. Reluctant and unwilling compliance rarely maximizes the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of security policy. Our results demonstrated that employees with psychological ownership show a high willingness to comply with security policies.

Lastly, our analysis illustrated the important mediating roles of psychological ownership between <sup>fl</sup>ow and SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness, and <sup>fl</sup>ow and security compliance intention. These relationships were developed based upon the psychological ownership framework. Psychological ownership has been examined in various contexts [28,58]. However, it has not been adopted in the domain of security compliance. By verifying the mediating relationships, this study con <sup>fi</sup>rmed that <sup>fl</sup>ow in SETA environments enhance psychological ownership, and consequently are connected to SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness, self-e<sup>fi</sup>- cacy and security compliance intention.

## 7.2. Managerial implications

This study has important implications for practitioners. First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings illustrated the importance of the SETA experience and its e<sup>f</sup>ect on psychological ownership. Many organizations invest a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of money in SETA to promote employees' compliance. Online security training, group meetings, email communications, and workshops are adopted for this purpose. However, not all methods produce the expected outcomes. Many employees regard attending SETA as an extra burden. Previous studies have described security compliance as being perceived by employees as an impediment to their regular work [14]. Under these circumstances, SETA is often not e<sup>f</sup>ective.

Incorporating the components of <sup>fl</sup>ow in SETA helps organizations achieve their e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness goals; it allows learners to have control over the program, interact with other learners, and be engaged in the learning process. These <sup>fl</sup>ow components can be implemented to SETA by considering multiple methods. For example, a badge system can be utilized to increase the sense of feedback from the system. When trainees complete certain tasks successfully, they can gain a certain level of experience points and di<sup>f</sup>erent badges. This method can stimulate a sense of achievement and ownership. Using intrinsic motivation can be more e<sup>f</sup>ective than strictly forcing employees to <sup>fi</sup>nish the training.

Consideration of the immersion experience and social interactions can be another important turning point in developing SETA. The computer environment has its own advantage, especially when the immersion experience and social interaction are enhanced due to advanced graphics and interactive channels. In fact, numerous organizations that provide online SETA have already started to track their trainees' immersion experience during the program. Training is best conducted based on a story that trainees can identify with and want to be involved with. Good computer graphics and interfaces stimulate trainees' curiosity. Chatting channels allow trainees to interact and help each other. Our analysis also demonstrated that interface of the system and adjustability of the education di<sup>fi</sup>culty should not be overlooked when SETA is designed.

Second, our analysis indicated that increasing SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness is one of the key paths that lead to employees' security compliance in tention. A path coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of the relationship between SETA e<sup>f</sup>ective ness and security compliance intention is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Our analysis illustrated that employees with high SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness are likely to comply with security policy. Organizations need to pay attention to recognize employees' attitudes toward SETA participation. If employees feel their SETA participation is burdensome, the training needs to be scaled back, or some other type of adjustment needs to be made. Organizations need to search for e<sup>fi</sup>cient ways to motivate their employees to participate in SETA.

## 7.3. Limitation and future research opportunities

While we believe this study was developed and based on a sound and rich theoretical model and was tested with a reliable survey instrument, there are limitations that can be addressed in future research. First, because the data came from organizations in similar domains, i.e., law enforcement, in South Korea, the results may re<sup>fl</sup>ect unique characteristics of the sample group. Even though our targeted organizations were not small organizations, results could be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by domain level factors. Cultural factors and national-level sensitivity toward information security could also be an issue when generalizing the results. However, considering the theoretical foundations of <sup>fl</sup>ow and psychological ownership were already well developed in diverse contexts in the literature, and given that our sample provides appropriate environments for testing our framework, we believe that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the data still provide valid contributions to the literature as exploratory research. Future studies could collect data from diverse samples of <sup>fi</sup>rms that have implemented security education and training protocols in di<sup>f</sup>erent parts of the world to address this issue.

Secondly, this study only examined the positive e<sup>f</sup>ects of having <sup>fl</sup>ow. However, previous literature on <sup>fl</sup>ow theory also illustrated that excessive <sup>fl</sup>ow could interrupt regular tasks [63]. For example, when learners feel too immersed in using the e-learning system, its original purpose- learning knowledge- can be overlooked and a negative outcome could result. However, in this study, we focused on cross-sectional association among focal variables. Therefore, we recommend that future studies collect longitudinal data, and compare the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of excessive <sup>fl</sup>ow on SETA e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness.

Lastly, this study focused on security compliance. There are numerous opportunities for subsequent investigation. The earlier literature on employees' compliance intentions did not fully explain malicious computer abuse or violation by internal employees [89]. And
