# Words Matter! Toward a Prosocial Call-to-Action for Online Referral: Evidence from Two Field Experiments

Abstract. The underlying premise of referral marketing is to target existing, ostensibly delighted customers to spread awareness and influence adoption of a focal product among their friends who are also likely to benefit from adopting the product. In other words, referral programs are designed to accelerate organic word-of-mouth (WOM) exposure using financial incentives. This poses a challenge, in that it mixes an intrinsically motivated process (stemming from the desire to share a customer’s delight with a product or a service) with an extrinsic trigger in the form of a financial incentive. Prior research has shown that mixing intrinsic and extrinsic motivations can lead to suboptimal outcomes, which, in turn, presents a conceptual dilemma in the design of referral programs. In this paper, we demonstrate how firms can benefit from framing calls-to-action for referral programs in such a way as to move closer to the original intent of organic, intrinsically motivated WOM marketing and yet at the same time reap the benefits of using a financial incentive to increase referral rates. In particular, given a fixed incentive scheme, ceteris paribus, we show the efficacy of a prosocial call-to-action over some of the more commonly used calls-toaction observed in practice. We posit, and causally demonstrate via a large-scale randomized field experiment involving 100,000 customers, that an intrinsically prosocial element in framing the call-to-action to initiate the referral process is a necessary condition for success. When contrasted with egoistic and equitable framing of calls-to-action, the prosocial framing yields a significantly higher propensity to initiate a referral, as well as a significantly higher number of successful referrals. Additional mechanism-level analysis that interacts the treatments with customer characteristics such as repeat purchase, net promoter score, and time since last purchase, an additional field experiment with more attractive referral reward and an Amazon Mechanical Turk experiment confirm the im portance of an altruistic element in generating a higher quality of advocacy and reducing referral frictions. Subjects in the prosocial group report lower levels of guilt associated with sending a referral and are more able to identify family and friends’ benefit as a motive for sharing referrals and therefore are more selective in sharing the referral message.

## 1. Introduction and

## Theoretical Background

It is commonly believed and accepted that people consult friends for advice in making purchase decisions. Not surprisingly, concomitant with the exploding the growth of digital social networks and the importance of word-of-mouth (WOM)–based product diffusion, firms recognize the importance of using referral programs to increase new business. Such schemes encourage existing customers with an incentive-laden call-toaction (CTA) to engage their social networks by informing them about products and ultimately influencing and stimulating friends’ purchase decisions. In other words, referral programs are designed to accelerate organic WOM referrals using financial incentives. This poses a challenge, in that it mixes an intrinsically motivated process (stemming from the desire to share a customer’s delight with a product or a service) with an extrinsic trigger in the form of a financial incentive. Prior research has shown that mixing intrinsic and extrinsic motivations can lead to suboptimal outcomes (Gneezy et al. 2011), which, in turn, presents a conceptual dilemma in the design of referral programs. Referrers now have to mentally disambiguate whether they spread product information via WOM as a result of intrinsic product delight and the desire to share such delight for the benefit of their friends or the financial incentive. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper to highlight the intrinsic–extrinsic dilemma in using the change in the framing of the CTA. Further, we not only highlight this dilemma but also provide a concrete solution that is tested via two large-scale randomized field experiments<sup>2</sup> to resolve it. Specifically, we provide robust causal evidence under two widely used types of promotional incentives that shows that invoking the sender through an intrinsically motivated prosocial CTA (hereafter, prosocial CTA) outperforms other more commonly observed alternatives. Further, we conduct additional mechanism-level analysis and an Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) experiment to understand why customers share or do not share after receiving different CTAs.

Our manipulation is, simply put, a play on words. We design a large-scale randomized field experiment where we fix (1) the allocation of the financial incentive to the industry standard practice, where both the sender and receiver of the referral are equally incentivized (the reader can relate this to popular equal-split reward schemes used by companies such as Uber, where both the sender and receiver get \$10 off the next ride) and (2) the referral message from the sender to the receiver. We only manipulate the framing—that is, the words used and emphasized— in the CTA to provoke the sender to action and initiate the referral. To be clear, all subjects in our randomized field experiments receive the exact same industrystandard, equal-split incentive, where the same reward is offered to both the sender and the receiver. This design allows us to use the change in the framing of the CTA to isolate the value of an intrinsic prosocial driver in the presence of an extrinsically motivated financial reward.

In summary, while referral marketing is growing, the underlying science behind understanding and optimizing its various dimensions is nascent. As alluded to earlier, the optimal design of online referral programs hinges on three key design choices: (1) incentive design for the person initiating the referral (hereafter, sender) and the person receiving the referral (hereafter, recipient), (2) CTA for online referral to the sender, and (3) message design to the recipient (Figure 1). Whereas previous research has investigated the incentive design (Bapna et al. 2014) and the design of the message from sender to recipients (Sun et al. 2020), no study has investigated how firms can optimally design a CTA to engage customers in initiating referrals in the first place. In this study, we take the research one step further and examine whether we can improve the effectiveness of the referral program by manipulating the framing of the CTA while fixing the incentive structure and the referral message.

Therefore, we keep the incentive structure identical for both senders and recipients and only vary the emphasis on the CTA. In this way, the senders would still perceive the incentive as fair but may be influenced by the CTA and take a specific perspective when making a referral decision. Given the increasing importance of online referral programs, it is crucial to close this gap.

We view online referrals as incentive-laden WOM mechanisms. The fundamental building block of successful WOM-based product diffusion is delight among the existing base of customers (Kornish and Li 2010), which then is communicated to relevant portions of their social networks, whose actors within might also experience similar delight or benefit or gain positive utility from adopting the focal product. This concern for the benefit of another suggests that a necessary condition for successful online referral is a prosocial consideration of the sender toward the recipient’s welfare in adopting the product. Another mechanism that could be at work with the pro social CTA is pure or warm-glow altruism (Andreoni 1988, 1990). It could also be the case that a prosocial CTA may reduce customers’ psychological cost of feeling guilty about gaining referral rewards (Ryu and Feick 2007). It is important for the reader to note that our research design is aimed at capturing the total effect (similar to the study on peer influence by Bapna and Umyarov (2015)) of the prosocial CTA under two widely used types of promotion in a robust field environment.

Taken together, these theoretical ideas suggest that an intrinsically prosocial frame of mind is likely to result in a higher quality of advocacy, which might be associated with reduced guilt in receiving a monetary award for someone else’s action, better targeting of people who are more likely to receive positive utility from the product, greater effort in communicating the potential benefits, or, in all likelihood, some mixture of all of these. Yet a close look at influential referral programs in practice<sup>3</sup> reveals that companies do not necessarily home in on this theoretical insight and exhibit significant heterogeneity (or, arguably, lack of thoughtfulness) in their design of all three aspects of online referrals. Consistent with the preceding theoretical argument, Bapna et al. (2014) and Hong et al. (2017) show that a prosocial component in the incentive scheme, either via splitting the incentive equally between the sender and the recipient or by purely rewarding the recipient, significantly outperforms purely rewarding just the sender. In the same vein, Sun et al. (2018) find that when a single-use promotional code targeted to the customers is made shareable, a significant number of customers are willing to pass on the code to their friends rather than using it themselves.

With respect to the framing of the CTA, the focus of this study, the current state of affairs reveals that companies are seen to be “experimenting” with multiple types of CTAs for online referrals, yet the prosocial CTA—homing in and emphasizing the recipients’ monetary benefit—is, anecdotally, seldom observed.<sup>4</sup> This is potentially driven by firms’ perception that the sender may be more likely to initiate a referral if his or her own monetary benefit is highlighted. In line with this thinking, we see a prevalence of what we call the egoistic CTA, where the firm highlights the reward (often a discount coupon or a free gift) to the sender and, also in several cases, what we call an equitable CTA, where the firm highlights that both the sender and his or her friends receive the reward. Whereas the exact distribution of the usage of these three types of CTAs is unknown because of a lack of comprehensive proprietary data,<sup>5</sup> what is more interesting from a research perspective is a controlled scientific “horse race” between these three schemes and a generic CTA, which will serve as our control group. We conduct this horse race using the methodology of a randomized field experiment (Acquisti and Fong 2015, Aral and Walker 2011, Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Ghose et al. 2015) that is the “gold standard” for causal inference and is increasingly used in the information systems literature. We further conduct an additional analysis as well as an online controlled experiment on MTurk to investigate the underlying mechanisms explaining our main result.

As mentioned earlier, we are particularly interested in the role of intrinsically prosocial framing in driving customers’ referral decisions and related outcomes as they contradict conventional wisdom and the observed norm in practice. Theoretically, from prior research around the suboptimality of mixing intrinsic and extrinsic drivers (Gneezy et al. 2011), we have clear directions to try to resolve the intrinsic–extrinsic dilemma in referral marketing, yet, in practice, it could be the case that what works is Homo economicus–styled self-maximizing thinking from economics (the industry norm is to emphasize the benefit of the referral incentive to the senders to stimulate their act of referring). Alternatively, in practice, it could also be the case that emphasizing benefits to both parties is a reasonable compromise as would be justified by equity seeking. Given the above-mentioned trade-offs and countervailing theoretical predictions, it is an interesting empirical question to determine whether prosocial framing leads to a larger volume of referrals or better outcomes for firms. Specifically, we test the following hypotheses via our research design.

Hypothesis 1a (Main Effect). The prosocial CTA outperforms the control, egoistic, and equitable CTAs with respect to the likelihood of making a referral and the number of referrals.

Because referral marketing is a two-stage process, it is of obvious interest to ask whether a particular type of CTA leads to better outcomes on the receiver side, as measured by the likelihood of purchase and the amount spent. It could be the case that customers become more selective in referral because they may place a higher value on their friends also experiencing equal delight. If this is the case, then the prosocial CTA is likely to result in fewer referrals; however, this potential downside arguably can be counterbalanced by the fact that conditional on the referral decision, such selective referrals driven by prosocial framing may be better targeted and therefore result in a higher conversion rate than those driven by other motives, such as the equitable and selfish. We thus view this as an empirical question and test the following.

Hypothesis 1b (Main Effect). The prosocial CTA outperforms the control, egoistic, and equitable CTAs with respect to outcomes of WOM referrals.

If altruism is one possible driver of online referrals, we should see the higher impact of intrinsically prosocial framing for customers with a higher affinity for the product, because customers will care more about their friends’ utility, and in this case, customers project their own evaluation onto others (Cronbach 1955, Ichheiser 1946). Customers with a higher affinity may be more likely to infer that their friends would also gain benefits or positive utility from the product and are thus more likely to share. Customers with high affinity for the product can be represented by the number of repeat purchases (Hoyer 1984) and by a high reported net promoter score (NPS; e.g., willingness to share the product; Reichheld and Thomas 1996). Motivated by the fact that there is no intrinsic–extrinsic dilemma for delighted customers and the prosocial CTA, we test the following hypotheses by exploring heterogeneity in the treatment effect.

Hypothesis 2a (Mechanism). Customer affinity will positively moderate the effect of the prosocial CTA on referral behavior.

Furthermore, we expect that the effect of prosocial framing may depend on the recency of customers’ last purchase, whereas the effects of other framings are less likely to have this linkage. This is likely to happen because sharing under prosocial considerations is more driven by intrinsic delight and enthusiasm for the product (Kornish and Li 2010) than external incentive, but such delight/enthusiasm to share the product may decay as the amount of time after customers’ purchases increases (Berger and Schwartz 2011). Thus, we expect the effect of prosocial framing, which is closely related to the altruism in sharing, would be highest for customers who purchased and received the product recently and would be lower for customers who made the purchase a while ago. In contrast, we expect the effect of egoistic framing, which provokes referrals by tapping into users’ extrinsic motivations, will not decrease over time. In such a condition, it can be argued that the extrinsic motivation for customers to make a referral is independent of when the customer made the last purchase. In other words, the value of the material payoff will never decay, but intrinsic motivations are expected to. We thus hypothesize and test the following.

Hypothesis 2b (Mechanism). The recency of the past purchase will positively moderate the effect of the prosocial CTA on customers’ referral behavior.

In addition, aligned with our theorizing about the mechanism underlying the effect of different CTAs, we hypothesize that prosocial framing would reduce senders’ guilt from engaging in referral programs with incentives, especially compared with other framings (Ryu and Feick 2007). It is also likely that those who share under prosocial framing are more likely to focus on the benefits of others (e.g., family/ friends) than one’s own payoff. As an outcome, such customers may also become more selective when making a referral because they care and deliberate more about their friends’ payoffs (Kornish and Li 2010). Therefore, with the objective of uncovering the underlying motives that distinguish the success of a particular type of CTA, we test the following.

Hypothesis 3a (Motive). Customers targeted with the prosocial CTA will report lower levels of guilt.

Hypothesis 3b (Motive). Customers who share referrals under the prosocial CTA will be more likely to identify family and friends’ benefits as the motive.

In this study, we rigorously test, identify, and understand the underlying motives behind the optimal CTA for online referrals. We do so in collaboration with a large U.S.-based online platform specialized in photo processing and related products (their revenue for 2015 was \$22 million). The company specializes in photo processing and related “durable goods”–type products. Our treatments manipulate the framing— that is, they solely manipulate the words and emphasis of certain phrases of the CTA—keeping all other aspects of the incentive and messaging constant. Our randomized field experiment design allows us to identify the causal effect of the framing of the CTAs on customers’ referral decisions, whethe they share, to what extent they share, and their induced referral outcomes as measured by the number of successful referrals. Specifically, we randomly assign 100,000 customers who have made purchases on the platform in the past into four test groups (10,000 in the control group and 30,000 in each of the three treatment groups) and email each group with different CTAs (Figure 2). We collected data on customers’ referral behaviors and outcomes within a 5-week window after the experiment. We further augment the data from field experiments with rich archival data, including product characteristics, in dividual characteristics, customers’ past purchases, and their NPS scores. In addition, with an eye to gen eralizability, and in the spirit of advancing knowledge by subjecting our hypotheses to stricter falsification tests (Popper 2005), we conduct additional experiments. These robustness checks serve as the bar for detecting a prosocial framing effect. We do so by carefully designing an experiment to test whether the results will hold when the referral incentive is attractive to all users, including senders who have previously purchased products. We also conduct a controlled ex periment on MTurk to identify our proposed mecha nisms. The data from the randomized experiment, th archival data, and the MTurk experiment allow us to identify the causal effect of different CTAs as well as explore the underlying mechanisms and motives.

Our primary finding, consistent across multiple econometric specifications, is in line with our theoretical prediction and in contrast to conventional wisdom. If we consider the entirety of our three outcomes, we find that the prosocial framing of the CTA for initiating a referral is most effective in driving referral behavior (decision to refer and the total number of referrals) and results in the best outcomes (recipients’ conversions) for the firm. Compared with the control group with information about the fixed incentive scheme, the email that highlights friends benefits significantly increases not only the likelihood of the sender making referrals but also the total number of successful referral conversions. In addition, we find that the effect of prosocial framing is significantly higher than the effect of egoistic framing and equitable framing across all the referral behaviors and the overall outcome (Hypotheses 1a and 1b). Second, we find significant heterogeneity in treatment effects across different customer segments, yielding interesting insights into the underlying mechanisms that support our main finding. Specifically, consistent with the underlying theoretical premise of customer delight as a necessary condition for effective WOM referrals, we find that the prosocial framing is more effective for users who made repeat purchases in the past and for those who reported higher NPS scores (Hypothesis 2a). This is aligned with the notion that customers who have higher levels of product and brand affinity are more likely to share said product with their friends when put under the prosocial framing. In other words, there is alignment between their organic motive and the intrinsically oriented framing of the extrinsic financial incentive. In contrast, delighted customers under egoistic and equitable framing face the intrinsic– extrinsic dilemma and do not experience this alignment. Finally, we find that the effect of prosocial framing is positively moderated by the recency of the purchase (Hypothesis 2b). In contrast, we did not observe such significant decay for other types of framing that highlight the referral incentive to the sender. This is consistent with the fact that customers’ inherent enthusiasm to talk about the product may decay over time after a new purchase (Berger and Schwartz 2011).

We further investigate the underlying motives by conducting an MTurk experiment. The results from the controlled experiment suggest that a prosocial CTA for online referrals is associated with a reduction in customers’ feelings of guilt in making referrals compared with other types of CTAs (Hypothesis 3a) We also find that customers who are under prosocial framing are more likely to report family and friends benefits as their motive for sharing (Hypothesis 3b). Overall, the evidence suggests that altruism is important in driving an increase in online referrals and that it can be spurred by a prosocial CTA.

In addition to the theoretical insights, our study also provides clear managerial implications for firms. Based on the results of our experiments, we offer concrete guidance to firms on how, to whom, and when they should initiate a call for sharing, as well as why they should do so. First, our main results suggest that in contrast to conventional wisdom, firms should more frequently use prosocial components in framing their CTAs compared with the status quo of current practice. Second, our finding of large heterogeneity in treatment effects across different customer segments suggests that given the costs associated with referral marketing, firms should target customers with higher product affinity first in their referral campaigns. Third, regarding timing, firms should send out a prosocial CTA for referrals to customers shortly after their purchase. Interestingly, as we discuss in the conclusion, the optimal CTA for a referral also complements the optimal CTA for purchase (on how, to whom, and when to call). Taken together, these insights have the potential to significantly impact and alter firms’ marketing communications. Given that egoistic framing is the dominant type of CTA in industry, the findings may provide significant implications that would change the current practice. In fact, based on the results of our experiment, the prosocial CTA (along with the timing and targeting strategy) has been implemented by the collaborating platform (Collage.com; more on this in the next subsection), impacting hundreds of thousands of customers each year and creating significant economic returns.

Our study draws from and contributes to several streams of literature at the intersection of information systems, marketing, and economics. First, our study enriches the literature on digital WOM (Godes and Mayzlin 2004, Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Duan et al. 2008, Zhu and Zhang 2010, Schmitt et al. 2011, Goes et al. 2014, Burtch et al. 2017) by identifying the causal effect of a CTA—a key element in online referral programs. Although designing key elements of an online referral program to drive social contagion has been of much interest to both academics and practitioners, identifying the causal effects of social diffusion of WOM is methodologically hard because of the reflection problem (Manski 1993). Using two large-scale randomized experiments, we show that, in contrast to the conventional wisdom and current practice, a prosocial component in a CTA is positively linked to driving online referrals and conversions. A seemingly minor change of phrasing in the framing leads to a significant increase in referral revenue. In this way, our study also closes the gap of identifying the optimal design of online referral programs (with Bapna et al. (2014) on optimal incentive structure, Sun et al. (2020) on optimal message design, and Belo and Li (2018) on optimal threshold design).

We present our empirical strategy in Section 3 and the results in Section 4. Then we present additional robustness checks in Section 5. Finally, we discuss implications and draw conclusions in Section 6.

## 2. Institutional Details and Experimental Design

Our experimental design is motivated by seminal research in economics that sorts individuals into three categories based on their self- and other-regarding preferences—that is, whether they mainly care about themselves or others (Andreoni and Miller 2002). Through a series of differentially weighted dictator games, we show that individuals can either be purely self-regarding, care about others’ benefits only as much as they care about themselves, or purely otherregarding. In line with this, we test the effect of three different types of CTAs for online referrals: (1) egoistic, where our framing highlights and emphasizes the reward to the sender, (2) equitable, where our framing highlights and emphasizes that both the sender and the receiver receive the reward, and (3) prosocial, where our framing highlights and emphasizes the reward to the receiver. More details on the operationalization of this can be found in the next subsection.

Our research site is an online platform, Collage.com, where users can design a collage by uploading photos and customizing the layout with the site’s proprietary software tools. Once a user creates a collage layout, he or she can purchase various types of customized printed products, such as blankets, photo books, canvases, etc. A large number of customers purchase a variety of products from the platform every day. The annual revenue of the platform is more than \$42 million, and the number of purchasers exceeds 1 million transactions per year. Given the volume of sales and the customer base, even a small increase in existing customers’ referrals would lead to a large increase in the firm’s revenue. Prior to this research, the platform did not have an online referral program at work.

To set up the experiment, we created four versions of emails and corresponding pages on the company’s website by varying only the framing of the same incentive scheme—namely, the equal-split incentive scheme in which both sender and receiver receive an identical reward (a 70% discount coupon). We randomly draw 100,000 unique customers who have purchased products in the past four months and randomly assign them into one of the four test groups as per Figure 2. Then we target the customers in the different groups with emails that only vary in their CTAs for initiating the referral process. It is critical to note that all other factors, including the equal-split incentive scheme for the sender and the recipient and the message received by the recipient (i.e., what the recipient knows about the program), are kept constant across the four cells of our experiment. In each email, we change the email’s subject, highlight different aspects of the same incentive scheme in the given context, and use different wording in the CTA button as detailed in Figure 3. Once the customers click on the CTA button in the email, they are directed to web pages featuring the consistent framing, where they can send a referral to their friends. To be clear, the same incentive scheme was offered to all participants across groups, and the only difference was in the framing of the referral’s CTA.

The emails were sent out on the same day, at the same time, and only once to each customer in the experiment.<sup>7</sup> The web page featuring the referral program is private and only links to the corresponding email, so the existence of the referral program and any financial incentives were all private and could not be observed by other customers on the website. Each customer was given a week to send referrals to their friends and can send up to five referrals. The referral message sent to the recipients did not contain any information about the sender’s incentives, and the message was identical across treatment groups.

Once a customer sends out a referral, a 70% discount coupon, which was valid for 30 days, was sent to both the sender and the receiver (the sender would obtain the discount coupon regardless of the referral outcome, and this is specified in the email).

The email implementation gives us very strong control over the randomized field experiment. First, by targeting predefined randomly drawn customers, we can ensure that our randomization procedure is valid. Second, by restricting the treatment to the messages in the email and by keeping the web page private and only linked to the corresponding email, we eliminate potential interference across test groups. Finally, by sending the email once, we reduce the possibility of selection bias in repeated trials.

The randomization ensures that the CTA in the email is orthogonal to the customers’ previous behaviors; therefore, any difference in the customers referral decisions and outcomes can be solely and directly attributed to the difference in the received CTA for initiating the referral process. We observe several outcomes from the experiment. First, we look at a binary indicator of whether a sender sent a referral. Second, we look at the total number of referrals sent by senders. Last, we check the number of recipients’ purchases originated by senders referrals. The three outcomes are closely interrelated The first two outcomes characterize the referral behavior, whereas the last outcome characterizes the referral outcome.<sup>8</sup>

The collaborating platform conducted surveys to collect the NPS score (intention of sharing about the company or its products through WOM) of the customer after each purchase. While NPS scores are widely adopted in practice, there have been only a few investigations examining the relationship between NPS scores and actual referral behavior (Keiningham et al. 2007). The data on NPS scores collected prior to the experiment allow us to estimate the main effect as well as the moderating effect of NPS scores on referral behaviors and out comes. In our experiment, 23% of the users had reported their NPS score prior to the experiment. We also include a survey response dummy variable (e.g., NPS\_Survey ) as a control, which would account for situations where NPS score information is missing.

In addition, we measure the recency of a sender’s purchase using WeeksSinceLastPurchase —that is, the number of weeks that have elapsed between the sender i’s last purchase and the day of the experiment. The lower the value of WeeksSinceLastPurchase , the more recent was the sender’s last purchase. As discussed in Hypothesis 2b, we are interested in the moderating effect of the variable—how the effect of a call for a referral campaign on the senders’ referral behavior changes over time after the purchase. Because the timing of the experiment was exogenous to the recency of the senders’ last purchase, the coefficient of the WeeksSinceLastPurchase variable captures this dynamic effect. Despite the anecdotal evidence indicating that users’ responses to the referral campaign changes over time, this dynamic property of response to referral programs has received scant attention in prior research. Last, we also control for senders’ behavior prior to the experiment, including purchase characteristics such as number of past purchases (e.g., NumPurchase<sub>i</sub>), amount of money paid $( \mathrm { e . g . } , S p e n d i n g _ { i } )$ , discount received (e.g., Discount<sub>i</sub>), and daily deal channel used (e.g., DailyDealPurchase<sub>i</sub>) across different product categories k (e.g., Blanket, Photobook, Canvas, and others).<sup>9</sup>

To further test how different customer characteristics moderate the effect of a CTA (Hypotheses 2a and 2b), we interact the moderating variables with the test group indicator and estimate the model using the following specification:

## 4.2. Moderating Effects: The Role of Intrinsic Customer Delight as a Mechanism

Having identified the effectiveness of prosocial framing in driving referrals, we further examine the possibility of the role of customer delight with the product in prosocial framing through a variety of moderating-effect analyses. Specifically, we test whether the effect of prosocial framing varies based on customer characteristics and the timing of the CTA, as articulated in Hypotheses 2a and 2b. As we posit, we should see a higher impact of prosocial framing for customers with high affinity for the product, because customers will care more about their friends’ utility, and in this case, customers will project their own evaluation onto others (Ichheiser 1946, Cronbach 1955). For a similar reason, if intrinsic product delight and the desire to share such delight for the benefit of their friends is one of the drivers of online referrals, we should expect that the effect of prosocial framing on referral behavior may decrease rapidly over time because such referral behavior relies on customers’ intrinsic delight and enthusiasm to share the product, which is known to decay over time after purchase.

We represent customers’ affinity for the product by measuring their degree of repeat purchases (Hoyer 1984) and by their reported NPS score (Reichheld and Thomas 1996), so we examine the moderating effects of repeat purchases and NPS score on the treatment effect of different CTAs. Using archival data on individual purchase history on the platform, we construct a binary indicator, RepeatPurchaser , that indicates whether a sender made more than two purchases in the past<sup>11</sup> and interact the variable with the treatment group indicator as specified in Equation (3). We report the results in column (1) of Table 4. We find that the effect of prosocial framing on referral behavior is significantly higher for customers who made repeat purchases. Using the same specification, we examine the moderating effect of NPS score (Table 4, column (2)) and find that the reported NPS score of a customer positively moderates the effect of prosocial framing on their referral behavior. Customers with high NPS scores are significantly (\~73%) more likely to share when targeted with a prosocial framing CTA. Overall, our results support Hypothesis 2a and align with the theorizing process that a prosocial CTA is more effective for delighted customers because they do not face the intrinsic–extrinsic dilemma.

We further explore the role of timing of the CTAs in moderating the treatment effect. As mentioned earlier, we expect that the effect of prosocial framing on referral behavior may decrease as more time passes between the customers’ last purchase and the CTA. This would be consistent with decay in enthusiasm or delight after purchasing the product. We present the moderating effect of the recency of customers’ purchase in column (3) of Table 4. We find that the effect of a referral campaign on customers’ referral behavior decreases over time (the coefficient of WeeksSinceLastPurchase is negative). More interesting, and consistent with our theoretical prediction, we find that the recency of customers’ purchases positively moderates the effect of prosocial framing on the number of referrals. This result supports Hypothesis 2b and helps further triangulate our main story, which centers on the fact that the prosocial framing activates users with high product affinity, which then may lead to more effective referrals. Practically speaking, our analysis suggests that firms should target high-affinity customers first in their referral campaigns and that the best time to initiate a CTA is immediately after purchase, when customers are most enthusiastic about the product.

## 4.3. Amazon Mechanical Turk Experiment on Referral Motives

To further investigate the underlying mechanisms explaining our main result, we recruited 2,000 “Turkers” and conducted a controlled online experiment on Amazon MTurk to shed light on customers’ motives for sharing the referrals. In the experiment, subjects were randomized into one of four groups: control, egoistic, equity, and prosocial. Each subject in the group saw the same CTA email design we used in the experiment as in Figure 3 and was asked whether he or she would send a referral to his or her friends or family. After that, we asked questions to uncover the motives related to their decisions to (hypothetically) share or not share. Our main interest is in identifying the differences in the motives between the treatment arms. We asked why each subject made his or her chosen decision in the form of a seven-point Likert scale. Figure 5 details the questions that were asked. The survey questions centered around uncovering treatment arm–level differences in the following four motives: (1) the monetary “incentive” the sender receives, (2) the level of “altruism” about others benefit, (3) the perceived “fairness” of the incentive design,<sup>12</sup> and (4) the feeling of “guilt” associated with receiving a benefit. We also asked questions about demographics and referral experience to ensure randomization (balance). Additional screening conditions (e.g., minimum amount of time a user must spend on each question) were embedded into the survey to make sure that subjects were paying sufficient attention.

## 5. Robustness Checks

## 5.1. Experiment 2: Different Referral Promotion

Because the context of our study is an online platform that sells durable goods to its customers, the referral reward in our experiment, a discount coupon, arguably may be more attractive to friends than to senders who already purchased products on the platform. This may raise an issue of generalizability in testing the effect of different framings of the CTA. It can be argued, for instance, that if the referral reward is less attractive to senders, it can undermine the effect of egoistic framing (i.e., highlighting customers’ own benefits, and other types of CTAs may be more effective in different reward settings); therefore, with an eye to generalizability, we conduct an additional experiment to test whether the results will hold when the referral incentive is attractive to all users, including senders who previously purchased products.

The referral reward in the second experiment is a free photo book voucher (with free delivery; \$25.98 value). We believe that this incentive reward is attractive to senders because (1) photo books are the most popular products on the platform and thus are attractive to all users, including those who have previously purchased products on the platform, (2) photo books are the second most repeatedly purchased product by customers on the platform, so they are less affected by previous purchases,<sup>18</sup> (3) the voucher has no expiration date and therefore is an attractive reward to all customers regardless of their past purchase time, and (4) delivery is free and does not require any additional purchase for redemption, so there is no cost to customers to redeem the voucher. Similar to our main experiment design, we chose a new group of 100,000 customers who had made purchases in the past 14 months (instead of 4 months) but were not exposed to the referral program from the firm. Including customers who purchased a long time ago and may be more likely to use the free voucher would allow us to address the “durable good” concern. We implement the new experiment following the same procedures and observe the same outcomes.<sup>19</sup>

## 5.3. Meta-analysis of Industry Practice on Referra Program CTA and Discussions

To further understand the potential impact and generalizability of our findings, we surveyed and identified more than 80 referral programs across different contexts<sup>21</sup> and focused on programs whose monetary incentives were similar to ours (rewards for the both sender and recipients). We manually coded the framing of referral program into egoistic, equitable, and prosocial. We observe large variations in the call-to-action framing used within and across industries. It is not entirely clear that a certain framing would be optimal or dominant in any industry. While equitable framing and egoistic framing appear to be most commonly used (\~59% and \~30% among all referral programs, respectively), prosocial framing is also observed in certain cases (\~11%). Such varied trends indicate that more systematic studies might be needed to understand the effect of CTAs. Further, besides the divergent practice, it is also unclear in the extant literature what the underlying mechanism is that leads to the effectiveness of a particular type of CTA. Our proposed theoretical mechanisms (of an increase in altruistic association and decrease in guilt) are aligned with the empirical evidence across both field experiments and the hypothetical context of MTurk, where no effort such as cocreation has to be expended in consuming the product and no cost is incurred in sharing the product. In this way, our experiments’ findings and mechanism analyses may provide some high-level implications and be generalized to firms across different industries. That said, it is still crucial for future studies to leverage field experiments to examine the effectiveness of CTAs in different contexts. We provide more discussion in the next section.

## 6. Conclusions and Future Research Directions

Firms increasingly rely on digital WOM to increase their customer base and product sales. Given the existing knowledge on the effect of incentive design and message design, it is important to understand the effect of different CTAs on customers’ referral behaviors and outcomes. In this paper, we design and conduct two randomized field experiments that are increasing in their falsification bar (Popper 2005) to conduct stricter and stricter tests of our hypotheses. In each experiment, we target 100,000 customers in different groups with emails that only vary in their CTA phrasing for initiating the referral process. Our design allows us to reduce the risk of various challenges of experimental analysis such as the validity of our randomization procedure, the likelihood of interference, and the possibility of selection bias across groups. We find that the prosocial framing of CTAs is most effective in driving more referrals and producing better outcomes.<sup>22</sup> In addition, we perform an MTurk experiment and conduct several analyses to examine the underlying mechanisms. We find supportive evidence that prosocial framing positively affects both referral decisions and outcomes through multiple mechanisms. Specifically, prosocial framing can improve the referral process by reducing senders guilt from getting referral rewards and by encouraging better targeting by senders, which may lead to higher conversion rates.<sup>23</sup>

Our findings have both theoretical and practical implications that are linked to our hypotheses. As is well known, the underlying premise of referra marketing is to target existing ostensibly delighted customers to spread awareness and influence adoption of a focal product among their friends who are also likely to benefit from adopting the product. In other words, referral programs are designed to accelerate organic WOM exposure using financial incentives. This poses a challenge, in that it mixes an intrinsically motivated process (stemming from the desire to share a customer’s delight with a product or a service) with an extrinsic trigger in the form of a financial incentive. Prior research has shown tha mixing intrinsic and extrinsic motivations can lead to suboptimal outcomes, which, in turn, presents a conceptual dilemma in the design of referral programs. In this paper, we demonstrate how firms can benefit from framing CTAs for referral programs in such a way as to move closer to the original intent of organic, intrinsically motivated WOM marketing and yet at the same time reap the benefits of using a financial incentive to increase referral rates. In particular, given a fixed incentive scheme, ceteris paribus we show the efficacy of a prosocial CTA over some of the more commonly used CTAs observed in practice. We posit, and causally demonstrate via two largescale randomized field experiments and an MTurk experiment, that an intrinsically prosocial element (e.g., emphasizing the benefit of the receiver) in framing the CTA to initiate the referral process is a necessary condition for success. The results of our experiments provide concrete and causal support to the hitherto understudied role of the alignment between a prosocial frame of mind and customer delight in creating social contagion.

Additionally, our study closes the gap in the literature around the optimal design of referral programs. Whereas previous research has investigated the incentive design (Bapna et al. 2014) and the design of the message from the sender to the recipients (Sun et al. 2020), no study has investigated how firms can optimally design CTAs to engage customers in initiating referrals in the first place. We contribute to the literature by examining how firms can optimally frame a CTA, keeping every other aspect—namely, the incentive split and the recipients’ message—constant across treatments. Our results show that prosocial CTAs for referrals lead to higher likelihood of referral and better referral outcomes. In addition, the effect of prosocial framing is significantly higher than the effect of egoistic and equitable framing for a variety of the sharing outcomes; therefore, companies that seek to maximize returns from their referral programs should use prosocial framing in their CTAs for referral. In addition, we find that this prosocial framing is more effective for users who have made repeat purchases, for those who reported a higher NPS score, and for those who just purchased a product, so firms should target loyal customers soon after their purchases in the referral campaigns.

We also acknowledge that there are some limitations to this study. First, our experiment is conducted in a specific context where customer cocreation is needed. Although we believe that our context is important because there is an increasing trend of customer cocreation in e-commerce, future research may use our design in other contexts to test whether our findings are consistent. Specifically, future research may further extend our research framework and conduct a cross-industry study to understand how CTAs should be customized based on industry characteristics. For instance, in the meta-analysis of industry practice, it seems that certain industries with a relative lower purchase frequency (e.g., Uber) and a higher price (e.g., home/pets, education/toys) are more likely to use egoistic framing. More insights on the relationship between industry characteristics and CTA effectiveness would be valuable. Second, because senders will always receive certain financial benefits regardless of the receivers’ actions in the experiment, there is a chance that some senders in the experiment might abuse or “game” the system in order to receive the referral reward. We use additional analysis to confirm that the fraudulent behavior did not influence our findings; however, future studies should verify the consistency of our results under condition in which the recipient needs to convert for the sender to benefit from the reward.

The study also provides several opportunities for further research. A logical next step is for researchers to examine the integration of two different types of widely used CTAs—namely, calls for referral and calls for purchase. Specifically, customers may play two roles in their life cycle, both purchaser and influencer, and firms may engage customers with either a call for referral or a call for purchase at different points in time; however, there is a fundamental trade-off between the two calls because of the limited attention of customers. Similar to a call for purchase, a call for referral is usually sent out to customers in the form of digital marketing communication—for example, through email and mobile messages. Given the limited bandwidth of marketing communication, it is important and interesting to compare calls for referral with calls for purchase, especially promotional advertising, which is the dominant form of marketing communication from a firm. Based on the results from our experiment, we identify three types of differences between the two forms of communication. First, for durable goods such as the printed products we studied in this paper, calls for purchase are, in general, not effective immediately after purchase; however, our results provide evidence that this may be the best time to engage customers with referral marketing. Second, promotional emails are more effective when targeted to less loyal customers using a call for purchase, although in calls for referral, a promotional email highlighting the reward to a friend is most effective for loyal customers because of high expectation of their friends’ utility of the promotion or product. Finally, while calls for purchase are always highlighting customers’ own benefits, the optimal design for calls for referral should highlight the benefits for their friends. Interestingly, all three differences— the timing, the targeting, and the design—make a prosocial CTA for referral complementary with the call for purchase in marketing communication (Godinho de Matos et al. 2018). With that, firms can optimally combine them to form an integrated communication strategy and engage customers throughout their life cycle. Although both marketing communications are widely used in practice, there have been no empirical tests comparing the performance of these two CTAs when they are used together. We expect future studies to deepen our understanding by providing guidelines on how firms can optimally integrate these two CTAs.

Understanding which type of call for referral is most effective in creating social contagion through WOM and why is a crucial step in developing optimal referral marketing strategies. We believe that our study, through a large-scale randomized field experiment, can contribute to the rich literature in information systems and marketing on WOM and social contagion by providing insights on the effect and mechanisms of different CTAs for online referrals.
