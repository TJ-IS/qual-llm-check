# From Links to Likes: Evidence From a Social Commerce Platform

Xiaoye Cheng,<sup>1</sup> Hillol Bala,<sup>2</sup> Xue (Jane) Tan<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Dept. of Accounting and Management Information Systems, University of Delaware, USA, xycheng@udel.edu <sup>2</sup>Dept. of Operations & Decision Technologies, Indiana University, USA, hbala@iu.edu <sup>3</sup>Dept. of Information Technology and Operations Management, Southern Methodist University, USA, janetan@mail.smu.edu

## Abstract

Product-related content created by social media influencers plays a crucial role in shaping consumer purchase decisions. Social media endorsements, as reflected in “likes,” serve as a key indicator of an influencer’s success and provide brands and platforms with valuable insights into how social media users perceive and engage with product-related content. This study theorizes and examines how and why incorporating a product link into influencer-generated content affects individuals’ social media endorsement behavior. Our empirical analysis, based on 216,973 product-related posts from 6,188 influencers on a social media platform, suggests that (1) the inclusion of a product link is associated with more social media endorsement, (2) this effect is more pronounced for organic (non-sponsored) content than for sponsored content, and (3) product links are more effective in increasing social media endorsements for influencers who generate content more intensively (i.e., frequently). To examine the underlying mechanisms driving these effects, we conducted a controlled experiment simulating our research context (N = 428). The findings suggest that the positive effect of product links on social media endorsement arises from enhancing perceptions of helpfulness while mitigating perceptions of ulterior motives, particularly in organic content. This research makes significant theoretical contributions to the literature on user-generated content, user engagement, and influencer marketing, offering actionable guidance for influencers, brands, and social media platforms.

Keywords: Social Media, Influencer Marketing, Product Link, Sponsored Content, Social Media Endorsements

## 1 Introduction

Social media platforms have become a crucial channel for users to access product-related information. A recent report revealed that 85% of Generation Z respondents acknowledge the influence of social media on their purchasing decisions (Walk-Morris, 2023). This trend presents lucrative opportunities for social media influencers (i.e., content generators) to create productrelated content that can shape the attitudes and behaviors of potential consumers (Casaló et al., 2020; Lou &

Yuan, 2019). Users interact with product-related content through endorsements, where individuals express recognition and approval of social media content through actions such as clicking the “like” button (Li, 2018). Operationalized by metrics such as the number of likes on platforms like Facebook and X (formerly Twitter) (Lee et al., 2018; Qiu & Kumar, 2017), social media endorsement goes beyond mere appreciation of content; it also provides valuable insights into user preferences for products, brands, and places (Yadav et al., 2013).

As a key indicator of user reactions to product-related content, social media endorsement has significant commercial implications for influencers, firms, and social media platforms. Influencers perceive endorsements not only as symbolic rewards but also as quantitative metrics (e.g., the number of likes) that reflect content popularity and help assess their potential for financial gain (Feng et al., 2021; Kay et al., 2020). For firms, social media endorsement has been shown to increase product awareness (Aral & Walker, 2011) and drive product sales (Brettel et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2018). Firms also leverage it as a primary metric to identify key influencers (Sammis, 2015; Wong, 2014) and evaluate the effectiveness of social media marketing strategies (Jaakonmäki et al., 2017; Peters et al., 2013). Moreover, social media platforms rely on endorsements to foster high-quality content and sustain user engagement, both of which are essential for attracting advertisers (Hallinan & Brubaker, 2021).

Despite its significant importance, the determinants of social media endorsement behavior have not been studied extensively in the literature (Hong et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2018). This paper examines the impact of a key content feature—product links—on social media endorsement. As a common practice, influencers often incorporate links to relevant products into their content. A product link is a hyperlink that directs users to a product landing page containing detailed product information and a call-to-action button, which can drive the purchase conversion (Culnan et al., 2010). In the realm of social media marketing, exposing and directing prospective customers to these product landing pages is one of the most critical marketing tasks (Page et al., 2012). Furthermore, as a ubiquitous element of influencer content, product links play a crucial role in contemporary marketing strategies by offering a direct pathway for consumers to explore and engage with recommended products. Given their widespread adoption, product links hold significant practical relevance. Therefore, it is imperative to understand how and why social media users endorse content featuring product links on social media platforms.

Links in social media content are generally viewed as helpful and contribute to content richness (Kim & Song, 2018). For example, research has shown that tweets that contain links were more likely to be shared on Twitter (now X) (Suh et al., 2010), and answers with links were perceived as higher quality in Yahoo Answers (Agichtein et al., 2008). A product link provides helpful product information to social media users and could thus positively influence their endorsement behaviors. However, when an influencer shares a product experience and includes a product link, it may raise suspicion among users, leading them to attribute monetary motives to the influencer’s content creation. This could lead to the perception that the influencer is attempting to persuade their followers to purchase the product. Given the substantial business implications, it is crucial to investigate the impact of integrating product links on social media endorsement. Hence, the primary objective of this study is to examine the effect of incorporating product links into influencers’ content on social media endorsement, as measured by the number of likes on the content.

Furthermore, we investigate whether the effect of product links varies between sponsored content and organic content. Sponsored content refers to product or brand-related content created by influencers who are guided and compensated by marketers, as opposed to unpaid organic content (Hwang & Jeong, 2016; Kim & Song, 2018). Sponsored content is often manifested through labels, tags, or icons (Lee & Kim, 2020). For example, sponsored posts are labeled “Paid partnership” on Facebook and Instagram. Due to the clear indication of the commercial nature of social media content, sponsored content tends to be less endorsed by people (Boerman et al., 2012; Evans et al., 2017). While the direct effects of sponsored content have been examined (e.g., Boerman et al., 2012; Eisend et al., 2020; Pfeuffer & Huh, 2020), the difference in the effects of product links in sponsored versus organic content remains an underexplored area in the literature. Understanding this dynamic is essential for influencers, as it speaks to content creation strategies to mitigate the potential negative perceptions associated with sponsored content. Therefore, our second objective is to examine how product sponsorship moderates the relationship between product links and social media endorsement, as reflected in the number of likes on the content.

Moreover, we assess the heterogeneous impact of incorporating product links by examining the moderating role of influencer activeness, a key factor that social media platforms use to segment users and that marketers rely on to select influencers. Active influencers often develop a more intimate relationship with their audiences, resulting in increased trust and recognition of their content. This deeper connection allows influencers to strategically adjust their content creation activities, potentially driving higher social media endorsement. Therefore, understanding how influencers’ level of activeness influences the effectiveness of using product links is both essential and meaningful. Our third objective is to investigate how influencer activeness moderates the effect of product links on users’ endorsement, as reflected in the number of likes on the content.

We conducted two studies to address our research objectives. In Study 1, we performed an empirical analysis using data from a popular Chinese social media platform, RedNote (Xiaohongshu), which features product-related user-generated content. We identified 216,973 product-related posts generated by 6,188 influencers in 2018. To account for the potentially endogenous decision of integrating a product link and to enhance identification, we applied an instrumental variable approach and incorporated individual-level fixed effects into the models. Additionally, we conducted multiple tests to assess the robustness of our findings. In Study 2, we conducted a controlled online experiment with 428 subjects that simulated the context of Study 1. Furthermore, we conducted a moderated mediation analysis to identify the underlying mechanisms driving the impact of product links on social media endorsement, with a focus on the moderating role of sponsored content.

We offer three key findings. First, incorporating a product link into social media content leads to more social media endorsements (4.3% more likes in Study 1). The experiment results from Study 2 further reveal that posts containing a product link are perceived as more helpful and less driven by an influencer’s ulterior motives, with perceived helpfulness positively predicting endorsement and perceived ulterior motives negatively predicting endorsement. Second, the positive impact of integrating a product link is more pronounced for organic content than for sponsored content. Results from Study 2 suggest that influencers are perceived as having stronger ulterior motives when inserting product links into sponsored content (vs. organic content), resulting in fewer social media endorsements. Finally, integrating a product link has an enhanced positive effect on the endorsement of content created by influencers who are more active in content creation.

This research makes three key contributions to the literature. First, we build upon and extend existing work on social media endorsement—an important indicator of content quality that influences the behaviors of social media users. Second, we contribute to the literature on user-generated content by focusing on product-related content from influencers rather than brands (Lee et al., 2018) and by empirically examining the incorporation of product links, a unique type of content feature that has received scant research attention despite its importance. Furthermore, our research contributes to the literature on content strategies by identifying the different effects of product links in sponsored vs. organic content. Finally, this study speaks to the rich stream of work on online communities by demonstrating the positive return of active content generation. Our findings offer practical insights for influencers, social media platforms, and brands. Influencers can leverage product links to lower audience search costs and enhance the endorsement of their content. The efficacy of this content strategy is diminished when they partner with brands. Moreover, influencers should actively create content to build an intimate relationship with the audience, thereby amplifying the positive impact of product links. Social media platforms can improve system design to encourage the active use of product links in content generation, while brands can refine their marketing strategies to encourage influencers to create organic content that incorporates product links by highlighting the value of fostering relationships with the audience rather than focusing solely on monetary gains.

## 2 Hypothesis Development

We develop hypotheses based on the heuristicsystematic model (HSM) and the literature on user engagement motivation and persuasion knowledge. HSM posits that people process persuasion information through two routes: the systematic route and the heuristic route (Chaiken, 1980). This is similar to another dual-process theory of cognition, the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986), both of which are widely used to understand information processing. While both HSM and ELM theorize two cognitive processes that shape people’s attitudes toward persuasive information, the two processes differ in the amount of cognitive effort needed in information processing. The systematic route needs more intensive cognitive effort and focuses on the content and topic of information, such as content quality, to make judgments, while the heuristic route requires less intensive cognitive effort and focuses on the various heuristics associated with the information, such as source credibility, to make decisions (Chaiken et al. 1999; Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2012; Cacioppo et al., 1986).

The key difference between the two theories is that ELM views the two routes as distinct pathways for information processing, whereas HSM suggests a continuum with each route at one end (Carpenter, 2015). Given the complex nature of social media content, which often combines multiple messages and cues (Winograd, 1986), we consider that social media users engage in both systematic and heuristic processes to varying extents when assessing product links embedded within content. Under systematic processing, product links could shape audience perceptions regarding content quality. Under heuristic processing, product links could influence perceptions regarding the influencer’s underlying motivations. These perceptions, in turn, can influence social media endorsement behaviors. Because HSM better accommodates the dynamic and overlapping nature of how users process information on social media, we use it as our theoretical foundation to hypothesize the impact of product links on endorsement, with a particular focus on the content and influencer aspects.

Furthermore, we explore how sponsored content and influencer activeness moderate the influence of product links on social media endorsement. Drawing on insights from HSM, we identify these moderators—one representing a post feature and the other an influencer characteristic—as key factors shaping how individuals cognitively process product links in social media content through the model’s dual-processing routes. As highlighted earlier, exploring the impact of product links, a ubiquitous feature of product-related social media content widely adopted by influencers, is imperative. Therefore, understanding how this impact varies across different contexts, at the post and influencer levels, is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of social media endorsement behaviors.

Sponsored content and influencer activeness hold theoretical and practical significance in social media. Sponsored content serves as a pivotal conduit between brands and potential customers, as well as a primary avenue for influencers to generate financial gain. Although the direct effect of sponsored content has been extensively studied in the literature (e.g., Boerman et al., 2012; Eisend et al., 2020; Hwang & Jeong, 2016; Pfeuffer & Huh, 2020), research on its moderating role remains scarce. One exception is the work from Giuffredi-Kähr et al. (2022), which examines the moderating effect of sponsorship disclosure on the relationships between influencer type, brand evaluation, and influencer likability. However, how sponsored content moderates the impact of product links on endorsement remains an important yet underexplored area in the literature.

Influencer activeness is a key indicator of an influencer’s dedication, engagement, and connection with their audience. It represents a posting strategy that influencers can actively control and adapt to enhance user interaction. Similar concepts have been studied. For instance, Leung et al. (2022) found that influencer activity, measured as the frequency of influencer posting, exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with influencer marketing effectiveness, which is measured by the number of reposts. Gross and von Wangenheim (2022) demonstrated that the number of followers weakens the relationship between social media engagement with sponsored posts compared to nonsponsored posts. Moreover, Jun and Yi (2020) found that interactivity, characterized by two-way communications between influencers and social media users, is positively associated with perceptions of influencer authenticity, emotional attachment toward the influencer, and brand loyalty. However, the role of influencer activeness in moderating the effect of product links on social media endorsement remains unclear in the current literature.

Therefore, we examine the moderating effects of sponsored content and influencer activeness on the relationship between product links and social media endorsements. Figure 1 shows our research model.

## 2.1 Product Links and Endorsements

Influencers often incorporate product links into their content to funnel social media users directly to the recommended products. <sup>1</sup> Product links are typically displayed in the format of explicit URLs or implicit clickable hyperlinks embedded in text, buttons, or pictures, allowing users to access product landing pages with a single click. For instance, Instagram enables users to add product tags, which contain product descriptions, prices, and links, to picture-based content in the story mode (Pytlak, 2018). Previous studies have explored several types of URLs in social media content and their effects on user behaviors, including URLs linked to general web pages (Lee et al., 2018; Suh et al., 2010), spam URL detection (Cao & Caverlee, 2015), and the effectiveness of affiliate URL disclosures (Mathur et al., 2018). However, a gap remains in understanding the specific impact of product links that directly connect users to product landing pages. This study aims to fill this gap and extends prior work by examining product links—a distinct category of URLs—presented in both simple and complex formats. Furthermore, we examine two potential moderating factors that influence the impact of product links on social media endorsement. We summarize the relevant literature in Appendix Table A1.

Building on HSM, we posit that product links embedded in social media content can be processed through both the systematic and heuristic routes by the audience. Systematic processing involves a thoughtful and deliberate evaluation of content based on its substantive qualities, while heuristic processing relies on cognitive shortcuts, such as perceived authenticity or trustworthiness. We argue that product links operate through both routes. By providing detailed productrelated information, product links facilitate a more thorough evaluation of content, which aligns with the systematic processing route in HSM. Simultaneously, by signaling the influencer’s effort, product links serve as a simple cue that allows audiences to quickly assess content, which corresponds to the heuristic route.

When evaluating social media content through the systematic route, the audience considers how incorporating product links affects content quality. Due to the easy access to comprehensive product information, product links are likely to drive a higher level of social media endorsement. Product links serve as a gateway for social media users to access details related to product quality, including product dimensions, materials, customer reviews, and sales rank. Research on user engagement indicates that obtaining useful information is a significant motivation for users to actively engage on social media, such as through liking, commenting, and sharing content (Azar et al., 2016; Barger et al., 2016; Khan, 2017; Liang et al., 2011; Park et al., 2009). As a result, by fulfilling the audience’s need for product information, product links can effectively elevate social media endorsement. Specifically, product links can help reduce search costs and information asymmetry—two major hurdles in the purchasing process (Park & Kim, 2003). This is likely to elicit positive attitudes and reactions from the audience. As evidence, product tags in user-generated content can improve consumers’ search experience in social commerce (Yi et al., 2017); tweets with URLs are retweeted more (Suh et al., 2010); and online answers with URLs are considered to be of higher quality (Agichtein et al., 2008).

Moreover, reducing the cost of searching for products can significantly improve customer buying experience (Hoque & Lohse, 1999; Wu et al., 2004). This aligns with current social media marketing practices, which promote campaigns that feature product links to lower search costs, in contrast to traditional advertisements that require consumers to conduct additional searches (Hoffman & Fodor, 2010). Furthermore, the attribution literature suggests that TV or radio advertisements, which do not include product links, typically only address the product awareness stage, while social media advertisements with product links are positioned closer to the product purchase stage (Kannan et al., 2016). Therefore, social media users are more likely to endorse content with product links because of their seamless access to detailed and beneficial product information.

When evaluating social media content through the heuristic route, audiences often consider the underlying motivations behind influencers’ use of product links. These links can trigger persuasion knowledge—a set of beliefs and conjectures about the motivations, strategies, tactics, and coping methods used in persuasion (Friestad & Wright, 1994)—leading users to question the influencer’s intent. Once this persuasive intent is recognized, social media users draw on their accumulated persuasion knowledge to infer whether an influencer’s recommendation is driven by hidden, selfserving motives, such as monetary gain (Campbell & Kirmani, 2000). However, on social media platforms where product-related content is prevalent, such suspicions tend to be minimal. Mandatory sponsorship disclosures, combined with the fact that users often visit these platforms to seek genuine product experiences and relevant information, generally foster a more positive attitude toward influencer recommendations.

Furthermore, an influencer’s dedication to finding and inserting a product link into their content may be interpreted as a gesture of goodwill aimed at helping users reduce search costs. This effort can further alleviate doubts about an influencer’s ulterior motives, leading to a more favorable evaluation of their content. Studies have shown that persuasion messages featuring customer-oriented or valuable information—such as a product’s ranking from a certificated third party—are perceived as credible, thereby positively influencing the evaluation of the persuading agent (Isaac & Grayson, 2017, 2020). Similarly, including product links in social media content can be positively interpreted as a credible and audience-focused strategy, reinforcing trust and leading to greater endorsement.

To illustrate how product links operate through both the systematic and heuristic routes of HSM, consider a social media influencer sharing their experience with a new kitchen gadget. Including a product link to a trusted retailer allows the audience to quickly access detailed product information, which supports the systematic route by enabling a thorough evaluation of the gadget’s features, reviews, and specifications. Simultaneously, the inclusion of the link demonstrates the influencer’s commitment to assisting the audience and fostering goodwill, which in turn helps build a closer relationship, aligning with the heuristic route by reducing concerns about the influencer’s ulterior motives and establishing trust. In contrast, omitting the product link decreases the richness of information provided and misses the opportunity to showcase the influencer’s effort and goodwill, potentially resulting in a less favorable evaluation of the recommendation. Therefore, we propose that incorporating product links positively affects social media endorsement by engaging both cognitive processing routes effectively.

H1: Integrating a product link into social media content positively affects social media endorsement.

Building on this theoretical perspective, we next examine how two key contextual factors—sponsored content and influencer activeness—moderate the effect of product links on social media endorsement. In line with HSM, both factors influence the relationship between product links and social media endorsement by shaping the audience’s cognitive effort and depth of processing. HSM suggests that systematic processing requires greater cognitive effort and deliberate evaluation, while heuristic processing involves less effort and relies on simple cues (Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2012; Cacioppo et al., 1986). Sponsorship and influencer activeness reduce the cognitive effort needed to process social media content, leading the audience to rely more on heuristic processing. However, they have opposing effects on the effectiveness of product links. Sponsorship weakens the effectiveness of product links by amplifying skepticism, while influencer activeness strengthens their impact by fostering trust and authenticity.

## 2.2 The Moderating Effect of Sponsored Content

Sponsored content, also known as paid posts, promoted posts, or sponsored reviews, refers to content (e.g., a post, a blog, or a review) published by influencers who receive compensation (e.g., gifts, money, and free products) from brands in exchange for promoting their products through personal accounts (Lu et al., 2014). This contrasts with unpaid organic content, which is often perceived as reflecting influencers’ genuine opinions about a product (Kim & Song, 2018). Government regulations and social media platforms mandate the disclosure of brand partnerships in productrelated content to ensure transparency and protect the truth-in-advertising principle.<sup>2</sup> Despite these disclosure requirements, sponsored content tends to naturally be less endorsed by consumers than organic content because it activates their persuasion knowledge, making them more aware of the commercial intent behind the content and its potentially manipulative nature (Boerman et al., 2012; Evans et al., 2017; Van Reijmersdal et al., 2016).

We propose that the positive impact of integrating product links into social media content on users’ endorsement is more pronounced for organic content compared to sponsored content. As previously discussed, the presence of a product link can help mitigate suspicions of an influencer having an ulterior motive in creating the content. However, this effect may be less pronounced in sponsored content, where the influencer’s ulterior motives—such as earning commission fees—are evident and transparent. Sponsored content is often perceived as guided by marketers with manipulative or commercial intent (Boerman et al., 2014), which may lead social media users to view the influencer’s use of product links as an attempt to persuade them into making a purchase. As a result, even with product links, users are less likely to enjoy the product experience shared by the influencer. Strong commercial intent has been shown to drive distrust. For example, directly informative content about products and brands generated by firms on Facebook has been linked to lower user engagement, possibly because such information is interpreted as direct selling (Lee et al., 2018). In addition, users are more likely to like a brand post on Facebook if its content is not overly commercial, namely not explicitly or directly calling for instantaneous purchases (Swani et al., 2013). Therefore, compared to organic content, the apparent manipulative intent in sponsored content attenuates the positive impact of product links on social media endorsement.

H2: Integrating a product link into social media content has a stronger positive effect on social media endorsement for organic content than sponsored content.

## 2.3 Moderating Effect of Influencer Activeness

Influencer activeness refers to an influencer’s intensity of content generation on social media (Kumar & Mirchandani, 2013), which reflects the influencer’s willingness to interact with social media users (Li et al., 2011; Xiao et al., 2018). We operationalize influencer activeness as the average number of posts generated per day by an influencer. Conceptually, a higher level of activeness corresponds to a greater intensity of virtual communication through self-disclosure and information exchange (Jahng & Littau, 2016).

We propose that influencer activeness positively moderates the impact of integrating product links into social media content on users’ endorsement. Active influencers cultivate lasting, trusting, and positive impressions through their frequent presence and continuous content contributions, thereby fostering deeper relationships with their audience (Bente et al., 2008; Stever & Lawson, 2013). Moreover, frequent content posting creates an avenue for interactive, twoway communication between influencers and their audiences. This interactivity is positively associated with the perceptions of influencer authenticity and emotional attachment toward the influencer (Jun & Yi, 2020). For instance, highly interactive journalists (who frequently generate tweets and respond to others) are perceived as more credible due to their proactive engagement, which fosters stronger connections with their audience (Jahng & Littau, 2016). Similarly, active content creators on YouTube garner higher credibility perceptions because of their sustained interactivity, which enhances their relationship with the audience (Xiao et al., 2018).

In addition, people tend to be more tolerant of advertisements in content from familiar influencers and are more willing to engage with their content (Cao & Belo, 2024), underscoring the critical role of intimate influencer-audience relationships in shaping audience attitudes and behaviors. Active content generation enhances these relationships, improving trust and further mitigating suspicions regarding the influencer’s ulterior motives, which leads to a heightened endorsement of the content with product links. Conversely, less active influencers, who have weaker connections with their audience, may be perceived as more manipulative due to lower trust, resulting in a diminished level of endorsement.

Furthermore, the inclusion of product links in social media content generated by active influencers is likely to be perceived as both benevolent and professional, in contrast to less active influencers. The level of activeness reflects an influencer’s ability to consistently generate high-quality content. As a result, social media users tend to place greater trust in more active influencers and value their use of product links more, thereby increasing the likelihood of social media endorsement. In summary, active influencers could benefit more from the positive impact of integrating a product link into their content compared to their less active counterparts.

H3: Integrating a product link into social media content has a stronger positive effect on social media endorsement for content created by influencers who are more active in content generation.

## 3 Study 1: Evidence from a Social Media Platform

## 3.1 Research Context

We collected data from RedNote (Xiaohongshu), one of the largest Chinese social media platforms, which facilitates sharing product-related content through photos and short videos. RedNote is a mobile application that features social media endorsements, such as likes, shares, and saves. It is often regarded as the Chinese counterpart of Pinterest and Instagram. As of February 2023, RedNote had about 260 million active monthly users.<sup>3</sup> On top of social networking functions, RedNote offers shopping features for retailers to list and sell products, providing a one-stop shopping experience for its users.

We consider all content generators on RedNote to be influencers because their opinions can influence others’ purchasing decisions (Bakshy et al., 2011). Influencers on RedNote often incorporate product links into their posts, as shown in Panel A of Figure 2. RedNote automatically analyzes a product link to retrieve product information, including pricing, pictures, discounts, and sales volume, to display in the post. Users can click on the link to visit the product landing page and make a purchase in the ecommerce portal of RedNote directly (see Panel B of Figure 2). In contrast to the simple format of product links represented by product URLs, we consider product links on RedNote to be in a complex format. Both formats are prevalent in practice and serve the same function of directing the audience to product landing pages. The primary distinction lies in the fact that complex format product links offer product information to the audience upfront and are typically more conspicuous in social media content than simple format product links. Therefore, using complex format product links here aligns with our hypothesis development.

## 3.2 Data and Variables

Our data collection involved two steps. First, we registered a new account on RedNote and completed the preference survey, where we selected all the content categories as areas of interest. We then programmed a crawler that mimicked a real user to record the IDs of content generators, which were individuals rather than brands, for all posts recommended by RedNote to the account between December 1, 2018, and January 10, 2019. During this process, the crawler never clicked on, liked, or replied to any post. In other words, it did not interact with any content generator in any other way to avoid providing preference information to the recommendation algorithm of the platform. Second, through the IDs, we identified the corresponding influencers and captured their profile information and all historical posts. We thus captured sufficient variations in content popularity and avoided focusing solely on popular posts, as not all posts from an influencer are popular. The collected data offers a reliable portrayal of the average user experience on RedNote, as no content preferences were specified or implied during the crawling process. Our data was a representative randomized sample from the platform, with each post included equally in the dataset.

We focused on posts published in 2018 and eliminated posts from extremely inactive influencers who published only one post during the year. We further filtered the sample to retain only posts that explicitly mentioned a brand because these posts were likely to be productrelated posts. Specifically, we first identified 4,548 brands from sponsored posts and posts with at least one product link in our sample. Then we retrieved the Chinese and English names of these brands as brand keywords and removed posts that did not include any brand keywords. There were 216,973 product-related posts from 6,188 influencers in the final sample.

Table 1 summarizes all variables. The outcome of interest—social media endorsement—is often manifested as users’ digital reactions in the form of likes, shares, or other forms of positive feedback. Social media users utilize endorsement to express their recognition of social media content publicly (Li, 2018). Social media endorsement also represents an influencer’s online reputation, which is a key motivation for content generation (Tang et al., 2012). In addition, it acts as an important index for firms to evaluate the commercial value of influencers (Gräve & Greff, 2018; Jaakonmäki et al., 2017). Previous research has typically used likes (the act of clicking on the like button) to operationalize endorsement on platforms such as Twitter and Facebook (e.g., Lee et al., 2018; Li, 2018; Li & Wu, 2018; Qiu & Kumar, 2017). Following these studies, we measure social media endorsement by the number of likes a post received (i.e., Likes).

The key independent variable, ProductLink, is a binary variable that indicates whether a post includes any product links. We also examine the moderating effects of Sponsored and InfluencerActiveness. Sponsored is a binary variable that indicates whether a post was sponsored by a brand. A sponsored post is indicated by a label at the bottom of the post showing the specific brand involved (see Panel A of Figure 2). InfluencerActiveness indicates an influencer’s posting frequency before a focal post. It is calculated as the ratio of the total number of posts before the focal post to the duration (in days) of the influencer’s membership until the focal post was published.

We controlled for post-level and influencer-level variables to account for the heterogeneities in posts and influencers, as detailed in Table 1. Because some topics were more attractive and thus received more likes than others, we controlled for topic heterogeneity in posts by applying a latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) approach to categorize post topics based on the titles, descriptions, and tags of posts. We tested different numbers of categories (from 10 to 20) and adopted the 14 topic categories that produced the most meaningful keywords (see Appendix Table B1). <sup>4</sup> Among these categories, skin care, makeup, fashion, travel, and food were popular topics discussed on RedNote (see Appendix Figure B1). Moreover, we included time-related variables to account for potential seasonal effects and the possibility that posts staying on the platform for a longer time might receive more likes. Table 2 presents the summary statistics for all variables at the original scale, and Appendix Table B2 shows their correlations.

## 3.3 Model and Results

## 3.3.1 Identification

A key challenge to constructing an econometric model is the possible endogeneity issue of product links, which may lead to biased estimations. The relationship between an endogenous regressor and the outcome may be spurious rather than causal if unobservable factors that correlate with the endogenous regressor are the actual drivers of the outcome (Antonakis et al., 2010; Nichols, 2007). The endogeneity of ProductLink may result from the unobserved attributes of influencers: Proficient influencers who generate high-quality content that attracts social media endorsement are likely to include product links to enrich content and reduce search costs for the audience. Unobservable content features of posts may also affect both an influencer’s decision to include a product link and social media endorsement from the audience.

To handle these potential endogeneity issues, we leveraged the panel data structure to devise a fixed-effects model that accounts for individual-level persistent unobservable factors like influencers’ benevolence, dedication, and capability to create high-quality content. We further constructed an instrumental variable (IV) for ProductLink to account for the unobservable factors that change over time and incorporated the IV with a control function approach. A valid IV should be correlated with the endogenous variable but not the error terms—it affects the outcome only through its impacts on the endogenous variable (Agarwal et al., 2009; Wooldridge, 2010).

We constructed BrandProductLink as the instrument for ProductLink, measured as the total number of posts with at least one product link mentioning the same brand and generated within one week before a focal post. This instrumental variable satisfies the two criteria for a valid IV. First, it should directly affect an influencer’s decision to adopt product links. Influencers are likely to incorporate product links into their content when observing peer influencers doing so because they actively learn content generation strategies from peers (Indeed, 2025). Second, the number of posts from peers that mention the same brand earlier is unlikely to directly affect the evaluation and endorsement of a focal post because of time and influencer segregations. As a result, the exclusion restriction is not violated.

Instrumental variables with a similar rationale have been widely applied. For example, the average level of health IT in peer hospital systems has been used as the instrument for the health IT level in a focal hospital (Bardhan et al., 2015) based on the idea that hospitals monitor and learn about health IT applications from their peers due to peer pressure. Similarly, the BLP-type model (e.g., Berry et al., 1995; Berry, 1994) has been extensively adopted in empirical studies to estimate demand, instrumenting the price of a focal product with the aggregate-level characteristics of competing products in the market. The intuition relies on oligopoly price models and substitution effects among similar products. For instance, a restaurant’s discount is instrumented with the sum of exogenous characteristics of competitive deals in the same restaurant category and city, reflecting the substitution effect among similar restaurants for customers (Cao et al., 2018).

We compared our IV with the potentially endogenous variable (see Appendix Figure B2). The mean of Log(BrandProductLink) for posts with at least one product link was higher than that for posts without any product link. Further, we tested the validity of our IV. The Cragg-Donald Wald F-statistic exceeded the critical threshold proposed by Stock and Yogo (2005), reducing concerns about weak instruments (Angrist & Pischke, 2009). In addition, the under-identification test and the Sargan-Hansen test for over-identification both confirmed that our IV is just identified.

## 3.3.2 Control Function Model

We employed a two-stage control function approach with the IV introduced above to account for possible endogeneity issues. The control function approach is an estimation strategy, similar to two-stage least squares (2SLS), with the added flexibility to accommodate nonlinear models (Wooldridge, 2015). In contrast to 2SLS, which substitutes the endogenous explanatory variable in the second stage by its prediction from the first stage, the control function approach keeps the endogenous explanatory variable in the second stage and adds its residual predicted from the first stage as an extra regressor (Imbens & Wooldridge, 2007; Wooldridge, 2010). It has been widely used to address endogeneity issues in social science studies (e.g., Terza et al., 2008), especially for nonlinear models (e.g., Saboo et al., 2016).

There are two stages in the control function model. In Stage 1, we performed a logistic regression by regressing the binary endogenous variable (i.e., ProductLink) on the IV and all control variables. We used i to denote an influencer and j to denote a post. The model is specified in Equation (1).

## 3.3.3 Robustness Checks

Alternative IVs: We employed several alternative instrumental variables to test the robustness of our main results in Study 1. The first alternative IV was constructed using a shorter time interval (one day rather than one week) because influencers may be affected in a shorter time when considering the use of product links in post design. The second alternative IV considers the ratio (instead of the number) of posts with product links of the same brand. Like the number-based IV, this ratiobased IV can help reduce endogeneity concerns; more importantly, it can account for the potential influences of brand popularity, as popular brands are mentioned more frequently. The third instrumental variable approach utilized the lagged independent variable as the instrument, a method that has been widely adopted in research. Using a product link in a previous post (the lag variable of ProductLink) is likely to be highly correlated with an influencer’s decision to insert a product link into a focal post. However, it should not directly influence the endorsement of the focal post. We constructed two instruments using the first lag and third lag of ProductLink and tested them independently. As shown in Table 5, our main results were robust with regard to the different constructions of instrument variables.

Additional content controls: Although we accounted for topic heterogeneity in our model, other content features of posts may have influenced likes in a disproportional way, which could have biased our results. To mitigate such concerns, we incorporated several additional variables into the second-stage model to account for the granular content characteristics of posts. These variables include the linguistic, stylistic, and inferred psychological features of posts, which were extracted using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) software (Boyd et al., 2022). The LIWC-based content features were measured as the percentage of a specific category of words appearing in a post. As presented in Table 6, our main findings remain robust when controlling for these LIWC content characteristics of posts.

Additionally, we constructed two sets of content feature variables and incorporated them into our model as additional controls. First, certain words in post descriptions related to extrinsic motivations for endorsement may have affected the number of likes. We constructed a set of variables representing the number of such special words (i.e., “like,” “save,” “comment,” “share,” “recommend,” and “gift”) in a post, as these terms can enhance the attractiveness of the post and potentially increase endorsements. Second, we constructed a set of variables that indicate the use of different types of “stickers” (customized sticker, user sticker, product sticker, brand sticker, and retail sticker) in a post. Stickers are tags attached to a post to deliver the content more richly, potentially affecting user likes. We added these two sets of variables to the second stage of the control function model and reconducted the data analysis. The estimation results (see Table 6) confirmed the robustness of our main results.

Alternative measure of influencer activeness: In the main analysis, we measured an influencer’s activeness as posting frequency, which reflects the average activity level of an influencer before a focal post and captures the general engagement an influencer maintains over time. However, to better capture an influencer’s recent activeness status, we constructed an alternative measure specifying the number of posts published by the influencer during the 30 days immediately preceding a focal post. This new measure provided a more timesensitive indicator of an influencer’s current activeness. We then reran the data analysis using the control function approach. The results (see Table 6) were highly consistent with our main findings, confirming the robustness of this alternative measure in capturing the influencer’s recent activeness.

Subsamples: Influencers with a larger following may invest more effort in content creation, making them more inclined to incorporate product links compared to those with fewer followers. To mitigate the potential bias introduced by influencers with smaller followings, we created two subsamples: influencers with more than 2,000 followers and influencers with more than 5,000 followers at the time of data collection in January 2019. Similarly, more active influencers may be more likely to use product links than less active ones. To capture this, we constructed two subsamples: influencers who had posted more than 50 times and influencers who had posted more than 100 times in 2018. These approaches helped reduce concerns about the differences in influencers’ likelihood of incorporating product links. We then reran the Stage 2 analysis using these subsamples (see Table 7); the results were highly consistent with our main findings.

Alternative outcomes: Social media endorsement is one type of user engagement that can also be measured by the number of shares, comments, and saves. Shares refer to the number of times a post is shared with others. Comments refer to the number of comments on a post generated by social media users. Saves identify the number of times a post is saved by users, allowing them to easily revisit the post later. Among the common measurements for user engagement, likes represent endorsement that definitively reflects positive feedback from social media users. Shares and saves are also indicators of high content quality and social media endorsement, but they focus more on the user’s selfinterest. Comments reflect users’ interest in a social post but can be positive, neutral, or negative. We checked the robustness of our findings by using shares, comments, and saves as alternative dependent variables. The results for shares and saves (see Table 8) are consistent with our main findings. For comments, we found that Sponsored had a positive and significant sign, indicating that there were more heated discussions regarding sponsored content (vs. organic content), all else being equal. However, the moderating effect of Sponsored was still negative, aligning with our main results. Overall, our main findings were not limited to likes and can be extended to other social media endorsement measures.

## 4 Study 2: Controlled Experiment

## 4.1 Experiment Design

Although Study 1 provided empirical support for our hypotheses, it did not elucidate the underlying mechanisms behind these findings. Furthermore, despite our efforts to mitigate endogeneity concerns in the empirical analyses for Study 1, it was challenging to eliminate them entirely. We therefore conducted an experiment to examine how integrating product links into social media content affects endorsement (the underlying mechanism driving this relationship) and whether sponsored content moderates this effect. We designed a 2×2 between-subject experiment (product link vs. no product link; sponsored content vs. organic content) featuring four posts that stimulated a realistic Instagram context (see Table 9). Instead of displaying detailed product information with a link in a panel, as in RedNote, we adopted a simpler but widely used format, the product URL. While RedNote supports a complex product link format, most other platforms, especially those without an integrated e-commerce portal, only allow for the use of product URLs.

We recruited 440 participants from the United States on Prolific, a widely used participant recruitment platform. Participants were first asked to confirm that they were at least 18 years old and then were asked to provide voluntary consent to participate in the experiment.

Following this, they were briefed on the experiment’s context and randomly assigned to view one of the four posts in Table 9. Since the product URL in the post was not clickable, we included a statement indicating its function on top of the post (i.e., “The product link embedded in the post can direct the viewers of the post to the purchase webpage of the toothpaste”). We chose to use a single rather than multiple product links in the experiment for simplicity. By adopting this straightforward design of product links in a simplified URL format, we were able to isolate the impact of a single product link and examine its direct impact in a controlled setting. This allowed us to apply a more conservative means of examining the effect of product links on endorsement, thereby also allowing us to test the generalizability of the findings from Study 1 to a simple format of product links.

Participants were instructed to read the post carefully and answer two attendance check questions. Eight responses that did not answer the check questions correctly were excluded from our sample. Additionally, responses that took less than 3 minutes or more than 15 minutes were also eliminated, resulting in a final sample size of 428 participants. Following the attendance check questions, participants were again presented with the same post to refresh their memory. Then, they were asked to indicate the extent to which they liked the post based on a 7-point Likert scale (the measurement of the Like construct is specified in Table 10), followed by two questions that checked the manipulations of including a product link and sponsorship disclosure. After this, they were asked to report their perceptions of post helpfulness and the ulterior motive of the influencer generating the post using a 7-point scale (the measures for Helpfulness and Ulterior Motive are shown in Table 10).

Subsequently, participants were required to indicate whether they had heard of the toothpaste brand and how frequently they used social media. Almost all participants (97.9%) declared that they had not heard of the toothpaste brand. Most participants used social media daily (70.33%) or several times a week (17.52%). Finally, demographic questions regarding the participants’ gender, age, race, and education were collected. In our final sample, female participants accounted for 49.53% of the participants, while male participants comprised approximately 47.66%. The remaining 2.81% of participants chose not to disclose their gender. The complete list of experiment questions is reported in Appendix Table C1.

## 4.2 Experiment Results

We first verified the manipulations related to including a product link and sponsorship disclosure. Participants were asked whether the post they viewed included a product link and whether the brand sponsoring it was disclosed. Approximately 90.2% of participants correctly identified whether the post that they viewed contained a product link, while about 93.2% of participants correctly recognized whether the post that they viewed included disclosed sponsorship. These findings indicated the effectiveness of our manipulations.

We then conducted an ANOVA test to examine the main effect of Product Link on Like (the extent to which a participant likes the post viewed) and the moderating effect of Sponsored. Results reveal that the overall effect of Product Link on Like was not significant (M = 4.19 vs. 4.42, F(1, 424) = 3.01, p = 0.083), but the interaction term of Product Link and Sponsored was significant (F(1, 424) = 4.05, p = 0.04). The nonsignificant main effect of Product Link on Like can be explained by two key reasons. First, we utilized a simple URL format for the product link in the experiment, which might not have been prominent enough for participants to recognize its functionality. Second, our experiment employed a 2×2 betweensubjects design in which participants viewed only a single post. The lack of direct comparison between posts with and without a product link may have diminished the perceived helpfulness of the link, contributing to the nonsignificant main effect. The pairwise comparisons (see Figure 3) indicated that the means of Like were significantly different across posts without and with a product link for organic posts (M = 4.23 vs. 4.73, t = -2.59, p = 0.01), but the difference did not reach significance for sponsored posts (M = 4.15 vs. 4.11, t = 0.2, p = 0.842). This demonstrates that the impact of product links was attenuated in sponsored content compared to organic content, which was consistent with our empirical results in Study 1.

We further explored the underlying mechanisms for the impact of Product Link on Like as well as the moderating impact of Sponsored. As illustrated earlier, the inclusion of product links can influence social media endorsement by affecting the evaluation of content quality and the influencer’s motivations. Specifically, product links can provide social media users with useful product information and alleviate the perceived ulterior motives of the influencer. We thus examined two potential mediators: Helpfulness, which relates to the informational value of the content itself, and Ulterior Motive, which pertains to the perceived intent of the information sender (i.e., the influencer). These aspects correspond to the two routes in HSM—systematic and heuristic—that individuals rely on when processing information. We thus tested these two underlying mechanisms.

We evaluated the validity and reliability of the measures through a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The results indicate that most factor loadings were larger than 0.9 (see Table 10), confirming the convergent validity of the constructs (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). One exception was the first item for Ulterior Motive, which asked participants to describe how they perceived the influencer’s motivation in promoting the toothpaste recommended in the post they viewed. We kept this item in the measurement model to ensure that the corresponding construct had enough items.

Table 11 presents the descriptive statistics of these constructs and their correlations. The reliability of the measurements was examined by Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Both reliability scores exceeded the suggested cutoff of 0.7, indicating sufficient reliability (Nunnally, 1978). The square root of each construct’s average variance extracted (AVE) in the major diagonal of the correlation matrix was much greater than the construct’s correlations with other constructs, demonstrating sufficient discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

We applied the SPSS PROCESS macro to assess the significance of the moderated mediation effect based on bias-corrected bootstrap 95% confidence intervals derived from 5,000 bootstrap samples (Hayes, 2017). Specifically, we used Product Link as the independent variable, Like as the dependent variable, Helpfulness and Ulterior Motive as two mediators, and Sponsored as the moderator in the model. We also included several demographic variables, including age, gender, education, and frequency of social media use, as control variables in our model. The regression results are presented in Table 12.

The direct effect of Product Link on Like was not significant (b = -0.169, p > 0.05). The indirect effect of Product Link on Like through the perceived helpfulness of post content was positive and significant (b1 = 0.392, $p < 0 . 0 1 ; b 2 = 0 . 8 7 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). As expected, the inclusion of a product link in a post was perceived as more helpful, which can increase the likelihood of liking a post. Moreover, the indirect effect of Product Link on Like through the perceived ulterior motive of the influencer was positive and significant (b1 = -0.975, p < 0.001; b2 = -0.279, p < 0.001). Inserting a product link into a post could cause users to perceive the influencer’s motivation in generating the post as less monetary, leading to a higher extent of liking the post. In summary, these results support H1 (suggesting a positive impact of integrating product links on endorsement) and demonstrate the significant mediating effects of perceived post helpfulness and perceived ulterior motives of the influencer.

We plotted the effects of Product Link on the two mediators, Helpfulness and Ulterior Motive, for sponsored and organic content in Figure 4. As indicated in Table 12, the moderating effect of Sponsored on the relationship between Product Link and Helpfulness was not significant (b = -0.214, p > 0.05), suggesting that the impact did not differ significantly between sponsored content and organic content. The moderating effect of Sponsored on the relationship between Product Link and Ulterior Motive was significant (b = 0.827, p < 0.001), indicating a potential moderated mediation effect of Sponsored.

## 5 Discussion

## 5.1 Summary of Findings

Our empirical analysis in Study 1 revealed that integrating product links into social media content had a positive influence on social media endorsement. However, this effect was attenuated in sponsored (vs. organic) content. We also found that this impact was more pronounced for highly active (vs. less active) influencers. Study 2 partially confirmed these findings in a controlled setting where user-generated content was comparable across experimental groups. Unlike Study 1, which used a complex product link format (a panel displaying product details), Study 2 employed a simpler format (a direct URL linking to the product page). Despite this difference, we still identified the positive impact of product links on endorsement in organic content and the negative moderating effect in sponsored content. Furthermore, we explored the underlying mechanisms by examining two mediators: perceptions of post helpfulness and perceptions of influencers’ ulterior motives. Our findings suggest that product links enhance perceived content helpfulness while reducing suspicions of ulterior motives, resulting in increased social media endorsements.

## 5.2 Theoretical Contributions

This study makes four major contributions to the literature. First, it contributes to the emerging topic of user-generated content regarding products marketed on social media by focusing on the outcome of social media endorsement. As a form of voluntarily provided user feedback, social media endorsements reflect content quality, which is crucial for the success of social media platforms (Tan et al., 2022). Moreover, endorsement represents an important aspect of user engagement and is widely used to assess the commercial value of influencers. It also plays a vital role in optimizing platform algorithms that identify and promote highquality content tailored to users’ diverse preferences. Therefore, empirically investigating the factors that drive endorsement provides valuable insights that enhance our understanding of user-generated content.

Second, our study complements research on user engagement by delving into a crucial yet underexplored aspect: product links. Existing studies have examined a variety of post attributes, such as post content (e.g., Yang et al., 2019), post type (e.g., De Vries et al., 2012; Sabate et al., 2014), and posting timing (e.g., Pletikosa Cvijikj & Michahelles, 2013; Sabate et al., 2014). A few studies have also explored the role of URLs in facilitating communications between content generators and their audiences (Agichtein et al., 2008; Suh et al., 2010). However, research on URLs has mainly focused on general URLs linking to any web page (e.g., Lee et al., 2018; Rodrigues et al., 2011; Suh et al., 2010), spam URLs that link to fake or malicious web pages (e.g., Cao & Caverlee, 2015; Cui et al., 2011), and affiliate URLs that typically link to an e-commerce landing page with a unique tracking code that allows firms to track clicks and sales (Mathur et al., 2018). Despite the importance of product links in user-generated content within social media marketing, relevant research remains scarce (Page et al., 2012). To address this gap, our study contributes to the literature by theorizing and empirically examining the impact of product links on social media endorsement. Furthermore, we investigate the underlying mechanisms that contribute to this impact. Our findings underscore the importance of product links as a post feature that signals heightened content quality and reduces the perceived ulterior motives of influencers.

Third, this study contributes to the literature on content strategies by theorizing and examining how product links are endorsed in sponsored content versus organic content. Product links and sponsorship disclosure can be viewed as information cues that users receive when engaging with content on social media. Understanding how these cues are collectively perceived is important for guiding influencers in optimizing their content creation strategies. Our findings indicate that the effect of incorporating product links into sponsored content (vs. organic content) is attenuated, shedding light on the nuanced relationship between commercial promotion and audience perception. This highlights the challenges that influencers face in sustaining high endorsement levels within sponsored content.

Finally, our study contributes to the influencer marketing literature by examining the often overlooked yet important moderating effect of influencer activeness. Our findings demonstrate the benefits of maintaining a high level of activeness in attracting user endorsements. Influencers who actively generate content can cultivate strong and intimate relationships with their audiences, which in turn increses endorsements, particularly when they incorporate product links into their content.

## 5.3 Practical Implications

This study offers valuable practical implications for influencers, social media platforms, and brands. First, we provide influencers with actionable insights into contentgeneration strategies that elicit higher levels of social media endorsement. Our findings suggest that influencers should consider incorporating product links into their organically generated, product-related content to enhance social media endorsements. However, the efficacy of this strategy diminishes in sponsored content. In addition, influencers should actively create content to gain greater benefits in engaging the audience by incorporating product links into their content.

Second, our findings offer important implications for social media platforms seeking to foster sustained content creation and user engagement. Platform designers can enhance system functionality to encourage influencers to create organic content that includes helpful product links, thereby increasing content endorsements. Moreover, they can improve the way product links are displayed within social media content. Our findings, based on two studies using two types of product links—complex product link format and product URL format—suggest that a richer format, providing product information directly, may be more effective in boosting endorsement because it is perceived as more helpful. Platform designers can create features that automatically extract and display key product details in product links, helping users reduce search costs and more efficiently evaluate product recommendations.

Furthermore, this study sheds light on brand marketing strategies. Our findings underscore the significance of product links in driving user endorsement of productrelated content. Brands can improve their marketing strategies by incentivizing influencers to incorporate product links in their recommendation content, thereby enhancing brand visibility and igniting consumer interest. By leveraging the persuasive power of influencergenerated content, brands can effectively elevate brand awareness, engagement, and sales through the widespread use of product links in influencer content.

## 5.4 Limitations and Future Work

This research has several limitations that suggest directions for future studies. First, in Study 1, we did not have time-stamped data on likes, so our main analysis was conducted at the post level rather than at the post time level. This suggests an opportunity for future researchers to investigate the temporal dynamics of social media endorsements. Second, despite the proven importance of social media endorsement (i.e., likes), this study did not directly measure the economic benefits for brands, social media platforms, or influencers. Future research could incorporate product sales data to assess the financial impact of product links. Third, we could not rule out the possibility that some influencers in our Study 1 sample may have purchased fake likes or modified their posts afterward. These issues are difficult to control with secondary data, leaving an opportunity for future research to address them using experimental data.

Fourth, accounting for the recommendation algorithm used by RedNote in Study 1 is challenging because its details are not available. Although we incorporated many possible observable factors that could serve as inputs of the recommendation system in our model, this concern could not be fully resolved. Future research might address this by cooperating directly with social media platforms. Fifth, we did not control for the visual features of photo and video posts in Study 1. While we believe that our constructed post features effectively captured content differences, we suggest that future research explore visual attributes to account for additional features. Moreover, we could not rule out the possibility of undisclosed sponsored content in Study 1. In addition, we used data from a single social media platform in Study 1. While RedNote is a representative social media platform with many similarities to others, future research could test and extend our findings to other platforms. Finally, we did not include multiple product links in the experimental design of Study 2 and could not validate our findings regarding influencer activeness due to the difficulty in replicating it experimentally. Future work could develop methods to examine the effects of multiple product links and the moderating role of influencer activeness in a controlled experiment setting.

## 6 Concluding Remarks

This study examines how and why social media users endorse product-related content generated by influencers. It focuses on the inclusion of product links in social media content and investigates the moderating roles of sponsored content and influencer activeness. Our findings show that product links have a positive impact on social media endorsement. This effect is more pronounced for organic content than for sponsored content. Moreover, influencer activeness positively moderates the effect of integrating product links on endorsement. This research deepens our understanding of product-related content generated by individuals. Given the proliferation of influencer marketing as a major channel to engage customers, this study offers unique implications for both theory and practice.
