# Outcomes and success factors of enterprise IT architecture management: empirical insight from the international financial services industry

## Abstract

Within the last decades, corporate information technology (IT) environments have approached considerable degrees of complexity. As a consequence, IT has become increasingly difficult to manage resulting in high costs and poor flexibility. Today, it is generally acknowledged that the sustainability of corporate IT environments can only be ensured through a continuous and long-term management on the level of the Enterprise (IT) Architecture (EA). To address this, many firms have implemented a dedicated Enterprise (IT) Architecture Management (EAM) function. However, little is known yet on the effectiveness of such functions and the factors influencing EAM success. Within this research, we thus seek to answer two main questions: (1) do firms adopting EAM perform better with regard to high-level information management objectives like IT flexibility and IT efficiency, and if so, (2) what are the critical success factors in attaining these goals? To answer these questions, a field survey was conducted within the international financial services industry. The results provide evidence that the implementation of an EAM function is in fact supportive in the creation and sustainment of IT efficiency and IT flexibility. Several factors are shown to be of critical importance for achieving these goals with architectural governance being the most important one. ## Introduction

Since the adoption of information technology (IT), the complexity of corporate IT environments has been growing steadily. By now, application portfolios of several hundred application systems – hosted on a variety of technology platforms and connected by diverse interfaces – are rather commonplace. This applies in particular to the financial services sector, which is highly dependent on the use of IT due to the information intensity of its products and processes (Porter & Millar, 1985).

In practice, IT complexity growth has given rise to various problems. First of all, IT management has become more and more difficult thereby increasing operational risks. Beyond that, IT complexity has also fueled maintenance and operating costs (cf. Namba, 2005, p. 3). Most severe, however, has been the loss of flexibility with respect to changes in business strategy and processes as reflected in high development costs and a growing time-to-market. In many firms, this has constrained the actionability of the overall organization. As a consequence, there is often a perceived deviation between a firm’s business strategy and the information systems (IS) available to support this strategy. This so-called ‘alignment problem’ has received considerable interest during the last years (Luftman, 2005, p. 271).

Today, it is generally acknowledged that the sustainability and strategic alignment of corporate IT environments can only be ensured through a continuous and long-term management on the level of the Enterprise (IT) Architecture (EA) (e.g., Schulte, 2002; Longepe, 2003; Rajput, 2004; Koch, 2005; Namba, 2005; Niemann, 2006). For this purpose, many organizations have implemented a dedicated Enterprise (IT) Architecture Management (EAM) function (e.g., Murer, 2005; Walbaum, 2005; Longepe, 2007). The overall concept of EAM is frequently characterized by means of a city planning metaphor (e.g., Sassoon, 1998, Longepe, 2003). That is, the role of the enterprise architect is compared to that of an urban city planner. However, EAM is still a rather young and immature discipline for which no common practice has emerged yet. As a consequence, different firms often follow a different approach depending on the corporate culture and tradition.

Various researchers and practitioners have argued on the potential benefits of an EAM implementation, and a number of frameworks and methodologies have been proposed (e.g., Zachman, 1987; The Open Group, 2003; The Office of Management and Budget, 2005). Yet, firms implementing EAM have often encountered a number of difficulties as well. First of all, implementing an EAM function will typically cause additional costs in the short run. Moreover, various conflicts may arise due to differing stakeholder interests. At the same time, the positive effects are generally long-term, global in scale, and difficult to measure. EAM is therefore often hard to legitimize.

For these reasons, there has been a growing interest concerning an evaluation of existing EAM concepts and an identification of success factors within the industry. However, until recently, empirical research on the practice of EAM has been rather limited. Although several case studies have been conducted (e.g., Boh et al., 2003; Martin et al., 2004; Hjort-Madsen, 2006; Pulkkinen, 2006), to the best of our knowledge, only one investigation has so far addressed the issue of EAM outcomes (Boh & Yellin, 2006). Yet, this study has focused on the use of EA standards and their effect on low level target variables like heterogeneity and application integration.

In order to address this research gap, the goal of our work is to capture the concept of EAM more comprehensively and to evaluate its actual outcomes with respect to high level information management objectives. More precisely, this research seeks to answer two main questions: (1) do firms adopting EAM perform better with regard to

IT flexibility and IT efficiency, and if so, (2) what are the critical success factors in attaining these goals?

To answer these questions, an empirical research approach was applied. The overall design consisted of two main building blocks. First, a series of expert interviews was conducted with EAM managers from within the banking sector. The purpose of this exploratory prestudy was to provide a firsthand insight into the practice and self-conception of EAM and to qualitatively examine the status quo of the discipline. Based on the results of the prestudy, a theoretical framework and a number of hypotheses were developed. In a second step, a field survey was conducted. The primary objective of this survey was to quantitatively assess the effectiveness of EAM and to test the proposed hypotheses.

In the next section, we will review the theoretical aspects of EAM and introduce a theoretical framework based on our prestudy results. We will then develop our hypotheses. This is followed by a detailed description of the main study research design in the next section. After that, the study results are presented and discussed. Finally, the implications for research and practice are summarized.

## Theorizing EAM

Although there is a growing number of publications on EAM-related issues, most of them deal with specific aspects regarding EAM practice. Zachman (1987), for instance, proposed a framework for organizing EA models in a consistent way. Similarly, The Open Group (2003) developed a comprehensive methodology for EAM known as The Open Group Architecture Framework (TOGAF). However, to the best of our knowledge, there has been no attempt by researchers to define the concept of EAM theoretically and to explain its role within the overall context of IT management. Therefore, in this section, we first develop an elementary theoretical foundation. Drawing on general systems, stakeholder, organizational, and software evolution theory, we demonstrate the need for a holistic management of the EA. Based on the results from our prestudy, we then derive a conceptual framework for EAM and develop our hypotheses.

## Theoretical foundation

The starting point for our theoretical considerations is the notion of a firm’s IT environment as a complex, open, and dynamic system (Bernus & Schmidt, 2006; Ferstl & Sinz, 2006). To avoid any ambiguities, we use the term Enterprise Information System (EIS) to refer to that system. The EIS can be defined as a firm’s information processing subsystem consisting of all information processing tasks, the applications used to perform these tasks as well as all the technical components necessary to run the applications. Just like with any other system, the fundamental organization of the EIS (as embodied in its components, their relationships to each other and to the environment) may be referred to as the system’s architecture (IEEE Computer Society, 2000, p. 3f.). In accordance with industry practices, we use the term EA for this.

In any larger organization, the EIS (and hence the EA) will be exposed to a number of stakeholders, that is, individuals or groups with dedicated interests in, or concerns relative to, the system (Pouloudi, 1999, p. 7; IEEE Computer Society, 2000, p. 3). Major stakeholders of the EIS include business managers and end users of different business lines, systems development and operations staff, customers as well as external business partners. According to stakeholder theory, all stakeholders of the EIS may have legitimate interests into the system and will strive for their individual benefits with no prima facie priority of one set of interests and benefits over another (Donaldson & Preston, 1995, p. 68). Moreover, in a competitive business environment, stakeholder interests will be subject to change (Pouloudi, 1999, p. 12). Main drivers for this are shifting market demands, changes in the legal regulation, emerging technologies, and the supply with standard software and services.

Because of this dynamics, a one-time construction or optimization of the EIS is generally inappropriate, if not unfeasible. Instead, the EIS has to be adapted continuously. In cybernetics terms, this may be considered a control cycle in which deviations from the target state are addressed by system modifications. In practice, though, there are two important constraints imposed on this change process. First, system changes may not interrupt the day-to-day operations. Second, from an economic point of view, all system changes represent investment decisions, which need to be justified with respect to costs and benefits. As a consequence, the EIS can typically not be thrown over and built from scratch again, but has to follow an evolutionary process.

Owing to its multi-polar and incremental nature, this evolution of the EIS is not necessarily aligned with global and long-term corporate objectives. Stakeholders may rather be assumed to drive the evolution process according to their current interests and their power within the organization. In the absence of a superordinate coordination mechanism, the solution scope of system changes will therefore typically be local and often also short-term. Hence, the evolution of the overall EIS may easily run into local optima leaving global synergy potentials unused. These problems are exacerbated in decentralized solution environments as common in today’s global corporations where system changes are typically implemented by different organizational units or even external contractors.

Apart from loosing synergy potentials, the continuous modification of the EIS may also give rise to complexity growth (Namba, 2005, p. 3). According to the Second Law of Software Evolution, the complexity of a software system in use will increase with time if no explicit action is taken to avoid this (Lehman, 1997, p. 1f.). This is based on the empirical observation that successive incremental system changes generally lead to an increase in dependencies between system components and a waning of the initial system architecture (Lehman, 1997, p. 2). Although originally targeted to individual software systems, it may be argued that the law will also hold on a macro level (as an aggregation of the many individual evolution processes). At the extreme, this complexity growth may lead to a situation where changes can no longer be implemented efficiently. In terms of systemic management theory, this may threaten the viability of the whole organization.

In order to avert the negative implications of an uncontrolled EIS evolution and to allow for a sustainable development, firms need to govern the evolution process from a holistic, systems-oriented perspective. More specifically, system changes must be coordinated with each other and be aligned with long-term corporate objectives. In doing so, all dependencies between future and existing components of the EIS must be taken into account. In terms of organizational theory, the need for this kind of architectural coordination arises from the division of labor in advancing the EIS and the fact that its subcomponents may be related in various ways.

While the issues described have often been neglected by the traditional IT planning systems (which generally focused on a short- to mid-term financial perspective and lacked a holistic system-oriented view), many organizations have addressed them more recently by implementing a dedicated EAM function. The role and mission of such a function is often illustrated by means of a city planning metaphor (Sassoon, 1998; Schulte, 2002, p. 6ff; Longepe, 2003). In the following section, we will hence briefly discuss the similarities between the domains of city and IT planning and outline the theoretical backdrop of the classic ‘city planning methodology’.

## City planning metaphor

Several authors have pointed out the structural similarities between the domains of city and IT planning (e.g., Sassoon, 1998; Schulte, 2002; Longepe, 2003; Namba, 2005). According to this, both cities and EIS may be interpreted as open, complex and dynamic supersystems consisting of a variety of subsystems. In the case of the city, these are given by the land lots and the buildings erected on it. For the EIS, this corresponds with the information processing tasks and the application systems used to support these. Also, within both types of systems an ‘infrastructure layer’ may be identified comprising all system components that are provided for common use (e.g., transportation and communication networks). Moreover, cities and EIS are both exposed to continuous modifications driven by various stakeholder groups. These system changes are generally initiated on a micro level by individual investors (land owners corresponding to business lines) and carried out by different subcontractors (building companies corresponding to internal or external IT service providers). Last but not least, in both cases, system changes must be executed while the system is in use.

Yet, there are also a number of differences that should be noted. Complexity, for instance, may be much bigger within an EIS because the relations between system components are not limited to spatial dimensions. Also, the autonomy of EIS stakeholders may be smaller since they are part of an overarching organization. Despite these differences and the limited validity of conclusions by analogy, the solution approach developed within the city planning domain may be considered a useful framework in conceptualizing the emerging field of EAM. In the following, we will hence briefly explore the fundamentals of the city planning methodology.

Similar to EIS, cities left to stakeholder forces alone have often encountered severe problems in their longterm development (e.g., missing space for future expansion, poor infrastructure facilities, ghettoization). To address this, most western countries have established an institutionalized planning and governance process for guiding the evolution of the city and aligning it with the public long-term interest. This process is usually centered around the concept of a zoning (or land development) plan. Based on cartography, a zoning plan depicts the current and/or future state of an urban area from a highlevel perspective (graphical part). As such, it partitions the urban space into districts, blocks and parcels which represent the unit of ownership and may be subject to building efforts. Based on that, specific rules and standards are assigned to be obeyed by building endeavors (textual part).

Generally, zoning plans are elaborated by a public authority and within an institutionalized planning process under participation of all relevant stakeholders. At the end of this mediation process, zoning plans are formally approved (e.g., by the city council). Implementation may then occur in two ways. First, building projects may be initiated by the municipality (‘public works’). This is most relevant for the infrastructure domain. In particular, all urban spaces must be connected to transportation, utility, and communication networks. Second, building projects may be initiated by private land owners. In this case, building permissions must be obtained first from the building supervising body of the municipality. Such permissions are to be issued only for construction projects that are conformant with the approved zoning plans.

The city planning methodology allows for a controlled evolution of the city on the macro level while maintaining a high degree of flexibility on the micro level. By leaving specific investment and design decisions up to local actors, the pitfalls of a centralized planning bureaucracy may be avoided. Still, individual stakeholder interests are balanced with the common welfare. From an organizational theoretic perspective, the city planning methodology may hence be considered a hybrid coordination mechanism that combines the benefits of centralized and decentralized coordination. As such, it represents a generic solution approach to cope with any large-scale, multi-polar, incremental, and highly distributed system evolution. In the following section, we will apply the city planning methodology to develop a conceptual framework for EAM based on our prestudy data.

## Prestudy results and derivation of a theoretical framework

In order to complement our theoretical analysis, we conducted a series of expert interviews with EAM managers of 14 Swiss and German banks. The purpose of this exploratory prestudy was to gain some firsthand insight into the practice of EAM and to derive a theoretical framework. Our sample included both universal and special banks of any size as well as three IT service providers. The study was carried out between December 2005 and March 2006. Interviews took between 90 and 180 min and were structured by means of a protocol covering the topics EAM goals, EAM tasks, EAM organization, EAM methods and tools, as-is landscape, to-be landscape, EAM outcomes, problems and success factors of EAM. Interview statements were documented by the interviewer and then analyzed using a form of qualitative content analysis based on an open categorization scheme. The following table shows some of the main results.

Generally, EAM was considered an important activity by all interviewees. However, in most firms, it was characterized as an emerging discipline that had been set up within the recent one to five years. Only three companies indicated to have experiences of more than five years. This immaturity was also reflected in the degree of variety found in the sample. For instance, we identified substantial differences with respect to the objectives followed, the methodology employed, and the organizational implementation (see Table 1).

However, using the city planning metaphor as a structuring means, we were able to identify six recurring EAM tasks which we partitioned into two main groups. The first group comprises the more strategic issues and broadly corresponds with the planning and mediation phase of the city planning methodology. According to our analysis, documenting the as-is architecture (which relates to cartography in urban city planning) typically forms the starting point for most organizations with respect to this (nine cases). The EA documentation is usually structured into different views and levels of abstraction (e.g., business architecture, application architecture, infrastructure architecture) in order to cope with complexity. However, only in very few organizations, all these architectural descriptions are based on a common metamodel and stored in an integrated repository tool.

Based on their documentation of the as-is architecture, many organizations engage in a more or less formal planning process for defining the to-be (or target) architecture (eight cases). This generally includes the definition of subdomains (corresponding to zoning in city planning) and the high-level modeling of the aspired target state within those domains. Complementing this, almost all firms define certain rules and standards to be obeyed by change projects (corresponding to building codes in city planning) (13 cases). This often includes architecture principles, books-of-standards and reusable reference architectures.

The second group comprises the more operational EAM tasks. It broadly corresponds with the implementation phase of the city planning methodology. Following this analogy, we can further differentiate between those tasks pertaining to an active type of implementation (relating to the infrastructure-related building endeavors sponsored by the municipality) on the one side and those pertaining to a passive type of implementation (corresponding to the regulation of landowner-driven projects through the building supervision body) on the other. However, it should be noted that these are complementary rather than mutually exclusive implementation mechanisms.

About half of the firms engage in an active type of EA implementation (seven cases). In these cases, EAM functions have the opportunity to initiate transformation projects. Some organizations even launch long-running (re-) architecture programs comprising various individual projects.

With respect to the passive type of EA implementation, most organizations are engaged in some sort of governance activities for ensuring the conformity of change projects with the defined target architectures and guidelines (11 cases). To this end, they have typically set up certain formal decision and approval processes.

In order to foster stakeholder acceptance and to ensure the practical applicability of the defined guiding frameworks, many firms also engage in communication and support efforts (eight cases). For this purpose, different channels like publications, tutorials or consulting services are employed. Beyond that, solution architects may be sent off to accompany selected projects.

Based on these insights from the prestudy, we developed a conceptual framework to describe the role and function of EAM within an organization. For lack of any stronger theoretical grounding, we followed the city planning paradigm in doing so. That is, we put the EIS change cycle into analogy with the building development process and assigned the planning and governing tasks of the city planning office (and building supervision authority) to the EAM function. In accordance with organizational theory, we introduced the terms EA planning for developing target architecture plans and EA programming for setting generic architecture rules and standards. The goals followed by the EAM function (EAM Goals) then correspond with the overarching goals of the city planning authority (‘public good’). The resulting conceptual framework is shown in Figure 1.

The framework describes the stakeholder-driven process of EIS evolution. During this process, system changes are planned and implemented continuously based on individual stakeholder requirements. As an enterpriselevel coordination function, the EAM function governs the evolution process striving to align it with the EAM goals. These represent high-level requirements from a global and long-term perspective and may vary depending on the internal and external situation of the enterprise. As such, they have to be elaborated in a political decision process and be updated regularly. Hence, the EAM function may be considered to represent a second-order control cycle superordinated to the primary cycle of EIS evolution.

## Research model

We will now conceptualize our endogeneous and exogeneous variables and develop our research hypotheses. According to the prestudy results, IT efficiency and IT flexibility may be regarded as the dominant EAM goals. They were named explicitly by eight interviewees each (see Table 1). Beyond that, some of the other goals mentioned (like reducing IT complexity and increasing transparency) can be linked to these objectives as well. This empirical finding is consistent with the theoretical analysis of Allen & Boynton (1991, p. 436) who concluded that the two main architecture goals on the enterprise level are typically IT efficiency and IT flexibility. For this research, we hence focus on these two endogeneous variables. They are conceptualized as follows.

IT efficiency Based on the general concept of organizational efficiency (Drucker, 1974), IT efficiency may be defined as the relation between the output of the IS function and its total efforts. In this context, the output is given by the extent and quality of business process support (or automation) through the provision, maintenance and operation of application systems for the required information processing tasks. The efforts, on the other hand, are represented by the total costs of the IS function, regardless of whether IT services are provided in-house or external.

IT efficiency will be to a large extent dependent on the architecture of the EIS. For instance, one might expect functional redundancies to be associated with increased maintenance- and operating costs. Obviously, IT costs will also be contingent on the heterogeneity of system components due to scale effects in procurement and operations. Finally, maintenance- and operating costs are generally dependent on the topology of system coupling. A centralized integration infrastructure with a minimal number of interfaces, for instance, is likely to be more cost efficient than a bulk of peer-to-peer connections.

IT flexibility IT flexibility, on the other hand, can be defined as the degree to which the EIS may be adapted to changing business requirements easily, that is, quickly and without major efforts (see Duncan, 1995, p. 44; Tallon & Kraemer, 2003, p. 2; Beimborn et al., 2006, p. 589). Flexibility enables organizations to control outside environments effectively (Byrd & Turner, 2000, p. 170). As such, it may be considered a key competitive advantage in more dynamic and unstable markets (Duncan, 1995, p. 38, 44).

Like with IT efficiency, IT flexibility is largely determined by the architecture of the EIS. According to Byrd & Turner (2000), it may hence be conceptualized along the two dimensions modularity and integration (see also Tallon & Kraemer, 2003, p. 10; Ness, 2005, S. 3). In this context, modularity refers to the capability of adding, removing or changing system components easily and without major side effects. Integration, on the other hand, is defined as the capability to establish connections between any system components (connectivity) and to exchange information (compatibility) (Byrd & Turner, 2000, p. 171).

EAM approach With respect to the exogeneous variables, our research focuses on a firm’s approach to EAM. In accordance with the literature on Strategic Information Systems Planning (SISP), we use this concept to capture the general manner (or style) of an EAM implementation within a firm (see Earl, 1993; Doherty et al., 1999). As our prestudy revealed, the six general EAM tasks are followed to a different extent and with different priorities by different firms. A firm’s approach to EAM may hence be roughly captured by evaluating its implementation with respect to each of these generic tasks. However, given the legitimization problem of EAM and the importance of maintaining stakeholder acceptance, we decided to take the degree of stakeholder participation into account as well. Therefore, within this research, the EAM approach is conceptualized along the seven dimensions EA documentation, EA planning, EA programming, EA implementation, EA communication and support, EA governance, and stakeholder participation. We will now briefly describe these dimensions and provide some arguments on their contribution in attaining the EAM goals defined.

EA documentation: EA documentation refers to the process of capturing and describing the existing EA by means of architectural descriptions. To cope with complexity, these are typically structured into different views and levels of abstraction (e.g., business, application, and infrastructure architecture). Ideally, all descriptions are based on a common metamodel and stored in an integrated repository. While some authors have stressed the necessity for a complete set of architectural descriptions (e.g., Zachman, 1987), this has generally not been feasible in practice due to the high efforts associated with the creation and maintenance of such models. Firms engaged in EA documentation therefore typically focus on the most important aspects and stick to a rather high level of abstraction.

A sound documentation base represents a prerequisite for any rational management of the EA. Beyond that, it may generally improve decision-making in planning and implementing system changes. For example, several prestudy interviewees reported that their EA documentation eased the identification of existing system components and relationships in project planning and execution. As a result, the integration of new system components could be facilitated. Moreover, reuse could be fostered and redundancies be prevented. This may positively affect both IT flexibility and IT efficiency.

EA planning: According to organizational theory, planning is a coordination mechanism that uses descriptions of a target state to achieve a desired outcome (cf. Thompson, 1968, p. 55f.). EA planning may hence be defined as a goal-oriented process of developing descriptions of the target architecture based on global and long-term requirements. As with documentation, this generally includes the structured storage and maintenance of EA models for different views and levels of abstraction. Often, also multiple target architectures are developed for different scenarios and time horizons. However, EA planning is a complex and rather demanding task. Like with EA documentation, it is hence often restricted to a high level of abstraction.

Target architecture plans are a pivotal instrument for managing the evolution of the EIS on the macro level. In particular, they allow for an ex-ante verification of the future system properties. By providing an integrated and consistent view of the future state, target architecture plans may form a primary decision base for selecting or realigning change projects, respectively. For example, some firms from our prestudy reported on using models of their applications supporting business products and processes (‘process-support-maps’) in order to identify overlaps and gaps and to initiate transformation projects. This way, operating and maintenance costs could be reduced. Also, systems engineering concepts were found to be applied on the enterprise level (e.g., modularization, decoupling).

Many organizations, for example, reported to organize their application landscape by means of (decoupled) application domains. That way, the overall flexibility of the EIS could be increased.

EA programming: In terms of organizational theory, programming is a coordination mechanism that uses generic rules and standards to achieve a desired outcome. EA programming may hence be defined as the process of setting architecture rules and standards to be obeyed by change projects. These may include high-level architecture principles as well as reference architectures and technical standards.

By means of EA programming, predefined solution patterns and components may be allocated to certain problem domains. This way, the solution space of change projects may be restricted so that the overall EAM goals can be attained. EA programming is particularly useful in controlling system variety. Our prestudy sample contained several cases in which system variety had been reduced substantially due to the persistent application of standard catalogues and reference architectures. As a result, these firms had been able to reduce their operations and maintenance costs (see also Boh & Yellin, 2006, p. 174). Beyond that, some reported also on improvements with respect to systems integration and changability (cf. Buxmann et al., 1999).

EA implementation: EA implementation refers to the initiation and/or execution of system changes through the EAM function itself. This is mainly relevant in the areas of ‘public interest’ that are not adequately addressed by ordinary business-driven change projects. Such endeavors may include general system consolidations as well as developing shared infrastructure components or reusable business services.

EAM-driven implementation efforts may accelerate the attainment of the EAM goals. This was also reflected in our prestudy. While some firms exclusively relied on a passive type of EA implementation, some others had also launched dedicated re-architecture and transformation programs. Those companies had generally reached much better positions with respect to IT flexibility and IT efficiency than the others. Beyond that, the provision of common components may foster reuse. For example, some of the firms in our sample strongly engaged in developing programming frameworks and reusable services. This was assumed to have a positive effect on EA conformance and, in the long run, lead to a decrease in development and maintenance costs.

EA communication and support: This dimension refers to the extent of communication and support efforts undertaken by the EAM function. By communication we mean the process of informing stakeholders about all EA-related issues. Beyond this, active support may be provided to stakeholders in the planning and implementation of EA conformant change projects.

A clear communication of all EA-related issues is a prerequisite for the passive EA implementation to be effective. For example, if project members are unfamiliar with the defined standards, they can hardly adhere to them. Beyond that, prestudy participants stressed the importance of actual project support in ensuring the acceptance of the EAM function. We encountered several cases in which poor or missing communication and support efforts had led to misunderstandings and noncompliant developments.

EA governance: EA governance refers to the degree that EA-related decisions and guidelines are binding to the organization and may be enacted based on formal processes. The standards conformance of change projects, for instance, needs to be verified regularly and enforced, if necessary. In practice, this is typically achieved by means of formal review and approval processes.

As with EA communication and support, an adequate degree of EA governance is of major importance for the passive EA implementation to be effective. Without this, the conformance of change projects with the defined EAM goals cannot be ensured. In our prestudy, for example, there were several organizations with formal review and approval processes. By contrast, some other EAM functions did not even have a veto right. The rate of non-compliant projects was generally much higher (about 30%) in these companies than in the others (3 to 20%).

EA stakeholder participation: EA stakeholder participation refers to the extent that stakeholders are involved in EAM decision making. In practice, participation is typically achieved by means of diversified decision committees (e.g., architecture board).

By having an adequate degree of stakeholder participation, the EAM function can make sure that architectural decisions resemble the long-term public interest. More importantly, stakeholder participation is assumed to have a positive effect on the overall acceptance of the EAM function. As our prestudy revealed, this acceptance may be lost if architectural decisions are made in a top-down manner only. We observed two cases where the EAM function had been first sidestepped and finally abolished due to missing acceptance within the organization.

Based on the analysis above, we would expect firms following a more comprehensive approach to EAM (as measured along the seven dimensions) to be more successful in attaining and maintaining their IT flexibility and efficiency. In the case of IT efficiency, this would require the annual efficiency gains resulting from an EAM implementation to exceed its added costs (cf. Ball et al., 2004, p. 3756). This is supported by our prestudy results as we encountered six firms reporting on a reduction of their total IT costs following the EAM implementation. In two cases, these claims could even be validated according to actual calculations. We may therefore propose our first hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: Firms engaged in a more comprehensive approach to EAM will expose higher degrees of IT flexibility.

Hypothesis 2: Firms engaged in a more comprehensive approach to EAM will expose higher degrees of IT efficiency.

However, managing the EA is a continuous process with many of its effects occurring with a certain delay. According to the results of our prestudy, it will often take years to make any significant progress in approaching the goals set. Some of our interviewees, for example expected the outcomes of their efforts to be realized within no less than 5 to 10 years. Therefore, firms with a long-standing history in EAM implementation should generally reveal a better performance than newcomers with a comparable EAM approach. Beyond that, we would expect the impact of the EAM approach applied to be contingent on the duration of its implementation (moderator effect). We hence propose two more hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3: The impact of the EAM approach on IT flexibility will be contingent on the duration of the EAM implementation.

Hypothesis 4: The impact of the EAM approach on IT efficiency will be contingent on the duration of the EAM implementation.

## Research methodology

In order to test the proposed hypotheses, a quantitative field survey was conducted using a firm-level ex-post-facto design. To eliminate inter-industry variations and to achieve a high degree of homogeneity with respect to other contextual factors, the survey was restricted to medium- and large-scale financial services providers. Beyond that, we decided to focus on the western countries (i.e., the European Union, North America, and Australia) in order to avoid any problems due to differences in culture.

Since there was no single listing of the world’s largest financial services providers available, we compiled a list from several sources. Those included the Forbes 2000 Ranking of 2006 (for Banking, Insurance, and Diversified Financials) and the TOP 1000 World Banks Ranking 2006 of The Banker Magazine. We removed companies with headquarters outside our target region and sorted the remaining companies by total assets. The TOP 500 enterprises were then defined as our target population.

For all firms in the target frame, we sought to identify a manager overseeing the EAM function or equivalent (e.g., Chief Architect, Head of IT Architecture). Where such functions did not exist or contact persons could not be detected, the head of the IS/IT function was chosen instead. Contact names were identified through general web research, social networking platforms, and telephone inquiries. In addition to this, a major consultancy and a large vendor of integration software were supportive in establishing contacts to some of their customers.

## Operationalization

In operationalizing our model constructs, we strived to reuse or adapt existing measurement scales from the literature wherever possible. All first order constructs were operationalized using at least four reflective indicators. Unless noted otherwise, each item was measured on a 5-point-Likert-scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ (1) to ‘strongly agree’ (5).

EAM approach As a literature review revealed, there was no measurement instrument available to capture a firm’s approach to EAM in a comprehensive way. We hence developed a new instrument based on our conceptualization (see section ‘Theorizing EAM’). In doing so, we focused on formal aspects realizing that the actual agenda (or content) is generally too firmspecific to be accounted for in a standardized survey. Accordingly, the EAM approach was operationalized as a multi-faceted construct with the dimensions EA documentation, EA planning, EA programming, EA implementation, EA communication and support, EA governance, and EA stakeholder participation. The items used to measure these dimensions were either taken from the literature or created based on the insights gained from our interview study. As proposed by Lewis et al. (2005), we tried to employ an item for each major characteristic (see Table 2 for details). To account for the close relationships and dependencies between the individual dimensions, we aggregated them into a second-order molar construct as described in Chin & Gopal (1995). Second-order factors generally allow for a more adequate modeling of complex concepts and are beneficial in reducing the overall model complexity.

Duration of EAM implementation To avoid any difficulties with respect to firm-specific terminology, the duration of EAM implementation was measured based on functional rather than institutional criteria as well. More specifically, survey participants were asked to estimate the duration of engagement for the first four EAM tasks using a 5-point scale (o1 Year (1), 1–3 Years (2), 4–6 Years (3), 7–10 Years (4), 410 Years (5)). The other dimensions were not considered here as we expected difficulties in measuring them in retrospective.

IT flexibility To measure IT flexibility, an adaptation of the instrument originally proposed by Byrd & Turner (2000) was employed. This has been used extensively within IS research (e.g., Chung et al., 2003; Tallon & Kraemer, 2003; Chung et al., 2005; Ness, 2005). Following this previous research, IT flexibility was operationalized as a second-order molecular construct with the subdimensions connectivity, compatibility, and modularity.

IT efficiency As far as we know, there has been no attempt to develop a measure for IT efficiency in the literature. This is somewhat surprising given its importance to practice. However, previous research has addressed the related concepts of comparative IT production cost advantage (Dibbern et al., 2005) and IT effectiveness (Ness, 2005). Aside from this, efficiency has been operationalized in other disciplines (e.g., Stock-Homburg, 2006). Using these references as an input, we developed a new construct for IT efficiency based on the definition given above (see Table 3 for details).

Controls In order to protect from third variable effects, we included control variables for firm size, firm age, firm decentralization, firm diversification, environmental competitiveness, frequency of mergers and acquisitions, and centralization of the IS function (see also Boh & Yellin, 2006). These were assumed to represent the most important alternative explanations for variance in IT flexibility and IT efficiency.

## Data collection

All items were integrated into a questionnaire and complemented with some notes on terminology. The initial version of the questionnaire was reviewed by several researchers and practitioners and refined based on their comments. After that, a pretest was carried out with several members of the target population. This allowed us to detect any ambiguities and other problems that participants had in answering the survey. Based on the results, the questionnaire was adapted iteratively. The pretest was terminated after the fourth iteration since there was no substantial revision.

The final version of the questionnaire was implemented as an online survey using the Globalpark Surveycenter 5.0 platform. In addition to this, a paper-based version of the questionnaire was created. Both were made available through a survey web portal which included additional background information on the research project. Access to the portal was controlled by means of participation codes (‘tokens’) in order to prevent from unauthorized or multiple survey participation.

Before the final study release, a pilot test was carried out with 10% of the target population. The pilot test produced a response rate of about 10% within a week and without any follow-up. This was considered to indicate an adequate overall survey attendance. Since the pilot test did not reveal any other problems, the study was rolled out subsequently keeping the questionnaire unchanged.

Data collection took place between January and July 2007. Participants were invited to the survey via e-mail.

## Sample characteristics

Altogether, we received 85 complete questionnaires which translates into a response rate of 17%. Survey participants came from 17 countries with an emphasis on Germany (31%) and Switzerland (18%). These rather high values may be ascribed to the geographical proximity as well as the comparatively large number of financial services providers in these countries. About 30% of the firms came from other European countries. After all, there were also 16% North American and 5% Australian firms in the sample. Participants came from all kinds of financial services providers including Commercial Banks (23%), Universal Banks (22%), Allfinance Groups (20%), Insurance Companies (18%), and Investment Banks (6%).

We performed Harman’s Single Factor Test (Harman, 1967) to test for common methods bias. In our analysis, 20 factors with an Eigenvalue greater than one emerged. The percentage of variance explained ranged from 2 to 10%. That is, no single factor accounted for most of the variance. Beyond that, we followed the unmeasured latent methods factor approach suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003, p. 894). That is, we introduced a latent common methods variable into our path model and allowed all items of the other constructs to load on it as well. We then examined the significance of the structural parameters both with and without the latent common methods variance factor in the model. We found that all significant relationships held after controlling for the latent common methods variance factor thereby providing evidence that common method variance is not an issue in our study.

Non-response bias was tested by comparing early and late respondents. This is based on the assumption that participants answering less readily are more similar to non-respondents (Armstrong & Overton, 1977). A comparison of the 25% earliest and the 25% latest respondents did not reveal any significant differences in any of our variables (t-test, Po0.05) thereby indicating that non-response-bias was not an issue in our study.

## Data analysis

For the evaluation of the research model, the Partial-Least-Squares (PLS) approach to Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was applied. PLS allows for a simultaneous examination of the relationships between multiple latent variables while explicitly accounting for measurement errors. It was chosen over the traditional covariance-based SEM mainly due to its lower sample size requirements. Also, PLS is better suited for the more explorative research (like ours) since it is prediction rather than explanation oriented and relies more heavily on the empirical data (Gefen et al., 2000, p. 44).

The research model was transformed into a structural equation model, and the model parameters were estimated based on the surveyed data using SmartPLS 2.0 (Ringle et al., 2005). In setting up the model, all secondorder factors were created according to the repeated indicators approach (Wold, 1982).

Three data records were excluded from the analysis due to inconsistencies and outliers. The sample size requirements for PLS as discussed by Chin (1998, p. 311) were met without difficulties as the largest structural equation in our model comprises seven input variables, which – when using a regression heuristic of 10 – translates into a minimum sample size requirement of 7 - 10 ¼ 70.

Initially, the model parameters were estimated based on the complete set of indicators. After that, single indicators were removed in an iterative process in order to improve the quality of the measurement model. The parameter estimates of the inner model were not affected by this in a substantial way. The moderator effect was tested using the two-stage approach (Henseler & Chin, 2010). That is, the main effect model was estimated first in order to obtain latent variable scores for the exogeneous variable and the moderator. An interaction variable was created then as the product of these latent variable scores. The interaction variable was added to the model and the model rerun in order to obtain the final estimates. The stability of the estimates was tested using a bootstrapping procedure with 500 re-samples of the original sample size. Significance was tested using twotailed t-tests.

## Model evaluation

Generally, PLS path models need to be evaluated with respect to the measurement and the structural model. In the following, we present the evaluation based on the final setting.

## Measurement model

A reflective measurement model may be evaluated with respect to content validity, indicator reliability, construct reliability and discriminant validity (Bagozzi, 1979; Churchill, 1979). In this study, content validity was qualitatively assessed via expert reviews and the pretest.

Indicator reliability was tested by inspecting the indicator loadings. Ideally, loadings should be above 0.7 (Hulland, 1999, p. 198). However, in the early stages of construct development, loadings between 0.5 and 0.6 may be acceptable as well (Chin, 1998, p. 325). As can be seen from Tables 2 and 3, our indicator loadings were mostly above 0.7. Only two indicators showed loadings below 0.6. We decided to retain these indicators because of their specific content dimension. Eliminating them did not change the model parameters substantially. All loadings were highly significant (Po0.0001).

Construct reliability was assessed by calculating the internal consistency. Generally, the internal consistency should be larger than 0.7 (Nunnally, 1978). As can be seen from Tables 2 and 3, the internal consistency of all our constructs was well above this threshold thereby attesting our measurement instrument a high degree of construct reliability.

In order to assess discriminant validity, the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) was calculated. According to Tables 2 and 3, the AVE was above the recommended 0.5 for all constructs. Also, in each case, the square root of the AVE was higher than the correlations with any of the other constructs (Hulland, 1999, p. 200) (see Table 4). Therefore, the discriminant validity of the measurement model was confirmed as well.

## Discussion

The model evaluation provides evidence that firms following a more comprehensive approach to EAM and firms engaged in EAM for a longer period of time reveal significantly higher levels of IT flexibility and IT efficiency. An EAM implementation may hence be considered an effective means in attaining and retaining these two important high-level information management objectives. However, the hypothesized moderator effect could not be confirmed. That is, the impact of the EAM approach could not be shown to increase over time. One explanation for this might be that the overall duration within our sample was too small for discovering such an effect.

As a comparison of the path coefficients shows, the impact on IT flexibility is generally larger than that on IT efficiency. A possible reason for this might be that IT efficiency is ‘further downstream’ the effect chain and could hence be affected by a larger number of other factors. In part, it might also reflect the efforts associated with an EAM implementation.

Taking a closer look on the path model reveals that all dimensions of the EAM approach show a positive contribution. They may hence all be considered relevant success factors in attaining the EAM goals. However, a relative comparison of the path coefficients shows that the dimensions of the EAM approach concerned with the development of abstract EA artifacts (i.e., the strategic EAM tasks) are generally of less importance than those concerned with implementation issues (i.e., the operational EAM tasks). This highlights the importance of a proper operational grounding of the EAM function.

On the strategic level, EA programming makes the largest contribution to the overall success with a path coefficient of b ¼ 0.18 (Po0.0001). This is consistent with the findings of Boh & Yellin (2006, p. 186) who revealed a positive relationship between the utilization of EA standards and certain variables pertaining to system homogeneity and integration. The higher impact ( þ 13%) compared to EA planning (b ¼ 0.16, Po0.0001) may be due to its easier application (and operationalization). The low impact of documentation (b ¼ 0.16, Po0.0001), on the other hand, may be ascribed to the fact that documentation is primarily addressing informational transparency and has no direct regulatory effect on the EIS evolution. This is consistent with our prestudy results which showed that firms focusing on documenting the EA alone did not improve substantially with respect to the EAM goals set.

On the operational level, EA governance makes the largest contribution to the overall success with a path coefficient of $\beta _ { G O V } { = } 0 . 2 4 \ ( P { < } 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ . With an additional impact of 50% compared to the weakest factors, EA governance represents the single most important factor in our model. This is not surprising given the importance of a sound governance framework for ensuring project conformity.

EA governance is followed by EA communication and support $( \beta _ { C O S } = 0 . 2 0 , \ P < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ . Together with stakeholder participation $( \beta _ { P A R } = 0 . 2 0 , ~ P < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ this factor represents the second most important determinant in our model (with an additional impact of 25% compared to the weakest factors). This is in line with our prestudy results and underlines the high importance of ‘soft’ factors for the long-term acceptance and legitimation of an EAM function.

Finally, EA implementation $( \beta _ { I M P } = 0 . 1 9 , \ P < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ is shown to be an important success factor as well. This again is consistent with our prestudy results which suggested that the attainment of the EAM goals set could be accelerated by launching dedicated EA projects.

The modeled relationships are independent of firm size, firm age, firm decentralization, decentralization of the IS function, diversification, environmental competitiveness, and mergers and acquisitions. Of those controls, only three show a significant relationship with the endogenous variables (see Figure 2).

Our study has several limitations that should be considered in the interpretation of the results and that provide various opportunities for future research. To begin with, all general shortcomings of ex-post-factodesigns apply (e.g., lacking control of the treatment/ ex-post randomization, concurrent measurement of cause and effect). Although these were partly addressed by introducing an explicit time dimension and including a number of third variables, future research could improve, for example, by applying a longitudinal research design. Beyond that, there may be concerns with respect to the data collection process. Although great care has been taken in the selection and development of the measurement scales, the constructs in scope remain complex and can only roughly be captured by means of a standardized questionnaire. Moreover, the reliance on single key informants might be problematic. Future research could address this by refining and extending the measurements scales to comprise different stakeholder perspectives. Also, the external validity of our conclusions may be limited due to the specific industry scope. There is hence opportunity for researches to validate our results for different types of populations. Beyond that, future research could investigate the impact of EAM implementations on other target variables (like IT complexity or the business value of IT, for example).

## Conclusion

The goal of this research was to evaluate the outcomes of EAM implementations and to identify significant success factors. For this purpose, an empirical research approach was applied comprising multiple stages. Based on a series of expert interviews, a theoretical framework was developed. In doing so, EAM was characterized as an enterprise-level coordination function that is employed to govern the multi-polar EIS evolution process and to align it with global corporate objectives. Based on this framework, arguments were presented on the benefits of an EAM implementation and a number of hypotheses were proposed. To empirically test these hypotheses, a field survey was conducted within the international financial services industry. Data were obtained from 85 organizations out of 17 countries. A PLS path analysis generally supported our propositions. It provided evidence that an EAM implementation is in fact supportive in the creation and sustainment of IT efficiency and IT flexibility. It was shown that several factors must play together to achieve these goals with architectural governance being the most important determinant.

Our research ventures beyond previous work by theorizing the concept of EAM more comprehensively and providing evidence for its impact on high level information management objectives. In effect, we are able to identify an important antecedent of IT flexibility and IT efficiency. By means of the new EAM approach construct, the characteristics of EAM implementations can be measured more holistically than via the constructs for EA standards management of Boh & Yellin (2006). Beyond that, the IT efficiency construct introduced represents a proven means to measure this important IT management objective.

Our findings suggest that EAM implementations are generally of value to larger organizations with a high information intensity (as given in our sample). In such organizations, investments in an EAM capability usually pay-off in significantly higher degrees of IT flexibility. According to previous research, this may in turn lead to improved IT effectiveness, strategic alignment, and IT business value (Tallon & Kraemer, 2003; Ness, 2005). Beyond that, there is evidence for a positive impact on IT efficiency. That is, the additional costs of an EAM function are typically outbalanced by the long-term savings.

However, these results will not emerge instantaneously. Setting up an EAM function must rather be considered a long-term effort. Also, several factors need to be taken into account to ensure implementation success. First of all, a clear definition of the overall architecture goals is necessary in order to give the EAM function direction. These should be derived from business strategy and be updated regularly. Clearly defined goals constitute the basis for a rational management of the EA. Beyond that, they are of crucial importance for the measurement and communication of the EAM success (e.g., through an EA scorecard). This in turn is a prerequisite for the legitimation of the EAM function within the organization.

With respect to the EAM methodology, the IT city planning approach represents a proven best-practice. In following it, firms should cover the whole palette of tasks on both a strategic and operational level. The foundation of any EAM initiative must be an adequate documentation of the as-is architecture. However, this should not be overdone nor be considered the main activity of the EAM function. Instead, a guiding framework for the future evolution of the EIS must be provided. This should include both, blueprints of the target architecture as well as general rules and standards to be obeyed by change projects. Ideally, all artifacts should be managed within an integrated EAM repository.

In order to be effective, the EAM function must pay special attention on implementation issues as well. First of all, this requires a regulation of business-driven change projects through an appropriate governance framework. The existence of such a framework was in fact shown to be the most important determinant in attaining the EAM goals set. However, this raises the question of how such a framework should be designed and how the concept of a ‘legal authority’ from the domain of city planning may be translated into a corporate setting.
