# Globalization, culture, and information: Towards global knowledge transparency

Dorothy E. Leidner

Ferguson Professor of Information Systems, Baylor University, One Bear Place, 8005 Waco, TX 76798, USA

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 20 January 2010 Received in revised form 18 February 2010 Accepted 19 February 2010 Available online 20 March 2010

Keywords: Globalization Culture Information transparency

## a b s t r a c t

In this paper, I seek to intertwine the IS research on globalization with IS research on culture. I first discuss various perspectives of globalization – cultural imperialism, interconnected businesses, and a global village – and the accompanying role of information technologies. I then present a brief overview of three waves of IS culture research: identifying differences, explaining differences, managing differences. Based upon the gaps identified in the overview, I suggest some propositions for future IS culture research. Finally, I seek to identify important future research that lies at the intersection of globalization and culture research, namely research into global knowledge transparency. Global knowledge transparency involves the dual objectives of providing more detailed product and service information to the consumer and gathering more detailed knowledge about the individual. The former helps individuals make more responsible choices in their purchase decisions, enabling them to understand the consequences of their choices on the well-being of others. The latter helps individuals make more informed decisions concerning their own well-being, enabling them to better understand the consequences of their choices for their own life and health. Taken together, the two objectives of global knowledge transparency can help societies promote better use of resources and more individual accountability.

## 1. Introduction

The term and concept of globalization has spawned much definition, debate, and interpretation. From an economic point of view, globalization typically refers to the reduction in, or entire removal of, foreign trade barriers in order to facilitate the flow of goods across borders. This view tends to equate globalization with economic development (Beck, 2000). Yet globalization entails far more than the mere exchange of finished goods: it also encompasses the exchange of production materials. the substitution of production processes, the relocation of services, the redistribution of resources, and the diffusion and infusion of cultural norms, artifacts, and values. And while globalization in its nascent forms of finished product exchange between the Orient and the Middle East date well back to the Grecian Empire (Attali, 2006), certainly the rate and scope of globalization has intensified over the past half century. As such, it has important strategic implications for organizations as well as society. And because globalization is greatly influenced by information and communication technologies, it is also of strategic importance to IS research and practice.

Information and communication technologies (ICT) play no small role in the globalization phenomenon: ICT enables managerial control over vast global supply chains, ICT itself is a product and service that can readily be offered to global markets; ICT becomes a trade platform joining suppliers and buyers from around the world, and ICT provides information and social networks that know no boundaries. One of the many outcomes of increased globalization is the need for greater information – information about suppliers, about markets, and consumers as well as information about distribution, logistics, and operations. And, as both the globalization phenomenon and the accompanying ICT expansion have become a common aspect of modern civilization, so too has it become apparent the influence that culture, and cultural differences, has on globalization and information dissemination as, at times, an enabler, and at time, a constraint. In this Viewpoint, I will present my interpretation of the IS perspective of these three inter-related topics of globalization, information, and culture. The inter-twining of these phenomena give rise to the need for several important research areas.

## 2. Globalization

While the broader economic and political community may take a decidedly product trade focus on globalization, the IS literature has adopted multifarious views of both the meaning of, and ramifications of, globalization. IS research on globalization includes such topics as the introduction of a global IS system (Lehmann, 2004; Pan and Leidner, 2003), managing offshoring relationships (Lacity et al., 2009; Rottman and Lactiy, 2004), structuring the global IT function (Weill, 2004), man aging globally dispersed team (Kayworth and Leidner, 2000), global ecommerce preparedness (Sarker and El Sawy, 2003), global requirements analysis (Kirsch and Hayney, 2006), and the plight of small firms attempting to operate in global market (Leidner, 1999). Here, I will focus less on topics of Global IS research than on conceptualizations of globalization in IS research. In an AMCIS panel on the topic in 2002 (Barrett et al., 2003), prominent researchers from different regions presented different perspectives of globalization. My interpretation of these perspectives led to the following three conceptualizations of globalization: cultural imperialism, interconnected business, and interconnected societies

## 3. Cultural imperialism

In its earliest form, cultural imperialism resulted from one nation-state occupying another and, in the process, infusing aspects its culture into the local way of life. But cultural imperialism hardly ended with the end of colonial and imperial rule. Rather, a new form of product, and increasingly service, cultural imperialism has emerged in which large, powerful organizations are able to infuse their products, all of which are embedded with a certain aspect of ‘‘culture” around the globe. Be it the powerful Disney purchasing local television stations around the world, translating, and showing its’ popular children and pre-teen shows, or Coca-Cola and its worldwide distributors ensuring that young people around the world develop a taste for its beverages, or Microsoft dominating the desktop in the developing world, one conceptualization of globalization is this view that globalization, pushed and dominated by large global concerns, results in cultural imperialism and, subsequently, to greater degrees of cultural homogenization. Homogenization of culture is largely thought of in terms of products, but the fear of course is that the use of products will eventually result in value change – so if you drink enough coke, you start to enjoy sweet drinks and this will become part of your daily habit, e.g., part of your culture. Cultural imperialism is at least partially buttressed by trade policies that advantage organizations in the developed world, policies that not only fail to level the playing field, but provide further advantages to the large global concerns (Beck, 2000). ICT plays a role in this cultural imperialism both as a product – ICT products themselves are ethnocentric cultural artifacts (Walsham, 2005) and as an enabler – ICT provides access to global products in markets that might previously have been isolated.

## 4. Interconnected business

A second perspective of globalization is that of the global inter-connectedness of businesses, or more specifically, the global interconnection of business processes. Fueled by global supply chains, business processes across organizations along the chain become connected, largely by information and technology. As the global supply chains become tighter, business processes become standardized, and, in essence, homogenized, so the first conceptualization of globalization is also evident in this second. And, as with the first conceptualization of globalization, it is the most powerful organizations that are able to push their processes on others. ICT plays a well-documented and central role in global supply chains. Not only is ICT the key enabler of such supply chains, but it also becomes an artifact of the supply chain itself: small firms must cope with multiple ICT standards if they wish to supply materials to more than one large organization. Thus, the ICT skills of the small firms must increase to meet the demands of the global supply chains. Some research is now examining the issue of how smal firms assimilate the ICT of larger firms and what influences their decisions to adopt multiple standards (Bala and Venkatesh, 2007). Subsidiaries of multinationals are also affected by ICT demands and expectations stemming from the corporate ICT strategy which may force upon them standardized processes that are antithetical to their culture or way of operating (Mohdzain and Ward, 2007; Lehmann, 2004).

## 5. Global village

A third conceptualization on globalization is that of the global village, or globally interconnected lives. The conceptualization portrays both an optimistic, as well as a darker side, of globalization. On the optimistic side, as large firms pursue cheaper and cheaper raw materials and supplies, they lean more on firms in the developing world. This may help fuel growth and development. Yet, simply involving firms in developing countries in the supply chain or selling products globally does not ensure development (Beck, 2000). It may even further increase economic disparity (Walsham, 2005). Moreover, it may deplete the natural resources of the less developed regions without substantially benefitting the region. But the notion of global village concerns not only how the practice of the developed world may affect the lives of people in the less developed world, but how the policies and behaviors of firms in the less developed world, may affect the lives of people in the developed world. In short, given the inter-connectedness of businesses, if suppliers from one region employ lax standards or use chemicals in quantities deemed dangerous in another country, unless there is very detailed information on these standards and contents of the raw materials, the end consumers will suffer. Just recently, the popular press has reported that Chinese makers of children’s jewelry sold by Walmart and other large chains uses a carcinogenic chemical in quantities that are dan gerous for children. The world is a global village: the processes used to produce the goods in China may affect the future health of the children in America (or other nations where the jewelry was marketed and sold) and vice versa. But as the real ization of how interconnected we are aggrandizes, we will demand higher accountability and, consequently, we will need more information and knowledge. This then will spur the need for increased global knowledge sharing across organizations and supply chains all the way to the individual consumer.

## 6. Summary

These three conceptualizations of globalization are related. As depicted in Fig. 1, as business become more interconnected, in order to operate most efficiently, they begin to share business processes. This sharing of business of processes leads to a certain cultural convergence among businesses. The interconnection of businesses also lead to interconnected lives (e.g., the global village). Collectively, these phenomena create the need for a tremendous degree of global knowledge and global knowledge sharing.

ICT plays a central role in this process. On the one hand, ICT makes the inter-connectedness of business more extensive and more efficient. ICT is also a product as well as a service that is a contributor to a certain degree of sameness across organizations around the world (cultural convergence). And finally, ICT will be essential in helping monitor and provide data/ information/knowledge about the materials and processes used to create products that are then distributed to a global market. Also central to the globalization phenomena is culture. Culture may be viewed as a constraint that poses challenges to managers trying to ensure a seamless supply chain. Culture may also be seen as a victim of globalization as global products and services threaten the sense, and reality, of local distinctiveness. Finally, culture may be an outcome of globalization: as individuals understand the repercussions of a global village, they become sensitized to their own role in the village and a new ‘‘village” mindset, and eventually culture, may ensue. To better understand culture and its role in globalization particularly as it pertains to ICT processes, services, products, and management, I next present a brief description of the IS-culture research.

## 7. Three waves of culture research

I here describe three waves of IS-culture research. The first wave I label ‘‘identifying the differences”; the second ‘‘explaining the differences”, and the third ‘‘managing the differences.” Obviously one cannot explain differences until they have been identified, nor can one manage cultural differences until one has identified them and understood them. Thus, these waves build upon each other. Because the first wave has received the most research attention, the discussion of this wave is also lengthier than the other two waves. But this does not imply that the second and third waves are less important, only that they are more emergent.

## 8. Identifying the differences

This first stream of IS research with culture as a significant component began largely with cross-cultural studies of IS phenomena at the national level. A key component to identifying cultural differences is to first define and measure culture. Toward this end, most of the work has drawn upon Hofstede’s (1980, 1991) taxonomy describing culture along the dimensions of power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism–collectivism, and masculinity–femininity. Hall’s division of culture into polychronism vs. monochronism was also sometimes used as the means of distinguishing national cultures (Hall and Hall, 1990).

With Hofstede’s definitions and measures of cultural variation as a foundation, the work focusing on identifying cultural differences treats culture as an independent variable or moderating variable: culture is seen to influence perceptions of IS design techniques (Kumar et al., 1990), perceptions of IS project risks (Keil et al., 2000), adoption of emerging IS (Thatcher et al., 2003), patterns of IS use (Chau et al.), relationships between IS use and outcome (Leidner et al., 1999), perceptions of service quality (Kettinger et al., 1995) and piracy and privacy attitudes (Husted, 2000; Milberg et al., 1995; Shore et al., 2001).

Ford et al. (2003) conducted a review of 57 IS papers that had used one or more of Hofstede’s dimensions. Based upon their analysis of the articles, they conclude that Hofstede’s dimensions have been applied to virtually all areas of IS research although some areas, such as IS management, receive more attention than other areas, such as IS Operations. In the review of the literature Tim Kayworth and I conducted (Leidner and Kayworth, 2006), 51 of the 82 papers reviewed examined culture at the national level (i.e., cross-cultural studies of IT) and of these, over 60% utilized one or more of Hofstede’s dimensions.

Other IS work on culture has emerged to challenge the reliance, and some might say, over-dependence, on Hofstede’s conceptualization. Straub et al. (2002), drawing from social identity theory introduced the notion of culture as a virtual onion. The virtual onion metaphor suggests that individuals have different ‘‘layers” of cultural identity. Different layers may hold more influence over a person at different points in time or when faced with different situational stimuli. Moreover, one layer may be particularly salient for an individual but have little influence over another individual’s identity (Gallivan and Srite, 2005; Karahanna et al., 2005). This notion of culture is based upon an individual level of analysis and hence, challenging the concept of culture as a ‘‘shared” system of meaning. Drawing upon the virtual onion model, Walsh and Kefi (2008) present their ‘‘spinning top model”. The latter model seeks to explain the ‘‘global culture” of an individual. The model suggests that some aspects of an individual’s culture are quite stable, represented by the core cylinders, while other aspects are more malleable, represented by the outer cylinders. The onion model and the ‘‘spinning top model” do lend themselves to research interested in understanding the relative importance of one ‘‘layer” – ethnicity, religion, occupation, nationality, gender, etc.) vs. another in influencing user beliefs and behaviors with regard to ICT. By broadening the scope of culture to include the core facets of an individual’s social identity, these models might give rise to new research within this stream on identifying cultural differences. And while the earliest research in this wave looked at national differences, and later organizational cultural differences, we are now seeing interest in what is essentially individual level culture, e.g. personality: Devaraj et al. (2008), for example, consider how individuals’ personality characteristics – openness, neuroticism, agreeableness, conscientiousness extraversion– influences their acceptance and use of ICT.

Another exciting development in this first ‘‘wave” of culture research consists of research that seeks to define and under stand IT department culture, ICT culture (Kaarst-Brown, 2004; Kaarst-Brown and Robey, 1999) or the occupational culture of IS/IT personnel (Guzman et al., 2008). Kaarst-Brown (2004) identified five IT cultural patterns: the fearful IT culture, the controlled IT culture, the revered IT culture, the demystified ICT culture, and the integrated IT culture. These five cultural patterns vary according to assumptions concerning who should control ICT, how central ICT is to strategy, how much ICT skills are valued at various levels within the organizational structure, how ICT expenditures are to be justified, and who benefits (or looses) from ICT. She suggests that IT cultural assumptions are influenced by the enculturation processes (individual and group socialization, knowledge set acquisition, leaders, heroes and mythos). The enculturation process is influenced by contextual factors (societal influences, individual influences, organizational influences) influence the enculturation processes (individual and group socialization, knowledge set acquisition, leaders, heroes and mythos) which influences the IT cultural assumptions, which then creates certain outcomes (conflict over ICT direction, innovation, etc.). Future research would be helpful to help identify different IT departmental cultures and how these cultures help, or hinder, such long-standing problems as strategic alignment and ICT turnover, among others.

## 9. Explaining the differences

A second wave of culture in IS research has an its aim not to identify differences, but to explain the repercussions of such differences to IT managers. This wave of research accepts that differences are present and that the differences themselves may be largely out of the control of managers. Thus, this stream of work attempts to explain when differences will be relevant to the IS manager. We know, for example, that Chinese managers are less likely to report news on bad projects and that such reluctance to report bad news might possibly lead to project escalation (Keil et al., 2000) but we do not know if, as a general rule, this difference is by and large desirable in contrast to the more open approach of western managers. Perhaps reporting the bad news would result in unnecessary responses and changes in strategy when none is really needed. So while a difference exists, in what circumstance is this difference important? Or, in terms of IT culture, might one IT culture be more likely to contribute to higher ICT performance than another, in a given context? If so, in what environments? There is notably less research in this wave than the first, yet it is emerging. In the tri-partide model of IT-culture conflict, Tim Kayworth and I try to develop a model to explain when culture will pose problems for IS implementations (Leidner and Kayworth, 2006). Specifically, when group member values, regardless of whether the driving values are national, organizational, sub-unit, occupational etc., diverge from the values embedded in a specific technology, or when group member values diverge from the general IT values held by the group members, or when the values embedded in a technology diverge from the general IT values held by a group, then there will be conflicts associated with the introduction and use of the technology. So to be able to successfully manage the introduction of the technology, IT managers must be cognizant of the value differentials and work to influence the perceptions of user groups. The model also suggests that as conflicts are reconciled – as users are exposed to system that initially clash with their values but then adapt the system to meet their values – values systems may change. The values associated with IT in general may be the first to change and as this occurs, subsequent introductions of technology will meet with less conflict.

## 10. Managing the differences

This third wave of IS-culture research picks up where the first two leave off: rather than looking at differences, or explaining when differences are important to IT managers, this third wave of IS-culture research seeks to provide insights on how to manage around differences. We know there are differences, and we know why there are these differences and what the implications are, but how do we manage across them? In essence, who should adapt to whom and how much? I recall reading a fascinating study from the inter-cultural communication literature many years ago that examined the extent to which cultural adaptations resulted in a favorable impression (Francis, 1991). Specifically, drawing upon social identity and simi larity/attraction theories, the study tested the effects of three degrees of adaptive behavior: substantial adaptation, moderate adaptation, and no adaptation. The subjects were Americans, Koreans, and Japanese. The results found that moderate adap tation resulted in a higher perception of inter-personal attractiveness. When the Japanese or Koreans engaged in the sub stantial adaptation (for example, shaking hands and using American slang even though they were not fluent in English), they were rated less favorably by the Americans than when they engaged in moderate adaptation (shaking hands and also bowing the head, using formal language). In cases of implementation of cultural artifacts – such as an IT solution – should the artifact be adapted to the culture or the culture to the artifact? Consultants will probably say that it is more economical to adapt the work behaviors/culture to the technology. Users will likely say that the system will have more positive outcomes if it is adapted to meet the users culture. We need studies to address this issue. Indeed, IS research is only recently taking on the questions related to managing around differences and who should know, and do, what. One study pushing the forefront of ideas in this area is Ang and colleagues (2007) work on cultural intelligence. Ang et al., 2007 develop measures for four dimensions of cultural intelligence (metacognitive, cognitive, motivational and behavioral) and suggest that it is not cultural difference per se that will influence decision making and performance but rather knowledge of the differences: individuals with high levels of cultural difference will be able to manage around the difference. It is when individuals are unaware of the potential for cultural difference that they are likely to engage in decisions and behaviors that create cultural conflicts.

## 11. The next wave of IS-culture research: the impact of ICT on culture

Having outlined three waves of IS-culture research, I will now pose some thoughts for future research. While there are numerous potential areas within each of the three above waves, I will focus on one particular area that I believe is ripe for study: the impact of ICT on culture.

Culture in IS research has served as the independent and moderator variables but has rarely been considered as a potential depended variable. One of the drawbacks of the extensive reliance on Hofstede’s dimensions, as well defined and operationalized as they are, is that they focus on those aspects of culture that are most stable. Culture is even conceptualized as something that is very slow to change. However, human existence is marked by change and adaptation: culture does change and I believe it changes more readily than our research has heretofore acknowledged.

The literature has traditionally viewed culture as being relatively stable, difficult to change, and persistent over time (Schein, 1999) with little emphasis placed on how culture might be dynamically shaped by other forces. This prevailing view has been reflected in numerous IS-culture studies that view culture as a stable variable used to explain variances across a wide range of ICT outcomes. Prime examples of this have been studies investigating the impacts of national culture on such things as GSS process and outcomes and ICT innovation (Chung and Adams, 1997; Mejias et al., 1996–1997; Tan et al., 1995). While these studies have added considerably to our knowledge of the relationship between culture and certain technology outcomes, they all presume a deterministic one-way relationship between culture, organizational behavior, and ICT outcomes. According to this deterministic view, culture is something that determines certain ICT outcomes (e.g. ICT diffusion across countries) through its effect on individual and organizational behaviors. The limiting aspect of this deterministic view is the notion that ICT must ‘‘fit” into the prevailing culture of the firm.

More recently, organizational theorists have begun to view culture as being more dynamic (Walsham, 2002); as something that is ‘‘continuously evolving, being constructed and reconstructed through interaction and intervention at the everyday level (Beck, 2000, p. 198). Consistent with this view, Robey and Azevedo (1994) argue that ICT is related to both cultura persistence as well as cultural transformation. Since a given information technology is laden with the cultural assumptions of its developer (Robey and Azevedo, 1994), its introduction may either re-enforce existing cultural values (persistence) or it may create cultural ‘‘shock” and become a catalyst for institutional cultural change (transformation) (Robey and Azevedo, 1994). The extent of ‘‘shock” will depend upon the level of congruence between the values embedded in the new ICT and the prevailing cultural values of the adopting group. These arguments suggest that researchers need to be prepared to discuss both cultural persistence as well as cultural transformation in their research efforts (Robey and Azevedo, 1994). Prior ISculture research has placed little emphasis on the possibility for a recursive relationship where culture and ICT may dynamically influence one-another (Robey and Azevedo, 1994). Therefore, while we would expect culture to influence information technology design, implementation, and use, we would also expect the introduction of new information technologies and their use to very gradually and subtly alter culture itself. Thus, as a first proposition, I suggest:

Proposition 1. The use of an information system designed to promote a particular behavior (such as best practice exchange, such as inter-departmental sharing of information, such as monitoring real-time information, such as regular communication) will, over time, alter the underlying value associated with the behavior.

What this proposition implies is that even if individuals are initially reluctant to use a system and do not see the value in the use of the system, if they continue to use the system over time (likely out of coercion or lack of choice), then eventually their values will reconcile with their behaviors. This proposition can be applied at the national, organizational, and sub-unit levels. Below I suggest propositions at each of these levels. These are not meant to represent an inclusive list of all possible propositions relating information technology to cultural change, but are meant to serve as examples of propositions that could be developed as part of a research project oriented toward understanding the impact of ICT on culture

National level cultures are arguably the slowest to change. However, there is already some evidence that technologies do impact national culture. For example, Walsham (2002) noted that while GIS systems were initially rejected in India because the Indian culture did not value maps, over the course of his study, there was an increasing awareness in India of the importance and usefulness of maps and map-based systems. So that even while in his particular study, the GIS were deemed failures because of lack of use, there was reason to expect that over a longer period of time, the culture would accept the technology and norms of behavior (i.e., reliance or lack of reliance upon maps in this case) would change.

In his anthropological work, Hall (1983) discovered major differences among cultures regarding values toward time-orientation. He found that individuals in more polychromic cultures have a greater tendency for the performance of multiple activities in parallel whereas individuals in more monochronic cultures prefer to focus on single issues one at a time before moving onto the next. Monochronic time-orientation tends to be oriented to tasks, schedules and procedures whereas ‘‘polychromic cultures are by their very nature oriented towards people” (Hall, 1983, p. 53). Furthermore, Hall (1983) notes that ‘‘all cultures with high technologies seem to incorporate both polychronic as well as monochronic functions,” (Hall, 1983, p. 58).

Therefore, it is plausible that the widespread availability of constant access to computer networks will indulge individuals in monochronic cultures to more polychronic behavior. For example, students can easily be seen working on assignments, browsing the Internet, sending emails all while taking notes in a classroom where their laptops are wirelessly connected to a network. In organizational virtual team research, some research has noted that managers of virtual teams complain of the polychronic behaviors of subordinates. Alavi and Leidner (2003) found that managers and virtual employees alike feel that their working life now involves doing many tasks simultaneously, such as listening in on a conference call while working on a presentation and responding to emails all concurrently. In monochronic cultures, such behaviors might seem very stressful and contribute to low satisfaction with the work environment, but it is likely that over time, these behaviors will become second-nature, having worked themselves into the culture itself such that the label ‘‘monochronic” would no longer apply in the business setting. With the onslaught of even smaller devices with wireless connection to the Internet and to corporate systems, we would expect such polychronic behaviors to become second-nature to even the traditionally monochronic cultures.

Proposition 1a. The widespread availability of 24 h IS availability will lead to more polychronic behavior even among monochronic cultures.

It is arguable that the potential impact of systems on organizational culture is greater when a larger part of the organization is affected, such as with systems integrated organization-wide, or even across organizations. It is notoriously difficult to implement global systems, not only because they are technically complex, but because they assume that what is best for one business unit will be equally good for others. Lehmann (2004) provides a detailed case study of a large Co-op where the CEO was pushing for a standardized enterprise system to be implemented in the various operations around the world. Some of the business units resented the implication that what worked well in the US branch would also work well in their branch. Others were simply culturally unprepared for such a maior change to their processes. Likewise, Pan and Leidner (2003 retrace the cultural challenges faced by a multi-national chemical company seeking to create a global knowledge sharing culture by implementing a global KM system. Enterprise systems are perhaps among the most challenging systems to implement globally because of the degree to which they impact core business processes. Even in relatively small companies, ERP implementations can be met with great resistance: Wagner and Newell (2004) provide the example of a large university’s attempt to implement an ERP and the difficulties posed by the academic culture. And while indeed many attempts at global IS and ERP implementations may ultimately be abandoned, for those organizations that stay the course and eventually succeed in changing their business processes, a corresponding change in organizational culture can be anticipated. Wilson (1999) makes the case that the control mechanisms embedded in ERP homogenize to a certain extent corporate cultures and that corporate change techniques are a powerful force that are often undertaken concurrent with ERP introduction Hence, while organizational cultures may be persistent, they are nevertheless malleable and may be more likely to change the longer a given system is used and the greater the use of the system is across organizational units.

Proposition 1b. The assimilation of systems requiring standardization and integration across business units will lead to more formalized, control-oriented organizational cultures.

Sub-unit ‘‘ownership” of information often presents a barrier for systems intended to share information across units. For example, in a study of executive information systems, Elliott (1992) found that decentralized government agencies in the state of Texas resisted, in the form of falsifying data, an EIS that was intended for the governor’s office. The agencies felt that they alone were entitled to monitor on a daily basis, their individual budgets. While such blatant resistance might be effective in disabling systems development efforts in some cases, it is unlikely that organizations will consistently tolerate such behavior. The plethora of systems designed to coordinate activities within and across sub-units in organizations will bring to the surface the unstated assumptions of sub-units and challenge these cultures such that, over time, the sub-unit cultures will adapt to the technologies, particularly when the technologies are strongly championed by senior management. For example, the introduction of team rooms among individuals who do not value open communication might initially be met with great resistance. If over time, the individuals widely use the team rooms to coordinate and communicate, this proposition suggests that the individuals would in fact begin to value open communication. This behavior was observed in an organization that implemented a knowledge management (KM) system. Sub-units began implementing their own KM and eventually, the success of the KM at the sub-unit level superseded that at the organizational level. Moreover, individuals in sub-units came to speak of contributing to the sub-unit KM as ‘‘second-nature” and as ‘‘the way we work” even though this entailed a cultural shift from a formally fairly closed culture (Alavi et al., 2005–2006).

Proposition 1c. The availability and use of horizontally integrated information technologies will lead to ‘‘closed” sub-unit cultures becoming more ‘‘open.”

While IS may inadvertently influence culture (Propositions 1a–c), there is also the possibility that senior managers can actively use ICT to help shape organizational culture. The IS-culture research has tended to view culture as something that is ‘‘fixed” and not subject of management intervention. This tendency has been reflected in the lack of IS-culture studies that specifically look at how a firm’s culture might be manipulated or transformed to achieve certain organizational objectives, and the role of information technology in achieving this change. Two exceptions to this are El Sawy (1985) study of cultural ‘‘infusion” within a research and development organization and Jarvenpaa and Leidner’s (1998) case study of a Mexican hitech publishing firm that reshaped its internal culture to cope with an environment hostile to innovation. Both of these case studies specifically addressed how firms were able to mold or shape their respective cultures to facilitate higher levels of ICT adoption (El Sawy, 1985) as well as innovative practices (Jarvenpaa and Leidner, 1998). There are many allusions in the literature to companies whose senior managers envision that the use of IS will help the organization achieve a new culture. For example, a study of the use of EIS by Mexican managers found that the senior managers believed that by changing the infor mation that they examined on a regular basis, lower level managers would begin to value achievement in the areas that were being monitored, and that eventually, the organization would move from a culture that valued quantity produced to one that valued profitability (Carlsson and Leidner, 1998). Similarly, senior managers at Buckman Labs believed that the introduction of a worldwide knowledge management system would create a more open and communicative culture (Pan and Leidner, 2003). Another study found that the norms held by business and professional circles had an important influence on decisions to adopt a given technology in organizations (Teo et al., 2003). The norms themselves could be influenced by the relationship channels of organizational members (Teo et al., 2003). Hence, the norms were malleable. These observations in the literature often arise as a by-product of the research rather than as a focus. I believe this is a ripe area for research, at the national, organizational, and sub-unit levels of analysis. Future IS-culture research should therefore take a more dynamic view of culture as something that can be shaped or manipulated to achieve their objectives. It is therefore proposed that:

Proposition 2. Senior managers may use information systems to purposefully effectuate cultural change in their organizations

Whereas Propositions 1, 1a, 1b, and 1c state that information systems will have a predictable, though possibly unintentional, consequence on aspects of culture, Proposition 2 suggests that IS can have predictable and intentional influences on culture. While it may be true that any cultural change will be imperceptible over the short-term (Walsham, 2002), minor shifts over the short-term will accrue such that long-term changes should be perceptible. This does not necessarily require longitudinal studies covering many years, but rather retrospective studies where individuals compare their work lives and values prior to the introduction and use of a system to the current.

## 12. At the intersection of IS-globalization and IS-culture research

In this final section, I will discuss some research topics that form at the intersection of the IS-globalization and IS-culture research. Specifically, I will expand upon the concept of global knowledge transparency depicted in Fig. 1. From my perspective, global knowledge transparency has two aspects: it involves providing more detailed product and service information to the consumer and it involves gathering more detailed knowledge about the individual. In terms of this first aspect, global ization is resulting in a greater need for individuals to be more informed about what they are purchasing and what they are ingesting and imbibing. Individuals may be aware that there are child factories in Indonesia where children, laboring for virtually no pay, are stitching brand name shoes. But unless an individual is aware, for example, that the particular pair of shoes that he/she is considering purchasing came from one such factory, then he/she is unlikely to be influenced by the knowledge that child-labor factories exist and are used by the firm that owns the shoe label. So, the consumer understanding of work practices of global firms has increased, but the ability to apply this knowledge, or perhaps the accountability associated with this knowledge, remains low as long as the knowledge remains general. We should, and eventually will, demand greater knowledge transparency on the part of organizations so that we can make informed purchase decisions. Individuals also will increasingly demand to be aware of the contents of the items they are purchasing – where the raw materials came from and perhaps even what the raw materials were exposed to. Consider for example the purchase of a piece of steak at a grocery. Currently, one may be aware of where the meat was packaged and where the animal was reared, but one is unaware, for the most part, of where the animal was slaughtered, what the conditions of the animal’s treatment were, or what constituted the animal’s diet. This latter may be particularly important in helping consumers make price/quality decisions. I personally look forward to the day when grocery shopping, I can point my i-phone at any item and see not only the raw ingredients, but where those ingredients were produced and when as well as where and when they were combined. I look forward to knowing not just from which country my clothing items were ‘‘made” but where the cloth and textile comprising the garment was produced. Having such information might, and most likely would, alter decisions on what to purchase according to the individual’s value system (e.g., culture). IS researchers can be at the forefront of studies looking at the implications of providing such precise information and knowledge to individuals.

The second aspect of global knowledge transparency involves gathering more detailed information about the individual, the individual’s lifestyle, health, consumption, and environmental habits and the consequences of these for others. Again, realizing that we do function in a global village and that each must take responsibility for their role in environmental pollution and quality of life, we can only be accountable for our contribution if we are informed about the consequences of our own behaviors. I believe that surveillance technologies, while currently still somewhat intrusive and negatively perceived in many cultures, will eventually become ubiquitous aspects of everyday life. It may begin rather benignly – one being grante a more favorable insurance rate if one agrees to health monitoring using a chip that keeps track of cholesterol, blood pressure, heart rate etc., but then become more pervasive as we discover cost-effective ways to monitor how much ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 }$ each individual contributes to the air, how much non-recyclable waste each individual contributes,

This latter area will bring with it sundry streams of interesting research – how people manipulate information to suit their purposes, how people engage in information deception, and how such detailed information does, or does not, change the value systems of individuals and eventually, the culture of societies. It is this latter that brings us full-circle back to the notion of ICT and culture: not only may technology exert influence on culture, but so too will the individual level information provided by ICT. In fact, we can potentially use information to change culture. For example, can we use the detailed provision of individual information concerning ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions and garbage production to change the attitudes, and eventually values, of the largely environmentally unconscious, mega-consumeristic, throw-away culture of certain parts of the US? If an individual starts having to pay according to the amount of non-recyclable waste he/she produces, would he/she make more conscious buying decisions and produce less garbage, or would he/she simply toss the excess garbage in a field? If an individual has to pay a surplus because his/her driving habits contribute more than an accepted level of ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emission per individual will that individual modify their behavior? When one sees aggregate numbers (by country or state or city), there is no accountability, but when one sees individual numbers, one might be more likely to change. And ultimately, a culture that is unable to change is a culture that will become extinct. Thus, in our studies of IS, globalization, and culture, we need to embrace the changes brought by ICT and look for creative ways to use ICT to improve not just the efficiencies of businesses operating in this global world, but the societies and individuals that comprise it.
