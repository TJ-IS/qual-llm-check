# How well do shopbots represent online markets? A study of shopbots’ vendor coverage strategy

Gove Allen<sup>1</sup> and Jianan Wu<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Brigham Young University, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Louisiana State University, U.S.A.

## Abstract

Consumers often use shopbots to search for information when making purchase decisions in Internet markets. Although they have varying sensitivity to shopbot bias, consumers generally prefer accurate market representation. However, in choosing the accuracy of market representation, shopbots must balance the desires of consumers with the costs of providing their services and with the desires of the vendors, who are often the largest source of their revenue. In this paper, we study how accurately shopbots represent a market and analyze the strategies shopbots adopt to achieve market representativeness. We theoretically identify two important drivers in shopbot vendor coverage strategy – how many vendors it covers (shopbot size) and which vendors it covers (shopbot affiliation) – and analytically show how the drivers affect shopbot market representativeness. We report the results of a large-scale study in which we collected 2.2 million vendor price listings from eight shopbots and develop metrics for measuring shopbot size, shopbot affiliation, and shopbot market representativeness. We found that (1) shopbots do not represent markets equally well; (2) size drives a shopbot’s market representativeness positively whereas affiliation drives a shopbot’s market representativeness negatively; (3) shopbots follow differnet vendor representative strategies to pursue market representativeness.

Keywords: electronic markets; shopbots; market representativeness; vendor coverage strategy

## Introduction

The broad adoption of the Internet as a channel for commerce has reduced consumers’ cost of comparing product offerings among retailers (e.g., Kocas, 2002). Simultaneously, it has dramatically increased the number of offerings from which consumers can choose for any given product. Thus, although the cost of individual offering comparisons is dramatically lower, the vast increase in the number of offerings among which to compare could still result in consumers facing high search costs. In some settings, online markets have been shown to result in poorer search performance than traditional markets (O<sup>¨</sup> o¨rni, 2003). Fortunately, several Internet sites consolidate comparison shopping and allow consumers to see offering information such as price, shipping terms, return policy, and vendor ratings from multiple vendors simultaneously. These comparison shopping sites (e.g., mysimon.com) use automated data retrieval techniques to collect offering information from multiple vendors and present it in a summary fashion to consumers (Etzioni, 1996).

A program for automated data retrieval in Internet environments is known as a software ‘robot’ or simply a ‘bot’ (Tan & Kumar, 2002). These software robots are ‘intelligent agents that use software tools and services on a person’s behalf’, including Ahoy!, MetaCrawler, Alta Vista, and Yahoo originally developed for general search purposes (Etizioni, 1996). In the mid-1990s, as online shoppers were becoming interested in comparing online vendor prices, a new type of software robot was developed specifically for online shopping purposes. Because many comparison shopping sites now use such bots to compile data for consumers, they have become known as ‘shopbots’.

Shopbots have played an important role as the Internet has become the primary channel for electronic commerce. Accordingly, they have attracted much attention in academic research in recent years (e.g., Wan, 2009). From the sellers’ perspective, the emergence of shopbots in online markets forces vendors to benchmark their retailing prices with each other and thus changes their competitive pricing structures (e.g., Haynes & Thompson, 2008). From the buyers’ perspective, shopbots lower buyer search cost (e.g., Smith, 2002). A reasonable projection of the effects of these forces predicts price convergence for identical products sold in online markets. For example, Tang et al. (2007) found that every 1% increase in shopbot use leads to \$0.41 decrease in price levels in the online book retailing industry. As such, the literature explains the persistence of price dispersion (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Smith, 2000) primarily as the result of the existence of a market segment of captive consumers who do not use shopbots (e.g., Waldeck, 2005) or of consumers who consider only a subsets of vendors (e.g., trusted ones) listed by shopbots (e.g., Kocas & Bohlmann, 2008), casting little double on whether shopbots represent vendors’ prices accurately.

There is good reason to believe that shopbots should represent markets accurately (Kocas & Bohlmann, 2008). Like most product development in competitive markets, shopbots should be developed to match user search preferences – if such preferences are known (Montgomery et al., 2004). While users may know their preferences for online vendor price searching (e.g., lower prices, certain price ranges, entire price distributions, etc.), shopbot designers might not be able to determine the search preferences of individual users. A simple strategy for shopbot developers to accommodate all search preferences is to let the shopbot cover all vendors in the market. Given complete market coverage, all users can meet their search requirements by sub-selection. Moreover, a shopbot that employs a full-coverage strategy would remove any question of market bias, increasing perceived fairness and building consumer trust (Datta & Chatterjee, 2008).

However, the full-coverage strategy leads to market inefficiency – search cost increase to consumers as a result of information overload (e.g., O<sup>¨</sup> o¨rni, 2003) and development costs increase for shopbots because of added complexity. Because shopbots are for-profit concerns, they must adopt a vendor-sampling strategy to balance their two competing goals: ‘[1] provide comprehensive and unbiased listing, and [2] have a workable revenue model’ (Michael Smith, quoted in Betts, 2001, p. 9).

In this paper, we examine the ‘market representativeness’ (defined as vendor price distributions in a given product market) that shopbots achieve in balancing these competing goals. In so doing, we seek to answer three questions: (1) Do all shopbots represent market prices equally well? (2) What contributes to shopbots’ market representativeness? (3) What vendor coverage strategies do popular shopbots adopt in pursuing market representativeness? To answer the questions rigorously, we present a large-scale study in which we tracked 459 products offered for sale by 84 online vendors as reported by eight separate shopbots for a 4-month time period (between March and July of 2002) in the Internet book market, totaling 2.2 million price observations.

We found the following: (1) Shopbots do not represent markets equally well. Some shopbots (e.g., mysimon .com) represent market (e.g., book market) more accurately than do others (e.g., isbn.nu); (2) Two important drivers of a shopbot’s vendor coverage strategy jointly determine a shopbot’s market representativeness: how many vendors it covers (shopbot size) and which vendors it covers (shopbot affiliation). The attribution to shopbots’ market representativeness is positive for shopbot size but negative for shopbot affiliation; (3) Shopbots follow different vendor coverage strategies to pursue market representativeness. They build market representativeness by focusing on size (e.g., dealtime .com) or affiliation (e.g., pricegrabber.com) or both (e.g., mysimon.com).

## Literature review and motivation

## Shopbots in information systems (IS) literature

From a broader perspective, the ideas of building computerized decision support systems (DSS) for organizations have been in the IS literature for quite some time (e.g., Keen & Morton, 1978). Early DSS were designed primarily to improve the productivity and effectiveness of managers and professionals (Keen, 1981). DSS designed primarily to improve the decision making of consumers are a relatively new development (Maes, 1994) and are becoming more prominent as Internet-based electronic commerce continues to advance. Although both types of DSS can be flexible enough to handle varied situations, individual buyer preferences for DSS serving mass populations (e.g., search engines and shopbots) may be less homogeneous and less structured than DSS designed according to the preferences of managers and professionals; this difference presents significant challenges for shopbot developers. Moreover, traditional DSS are developed within an organizational context, often at significant expense, whereas shopbots are developed by organizations independent of their users and are offered for use free of charge. Accordingly, while expert users of traditional DSS may seek comprehensiveness and in-depth insights, novice users of shopbots may be more concerned with user friendliness and fairness. Nonetheless, shopbots have made significant progresses in the past 15 years. Similar to how computerized DSS (e.g., BRANDAID; see Keen, 1981) changed the way firms conduct businesses, shopbots have revolutionized the way many consumers shop online.

BargainFinder (an agent reporting vendor pricing on music CDs), which debuted in 1995, is commonly referred as the first shopbot for consumers in online markets (e.g., Greenwald & Kephart, 2000). Early shopbots such as BargainFinder, aLogic, and PriceScan are recognized as first-generation shopbots. The major service of these first-generation shopbots is the presentation of vendor prices for a given product market (Fasli, 2006, 2009). First-generation shopbots were entirely independent from vendors and did not accept payment for inclusion or preferred listing status. Most current shopbots (e.g. MySimon, PriceGrabber, Bizrate) are among the second generation (Fasli, 2009) and began emerging in electronic markets in the early 2000s. This generation of shopbots enhanced consumers’ online shopping experiences by providing additional information including vendor reputation, shipping costs, tax information, and others. A salient characteristic of this generation of shopbots is that they are commercial products. Many vendors now pay shopbots for preferred listing placement through affiliation programs (Wan, 2009).

User search preferences have served as an important foundation for designing electronic agents (e.g., search engines, shopbots, etc.) because market orientation often leads to higher customer satisfaction (Wu & Padgett, 2004). This is particularly the case when user search preferences are structured and known to agent designers. For example, the recommendation system by Amazon .com analyzes the past browsing and purchase history of users to make product recommendations. This design approach for shopbots also creates value for users if it includes users’ preferences on which store to search, how long to wait, and which offers to present (e.g., Montgomery et al., 2004). The basic idea of designing efficient shopbots has recently been extended to other situations. For example, Garfinkel et al. (2006) examined extending the design of shopbots from comparisons on single items to bundles of items, presumably because the search preferences for a bundle may not be a simple addition of search preferences of single items in the bundle. Garfinkel et al. (2008) further addressed adding recommender systems to the basic functionality of shopbots.

## Shopbot dilemma

Market representativeness Regardless of the generation of shopbots, the most important information users seek from shopbots is impartial vendor price information, which is at the center of shopbots’ market representativeness. We define the market representativeness of a shopbot by how close it comes to representing the vendor-pricing distribution of a product market.

A shopbot with accurate market representativeness may be preferred by both shopbot designers and users for important reasons. From a shopbot designer’s perspective, such representativeness allows for the simultaneous accommodation of users with very different or unknown search preferences (Jahng et al., 2002). A bargain hunter may simply want the lowest price. A conservative buyer may purchase only from reputable vendors but would want to know what the lowest price is so they can quantify the vendor reputation premium. A loyal consumer may have a preferred set of vendors (a consideration set; Wu & Rangaswamy, 2003) and wants to select the lowest price from that set. Still, a cross-channel buyer may just want to get an idea of the price distribution (e.g., average price or price range) at the shopbot so she can make an informed decision when making an offline purchase. Moreover, shopbots do not require user registration and hence do not record users’ search history. To accommodate these heterogeneous and unknown search preferences, shopbots offer users the option to organize their results in a way that is more congruent with individual search preferences (e.g., allowing results to be sorted by base price, by total price, by vendor reputation, or by product availability).

From the users’ perspective, on the other hand, accurate market representation facilitates informed purchase decisions (O<sup>¨</sup> o¨rni, 2003), which increases the likelihood that their search will culminate in a purchase (Heijden et al., 2003). Unbiased listing of vendor prices increases perceived fairness, building consumer trust in shopbots (Datta & Chatterjee, 2008). These two perspectives may lead to the conclusion that shopbots are developed to accurately represent the market, as evidenced by the bulk of early studies relying on shopbots to study vendor-pricing behavior in online markets (e.g., Kauffman & Wood, 2000; Baye & Morgan, 2001; Ellison & Ellison, 2001; Baylis & Perloff, 2002).

Vendor coverage strategy A basic question for shopbot designers is to decide which vendors to represent. Vendor coverage strategy determines a shopbot’s revenue model (Bhargava & Feng, 2002) and is the solution to the shopbot dilemma. Shopbots are not the products of altruistic entities whose sole purpose is to serve the public. Their creators are profit-motivated enterprises that must balance the desires of consumers with the costs of providing their services and with the desires of the vendors, who often ultimately are the largest source of their revenue (Smith, 2002). Shopbots typically earn revenue by selling advertising space on their pages, by charging a fee to vendors for listing their products or for prominent placement in the listing, by earning a percentage of sales that come through their channel, by collecting affiliation program fees, or by selling aggregate marketing research (Clark, 2000). As such, while an impartial shopbot may match user search preference, shopbots have to take the bottom line into consideration. Similar to the issue for search engines (Bradlow & Schmittlein, 2000), this dilemma raises concern about whether shopbots are as representative as users perceive them to be.

Indeed, Bhargava & Feng (2002) argue that shopbots are motivated to bias the market representation unless users’ sensitivity to bias is very high. They argue that choices about bias are central to the strategic decisionmaking process of information gatekeepers. They also show that an optimal level of bias can be chosen to balance the desire of the consumers (i.e., users’ sensitivity to bias) and shopbots’ cost structure. A study of the reported revenue structure of shopbots may provide insight into which shopbots have higher motivation for bias but there is no guarantee that such motivation would produce actual bias in market representation.

Two fundamental components of shopbots’ vendor coverage strategy – shopbot size and shopbot affiliation – are particularly salient on biasing shopbots’ truthful representation of a product market. We define shopbot size as the number of vendors a shopbot chooses to represent. Because the benefits (revenues, user perceived fairness, etc.) and costs (e.g., technological capabilities, user cognitive costs, etc.) for shopbot developers are both increasing functions of shopbot size (Johnson & Payne, 1985; Montgomery et al., 2004), shopbots will choose an optimal shopbot size at which the marginal benefits and marginal costs are balanced. In practice, virtually any shopbot size that is less than complete market coverage will result in a biased market representation.

We define shopbot affiliation as the set of vendors a shopbot chooses to represent. A shopbot that lists offerings only from vendors who have paid to be listed employs a selection mechanism that does not attempt to fairly represent the true market. Although it is possible, such a result would be entirely serendipitous, particularly for second-generation shopbots (Wan, 2009). Similarly, shopbots that choose which vendors to report based solely on favorable commission structures have little likelihood of accurately reflecting the market. Finally, although the advertising model of revenue generation does not directly introduce a motivation for bias in shopbot reports, its efficacy as the sole source of revenue is questionable.

We investigate how the two components (size and affiliation) of shopbots’ vendor coverage strategy contribute to their market representativeness in order to understand how shopbots address the dilemma of satisfying user search preferences while meeting their revenue needs. In our investigation, we adopt the quantitative IS research method (or Quantitative Positivist Research), which has three steps: (1) we develop the hypotheses on how the shopbot size and shopbot affiliation contribute to shopbots’ market representativeness from statistical theory; (2) we design a field study to collect data and develop appropriate measures for the three important constructs (i.e., size, affiliation, and market representativeness); and (3) we use statistical methods to formally test the hypotheses. ## Hypothesis development

To quantitatively understand why shopbots do not represent a market equally well, we decompose shopbot mean square error because statistically, a shopbot that represents the market well should have a small mean square error (or a quadratic loss in decision theory). The shopbot size indicates how many vendors a shopbot can find. ## Research design and measurement development

## Research design

We study shopbots’ vendor coverage strategy and their market representativeness by examining their performance. The advantage of this approach is twofold: (1) we limit our findings to what shopbots actually do, rather than to what they intended to do; and (2) shopbots give public access to vendor-pricing data they choose to represent a given product market. As such, our approach not only allows a direct examination of how shopbots actually perform in electronic markets, but it also allows an assessment of the external validity of our hypotheses. Because our approach examines shopbot behavior only (to the exclusion of shopbot intention), our analysis will provide no insight into how the performance of a specific shopbot may be expected to change over time.

First, we consider the products of interest for the study $( k = 1 , \ldots , K )$ . The book market is commonly selected in studying electronic markets because the universal use of the international standard book number (ISBN) allows the precise identification of exactly identical books offered by different vendors (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Smith, 2000; Clay et al., 2001; Smith & Brynjolfsson, 2001; Montgomery et al., 2004). Additionally, the book market is one of the most active online markets in terms of both number of transactions and dollar volume. We then focused on the four subcategories of books: bestseller hardcover, bestseller paperback, classics, and new titles. The best sellers are from the New York Times Best Seller lists (nytimes.com). Classic Books were selected at litrix.com (a site for full text of public domain books) and their titles were converted to ISBN number at bn.com, which was selected to acquire the ISBNs because (1) it provides a comprehensive list of titles; and (2) it provides titles even if they are not offered for sale. New Titles were collected from the ‘Coming Soon’ section of randomhouse.com on a weekly basis. We identified a total of 459 different books in the four subcategories.

Second, we consider the time window $( t = 1 , \ldots , T )$ . One random snapshot from a shopbot will give a good assessment of its performance at a specific time, but it does not provide a measure of the reliability of such an assessment for the shopbot; Lawrence & Giles (2000) observed that search engines’ performance changes across time. Moreover, shopbots update their prices from vendors differently. Therefore, reliable assessment cannot be made from a random snapshot. To increase the reliability of the assessment, repeated sampling across time for the same product at the same shopbot is desired. For this purpose, a duration of time for the assessment has to be chosen. We have tracked the daily prices of the selected products from the selected eight shopbots at the same time everyday for four consecutive months (March through June 2002).

Third, we consider the set of shopbots $( i = 1 , \ldots , I )$ . For some research objectives, shopbots should be selected such that the union of the shopbots represents the true market well. For others, market representativeness is not as important. For example, in the current study, our first objective is to identify if biases exist in the way that shopbots represent a market. To demonstrate this, one need only collect parallel data sets from two or more shopbots and compare how they characterize the common market. If the statistical analysis of the two data sets indicates significant difference, then a bias clearly exists. Such analysis does not require a reasonable assessment of the true market characteristics because if the two samples are significantly different, then they cannot both fairly represent the market simultaneously. However, some of the findings of this study will be strengthened if the assertion is deemed reasonable that data gathered from the collection of eight shopbots adequately represents the market. As such, in the selection of the shopbots, we attempt to identify a set that will offer broad market coverage. Since the selection of shopbots to adequately represent a market is the very question that this study examines, we employ a heuristic selection technique. This approach features two heuristics to compensate the lack of prior knowledge on this issue. First, the more shopbots, the better. Theoretically, if we assume that data reported at each shopbot represents an independent sample of the real market. then the union of the data reported at all shopbots should represent a sample with smaller sample variance. Ideally, every active shopbot that provides information about any of the products in the basket of products should be included. However, the dynamic nature of the shopbot arena makes attaining the ideal only remotely possible and the conclusive demonstration that the ideal has been achieved virtually impossible. Second. the more diversification. the better This set should include both shopbots that try to cover a wide array of products (better economy of scale) as well as those that focus on a narrow market (better niche knowledge). We included eight shopbots in our study: Addall. Bizrate. Dealtime. ISBN. MySimon. PriceGrabber PriceScan, and Yahoo. This set of shopbots was identified through a web search using the Google search engine. Although the search revealed dozens of shopbots, these eight were selected because they provided substantial coverage of the online book market and they presented vendor and product data in a summary manner.

Finally, we needed an agent that can automatically fetch the data from the shopbots simultaneously for each of the products in the basket and for each day within the time window. We developed a distributed Internet data retrieval agent, named ‘Synchronous Electronic Commerce Research Engine and Toolkit’ (SECRET) Agent, which can collect both longitudinal and cross-sectional data from publicly available web sites. Developed in Practical Extraction and Report Language, SECRET Agent consists of a dedicated server that manages the list of web pages to be archived. This server accepts connections from an arbitrary number of clients, which may be geographically disperse, to deliver individual data collection tasks, consisting of both the location of the page to archive as well as the script needed to parse and save the data. A second server is dedicated to storage of the web pages retrieved by the data collection nodes as well as the data parsed from those pages (e.g., book titles and prices). Its distributed nature allows for very rapid parsing and storage of hypertext data. The configuration used for the current study involved just 12 client machines, each running up to 20 copies of the data collection client program. Under this configuration, SECRET Agent processed more than 1,000 web pages per minute, archiving approximately 600 megabytes of data per hour. The client operates in various environments, including Unix, Linux, and Mac OS X.

## Measurement development

Shopbot market representativeness We measure shopbots’ market representativeness in three different ways: (1) Average Price (AP); (2) Minimum Price (MP); and (3) Price Dispersion (PD). These three measures were chosen because they represent the key vendor price information users need to make informed decisions. First, as the first moment of the VPD, AP is a quick summary for the market and provides the benchmarking point for any price quotes the customer can obtain (e.g., offline price quotes). Second, MP may be the focus of customers who use shopbots to search for the lowest prices. Third, PD signals the market competition and quantifies the savings a customer can experience as a result of conducting the search and choosing to purchase at the lowest price in the market.

The definitions of AP and MP are unanimous, but that of PD is not. We examine two commonly adopted measures of PD in the literature (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Smith, 2000): (a) Price Range (PR), an absolute measure with dollar units, and (b) Price Percentage Range (PPR), a unit-free, mean-adjusted relative measure. From customers’ point of view, PR represents the absolute dollar amount of the savings and PPR represents the percentage of the savings.

Eq. (1) delivers the shopbot errors on market representativeness with respect to a particular measure (e.g., AP), provided that we know the true population value for that measure. Although identifying the true population value is operationally infeasible, we approximate the true population value as that obtained from the union of the vendor prices identified by all eight shopbots. We calculate the shopbot errors using Eq. (1). For example, the shopbot error of shopbot i for book k at time t on average price $E \_ A P _ { i k t } = A P _ { i k t } – A P _ { U k t } ,$ where $A P _ { i k t }$ is the average price of book k at time t across the vendors identified by shopbot i, and $A P _ { U k t }$ is the true average price for book k at time t (i.e., the average price for book k and time t across the vendors identified by all eight shopbots combined).

Shopbot size If a shopbot’s vendor coverage strategy includes a particular vendor, it should cover that vendor fully (i.e., all products at all times from the vendor).

However, this is often not the case in practice. A shopbot’s coverage for the vendor may be inconsistent across product and time. One main reason is the shopbot’s technology capability. To justify this capability, we measure a shopbot’s size and affiliation at product and time level.

For a given product and a shopbot (or a set of shopbots), an appropriate operational measure of shopbot size (SS) should take the following important issue into consideration: Conditional on the product being offered by a vendor at a given time, does the shopbot identify the vendor(s) at the time? Let i be the index for a subset of shopbots $( i = 1 , \ldots , I ) _ { i }$ , k the index for product $( k = 1 , \ldots , K ) ,$ j the index for vendors $( j = 1 , \ldots , J ) ,$ and t the index for time $( t = 1 , \ldots , T )$ . Let $\gamma _ { i j k t }$ be the capability of a shopbot set i in finding product k at vendor j at time t. $\gamma _ { i j k t } = 1$ if the shopbot set i does and $\gamma _ { i j k t } = 0$ if it does not. Therefore, $C _ { i k t } = \Sigma _ { j } ^ { J }$ y<sub>ijkt</sub> represents the size of shopbot i for product k at time t by assuming that vendor j does offer the product k at time t.

To justify the product availability, we let U be the union of all shopbots considered. Let $\gamma _ { U j k t } = 1$ if any one of the shopbot identifies product k at vendor j at time t and 0 otherwise. Then $C _ { U k t } = \Sigma _ { j = 1 } ^ { J } \gamma _ { U j k t }$ approximates the condition of whether product k is available at vendor j at time t. By taking this into consideration, we define the size of shopbot i for product k at time t as

$$
S S _ {i k t} = \frac {C _ {i k t}}{C _ {U k t}}.\tag{3}
$$

The range of the SS measure is between 0 and 1. $S S _ { i k t } { = } 5 9 \%$ indicates that for product $k ,$ shopbot i identifies 59% of the vendors’ offerings identified by the union of eight shopbots at time t. Although to state that it also can be interpreted as identifying 59% of the true market’s offerings requires an assumption that the union set of shopbots identifies the entire market’s offerings, it is worth recognizing that such an assumption does not change the relative scale of $S S _ { i k t }$ across shopbots $( i = 1 , \ldots , I )$ for any given product k. In other words, whether the union set of shopbots identifies the entire market’s offerings or not does not change the relative validity of our proposed SS measures.

Shopbot affiliation Shopbot affiliation captures the attribution of the price biases induced by the affiliated relationship between a shopbot and a vendor. An appropriate operational measure of shopbot affiliation (SA) should capture how consistently shopbots affiliate with particular vendors in the observation window. Let $w _ { i j k } { = } ( f _ { i j k } ) / ( \Sigma _ { j { = } 1 } ^ { J } f _ { i j k } )$ , where $f _ { i j k } = \Sigma _ { t = 1 } ^ { T } \gamma _ { i j k t }$ is the frequency that a shopbot set i in finding product k at vendor j for the time window, then $w _ { i j k }$ is the proportion of the time a shopbot set i in finding product k at vendor j for the duration of the time window $( \Sigma _ { j = 1 } ^ { J } w _ { i j k } = 1 )$ and represents the strength of the affiliation between shopbot set i and vendor j for a product k at the duration of the time window. Therefore, we define shopbot affiliation of shopbot set i for book k at time $t , S A _ { i k t } ,$ as the aggregation of such affiliation strengths for each vendor that the shopbot set i can identify for book k at time t. Mathematically, it can be written as:

$$
S A _ {i k t} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} w _ {i j k} y _ {i j k t}.\tag{4}
$$

Just like the measure of SS, the measure for shopbot affiliation is also intuitively appealing. The range of the shopbot affiliation measure is also between 0 and 1. If a shopbot represents few vendors with small affiliation strength for a book at a particular day, then its shopbot affiliation is weak for that book on that day. However, if a shopbot consistently identifies a set of vendors for a book on each day of the time window, then the shopbot affiliation will be 1 for each day of the time window, suggesting the highest degree of shopbot affiliation.

## Hypothesis testing

## Data and preliminary analysis

Our data set consists of over 2.2 million product/price observations in the online book market. We conduct a preliminary analysis here before we formally test the hypotheses. We examine the shopbots along two important dimensions: (1) How well does a shopbot represent vendors’ products (a shopbot’s vendor coverage strategy)? and (2) How well does a shopbot represent vendors’ prices (a shopbot’s market representativeness)?

We use a shopbot’s vendor coverage distribution (VCD), defined as the distribution of the number of times a vendor was identified by the shopbot for a product, to understand its vendor coverage strategy, whereas we use a shopbot’s vendor price distribution (VPD), defined as the distribution of the vendor prices identified by the shopbot for a product, to understand its market representativeness. For each product, we compared the first two moments (mean and variance) for both VCD and VPD for all eight shopbots together and for all possible pairs if enough data exist to perform such tests. Because of the volume of the tests performed (19,029 tests for mean and 18,364 tests for variance), we report in Table 1 only the percentage of the products in which the means or variances between involved shopbots are unequal at a 0.05 level.

The test results of all eight shopbots together clearly show that the shopbots do not provide consistent results in either VCD or VPD. The hypotheses that all eight shopbots have equal mean (equal variance) on VCD is rejected at 0.05 level for 75% (54%) of the products. The hypotheses that all eight shopbots have equal mean (equal variance) on VPD is rejected at 0.05 level for 99% (90%) of the products, an even higher percentage of inequality.

Although the pairwise test results appear to support the conclusion made for all eight shopbots evaluated together, they also show that some shopbots resemble each other more than others. For example, the difference between MySimon and PriceScan (7% on mean and 13% on variance) is much smaller than that between MySimon and Dealtime (91% on mean and 76% on variance) for VCD. The same pattern seems to hold for VPD as well.

## Hypothesis testing

Our preliminary analysis shows that shopbots report inconsistent results regardless of VCD or VPD. This finding is not surprising. Shopbots are not magic. All of the information displayed at a shopbot’s web site is made available by a set of computer programs that collect data from online vendors (or potentially from other shopbots). These programs, whether developed in cooperation with online vendors or not, must be kept up to date as vendors change the way in which they make information about their products available. The need to continually update programs to keep pace with changes in vendors’ sites represents a substantial cost for shopbots. Moreover, the effort to update a vendor is independent of the vendor’s volume. As a result, shopbots must decide which vendors to use in their service. The drivers of this decision are different for different shopbots. Some shopbots have broader breadth of product interests, such as MySimon, Dealtime, and Bizrate. Other shopbots are niche players. For example, ISBN and Addall aggregate information for books only. Shopbots’ different strategic goals combined with different scarce resources necessitate that shopbots will not cover the same set of vendors.

We used the measurement developed for shopbot market representativeness and calculated it with respect to average price, minimum price, and price dispersion, and summarize them in Table 3. As with reporting shopbot size and shopbot affiliation, we report only the averaged results across products (k) and time (t) for each shopbot.

Apparently, not all the shopbots report the same average prices. For example, Dealtime reports prices 99 cents higher than the union on average, and MySimon reports prices 47 cents higher than the union on average. Others report lower average prices: Bizrate reports prices 55 cents lower than the union on average and PriceGrabber reports prices 53 cents lower than the union on average. PriceScan seems to be the shopbot which reports the closest average prices to the union. Dealtime’s errors on average prices are more than 10 times the size of those of PriceScan’s.

Similar to average prices, shopbots are quite unequal in reporting minimum prices. Unlike average prices, the minimum price reported from a single shopbot has to be higher than (or possibly equal to) that from the union. At one end of the spectrum, Dealtime reports minimum prices \$5.07 higher than the union on average. However, even at the other end, PriceScan reports minimum prices \$1.63 higher than the union on average. The magnitude of the errors on AP and MP suggests that shopbots represent average prices better than extreme prices (minimum price or maximum prices) in general.

The above analysis shows how a shopbot may choose to improve its market representativeness through three different vendor coverage strategies: (a) focusing on shopbot size by covering more vendors; (b) focusing on shopbot affiliation by affiliating with fewer vendors; or (c) taking a balanced approach to both size and affiliation. To see how such strategies are used by shopbots, we plotted shopbot size and shopbot affiliation together in a two-dimensional space and report it in Figure 2. Figure 2 seems to show that MySimon and PriceScan pursue the ‘more vendors’ strategy, Bizrate and Dealtime pursue the ‘less affiliation’ strategy, whereas Addall follows a ‘balanced’ strategy. However, PriceGrabber, Yahoo, and ISBN seem not only to cover fewer vendors than others, but also to have higher affiliations with these fewer vendors; thus they are less likely to accurately represent the market.

## Discussion

## Implications for practice

Our research identifies two important factors that play essential roles in solving the dilemma of shopbot management. First, shopbot size contributes to shopbot market representativeness positively. A shopbot may increase its market representativeness by representing more vendors in the market. As such, the implication for shopbot management is to understand the marginal return of market representativeness with respect to the information technology investment to the shopbots. Second, shopbot affiliation contributes to shopbot market representative negatively. Affiliation with certain vendors is an important strategy for shopbots to generate revenue. Therefore, the question for shopbot managers is not whether they should be affiliated with vendors, but rather which vendors they should be affiliated with. As a whole, shopbot managers may choose different combinations of shopbot size and affiliations to gain market representativeness.

Our research suggests that there is a vendor coverage strategy space for shopbots to make winning choices in pursuing their desired market representativeness. Indeed, our empirical analysis of eight well-know shopbots shows that they have adopted differentiated strategies. Some shopbots (e.g., MySimon) followed the shopbot size strategy to reach more vendors. Other shopbots (e.g., Bizrate) followed the shopbot affiliation strategy by exhibiting lower affiliation. Still other shopbots (e.g., Addall) followed a balanced strategy by increasing shopbot size and decreasing shopbot affiliation simultaneously. Smart choices are made not only to fulfill their targeted market representativeness, but also to avoid direct competition with each other.

Our research suggests that consumers should be aware that shopbots may strive to truthfully represent market, but do not always do so because of other important considerations and constraints. As such, consumers may want to search more than one shopbot to get an impartial picture of the markets. By the same token, vendors should also be aware that their listings at different shopbots have different effectiveness because of the set of competitors they are listed with. However, avoiding direct price competition by using other searchable attributes (e.g., customer service reviews) may be critical for higher priced vendors to maintain their premiums while gaining exposure from shopbot listings.

## Implications for research

The availability of vast amounts of information provided by shopbots allows academic researchers to use shopbots as data collection agents for examining a wide array of research questions on vendor-pricing behavior, including price dispersion (e.g., Clay et al., 2001; Smith & Brynjolfsson, 2001; Clemons et al., 2002), price distribution (e.g., Kauffman & Wood, 2000), price elasticity (e.g., Ellison & Ellison, 2001), price competition (e.g., Baye & Morgan, 2001), and price discrimination (e.g., Baylis & Perloff, 2002). These early studies have significantly contributed to our understanding on vendor-pricing behavior in Internet markets, but these studies rely on shopbots’ market representativeness for their empirics. Although some researchers have cast doubt on the belief that a single shopbot should give an accurate representation of a market (e.g., Kauffman & Wood, 2000), no prior research has formally investigated this important issue. Consequently, researchers have continued to rely on individual shopbots as accurately representing online markets (Kocas & Bohlmann, 2008).

Thus, an immediate implication for research is: Will the significant biases in market representation shown in the current study lead to conclusion errors when researchers rely on a single shopbot for data collection? This is an important question because early studies via quantitative positivist research (QPR) methods in IS literature (in which data representativeness is important) primarily rely on data collected from a single shopbot. If the data source from a single shopbot does not represent the market well (which our study shows is likely to be the case), the empirical generalizations should be reassessed using more representative data. A good guideline for researchers to mitigate the problem is to conduct a small-scale screening of the sample representativeness of the data collected from different sources. If conditions allow, researchers should collect data from two or more shopbots, rendering higher sample representativeness in general.

From the IS design and development perspective, it is important to realize that the dilemma shopbots face is not unique to shopbots alone, but is in fact quite general among web-based agents serving mass markets. Search engines rely on advertising revenue. Reputation systems, such as are used by eBay, facilitate transaction volumes necessary to maintain profitability. To design these agents, developers must balance the bottom line with the impartiality of market representation in which users’ perception and attitude toward the agents should be integrated (Theotokis et al., 2008). Our study suggests a method that agent developers can adopt for performance evaluations. Even if biases are unavoidable, it might be useful for agents to communicate with users in some ways (e.g., Google marks paid placements as ‘Sponsored Links’). After all, trust building is key for such agents to be successful in mass online markets (Ibrahim & Ribbers, 2009; Qureshi et al., 2009).

## Limitations and future research

Our study has several limitations that invite future research. First, we use the posted price (price seen) to measure shopbot market representativeness. An interesting research question is whether our results are robust if we use the weighted price (price paid). The weights can be specified as the sales volume at the vendor level (Brynjolfsson & Smith, 2000) or at the vendor and product combination level.

Second, we have shown in this paper that shopbot size and shopbot affiliation are two important sources of shopbot error, but we are silent in offering methods in correcting such errors. The lack of an approach to removing bias from the data for a specific shopbot is a limitation for practical considerations. Removing bias unavoidably calls for a formal (statistical) approach that relaxes the assumption that the vendor prices distribution that resulted from the union of eight shopbots represents the true distribution.

Third, we have used an empirical approach to test our hypotheses. Although this research method is commonly used in the IS literature and tests the external validity well, it does not address the internal validity of our hypotheses. One approach to testing the internal validity is to measure each shopbots’ vendor coverage strategy by directly surveying the company managers.

Finally, we have used data from the book industry to test our hypotheses. Although the book industry has many advantages for research and has been used extensively in IS literature because of its early success at Amazon.com, more evidence is needed for empirical generalization from other industries. This is important because business-to-consumer enterprises in online markets have changed dramatically in both industry depth and breadth in the past decade.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated whether different shopbots represent the online book market equally well. Using a data set collected from eight shopbots on 459 products for 4 months with 2.2 million observations in the Internet book market, we found that shopbots do not represent the market equally well. Some of the shopbots (e.g., MySimon.com) represent the market more accurately than others (e.g., isbn.nu).

Keen (1991) suggested two important dimensions for measuring IS research: relevance and rigor. On relevance, how significant will it be for stakeholders (e.g., researchers, policy makers, consumers, shopbots etc.) if a shopbot is not representative? ## Appendix

Assume $p _ { i } { \sim } U [ 0 , 1 ]$ , and its probability density function (PDF) and cumulative probability function (CDF) is $f ( p )$ and $F ( p )$ , respectively.

Thus, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname{Var} (p _ {i}) = E (p _ {i} ^ {2}) - (E (p _ {i})) ^ {2} \\ = \int_ {0} ^ {1} p ^ {2} f (p) d p - \left(\int_ {0} ^ {1} p f (p) d p\right) ^ {2} = \frac {1}{3} - \frac {1}{4} = \frac {1}{1 2}, \end{array}
$$

and

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Var(average   price) } \\ = \text { Var } \left(\frac {1}{n _ {i}} \sum_ {1} ^ {n _ {i}} p _ {i}\right) = \frac {1}{n _ {i} ^ {2}} \sum_ {1} ^ {n _ {i}} \text { Var } (p _ {i}) = \frac {1}{1 2 n _ {i}}. \end{array}\tag{A.1}
$$

The CDF and PDF of the minimum price $\operatorname* { m i n } ( p _ { 1 } , . . . , p _ { n _ { i } } )$ are

$$
\begin{array}{l} G (p) = \operatorname * {P r} [ \min (p _ {1},..., p _ {n _ {i}}) <   p ] \\ \quad = 1 - \operatorname * {P r} [ \min (p _ {1},..., p _ {n _ {i}}) > p ] \\ \quad = 1 - \operatorname * {P r} [ p _ {1} > p,..., p _ {n _ {i}} > p ] \\ \quad = 1 - \prod_ {1} ^ {n _ {i}} \operatorname * {P r} [ p _ {i} > p ] \\ \quad = 1 - \prod_ {1} ^ {n _ {i}} \left\{1 - \operatorname * {P r} [ p _ {i} <   p ] \right\} \\ \quad = 1 - \prod_ {1} ^ {n _ {i}} \left\{1 - F (p) \right\} = 1 - (1 - p) ^ {n _ {i}} \end{array}
$$

and

$$
g (p) = \frac {d G (p)}{d p} = n _ {i} (1 - p) ^ {n _ {i} - 1}.
$$

Similarly, the CDF and PDF of the maximum price max $( p _ { 1 } , . . . , p _ { n _ { i } } )$ are

$$
\begin{array}{l} H (p) = \operatorname * {P r} [ \max (p _ {1},..., p _ {n _ {i}}) <   p ] \\ = \operatorname * {P r} [ p _ {1} <   p,..., p _ {n _ {i}} <   p ] \\ = \prod_ {1} ^ {n _ {i}} \operatorname * {P r} [ p _ {i} <   p ] = \prod_ {1} ^ {n _ {i}} \{F (p) \} = p ^ {n _ {i}} \end{array}
$$

and

$$
h (p) = \frac {d H (p)}{d p} = n _ {i} p ^ {n _ {i} - 1}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Var(minimum   price) } & = \int_ {0} ^ {1} p ^ {2} g (p) d p - \left(\int_ {0} ^ {1} p g (p) d p\right) ^ {2} \\ & = \frac {n _ {i} - 1}{(n _ {i} + 2) (n _ {i} + 1) ^ {2}} \end{array} \tag {A}\tag{A.2}
$$

and

$$
\begin{array}{c} V a r (\text { maximum   price }) = \int_ {0} ^ {1} p ^ {2} h (p) d p - \left(\int_ {0} ^ {1} p h (p) d p\right) ^ {2} \\ = \frac {n _ {i}}{(n _ {i} + 2) (n _ {i} + 1) ^ {2}}. \end{array}
$$

Therefore, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Var(price range) } \\ = \text { Var(maximum price   -   minimum price) } \\ = \text { Var(maximum price) } \\ + \text { Var(minimum price) } \\ = \frac {2 n _ {i} - 1}{(n _ {i} + 2) (n _ {i} + 1) ^ {2}}. \end{array}\tag{A.3}
$$
