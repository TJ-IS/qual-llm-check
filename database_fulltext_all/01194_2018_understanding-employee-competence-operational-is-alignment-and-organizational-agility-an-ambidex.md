## Abstract

#

Views on whether business-information systems (IS) alignment enhances or impedes organizational agility vary. The present study addresses this paradox by examining business-IS alignment at the strategy implementation stage, that is, the operational level. We conceptualize operational-level IS alignment (hereafter OISA) as an ambidextrous capability consisting of both structural and social alignments and examine its effect on organizational agility. Furthermore, we study the role of shared competence between business and IS departments in achieving OISA ambidexterity. Survey data of 119 departmental managers empirically support our research model. We summarize the implications and suggestions for future research.

Keywords: Employee competence; Operational IS alignment; Paradox; Organizational agility; Ambidexterity

## 1. Introduction

Contemporary firms rely on information systems (IS) to enhance organizational agility to thrive in the current volatile market environment characterised by rapidly changing consumer demand, frequent product obsolescence and intense competitive rivalry [9, 39]. Business–IS alignment, which reflects the extent of fit between business and IS [31], is crucial in achieving agility [23]. However, related studies have failed to reach a consensus on whether such alignment improves or impedes organizational agility, which is known as the alignment–agility paradox [23, 69]. Most previous studies have claimed that business–IS alignment positively affects organizational agility [23, 69] while some available evidence has indicated that it impedes agility of firms [13, 54, 55, 64]. Recently, Tallon and Pinsonneault [69] compared these contradictory views and empirically demonstrated that business–IS alignment improves agility. However, they neither explained why business–IS alignment does not impede agility, nor did they provide a theoretical reason or solution for the paradox.

Our study primarily aims to resolve the alignment–agility paradox, which we contend exists for two reasons. First, many studies have focused on business–IS alignment at the strategic formation stage [23, 42, 57, 69, 79], thereby neglecting the key role of alignment at the strategic implementation stage; that is, operational business–IS alignment (OISA) [23, 76]. Research works that did not find the anticipated positive effect of business–IS alignment on agility [2, 13, 54, 55, 64] attribute such results, in a post-hoc manner, to the challenge of maintaining alignment during strategic execution, implying the importance of IS alignment at the operational level [54, 55, 69]. However, few studies have formally examined operational alignment and its effect on agility. This scarce stream of research led Chan and Reich to call for “more attention on operational alignment” through “new theoretical underpinnings” [10].

#

Second, the paradox is attributed to the focus of prior research on one aspect of business– IS alignment. Researchers have extensively investigated the intellectual dimension of business–IS alignment, referred to as the extent to which IT strategies and processes are formed and implemented to support key business strategies and processes [69]. However, the other important dimension of alignment—social alignment—has received minimal research attention among studies on organizational agility. Recent research has provided valuable theoretical and practical insights into the social aspects of business–IS alignment [42, 57, 76], such as informal relationships between business and IT, thereby demonstrating its impact on organizational value. However, we have limited understanding of social dimensions of IS alignment at the operational level. As we argue later, the co-presence of intellectual and social dimensions of operational IS alignment is instrumental for ensuring agility.

In the backdrop of these research gaps, we aim to address the paradox by answering the first research question: How should IS and business of a firm align at the strategy implementation stage to enhance organizational agility? To answer this question, we follow Reich and Benbasat [58] in conceptualizing OISA as having two dimensions, namely, structural and social alignments. Structural alignment reflects the alignment of formal business and IT structures, which refers to the fit between IT infrastructure/processes and business structure/processes [10, 40]. Social alignment focuses on the alignment of informal business and IT structures (i.e., human relationships), which refers to “social integration” among human actors in business and IT departments [58, 76]. We argue that agility is attained when both dimensions of OISA are pursued simultaneously as both are important for developing organizational agility [69]. Although prior studies have suggested that the two alignments are complementary at the strategy formation stage, we contend that structural and social alignments at the strategy implementation stage require somewhat different structures (i.e., formal versus informal structures); it may be challenging to develop these structures

#

simultaneously because they require different development routines and compete for scarce resources [52]. To this end, we draw from ambidexterity theory [51, 52] to conceptualize OISA as an ambidexterity that emphasizes the synergistic value of simultaneously pursuing the competing goals of structural and social alignments at the operational level. We refer to this relationship as “OISA ambidexterity.”

After examining the effect of OISA ambidexterity on agility, we answer the second research question: What should firms do to develop OISA ambidexterity? Previous literature suggests that a key perspective for explaining business–IS alignment at the strategy formation stage is the shared competence between business and IT domains [3, 4, 57]. We adopt and theoretically extend this perspective to explain OISA at the strategy implementation stage. Specifically, we extend Bassellier and Benbasat’s [3, 5] concept of “shared domain competence” from the executive to the department level, which captures the competence shared between business and IT departments and examine the impact on OISA ambidexterity. We empirically test our research model via a field survey of operational managers across 119 firms. Table 1 summarizes the two intended contributions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1 Alignment–agility paradox

The term “agility” describes how an actor senses and responds to change [25]. Organizational agility is the ability to survive and prosper in a competitive environment by sensing and reacting to external changes rapidly and effectively [27, 45]. Business–IS alignment is conventionally acknowledged as a significant and positive determinant of agility [11, 37, 53]. However, a rival view has emerged, giving rise to the alignment–agility paradox [23, 69]. On the one hand, business–IS alignment positively affects agility [11, 37, 53]. For instance, Oh and Pinsonneault [53] confirmed a positive relationship between business–IS alignment and agility. They suggested that aligned IT infrastructures and processes provide an integrated platform for business operations, thereby enabling firms to compete with other by quickly seizing opportunities. Tallon and Pinsonneault [69] showed that business–IS alignment is a key source of competitive advantages because of its positive link to organizational agility. Other researchers have presented similar views in which business–IS alignment improves agility [45, 66].

On the other hand, it has been argued that the tight alignment between IT and business processes leads to competency traps and resource commitments that inhibit agility. For instance, Tallon and Kraemer [68] reported that emphasizing business–IS alignment may result in unintended process rigidity when large and embedded IT processes limit an organization’s ability to deal with emergent changes quickly. Thus, when structures and processes are deployed elaborately in advance, flexibility to incorporate business requirements in a specific context may decline [23]. Furthermore, firms tend to commit to the benefits induced by initial success, and thus, they stay with the status quo of business–IS alignment, exploit IT resources and ignore pressures of environmental changes [30]. Empirical evidence supports this view. Tallon [64, 67] reported that 30% of 238 mid-size firms indicate no improvement or even a decline in performance when business and IS are aligned, as they create an inflexible backbone of IT systems. Similarly, Shpilberg, Berez, et al. [63] showed that highly aligned firms do not enjoy enhanced business performance by responding immediately to change, an essential element of agility. Arvidsson, Holmström, et al. [2] found that organizations that have successfully implemented new and strategically aligned IT capabilities do not respond quickly to environmental changes. Moreover, Chan,

#

Sabherwal, et al. [13] found that a high level of business–IS alignment does not bring expected benefits for firms pursuing cost and operational excellence.

A careful literature review has revealed two possible reasons for the alignment–agility paradox. First, business–IS alignment exists at both strategy formation and implementation stages [31], with the latter referring to IS alignment at the operational level. Extant literature has concentrated on strategy formation [13, 42, 61, 69] while very few studies have focused on the relationship between operational IS alignment and organizational agility at the strategy implementation stage [23, 31]. However, strategies should be carefully executed and deploy resources prudently to realize quick and effective responses to environmental change. For instance, Wagner, Beimborn, et al. [76] and Chan [10] proposed that agility is not simply a matter of ensuring the surface fit state between business and IS strategic plans in today’s hyper-turbulent environment, it is also one of execution. Thus, the investigation of the role of operational IS alignment can lead to a better understanding of the effect of alignment on agility.

Another possible reason is the negligence of social alignment in explaining agility. An organization has to maintain two approaches to IS alignment, namely, intellectual and social dimensions [42, 58]. However, most of extant literature has focused on the intellectual dimension (structural or strategic alignment) [54, 55, 63, 64, 69], with very limited attention to the social dimension, particularly at the operational level. Social dimension too may have significant value for business operations. For instance, human relationships may encourage effective knowledge-sharing between business and IT, and thus generate innovative IT solutions [57] to eliminate the rigidity induced by intellectual dimension of IS alignment. Such theoretical discussions on social alignment have yet to be applied to understand the relationship between operational level of IS alignment and agility. Thus, collectively, incorporating both dimensions of IS alignment at the operational level (named as operational

IS alignment, OISA for short) need to be accomplished to understand the link between IS alignment and agility.

## 2.2 Defining operational IS alignment (OISA)

What OISA concerns is the choices a firm makes regarding its organizational structure to execute IT and business strategies [31], and this includes formal as well as informal structures inside the firm. The structural alignment of OISA [6] focuses on the fit between formal structures and is defined as the extent to which IT infrastructures/processes support and fit business structure/processes [10, 81]. Structural alignment highlights that the implementation of an IT infrastructure (e.g., technologies, applications) should be in line with internal formal structures, including organizational decision-making rights, managerial principles and organizational forms [71]. By contrast, IT processes should conform to the demands of business operations [77].

The social alignment of OISA focuses on the fit of informal structures, defined as the alignment of human relationships among employees in business and IT departments [58]. The implementation of IS strategies and projects usually does not involve top managers or it involves them only minimally [76]. Accordingly, social alignment refers to interaction patterns, mutual collaborations and values shared between employees of business and IT departments [76]. These social resources provide the informal relationship structure for IT and business, which forms the foundation for future knowledge-sharing and -creation. Each dimension is important for developing organizational agility. Structural alignment is associated with formal and planned structures, tightly coupled systems, path dependence and routinization. Structural alignment enhances efficiency in response to business needs by quickly mobilizing integrated IT resources [75]. By contrast, social alignment is related to an informal structure which enables a flexible process through emergent social coordination. This type of alignment is associated with path-breaking, improvisation and chaos that encourage business and IT departments to autonomously solve emergent problems without formal planning or hierarchical management [35]. This process adjusts IT platform to better cover changing business demands.

However, an OISA dimension by itself is insufficient to ensure agility that simultaneously entails efficiency and flexibility. Scholars [45, 80] have argued that an IT department should fulfill dual demands from business departments. On the one hand, an IT department should provide a formal and planned IT platform to ensure efficiency in operations. On the other hand, an IT department should be informally aligned with business departments to be sufficiently flexible to cope with emerging requests that require dynamic interaction between IT and business. Overemphasis on structural alignment may initially enjoy the efficiency benefits of aligned formal business structures and IT architectures. This success makes firms overconfident in their IT-supported business processes and eventually results in reluctance to change the status quo. This overconfidence has a major negative effec in the development of a myopic view wherein IT processes cannot be updated with business demands [72]. The IT department can remain focused on what has been planned and ignore emerging requests from business departments and thus fail to respond effectively to external changes.

On the other hand, social alignment alone is insufficient or even detrimental to agility and thus having only informal structures can lead to disorder and inefficiency. Business

departments may adopt the mindset that their IT professionals can fulfill every request, which results in IT departments constantly trying out new information systems rather than maintaining a stable operating platform [45]. This self-reinforcing process encourages business departments to adapt slowly to the system, rather than efficiently operate with a stable platform. Social interaction stimulates flexibility and responsiveness; however, relying especially in cases that require a fast and stable foundation for adjusting internal processes to take advantage of external opportunities.

However, it is difficult to achieve structural and social alignments simultaneously at the operational level. Previous studies have shown that social alignment is positively linked to the development of strategic alignment; however, those studies have focused on alignment at the strategy formation stage [35, 58]. Thus, the social alignment between business and IT executives contributes to the formation of IT strategy corresponding with the business strategy [57]. Nevertheless, the situation at the strategy implementation stage, that is, operational alignment, is quite different. The implementation stage is related to a complex situation involving informal relationships between business and IT departments and the implementation of formal strategic plans by executives. In this situation, tension exists between the two aspects of alignment since they require substantially different structures. Whereas structural alignment is associated with alignment of formal structures, social alignment is related to alignment of informal structures. The two fundamentally different structures lead to the situation that structural and social alignments compete for scarce resources and require contrasting development routines [52]. Specifically, structural alignment focuses on the planned resources (structures and processes) arranged by business and IT executives, whereas social alignment concentrates on informal structures (emergent

#

social coordinating) that rely on autonomous collaboration between business and IT departments. Conflicting routines need to resolve the differences since firms may choose to develop one aspect by sacrificing the other [40] because top level-planned formal structures and processes may limit the autonomy of cross domain communication and collaboration among employees. For instance, without limitations from organizational structures, social routines among employees usually function well when formal processes fail [26]. When firms establish stable business and IT structures and processes, employees may prefer to rely on these artifacts to execute business operations, rather than seek assistance from informal human relationships [7].

In summary, structural and social alignments are necessary but difficult to achieve simultaneously due to the competing nature at the operational level. Firms must balance the tensions between the two. Thus, we refer to the ambidexterity literature to understand operational IS alignment. Tushman and O'Reilly [72] argued that managing the trade-offs between two competing goals is a form of ambidexterity. They used the metaphor of a juggler to suggest that firms can be sufficiently ambidextrous to pursue two competing goals, in terms of reconciling manufacturing efficiency and flexibility. These authors also contended that ambidextrous firms, which can use both hands, are more competitive than firms who only have one hand by sacrificing another. Accordingly, Tallon and Pinsonneault [69] suggested that “ambidexterity might be a useful lens for thinking about alignment and agility in IS research” (p. 479).

Based on ambidexterity literature, OISA is ambidextrous when it simultaneously pursues (1) structural alignment, denoting a formal structural alignment that IT infrastructures/processes support business structures/processes; and (2) social alignment, denoting an informal structural alignment that is a solid IT–business-human relationship. Both are indispensable for striking a balance between efficiency and flexibility underlying

agility. Thus, only firms which excel in both aspects can maximize the value of operational alignment for organizational agility.

## 2.3 OISA ambidexterity and shared competence

Given the instrumental role of OISA ambidexterity, it is critical to understand the manner in which OISA ambidexterity is achieved. Table 4 summarizes the state of the literature on IS alignment, revealing that prior works have tended to focus on antecedents of strategic alignment, rather than of operational alignment. Studies on operational alignment have focused on only one aspect of alignment [65, 76].

As we conceptualize OISA as an ambidexterity, we extend a theoretical perspective that addresses both dimensions of alignment, namely, shared competence between business and IT personnel from the executive level to the department level. Employees in departments should possess a comprehensive competence shared between business and IT departments. Business personnel should be IT-savvy and IT professionals should be competent in business.

The use of comprehensive shared competence at the department (rather than executive) level is appropriate for two reasons. First, we focus on operational IS alignment—employees in business and IT departments have an emerging role in this context because most IT implementations and business operations involve departmental employees [20]. Shared domain competence is proposed as an essential competence that executives should master to achieve intellectual (IT/business strategy fit) or social alignment. Actors should be changed to employees in business and IT departments in operational IS alignment context. As such, competencies shared between business and IT department in which employees are the main actors is relevant to our research context.

Second, from an ambidexterity perspective, business employees and IT professionals should simultaneously master two competing competencies—one inside their respective domains and the other from outside. Individuals have limited time and can put in only limited effort to acquire both business and IT competence [57]; thus, the business competence of IT personnel and IT competence of business personnel can be interpreted as ambidextrous resources which may be leveraged to enhance firm-wide ambidexterity, that is, OISA ambidexterity [34]. Shared competence can span across resource boundaries to balance the tension between formal and informal structures when employees not only have internal professional domain competence to understand formal structures/processes and implement them appropriately, but also have the outside domain competence as the communication base for developing human relationships with outside employees.

Thus, we argue that the business competence of IT professionals and the IT competence of business employees constitute basic resources for developing ambidextrous capability (i.e., OISA), which in turn can directly affect organizational agility. Figure 1 shows the research framework.

## 3. Hypotheses development

## 3.1 OISA ambidexterity and organizational agility

We posit that OISA ambidexterity is positively related to organizational agility. First, each OISA dimension can facilitate organizational agility. Structural alignment specifically provides a technically suitable platform to aid firms efficiently cope with external threats and opportunities [9, 62]. A technically suitable platform implies that the IT infrastructure is

#

aligned with organizational architecture and that IT and business activities are well synergized. A fit and integrated IT infrastructure can provide a seamless platform for automating and integrating daily operations, such as customer acquisition, order processing, logistics and purchasing, product manufacturing and delivery [45]. A digitalized platform of processes permits firms to operate with agility through rapid and advanced information collection and reconfiguration of business processes and information systems [48].

In contrast, social alignment enhances collaboration between business and IT people by facilitating close interactions, fostering mutual respect and trust and encouraging knowledge exchange [82]. Knowledge-sharing can lead to more innovative ways of coping with unexpected changes [36]. Previous studies have provided an example of this collaboration: a marketing team which collaborated with IT professionals in their firm extensively utilized new technologies and developed a “voice of customer” application to achieve immediate user feedback from mobile terminals and websites [60]. This collaboration enabled the marketing department to reach out to customers whom they could not have reached through traditional communication channels. Thus, social alignment improves agility by facilitating collaboration between business and IT to explore new IT and market opportunities, in order to help an organization innovatively respond to market changes [76].

Second and more importantly, the positive effect of the two OISA dimensions on agility is not guaranteed unless they are pursued simultaneously (i.e., ambidextrously), because pursuing only one can eliminate the negative effect of the other dimension. The negative effect of focusing solely on structural alignment is that IT cannot be reconfigured to respond to business requests. Simultaneously pursuing social alignment addresses this shortcoming because it relies on communication and collaboration between business and IT units to identify and respond to external changes [76]. By contrast, inefficiency is the negative effect of focusing solely on social alignment, which includes relational conflicts, negative politics and disorders. Structural alignment has formal processes, orders and routines that are unconcerned with relational conflicts, thereby overcoming the negative implications of social alignment by providing a fast and stable foundation for adjusting internal processes to seize external opportunities [48].

H1: OISA ambidexterity is positively related to organizational agility.

## 3.2 Employee competence and OISA ambidexterity

## 3.2.1 IT competence of business employees

The IT competence of business employees refers to their IT-related knowledge and experiences that enable them to exhibit IT leadership in their areas of the business [5]. IT knowledge refers to specialized knowledge whereas IT experience denotes the intellectual output of activities and projects that business employees engage in to expand their IT understanding [4]. In this research, we evaluate the breadth and depth of the IT knowledge and experience of business employees to assess their IT competence level. This group should possess knowledge and experience in the following areas: (1) different technologies used in the organization, such as personal computers, client-servers, LAN, imagery technology and multimedia; (2) different software applications, such as e-mail, internet, electronic data interchange, e-commerce, groupware and enterprise resource planning; (3) system development methods and project management practices, such as experience in managing, developing and implementing information systems; and (4) goal setting, resource allocation and performance monitoring regarding IT, in order to understand IS budget, policy and strategy. We consider and combine all these areas to represent the broad range of competence a person can possess in the IT domain.

Business and IT competence represent business employees’ mastery of business and IT domains, which is a prerequisite to balance the tension between structural and social

#

alignment. The IT competence of business employees helps bridge the boundaries between planned structures/processes and informal relationship structures; hence, business departments can clearly understand the formal processes they should follow to use IT for executing business operations. With IT competence, business employees can actively interact with the IT unit to support, promote and develop IT applications. The end result is aligned structural alignment. First, competence in different technologies and the use of software enables business department employees to align IT applications with their daily work, thereby improving how IT processes correspond with business requirements [18]. Second, competence in IT system development and project practices allows business departments to offer ideas in IT development, thereby improving IT technical support for business processes [17].

In terms of social alignment, business people with IT project experience are motivated to proactively communicate with and provide feedback to IT departments [35], which constitute a central step for social alignment between the two. In addition, competence in sharing visions and establishing goals about the role of IT makes business departments aware of the value of IT for their own work [19]; hence the business departments are motivated to extensively collaborate with the IT department. Thus, the IT competence of business employees can improve OISA ambidexterity by simultaneously enhancing structural and social alignment.

H2: The IT competence of business employees is positively related to OISA ambidexterity.

## 3.2.2 Business competence of IT professionals

The business competence of IT professionals refers to business knowledge and experience acquired by IT professionals, which can be employed for understanding business

#

demands, using the language of business and collaborating with business partners [3]. Business competence includes IT professionals’ knowledge in (1) the business environment; (2) the operational processes and language (e.g., jargon and keywords) of business departments; and (3) the use of IT solutions to solve business problems.

The business competence of IT people represents competencies across boundaries that enables an IT unit to not only understand IT implementation and follow the formal structure and processes of their domain, but also to interact with business departments based on shared business knowledge. This competence improves structural and social alignments simultaneously in several ways. First, knowledge of the business environment serves as a foundation for the IT unit to develop new information systems to respond to changes in business operations. Second, familiarity with other departments’ operational processes and the language of business people enables IT professionals to understand the needs and messages of the business departments to further establish collaborative relationships [18]. Third, the ability of IT professionals to speak the “language of the business” facilitates effective communication with business employees to exchange knowledge with them and enhance IT decisions [3]. The knowledge sharing helps IT professionals acquire positive feedback to improve IT applications, thereby enhancing user experience for business employees.

## H3: The business competence of IT professionals is positively related to OISA ambidexterity.

## 3.2.3 Complementary effects of the two kinds of competence

IT and business competence directly affect OISA; however, they also have a synergetic interactive effect on OISA ambidexterity. They interact with each other in reinforcing OISA by amplifying the impact of the other. According to interpretation of synergetic interaction in

##

economic theory, the joint value of synergetic resources is greater than the sum total of their individual values [47]. The returns to one resource vary from those of other resources [70]. In our case, when IT professionals achieve a high level of business competence, it also becomes more valuable for the business people to develop a high level of IT competence. The business competence of IT professionals enables the IT department to build a business vision of IT and increase the value of business competence of an IT professional; hence, business people with high levels of IT competence can easily understand the exchanged information (e.g., tech instructions and opinions) from IT professionals. Business people are interested in IT suggestions to integrate them with job routines. Meanwhile, the significant communication between business and IT professionals further enhances mutual social relations and establishes long-term partnerships.

By contrast, when either kind of competence is weak or absent, values of the other kind of competence also diminish. For instance, if business people have no comparable level of IT competence, it becomes less valuable for IT professionals to develop a high level of business competence. The business competence of IT professionals still enables them to have a business vision of IT to develop aligned IT processes with business and to communicate business understanding of IT to other business departments and build social relations with them. However, aligned IT processes with business operations are not exactly creating value because business people cannot immediately overcast aligned IT applications or integrate IT into daily business operations [76]. Meanwhile, business people have no interest in responding to the prosocial attitude of IT employees, which leads to imperfectly aligned social structures between business and IT.

H4: The business competence of IT professionals and the IT competence of business employees have a synergetic interactive effect on OISA ambidexterity.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1 Sample and data collection

We empirically tested the research model using data collected from a field survey of Chinese firms. These firms are an appropriate research context for examining organizational agility because their environments and customer markets present significant challenges [59]. Moreover, compared with traditional firms, operational IS alignment is more significant for digitally enabled firms [53]. We focus on digitally enabled firms which rely heavily on IT to conduct business operations.

We conducted our survey in collaboration with a Chinese educational institution offering advanced training courses in information systems and strategic management. Our survey respondents were those enrolled in the program and all of them held management roles in their respective organizations. We focus on the determinants and consequences of operational IS alignment and posit that these features should be examined from the perspective of department supervisors, rather than from the members of top management team (TMT), who do not have regular contact with employees and daily business operations [76]. By contrast, department supervisors are usually familiar with employees and operational details of business and IT departments. They are responsible for business functions of their respective departments and are informed of the operational use of IS. We employed a single rather than matched survey, which is consistent with the operations IS alignment study of Wagner, Beimborn, et al. [76].

The sample pool for this research consisted of 500 respondents. We allocated an identifying code to each questionnaire, but the respondents could remove this code if they preferred to be anonymous. We considered 119 of the 139 returned questionnaires based on the following criteria: the survey choices were clearly indicated and the individual background information was complete. This audit led to an approximate response rate of 20%.

Table 5 presents the demographic information of respondents. The sample firms were from diverse industries, but most were manufacturing firms. On average, the sample firms have been operating for more than 10 years with 500 employees. The mean of IT department size is 11 employees and approximately 50% of firms have more than 15 employees in their respective IT departments. Their IT budgets accounted for 5.1% of sales revenue, representing a high level of IT investment [32]. The department supervisors and senior managers have more than 7 years of experience in the present firm and 15 years in the industry.

We tested non-response bias as follows: (1) by comparing the organizational demographic features (i.e., firm size) of the responding firms with those of the nonresponding firms; and (2) using the method suggested by Armstrong and Overton [1] to compare the first 25% with the final 25% of respondents using chi-squares of the critical measurement items from the responses. We did not discover significant differences between the two groups. Hence, non-response bias was not detected in our research.

## 4.2 Measures

We adapted measurement items from previous literature and fine-tuned some terms to suit our specific research context. The respondents in our study were native Chinese speakers; thus, we translated the questionnaire through a committee approach [74] involving a three-

step procedure to ensure that the items have good face validity [73]. Three native Chinese speakers, who are also fluent in English, were invited to translate all items in the English questionnaire into Chinese independently; their translated items were then individually discussed to achieve consensus. Next, we verified the questionnaire through a three-step process. First, we invited three academic domain experts in employee competence, IS alignment and agility to assess the questionnaire. Second, the three experts and several managers discussed the questionnaire for feedback. Finally, we distributed the questionnaire to five managers in other companies for further validation.

The IT competence of business employees was assessed through a five-item measure verified by Davis [17]. These items were employed to measure the perceptions of department employees. An example of IT experience measure is “Employees in business department have valuable knowledge and experience regarding the vision, goal-setting, resource allocation and performance monitoring of IT in my organization.”

The business competence of IT professionals was measured using the five items adapted from Lee and Trauth [38]. A sample item is “IT professionals know the organization's external environment (e.g., government, competitors, suppliers, and customers) very well.”

## 5.3 Common method variance (CMV) detection

#

We conducted three complementary analyses to detect common method bias. First, we implemented Harman’s single-factor test [28]. We conducted a principal components analysis on all constructs in our model. We identified eight factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0, which explain 71.8% of the total variance, with no single factor explaining the majority of the variance. Hence, common method variance is not a concern.

Second, we re-conducted an estimation procedure for the structural model, as described by Podsakoff and MacKenzie [56], considering three components, namely, trait, method and random errors, that lead to the possible variance of indicators. We follow Liang, Saraf, et al. [41] to assess CMV in PLS. Every indicator is operated as a latent variable and the trait and method factors are taken as second-order factors. Trait factors are used to predict the corresponding indicators while method factors lead to all indicators. Using PLS, we get loadings from trait and method factors to the indicators and the variance they explain. The loadings of all items on the common method factor were found to be insignificant (t < 1.96). The proportion of variance in each observed indicator (computed as square of the item loadings) explained by its focal construct (more than 70%) surpassed the variance explained by the method factor.

Third, we implemented the correlational marker variable to detect the CMV. As described by Lindell and Whitney [43], a reasonable estimation of CMV could be the second smallest and positive correlation among measurement items (r=0.17). We adjusted all correlations among measurement items by removing the CMV estimation. Results suggest that there is no significant change in the correlations. Thus, common method bias is generally not a major concern.

## 6. Discussion

The “alignment–agility paradox” has been previously discussed in IS literature; however, no theoretical solutions have been proposed to address this research concern [23, 45, 69]. The present study unravels this paradox by accounting for IS alignment as an ambidextrous force at the strategy implementation stage. Specifically, we build on the ambidexterity perspective to conceptualize OISA as an ambidexterity consisting of two dimensions, structural and social alignments. Subsequently, we draw on shared competence perspective to explain the antecedents of OISA ambidexterity. Table 11 demonstrates the empirical results supporting all the hypotheses.

## 6.1 Research implications

First, our study confirms that OISA ambidexterity is positively related to organizational agility. An in-depth post-hoc data analysis on this relationship reveals a more important role of OISA in facilitating agility compared with alignment at the strategy formation stage. Table 10 shows that the control variable of strategic IS alignment (strategy formation) is insignificant when OISA is introduced to the model, indicating that OISA considerably subsumes the effect of strategic IS alignment on agility. One possible reason for the alignment-agility paradox could be that extant literature has mainly focused on alignment at the strategy formation stage [69], thereby overlooking the importance of IS alignment at the strategy implementation stage as a factor more proximate to agility. This finding extends the IS alignment literature by empirically demonstrating the importance of examining IS alignment at the operational level. Accordingly, this finding also responds to the calls for more empirical studies about OISA [12] and resonates with the view that alignment is both a strategic and an operational concern (Vessey and Ward [75]).

We conducted another post-hoc analysis to achieve additional insights and to further examine the value of OISA ambidexterity in unraveling the alignment–agility paradox and to further validate our findings. We identified significant clusters according to various levels of structural alignment and social alignment, using the K-means cluster technique [29]. The best fit was presented by four groups. Table 12 demonstrates the structural alignment and social alignment scores for the four cluster centers. Group 1 consisted of 46 organizations with “moderately ambidextrous” IT units and average ratings on both dimensions. Group 2 consisted of 40 organizations with “ambidextrous” IT units and high ratings on both dimensions. Group 3 included 9 organizations with “structural-oriented” IT units and a higher rating on structural alignment than social alignment. Group 4 consisted of 24 organizations with “socially-oriented” IT units and a higher rating on social alignment than structural alignment.

ANOVA F-test indicates that we could reject that all four groups have the same level of agility (F = 17.69, p < 0.001). Group 2 (highly ambidextrous) exhibited the best agility rating, followed by Groups 1 (moderately ambidextrous), 4 (social-oriented) and 3 (structuraloriented). We established that the differences between each and every group were significant, by using post-hoc Student-Newman-Keuls analysis [29].

The intriguing post hoc analysis reveals the scenario where alignment does not lead to agility. Table 12 shows that in Group 3, where firms score high on structural alignment but low on social alignment, has the lowest average agility (mean rating on agility = 3.06). This finding is consistent with several previous research workers in that the intellectual IS alignment and organizational agility are not or even negatively related [6, 61]. Second, our work extends understanding of the alignment–agility paradox by conceptualizing OISA based on the ambidexterity literature. Group 2 firms (which score highly on both structural and social alignments) are the most agile (mean rating on agility = 4.23). This result supports an ambidextrous perspective of OISA that agility requires “two hands” to be aligned. Thus, the positive effect of IS alignment on agility is only derived when structural and social alignments are combined. Previous studies have only investigated one dimension of IS alignment and have found the paradox in the relationship between IS alignment and be pursued simultaneously. With this ambidextrous view of IS alignment, our work corresponds to recent studies on the role of ambidexterity in organizational agility [39].

IT and business competence are significantly related to OISA ambidexterity. In addition, human capital, specifically employee competence, is consequential for creating value at the strategy implementation stage and provides a breeding ground for OISA ambidexterity. Previous studies have focused on IS alignment at the strategy formation stage; thus, shared competence is recognized as a significant factor on how senior IT and business executives formulate aligned business and IT strategies [57]. Our findings advance the literature by revealing the role of individual employee competence in developing alignment at the implementation stage [78]. Thus, our study also responds to the call for empirical research that explores the role of employees in achieving IS alignment [76, 78].

## 6.2 Practical implications

This research has several practical implications. First, our study offers two insights for IT and business managers concerned with IS alignment and agility. One is that studies should focus more on IS alignment at the implementation stage, which may improve agility. As strategic alignment still has mixed effects on organizational agility, operational IS alignment aligned business and IS strategies. Operational IS alignment is more directly related to the execution of response activities, such as responding to market turbulence through an aligned IT platform [20]. Moreover, managers should pursue structural and social alignments simultaneously, at least in digitally enabled firms. On the one hand, managers should align IT infrastructures and processes with organizational structure and business processes, thereby responding to business requests faster and more efficiently (structural alignment) [40]. On the other hand, business people should focus more on the value of close collaboration between business and IT departments, which is instrumental to flexible adjustment of information systems to business changes (social alignment) [76]. Tangible investment in IT infrastructures is the top priority for these firms; however, they cannot gain improved agility in most cases [69] because the social alignment or the development of social relationships between business and IT departments is ignored by IT and business managers. The intangible resource investment has the same priority as the tangible IT investment because of the flexibility induced by cross-departmental collaboration. Managers should balance these two kinds of investments, as they have distinct development approaches [30].

Second, millions of dollars are invested in training designed to improve employee competence and ultimately enhance firm performance [3]. In addition, managers should target this investment at building cross-domain competence for training employees to be experts in their own domains. IT professionals should have a comprehensive business

#

knowledge base, such as the knowledge of dynamic business environments and how IT can play a role in responding to such dynamics; business employees should be excellent IT users that can provide suggestions to IT professionals. Shared domain competence functions as the base for the development of operational IS alignment, including structural and social alignments.

## 6.3 Limitations and future research

The findings of our study should be interpreted with several limitations serving as a point of departure for future research. First, although non-response bias tests have showed insignificant differences between responding and non-responding firms, systematic differences are not considered [35]. Second, we used the perceptions of individual respondents (subjective measures) to measure all the constructs. Although common method bias is not a critical concern according to our tests, further studies are recommended to use objective data or multiple informants’ data. Third, any generalization of the findings may be limited by the homogeneity of the respondents, who were all located in China. Future studies can be performed in other countries to generalize our findings. Fourth, we only controlled for four variables (firm industry, firm size, firm history and IT department size); future research can consider other potential control variables.

## 6.4 Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate two dimensions of IS alignment at the strategy implementation stage to examine how OISA ambidexterity influences agility and how it is achieved. OISA ambidexterity can improve organizational agility and shared competence (i.e., business competence of IT professionals and IT competence of business employees) positively interact to enhance OISA ambidexterity. This study has two intended contributions to the IS literature. First, we explore the alignment paradox by incorporating both dimensions

#

of IS alignment as an ambidexterity concept at the strategy implementation stage. Thus, we add to the existing literature on organizational agility by introducing the strategic implementation stage of IS alignment, that is, operational IS alignment, as a possible solution to the paradox. We also add to the existing literature on single-dimension IS alignment by elaborating that the two-dimensional understanding of OISA is necessary for solving the paradox. With support of the ambidexterity theory, OISA is proposed to simultaneously extending shared competence between business and IT from the executive to the department level [12].

## Theoretical Contribution

1. We add to the existing literature on organizational agility by introducing the strategy implementation stage of IS alignment, operational IS alignment in our case, and as such, offers a possible solution to alignment paradox that operational alignment should not be ignored as it is more closely associated with agile operational activities.

2. We add to the existing literature on singledimension IS alignment by elaborating that the twodimensional understanding of OISA is necessary to solve the paradox. With the support of the ambidexterity theory, OISA is proposed as pursuing structural alignment and social alignment at the same time.

3. We explain OISA by extending shared competence between business and IT from the executive level to the department level [12]

## State of the literature

Previous studies mainly focus on IS alignment at the strategy formation stage (i.e., strategic IS alignment) [14], and are yet to introduce the alignment implementation to the agility literature.

The status quo of IS alignment literature presents that researchers only focus on one of them - intellectual dimension of IS alignment (structural or strategic alignment) and prove the existence of alignment paradox [52, 53, 61, 63, 68].

Prior research implies that CEO/CIO play important roles in enhancing IS alignment at the strategy formation stage [11, 14]. Shared domain competence has been proposed to be significant and necessary skills for CEO/CIO. while we extends its applicability to the strategy implementation stage by adapting it for the department level [2, 17].
