# “What can I(S) do for you?”: How technology enables service providers to elicit customers’ preferences and deliver personalized service

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Effective use Faithful representation Customer value Service personalization Customer service systems

## A B S T R A C T

This research studies the efective use of an IT-enabled Customer Service System (CSS) in the hospitality sector. Drawing on Efective Use Theory, we study how digital representations of customers’ preferences support service providers in the service personalization process. Our model posits customers’ preferences elicitation as the antecedent of CSS’ efective use, which yields superior customer value. This representational perspective matches recent eforts to study customer value as cocreated by customers and service providers. Based on a longitudinal dataset of 118.647 hotel reservations. we explain how digital representations of customers' preferences enable service providers to personalize service and increase customer value.

## 1. Introduction

The role of information systems (IS) in supporting the delivery of personalized customers’ experiences has attracted scholarly attention for over two decades. Service personalization is the process of using a customer’s information to tailor the service to improve the benefits accruing to them [1,2]. There is a wide range of areas where IT-enabled personalization may increase benefits for customers, such as using recommendation-based product matching in online retailing [3] or online product design [4]. Yet, the focus on service personalization vis-à-vis the personalization of tangible products is more recent. Hospitality is an industry sector that has been receiving increasing attention from IS scholars, who focused on how IT can support service personalization and increase customer value [5]. The hospitality industry focuses on selling experience goods and services; therefore, it is particularly apt for studying personalization. The value of hospitality products depends on their perceived quality—a function of the customer’s a priori expectations and how they are satisfied [39,40]. Given that service personali zation afects customers’ expectations and influences their appraisal of the service, studying service personalization within an industry that relies on individual service encounters between the customer and the service provider seems ideal [41].

Historically, IT-enabled service personalization has been a prerogative of large hotel firms for two reasons. First, efective IT use for personalization may reduce operational costs [6], such as when adopting self-service kiosks (Piccoli and Lui, 2013). Thus, IT enables service providers to ofer personalized, high-quality service while reducing operational costs [7,8]. Second, the byproduct of the IT-enabled personalization process is data to support customer research initiatives [6]. Typically, however, only large hotel brands can generate a significant amount of customer-related data suficient to pursue customer research initiatives. While the benefits of IT-enabled service personalization seem obvious, scholars have also observed how IT-enabled, selfservice personalization systems may hinder the customer-firm relationship––especially when such a relationship is incipient [9]. Thus, switching from in-person, high-touch to IT-enabled customer service systems (hereafter, CSS) may reduce customer value. That explains why—in contrast with large hotel firms—smaller hotels tended to di minish IT importance in managing guests’ requirements [6].

In smaller hotels, managers are often the hotels’ owners, and hoteliers feel more self-aware of the status of the operations—including service personalization. Thus, there is lesser interest in IT as a tool for reducing operational costs. For smaller hotels, using IT efectively for service personalization means proving that technology generates value that exceeds operational eficiency. Small and medium hotels may deem the use of IT that reduces operational costs but hurts the customer-firm relationship as inefective. Efective use of an IT-enabled customer service system (CSS) must generate value for customers and

not for service providers only.

To study how using IT-enabled CSS benefits customers, we follow the service-dominant logic, which views service as a cocreation process [10]. This perspective suggests that when delivering any service, customers and service providers collaborate to generate value for the service beneficiary [11]. In the hospitality industry, where personalized service has been shown to be valued by customers, it is paramount to understand how IT-enabled CSS may support the personalization process and generate additional value for hotel guests. IT can support value cocreation by enhancing the service providers’ ability to understand customers’ preferences, for instance, by increasing customer preferences’ elicitation. Investigating the use of CSS from this angle ultimately contributes to prior research in the hospitality industry by enhancing the understanding of IT-depend competitive value in service personalization [12].

This research proposes a model to study value cocreation in service personalization using IT-enabled CSS. The model can be used to study the benefits of implementing IT-enabled CSS versus personal, high touch customer service processes to gather information for service personalization. We draw on efective use theory (EUT) [13] to conceptualize how the efective use of a CSS may increase (cocreated) customer value. EUT draws on research on representation theory, which hinges on the premise that humans devise information systems as artifacts to capture faithful digital representations of a domain of in terest [14]. In the context of service personalization, that means to represent customers’ preferences faithfully; efective use of CSS, in turn, means to leverage digital representations of service personalization options to elicit more efective use of the CSS. Thus, EUT provides IS researchers with the lens to understand how CSS enables customer value cocreation. However, value cocreation from service personaliza tion has never been framed as a problem of efective use of technology. By efective use, we mean using the system to learn about customers’ preferences which then allows providers to personalize the service to meet customers’ elicited preferences. Thus, this research answers the questions:

RQ1. How do IT-enabled CSS facilitate the service provider’s learning of the customers’ preferences?

RQ2. How does the matching of service to the learned preferences increase customer value?

The answers to these questions bear a twofold contribution. First, we contextualize EUT and the representational perspective to the novel context of IS use in hospitality. Second, we extend IT-enabled CSS lit erature by investigating how technology assists organizations in improving their ability to cocreate customer value. Thus, we demonstrate that EUT allows to conceptualize how CSS supports the personalization process and to measure the efective use of the CSS.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Efective use theory

Efective Use Theory (EUT) hinges on the premise that humans devise information systems as eficient instruments to represent real world domains [13]. In this view, a representation is the essence of an information system, which is designed and built to “track states of and state changes in other systems” ([15], p. viii). The theory distinguishes three main components of an information system: physical structure, surface structure, and deep structure.

Physical structure elements comprise the technology used to implement the system—i.e., “the machinery that supports the other structures” ([13], p. 636). That is the realm of hardware. The surface structure entails how the system appears and allows users to interact with it. That is the realm of the user interface. Deep structure elements “represent stakeholder perceptions of the meaning of the focal realworld phenomena (e.g., data objects and business rules embedded in program code)” ([16], p. 3). The emphasis on deep structure is the defining feature of EUT. In particular, while the physical and surface structures are means for accessing the deep structure, the latter encapsulates the primary purpose of an information system: to represent a phenomenon of interest faithfully. By faithful representation, we mean “the extent to which a user is obtaining representations that faithfully reflect the domain that the system represents” (Burton-Jones and Grange, p. 654). Thus, the usefulness of an information system is a function of the representational fidelity of these representations to the real-world domain [14]. As individuals and organizations build and use information systems to enable decision making and informed action, higher degrees of faithful representation of the domain of interest provide a superior basis for action by giving access to more accurate information [13]. Thus, faithful representation is a necessary, enabling condition for using the system efectively—i.e. “in a way that helps attain the goals for using the system” ([13], p. 633), arising from the congruous interplay between the user, system, and task [17]. The assessment of system use, which means the “user’s employment of a system to perform a task” ([18], p. 659), focuses on three dimensions of use [13]:

Transparent interaction: The extent to which users seamlessly access the system’s representations unconstrained by its surface and physical structures.

Representational Fidelity: The extent to which users access representations from the system that faithfully reflect the real-world domain.

• Informed action: The extent to which users act upon the faithful representations they obtain from the system to improve their state.

The three dimensions of efective use are hierarchically related, such that a user must be able to access the representations housed in the system (transparent interaction) to make an informed action. That is, “transparent interaction activates the informating potential of an IS, representational fidelity ensures that this potential is positive, and informed action leverages it” ([13], p. 644). Ideally, informed actions should lead to improved performance, but the relationship is determi nistic because of possible intervening factors. For instance, consider customer value, the central dimension of performance in a service process. While, on average, informed action by the service providers may lead to higher customer value, contingencies may impact such an outcome (e.g., the customer experiences emotional discomfort due to personal reasons).

To apply the EUT lens to CSS means to conceptualize a CSS as a representational system and to map the dimension of use to the service personalization process. From the EUT perspective, the goal of using a CSS is to faithfully represent customers’ preferences to enable service providers to better understand and act upon them. That means customers’ preference elicitation is the antecedent for efective use of the CSS. The outcome, which measures the CSS performance, is to increase the benefits for customers by delivering personalized service. The next sections discuss the antecedent and outcome of CSS efective use.

## 2.2. Preference elicitation

The notion of personalized service as cocreating value inherently hinges on the customer’s ability to communicate their preferences. Prior literature distinguished between two ways of eliciting customers’ preferences depending on who initiates the process, whether the service provider or the customer [8]. Service provider elicitation occurs when the customer is presented with personalization options that the service provider can deliver. For instance, a self-check-in kiosk can be used to select the number of keys. For efective service provider elicitation, the surface structure of the CSS must convey rich signifiers to support preference elicitation. A signifier is a sign (digital or not) that represents a real-world object (i.e., a signified). In a face-to-face situation, preference elicitation may use the sound of the words “extra pillow” to elicit a personalization and the object “pillow,” but the same signified (i.e., the pillow) can be referred to by a digital representation (e.g., an image) of the object “pillow.” However, the IT-enabled CSS afords the use of richer signifiers that facilitate preference elicitation. For instance, a sign representing the object “pillow” (e.g., an image) may have additional attributes such as category, price, or description, thus facilitating preference elicitation.

When preference elicitation occurs before the service encounter $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ , before a guest arrives at the hotel), one approach is to employ ITenabled CSS, which has shown to increase the volume of personalization options from customers as compared to personal, manual channel [5]. This is because digital representations enable the use of rich sig nifiers [42] to represent personalization options. Even the mere categorization of products has shown to increase customer value when the consumer does not hold a preexisting preference [19]. Thus, IT arms service providers with better instruments for linking product features and individual evaluation criteria [20], which helps preference formulation.

Individuals generally hold well-diferentiated values only for the most basic attitudes and frequently encountered experiences [21]; otherwise, preferences are constructed on the spot in response to task demands [22,23]. Consider a frequent traveler who is allergic to feathers. She will have a strong and well-defined preference for foam pillows. Conversely, if the same guest is ofered to customize the print on the toilet paper the hotel will place in the bathroom (a customization available in a few hotels in our study), she is unlikely to hold a strong a priori preference having never encountered such a request. Previous research shows that in the service context, people generally formulate their attitudes and requests when they are asked to express them [24].

User-initiated elicitation is when the customer proactively poses a request for personalization, which the provider may—or may not—be able to address. Prior research discussed the tradeof that characterizes the two approaches in terms of customer value/efectiveness [8]. The advantage of service provider elicitation is that the provider ofers preferences that are ready to be delivered. The CSS focuses on collecting preferences for existing personalization options. User-initiated preferences, instead, cannot be easily foreseen—and delivered—by the provider. Within this area of user-initiated preferences, we include preferences that emerge from customer research. For that, providers may use contextualized customer transaction information [25]. Thus, customer research difers from service provider elicitation. While cus tomer research is a product or service design activity, preference eli citation is a service delivery activity.

Customer research is about finding out what potential preferences the majority of the customer base holds in order to design, build, or introduce appropriate services. Conversely, the service provider elicitation is concerned with eliciting specific customer’s preferences. This limits the providers’ capability to learn about customers’ preferences to the set of personalization options that the provider can feasibly deliver. For example, while a hotel guest may value personalized music from her iPhone in the room, a hotel can only ofer such service if it had previously installed iPhone adapters for the room’s sound system. During a customer research initiative, the hotel may discover that the majority of potential guests would value personalized music. It may then decide to invest money to retrofit rooms with adapters to make the service available in the future. Once an individual guest’s learned preference is in the feasible options set (e.g., higher floors away from trafic noise, foam pillows), the hotel can accommodate such preference during service provision.

## 2.3. Efective use

In the context of CSS, the output of service personalization $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { i } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . ,$ , an elicited preference) is a customer’s personalization proclivity drawn from a set of available service customizations. Efective use of the CSS means to use the system to enable the provider to successfully fulfill elicited preferences, which occurs via two subsequent subprocesses: learning and matching [26]. Learning consists of collecting specific customer preferences [27] to learn about the customizations that customers value. From the EUT perspective, the primary design objective of a CSS is to support the service providers in eliciting preferences and enable them to fulfill such preferences during service delivery [5,28]. Efective learning occurs if the CSS enables representational fidelity of the customers’ preferences. Given a set of data objects and rules $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , the deep structure) that describe the customer’s preferences $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the realworld domain), the CSS must accurately represent those preferences. What enables learning is the capability of the CSS to store customer preferences and faithfully represent personalization items that the service provider can feasibly deliver. Eliciting more personalization requests within the set of available options allows for the measuring of the service providers’ ability to identify the elements of the experience that customers value.

Matching consists of customizing the ofering to accommodate the learned customers’ preferences [3]. The matching subprocess captures the extent to which learning about preferences enables acting upon those elicited preferences (i.e., informed action) to personalize service. EUT posits that efective use occurs when system users perform the tasks in a way that helps the user to attain the organizational goals for using the system (i.e., performance) [13,17]. The outcome of the matching subprocess determines whether the service provider uses the CSS efectively, thereby increasing the performance of the service personalization process.

EUT provides a conceptual framework to map both the learning and matching subprocesses within its ontological network. To learn and match elicited preferences, the provider must be able to retrieve them from the CSS. The ability to seamlessly operate the system to retrieve digital representations of customers’ preferences is called transparent interaction.

## 2.4. Performance

Performance, contextualized as customer value, depends on the benefit that a customer attains from consuming the service, which is “based on perceptions of what is received and what is given” by the recipient ([29], p. 14). Inherent to the notion of customer value is the idea that its value is perceived in-use. However, to obtain a measure of customer value $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ performance) after use has been the main challenge in IS research. Prior studies have measured IT-enabled service performance using, for instance, revenues [30] or service managers’ perception of customer satisfaction [31]. Others measured service performance after use as consumer satisfaction [32]. The EUT perspective aligns with measures of performance after the service is consumed by distinguishing efective use of the system (i.e., supporting personalization) from its outcome $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ enhancing customer value).

## 3. Hypotheses development

Mapping the service subprocesses (i.e., preference elicitation, learning, matching) to the EUT nomological network (Fig. 1) shows our EUT-informed research model for studying IT-enabled service personalization. We posit that the efective use of the CSS must increase performance $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ customer value generation). We test whether efective use enables higher performance by testing the relationship between the antecedent of efective use $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , preference elicitation) and its outcome $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ customer value). Efective use manifests in the service provider’s ability to take informed actions (i.e., delivering the elicited personalization options) based on faithful representations of customers’ needs.

In principle, learning and matching may apply to any service personalization process, whether it is personal or IT-enabled. For example, customers returning to a local restaurant can expect their preferences, learned through multiple previous human interactions with employees, to be simply remembered (i.e., learning) and acted upon by the wait staf (i.e., matching). We can think of it as a rudimental information system that builds on mental representations (stored in employers memory) of customers’ preferences.

## 3.1. Learning through a digital representation of elicited references

EUT posits that people rely on IS to obtain digital representations of a real-world domain to comprehend it better. In the service context, the provider must be able to get a faithful representation of the set of preferences that customers will value. Thus, we study preference elicitation from a representational perspective [16]. The intended use of IT-enabled CSS is to facilitate the customers’ preference elicitation process through a more efective channel for conveying personalization options to customers.

The inherent assumption is that IT-enabled CSS are more likely to elicit faithful representations from customers than the in-person channel, which leverages non-digital representation (e.g., verbal). Previous research shows that IT-enabled representations elicit more personalization options from more customers as compared to in-person approaches that leverage verbal representations [5] as digital systems convey more and richer signifiers [42]. Moreover, as we can assume that customers select customizations that they are genuinely interested in, each elicited personalization request improves the provider’s learning of the customer’s preferences. That means more elicited personalization requests mean a more complete and faithful representation of a customer’s domain of preferences. It also opens the possibility to elicit personalization requests from customers who would not personalize through the in-person system because they do not have access to the full set of personalization options. In summary, the IT-enabled CSS leads to a more faithful representation of the customers’ preferences by enabling a) superior preferences elicitation and b) maximizing the number of customers that communicate their preferences to the service provider. We formalize those questions into:

Hypothesis 1a. An IT-enabled CSS produces more faithful representations of customers’ preferences by increasing the number of learned preferences.

Hypothesis 1b. An IT-enabled CSS produces more faithful representations of customers’ preferences by increasing the number of customers who express learned preferences.

## 3.2. Matching service to elicited preferences

During the learning phase of the service personalization process, ITenabled CSS service providers elicit personalization options from the set of possible customizations available. For service personalization to enhance the customer experience, the service provider needs to match the learned customers’ preferences with the available items and deliver

the requested customizations.

Those customers who invest time and efort in personalizing expect a narrower expectation-delivery gap when receiving and experiencing service [33]. As documented in the literature, the narrower the gap, the higher the perception of service quality with a subsequent improvement in perceived satisfaction and value [5,34]. In the matching phase, the goal is to deliver the personalized service experience expected by the customer [35]. By doing so, the service provider creates customer value.

In other words, for performance to improve, it is not enough for the system to capture and store faithful representations of customer preferences (learning phase). The provider must still customize the service accordingly (matching phase). It is the extent to which the service providers successfully act upon the faithful representations they obtain from the system (i.e., informed action) that enhances task performance. Within EUT, performance (i.e., customer value generation) is a function of “how well the user is leveraging faithful representations obtained from the system in a task” ([13], p. 643). In the context of service personalization, informed action refers to employees’ personalization fulfillment activities based on customers’ requests. The greater the number of personalization requests from customers that are fulfilled by the employees, the smaller the expectation-delivery gap, and, in turn, the higher the users’ perception of customer value received. Formally:

Hypothesis 2. Higher rate of matched preferences during the matching phase increases customer value.

## 4. Methodology

This paper uses a longitudinal field study, rather than a laboratory experiment, to investigate efective system use. We rely on archival data in the context of the lodging segment of the hospitality industry. This research setting is particularly well suited to the study of efective use in service personalization because hotels represent prototypical service businesses. Specifically, we gather data from a network of independently run, three- and four-star hotels in Switzerland: Swiss Quality Hotels International (SQHI). In partnership with Innotour, a Swiss organization responsible for the promotion and development of the tourism sector in Switzerland, SQHI sponsored a project aimed at improving the personalization of guest experience in the participating hotels [36]. As the participating hotels belong to the same brand group, they are comparable in terms of operational standards, amenities quality, and organizational procedures. All the participating hotels installed Hoxell, an IT-enabled CSS that includes a module for service personalization called MyPage. The developer of Hoxell supported the endeavor by hiring two dedicated support staff who oversaw installations, training, and support for the participating hotels. Eight hotels were selected for the initiative, but only seven committed to the service personalization project. The hotels represent a range of sizes (from 45 to 106 rooms), segment focuses (leisure and business), and locations (city and resort). The installation of the personalization module began in April 2015 for the first hotel and concluded in October 2015 for the last one. For each hotel, we gathered time series data spanning from six months before the treatment (the introduction of the IT-enabled CSS) to twenty months after. This approach ensures sample comparability despite the diferent implementation dates.

## 4.1. Learning phase

Before adopting the IT-enabled CSS, all the hotels allowed guests to personalize through a personal process. During reservation, check-in, or the customers’ stays, the staf can verbally elicit preferences (e.g., “if you have any other questions or requests, please do not hesitate to contact us,” or “should you have any questions or requests, please dial 0 from your room”). Thus, customers’ preferences were elicited through open-ended conversation annotated in a specific field of the Property Management System (PMS) called “traces”. Traces are digital representations of the customers’ preferences, which employees can retrieve to communicate to the appropriate department the requests to fulfill during the matching phase. This process is compared against the IT-enabled preference elicitation (i.e., learning) process that became available when the new system was implemented.

Upon the implementation of the CSS’s personalization module, each hotel adds an IT-enabled preference elicitation process, which enables guests to record their preferences directly in the CSS. When guests re ceive a reservation confirmation, they are directed to a web page—i.e., MyPage—where they can select diferent options for personalizing their hotel stays. Guests used personal devices (e.g., tablets, laptops) to browse structured information with richer signifiers (i.e., descriptions, categories, images) than the personal/verbal channel for representing the options available.

The hotels in the study provided a range of personalization items (from 52 to 133)—e.g., room temperature, pillow and bedding types, drinks, and touristic amenities. The preferences are laid out by categories with images and restrictions, thus serving as signifiers [5,37] that make guests aware of the specific possibilities to customize their service experience (Fig. 2). The two systems enabling preference elicitation during the learning phase, the traditional in-person system and the ITenabled CSS, difer across the three layers of their structure (Table 1). Still, they aim at representing the same real-world domain: customers preferences. Once customers’ preferences are elicited on MyPage, they are recorded in Hoxell and available to hotel staf (in the matching phase).

## 4.2. Matching phase

The process of delivering personalization requests during the matching phase of the service personalization process does not difer regardless of how the preferences were elicited during the learning phase. On the day of guest arrival, rooms are assigned to each reservation by management. Matching is executed by the housekeepers responsible for preparing the rooms. They use an iPad to access the CSS (i.e., Hoxell) and retrieve the list of preferences associated with the reservation. The housekeeping, which uses the system on a daily basis, has not reported issues of interacting with the system, which suggests that interaction is transparent. Upon delivery of each personalized item, the housekeeper marks the record as completed. Requested items that are not delivered remain unchecked (Figs. 3 and 4).

We operationalize the performance of the service personalization process (H2) as customer value. Customer value is a perceptual measure as service quality is subjective rather than objective [35]. We extract customer value ratings from Booking.com which, unlike other review sites (e.g., TripAdvisor), enables only verified guests to post (those who actually completed a stay at the hotel they review). The review scores are assessed on a four-point ordinal scale with anchors: poor, fair, good, and excellent. An internal identifier on the CSS allows matching Booking.com review data with reservation and personalization data from each participating hotel. The result is a complete record which links customer value perceptions to their specific service experience, including personalization details (e.g., how many personalizations were requested and delivered). The dataset is therefore uniquely suited to evaluate the impact of informed action on the performance of the service personalization process.

We operationalize informed action during the matching phase as the degree to which an employee leverages faithful representation of customers’ preferences, as stored in the MyPage database, to provide customer value. The IT-enabled CSS we study requires that employees check out the specific personalized item requested by a guest (e.g., diet coke for the minibar, extra pillows) before placing them in the room. This data allows the computation of a personalization delivery percentage (PDC) measure: the ratio between the number of personalization items delivered and the number of personalization items requested by individual guests. Based on the definition of informed action, we interpret PDC as a measure of “the extent to which a user acts upon the faithful representation he or she obtains from the system” ([13], p. 642). Housekeeping staf use the system to access representations of the customer preferences and subsequently deliver the items to customize the room.

As all staf members have access to the same application and there was no indication of any dificulties in accessing and understanding the personalization preferences (i.e., transparent interaction), variations in PDC are due to variations in the employees’ ability to leverage preferences’ representations (e.g., an item becomes unavailable). PDC is a measure of “how well the user is leveraging faithful representations obtained from the system in a task” ([13], p. 643). It quantifies how accurately employees are able to perform the matching task, based on learning about customer’s elicited preferences. Thus, the relationship between PDC and Booking’s ratings reflects the one between informed action and performance (i.e., link 1b in Burton-Jones and Grange [13] general framework on efective use).

We note that in the case of low preference fulfillment, it is not important why the matching task cannot be fulfilled (e.g., the item requested by the customer is momentarily unavailable). What matters is the informed action as an enabler of performance [13]. Thus, as posited by EUT, if an employee is informed about the preferences of a customer but does not (or cannot) take action to complete the matching phase of the service personalization process, performance will sufer. To align with recent service personalization literature, we control for the following potential confounding factors: average daily room rate (ADR), length of stay in days (LOS), the price paid for personalized items (PPrice), and number of adults and children for each reservation [5].

## 6. Discussion

This research began to answer two research questions—the first is how CSS improves the service provider’s ability to learn about customers’ preferences. As our research model shows (Fig. 1), preference elicitation is the antecedent of efective use. The CSS enables customers and service providers to cocreate more customer value by using technology to elicit, collect, and deliver personalization preferences. From the customer perspective, the IT-enabled channel provides inherently richer signifiers (i.e., pictures and descriptions) than the personal one to express personalization preferences. Using representational jargon, as it befits our EUT perspective, we say that the IT-enabled CSS provides customers with a superior grammar for formulating preferences that map to available personalization options.

From a service provider’s perspective, instead, the CSS facilitates the capability to collect customers’ preferences because it elicits preferences that may otherwise be unelicited when using the personal channel. This explanation is supported by the significant increase in the number of preferences elicited from each customer (H1a) and the number of customers expressing preferences (H1b).

Our second research question concerns whether matching the ser vice to learned preferences increases customer value. This means understanding how efective use of the CSS impacts performance. Our findings support the positive relationship between employees’ efective use and customer value, measured by customers’ online review ratings (H2). It is worth noting that there were no significant diferences between the hotels. One possible explanation is that the participating hotels belong to the same lodging brand. Thus, they are comparable in terms of operational standards and procedures. This result highlights the impact of efective use on the improvement of overall performance. While previous literature claims that service personalization improves hotel guests’ evaluation of service and value [5], our research expands on this finding. It shows that it is not the personalization itself but the ability to act upon faithful representations of customers’ preferences (i.e., percentage of personalization delivery) that enhances customer value $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ performance). Another concern from prior research is that IT-enabled preference elicitation may—in some cases—underperform against face-to-face, personal elicitation. Our results are insuficient to conclude whether replacing the personal channel with the CSS implies losing in-person elicited preference. Thus, to maximize learning about customers’ preferences, service providers should use both channels. The IT-enabled CSS ofers an of-the-shelf solution for preferences’ elicitation, which is synergic to the previous personal process and increase overall customer value.

From a theoretical perspective, the model we propose enables the study of value cocreation from an IS (i.e., efective use) perspective. We depict efective use of IT-enabled CSS as enhancing customers’ ability to express their needs and, as a consequence, the service provider’s ability to learn about guests’ preferences. Others have studied CSS and personalization from diferent perspectives, analyzing the role of perception biases [19] or the introduction of personalization through persuasion [23]. For instance, providers may practice upselling by persuading customers to personalize their stays; but customers may perceive this tactic as a sale transaction rather than as a service that is rendered to them. Conversely, to draw on EUT means to study service personalization mostly as a representational problem.

The goal of any CSS, IT-enabled or otherwise, is to provide faithful representations of customer preferences. An IT-enabled CSS afords digital representation of customers’ preferences, which in turn enables customers to share larger preference sets with the service provider. At the same time, managers who oversee the implementation and operation of an IT-enabled CSS ought to focus on the antecedents and enablers of efective use. This includes keeping the list of available preferences updated and communicating the importance of matching elicited preferences to employees along with the negative impact of failing to match elicited preferences. During preference elicitation, customers’ expectations about personalized service increase, widening the expectation-delivery gap and resulting in dissatisfaction and lower customer value. Implementing CSS requires committing to deliver upon elicited preferences.

Perhaps, such an increase in expectations provides an alternative explanation to the loss of customer value that prior researchers observed when service providers switched from high-touch versus high-tech customer service. Future research may consider whether it is not high touch, personal service which provides a higher value per se—but rather the providers’ ability to deliver on expectations. Therefore, service managers who embrace high-tech service need to focus on efective use of CSS as an enabler of high touch, rather than thinking of high-tech as hindering high-touch service.

From a theoretical standpoint, this research contextualizes the construct of informed action in the IT-enabled CSS domain. Informed action consists of successfully delivering elicited personalization re quests, thereby matching customer service to customers’ preferences. We empirically validate the role of informed action in achieving performance goals (i.e., customer value). We defined informed action as the extent to which employees act upon the available representations in the system (i.e., customer preferences) during the matching phase of the service personalization process. In other words, a higher level of in formed action means that hotel employees fulfill a higher rate of personalization requests. Thus, efective system use will be higher when both the rate of learning of customers’ preferences and the share of preferences delivered increase.

## 7. Limitations and future research

While our research focuses on the antecedents and outcomes of effective use, our results are limiting in explaining the impact of two critical dimensions of efective use: transparent interaction and representational fidelity. As for representational fidelity, we could only assume that recorded preferences are “learned,” but do not know how efectively that process occurred. For instance, a CSS that systematically records “Soda” instead of “Coke” may enable informed action but through an ambiguous representation of the customer’s preference (low “representational fidelity”). Similarly, a CSS that is dificult to use for employees (low “transparent interaction”) may hinder their ability to match the service to customers’ preferences, and, eventually, decrease customer value. Other reasons for failing to match may be linked to each hotel’s work system (e.g., hotel service policy, processes). We controlled for hotel-specific sources of variance by modeling the individual efects of the hotels. Future research might investigate implementation-specific sources of variance, such as the ability to match elicited preferences as a function of the number of items ofered by the hotels, the general service quality, force majeure events, and the like. In this study, we expect minor diferences between hotels as installation, training, and support were standardized across hotels.

The research model suggests that, for an analysis of efective use to be complete, scholars need to tease out how users’ ability to attain transparent interaction and representational fidelity (i.e., learning) impacts their ability to take informed actions (i.e., matching). Moreover, there are contingent factors that impact informed action, which secondary data cannot capture—amongst all, why may certain hotels fail to match elicited preferences?

This archival study used a one-dimensional measure of customer value: online review ratings, which prior research considered as reflective of customers’ perceived value [38]. Online ratings allow capturing perceived value from actual guests after they experience the service. Still, this measure falls short on sample size and in distinguishing between the multiple factors that may concur to determine the review scores.

## 8. Conclusions

Our results show that the IT-enabled CSS improves the elicitation of customers’ service personalization needs, ofering opportunities to act upon learned customers’ preferences to increase customer value—the intended outcome of a service personalization initiative. We conjectured that this occurs because IT-enabled CSS yields more faithful representations of customers’ preferences to service providers. However, adopting such a CSS to elicit customers’ preferences is a double-edged sword. On one side, IT-enabled CSS elicits more personalization requests from guests. That means hoteliers who support personalization processes using IT-enabled CSS attain more complete representations of customers’ preferences. On the other side, service must fulfill customers’ expectations once customers have invested time to personalize.

To study customer personalization from the EUT perspective means conceptualizing service personalization as a problem of efective use of the CSS for faithfully representing customers’ preferences. To develop this conceptualization, we contributed a research model that heeds the recent call for applying the Representation Theory, which EUT draws upon, in new contexts, to assess its generalizability [16].
