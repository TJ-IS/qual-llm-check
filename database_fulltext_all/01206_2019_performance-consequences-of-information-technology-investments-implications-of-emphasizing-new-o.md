Abstract. Senior executives seek investments in information technology (IT) initiatives that enhance the performance of their firms. They frequently must decide whether to emphasize the adoption of new IT (ENIT) or the maintenance and refinement of current IT (ECIT). This research examines how the combination of ENIT and ECIT with the firm’s business strateg affects firm performance at varying levels of organizational commitment to IT (OCIT). Using the resource-based view, we argue that a firm can benefit from new IT or current IT to a greater extent when it possesses the resources needed for exploration (as is more likely for firms with a Prospector strategy) or exploitation (as is more likely for firms with a Defender strategy), respectively, with these effects increasing as the firm’s OCIT increases. The results, based on an eight-year panel data set developed from multiple secondary sources, support our argument that firms are not homogeneous in the benefits they reap from ENIT and ECIT. Thus, a joint consideration of the core business strategy and OCIT in a firm provides insights into whether the necessary organizational resources are available and leveraged to effectivel reap benefits from ENIT or ECIT. For Defenders emphasizing new IT, the marginal benefit decreases with an increase in OCIT, but for Defenders emphasizing current IT, the marginal benefit increases with an increase in OCIT. By contrast, for Prospectors emphasizing new IT, the marginal benefit increases with an increase in OCIT, but for Prospectors emphasizing current IT, the marginal benefit decreases with an increase in OCIT. Theoretical and practical implications of these results are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The Roman god Janus possessed two pairs of eyes, one set for looking ahead and the other for looking behind. Information technology (IT) executives can relate. On the one hand, the vibrant IT market, with frequent new releases, designs, and options (Lu and Ramamurthy 2011), requires these executives to look forward to new technologies that influence the firm’s future. On the other hand, they also need to constantly look backward, attending to the technologies the firm acquired earlier, as IT generates benefits over time (Kohli et al. 2012). As firms face more tightly controlled IT budgets (e.g., Gartner 2016), allocating IT resources to new or current technologies becomes more important. Therefore, should a firm emphasize new IT (ENIT) or its current IT (ECIT) when allocating IT investments?

The prior literature on the business value of IT focuses on the impact of IT investments on firm productivity at an aggregate level, combining all IT resources (e.g., Mithas et al. 2012). Departing from this focus on aggregate IT investments, recent studies examine more specific IT investments. Two notable examples are a study of the increase in efficiency from a firm’s current IT investments (Chang and Gurbaxani 2013) and a study of the performance impacts of new IT investments such as cloud infrastructures (Son et al. 2014). Dos Santos et al (2012) recognize the trade-off between new and continuing investments and disaggregate IT investments into these two types. They examine the market demand for both types of investment but not their impacts on firm performance. This paper departs from the prior literature by being, to the best of our knowledge, the first to theorize about and empirically examine the impacts of both ENIT and ECIT on firm performance.

To theorize about the impacts of ENIT and ECIT, we draw on the resource-based view (e.g., Wade and Hulland 2004, Aral and Weill 2007), according to which the value of IT resources depends on the availability of needed supporting resources. We link ENIT and ECIT to exploration and exploitation, respectively (Dos Santos et al. 2012). We then view business strategy as influencing the presence of resources that support exploration and exploitation, and thereby, ENIT and ECIT. Specifically, we adopt the business strategy typology of Prospector, Defender, and Analyzer (Miles and Snow 1978), which has been linked to resource allocation<sup>1</sup> (e.g., Sabherwal and Chan 2001, McLaren et al. 2011). Prospectors and Defenders allocate resources to exploration and exploitation, respectively, whereas Analyzers allocate resources to both aspects (e.g., Conant et al. 1990, Sidhu et al. 2004). Thus, Prospectors and Defenders have the underlying resources to support ENIT and ECIT, respectively, whereas Analyzers have the resources to support both ENIT and ECIT.

Furthermore, this paper recognizes that a firm may allocate resources to support exploration or exploitation, but these resources may or may not be leveraged for IT initiatives (e.g., Jarvenpaa and Ives 1991). Instead, the extent to which a firm’s resources support exploration or exploitation through IT depends on the organizational commitment to IT (OCIT), which may be viewed in terms of the extent to which the firm commits resources or managerial attention to IT (Bharadwaj 2000, Preston et al. 2008). In firms with greater OCIT, senior business executives’ greater attention to IT and closer interaction with IT executives enable greater use of organizational resources to leverage IT (Preston et al. 2008). Thus, a firm with greater OCIT may be able to better use its resources for exploration and exploitation when emphasizing new and current technologies, respectively.

Overall, by connecting both IT emphasis (ENIT and ECIT) and business strategy emphasis with exploration and exploitation, we develop the argument that a firm can benefit more from new (current) IT when it possesses the resources needed to support exploration (exploitation), as is more likely in Prospectors (Defenders). Moreover, a firm with a greater commitment to IT is more likely to bring its relevant organizational resources to bear on IT. As shown in the research model (Figure 1), we expect business strategy (viewed as a pattern of business resource allocation) to influence the extent to which ENIT and ECIT affect firm performance, and OCIT to moderate this relationship. Thus, the research model connects business strategy and OCIT to the firm performance consequences of ENIT and ECIT.

We test the research model using a panel data set compiled from multiple secondary sources. The final sample consists of 268 firms and 687 unique firm-year observations. As expected, we find that a firm’s OCIT, ENIT, and ECIT directly affect firm performance. However, as OCIT increases (decreases), the effect of the consistency between the firm’s business strategy emphasis and its IT allocation emphasis on firm performance increases (decreases) for Prospectors and Defenders. We find no such relationship for Analyzers.

## 2. Theoretical Development

## 2.1. Exploration and Exploitation Through IT

An enduring notion in the literature is that an organization’s long-term success depends on how well it explores new resources and exploits current resources (March 1991, Uotila et al. 2009). Exploration refers to the pursuit of new knowledge, technologies, and opportunities. Exploitation refers to the use and refinement of prior knowledge and technologies (Im and Rai 2008, Raisch et al. 2009). IT assets can also take on either an explorative or exploitative role (Sambamurthy et al. 2003). A firm may pursue exploration with ENIT. ENIT involves searching for and adopting new IT and solutions (e.g., Benaroch et al. 2006). Assimilating new IT into the existing architecture entails a high risk of failure. By contrast, a firm may engage in exploitation with ECIT. ECIT enables firms to assimilate existing IT within the firm and address their deficiencies but may not facilitate innovation.

The correspondence between ENIT/ECIT and exploration/exploitation finds support in the prior literature, which suggests that exploration relies on new technological resources whereas exploitation relies on current technological resources (Jansen et al. 2006, Wei et al. 2014). Along similar lines, Dos Santos et al. (2012) classify investments by firms into investments in current IT applications (CBI) or new IT applications (NII), and they connect them to exploitation and exploration:

The exploitation versus exploration categorization of the adaptive processes used by firms is similar to our own categorization of IT investments. A CBI is aimed at exploiting the firm’s current capabilities whereas a NII takes advantage of new opportunities and helps a firm adapt to potential threats. (p. 289)

The categorization of an IT investment into explorative or exploitative is based on both the IT and the firm allocating its resources. Although technologies are made available in the market frequently, their adoption by firms is not instantaneous and is not tied directly to the novelty or innovativeness of the technology. For example, if firm A invests in a business intelligence platform it did not have previously, this would be considered an exploratory investment and thus ENIT. However, if firm B invests further in its existing business intelligence platform, this would be considered as exploitative investment and thus ECIT. Although the underlying IT may be identical in both the firms, its implementation would be exploratory for firm A and exploitative for firm B because firm A is looking at new IT opportunities and firm B is continuing to exploit existing IT opportunities.

## 2.2. Resource-Based View and IT Investment

The resource-based view of the firm postulates that a firm’s competitive advantage rests on the value, rarity, and immobility of its resources (Barney 1991). The information systems literature has used the resource-based view to argue that IT investments do not operate in isolation to generate benefits for a firm (Wade and Hulland 2004). Rather, other supporting resources should be in place for a firm to benefit from IT investments (Wade and Hulland 2004, Nevo and Wade 2010).

Being discrete approaches, business exploration and exploitation require distinct supporting resources (He and Wong 2004). Exploration requires the support of resources that enable innovation, such as research and development (R&D) investments (Gupta et al. 2006), and flexible structures that enable learning and quick adjustments to new initiatives (March 1991). By contrast, exploitation requires resources that provide the infrastructure needed to maintain, complement, and assimilate existing investments (e.g., Rothaermel 2001) and support continuous improvement and efficiency (e.g., He and Wong 2004). Likewise, ENIT and ECIT, which focus on exploration and exploitation, respectively, require supporting resources from across the organization to leverage these IT investments. In the following section, these resources are linked to the firm’s business strategy.

## 2.3. Business Strategy, Exploration, and Exploitation

A firm’s business strategy reflects its pattern of resource allocation, especially when the focus is on the realized strategy (Mintzberg 1978, Mintzberg and Waters 1985, Oh and Pinsonneault 2007). For example, Venkatraman and Prescott (1990) conceptualize and measure business strategy “as a pattern of strategic resource deployments in key areas” (p. 7). Moreover, as discussed earlier, the resource allocations by a firm enable it to pursue explo ration or exploitation.

Prospectors make frequent changes and improvements in their products, services, and markets, seeking to be the first to develop new products and services (Miles and Snow 1978, Sabherwal and Chan 2001). They allocate resources in a way that supports and strengthens innovation, enabling them to quickly respond to changes in their environment (Peck 1994, Delery and Doty 1996). Accordingly, Prospectors have been argued to focus on exploration (Menguc and Auh 2008). By contrast, Defenders offer a relatively stable range of products and services and try to do the best they can in their area of activity (Miles and Snow 1978, Peck 1994) They allocate resources in a way that enables exploitation and helps them pursue efficiency, specialization, and stability (Camelo-Ordaz et al. 2003). Thus, Prospectors (Defenders) are more likely to possess the resources needed for exploration (exploitation).

Combining the strengths of Prospectors and Defenders, Analyzers simultaneously pursue growth while minimizing risk (Sabherwal and Chan 2001). They maintain a stable domain of core products while seeking new product/market opportunities, usually by quickly following Prospectors’ new products rather than initiating their own new products. To address these conflicting demands of efficiency and innovation, Analyzers allocate resources to pursue both exploration and exploitation to some extent. Moreover, Analyzers are more likely to possess the resources needed for both exploration and exploitation, although not to the extent of Prospectors and Defenders. This may be due to Analyzers’ desire to remain balanced (Sabherwal and Chan 2001) and not dedicate resources to establish deep routines, processes, skills, and knowledge supporting only exploration or exploitation.

Although a firm’s business strategy reflects its pattern of resource allocation, firms pursuing the same strategy may differ in the extent to which they bring their resources to bear on IT activities (Sabherwal et al. 2019) Next, we discuss how OCIT affects this relationship.

## 2.4. Organizational Commitment and Leveraging Organizational Resources for IT

Considerable prior literature on the strategic management of IT and the management of IT projects highlights the importance of commitment to IT by the organization (e.g., Kearns and Lederer 2004, Preston et al. 2008), the top management (e.g., Newman and Sabherwal 1996), and the chief executive officer (e.g., Powell and Dent-Micallef 1997). Recognizing the similarity among these terms, we focus on OCIT, which may be viewed in two interrelated ways: as verbal support for IT and as the allocation of greater organizational resources to IT (Preston et al. 2008).

Firms with a higher level of OCIT are characterized by senior business and IT executives having a stronger relationship (Preston et al. 2008) and improved communication (Kuruzovich et al. 2012), greater topmanagement involvement in IT (Jarvenpaa and Ives 1991), and greater inclusion of IT executives in business planning (Kearns and Lederer 2004). As a result, a greater level of OCIT is expected to enable greater use of, or access to, organizational resources for IT activities (Powell and Dent-Micallef 1997). By contrast, when OCIT is low, IT activities receive less attention and may not benefit from relevant organizational resources or receive the support needed for their successful implementation.

Therefore, when a firm has resources associated with exploration (i.e., pursues a Prospector strategy) and has greater access to and use of those resources (i.e., has greater OCIT), the performance benefits from ENIT, which is consistent with exploration, are higher. By contrast, the performance benefits for such a firm from ECIT, which is consistent with exploitation but not exploration, are lower. Investments in new IT are better used when the IT function has greater access to organizational resources that enable the acquisition and leveraging of new IT, such as R&D. For example, if a Prospector firm, which has liquid assets and invests resources in $\scriptstyle \mathrm { R } \& \mathrm { D } , ^ { 2 }$ has a high level of OCIT, topmanagement support may facilitate the IT function’s use of these resources to gain more benefits from new IT. Specifically, investing in new IT is associated with uncertainty, and additional liquidity enables the firm to bear the costs of addressing the uncertainty without hindering its organizational and IT operations. Moreover, a stronger R&D function better orients the firm to track the movements of competitors and the broader environment, providing the firm with better insights into new technologies, their potential drawbacks and merits, and how to deploy them. Additionally, greater R&D resources enable a better understanding of innovative technologies that are currently used in one sector but can be deployed in other sectors. Such insights are more accessible to the IT function as OCIT increases and IT managers are better integrated with the rest of the organization. By contrast, if the same firm has a low level of OCIT, lack of top-management support and IT managers access to organizational resources limits the opportunities to deploy new IT. Hence, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 1 (H1). The greater the commitment of a Prospector firm to IT, the greater the increase in its performance as its emphasis on new IT increases.<sup>3</sup>

However, a Prospector firm lacks the exploitation resources needed to increase the efficiency of existing resources or maintain current assets, as reflected by lower sales<sup>4</sup> generated from the same assets and lower investments in equipment. Hence, a Prospector focusing on current IT may not be able to exploit resources as well as other firms and derive less value from such IT. As OCIT increases in a Prospector, internal resources within its IT function, such as IT employees, are likely to be more focused on exploration. Because a Prospector lacks resources that enable exploitation, reaping benefits from ECIT requires its IT unit to refocus its resources on exploitation. For instance, IT employees in a Prospector with high OCIT are likely to concentrate on screening new developments in IT and may have been hired because they can seamlessly transition to new IT and train others to use new IT. To reap benefits from ECIT, they would need to be either retrained or replaced by employees with greater experience in system efficiency and maintenance. Furthermore, the adverse effect of pursuing ECIT in Prospectors increases with an increase in OCIT as a result of the increased opportunity cost of not pursuing the appropriate IT for the business strategy (i.e., ENIT). By contrast, if OCIT is low, restructuring and opportunity costs may be lower because the firm may not have invested resources in exploration for IT. Hence, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 2 (H2). The greater the commitment of a Prospector firm to IT, the greater the decrease in its performance as its emphasis on current IT increases.

Similar to the above arguments, when a firm focuses on resources needed for exploitation (i.e., pursues a Defender strategy), an increase in ECIT (which is consistent with exploitation) improves performance. By contrast, an increase in ENIT, which is consistent with exploration but not exploitation, hurts performance. A Defender firm, which typically generates greater sales from the same assets and invests more in equipment, allocates greater organizational resources to repair and maintenance across various areas of the firm. These organizational resources can enable exploitation and enhancement of existing IT, and thereby help generate greater benefits from ECIT. A higher OCIT would provide the IT unit greater access to such organizational resources. For instance, in a Defender firm with high OCIT, top-management support for IT may provide the IT unit greater access to the organizational budgetary resources allocated to repair and maintenance. The IT unit can then leverage these resources to review, maintain, and enhance existing IT and subsequently have a greater impact on performance. Additionally, greater OCIT can lead to some of a Defender’s considerable budget for assets and equipment to be allocated to continuing IT infrastructure investments. By contrast, when OCIT is low, the IT unit is not as well positioned to use the organizational resources of a Defender to benefit from ECIT.

Hypothesis 3 (H3). The greater the commitment of a Defender firm to IT, the greater the increase in its performance as its emphasis on current IT increases.

However, a Defender firm does not invest in exploration resources, such as R&D. As OCIT increases in a Defender firm, internal resources within its IT unit, such as IT employees, may be more focused on exploitation. Because a Defender lacks resources to enable exploration, reaping benefits from ENIT requires its IT unit to refocus its resources on exploration. For instance, IT employees in a Defender with high OCIT are likely to focus on, and may even have been hired for, the maintenance and refinement of existing systems. To reap benefits from ENIT, these employees need to be either retrained or replaced by individuals with greater experience in functions such as developing new vendor relationships, identifying new IT opportunities, and training users about new IT. Moreover, the adverse effect of pursuing ENIT for Defenders increases with an increase in OCIT as a result of the increased opportunity cost of not pursuing the IT appropriate for the business strategy (i.e., ECIT). Also, if OCIT is low, restructuring and opportunity costs may be lower, as the firm may not invest resources in exploitation of IT.

Hypothesis 4 (H4). The greater the commitment of a Defender firm to IT, the greater the decrease in its performance as its emphasis on new IT increases.

Unlike Defenders and Prospectors, the role of OCIT is less clear in Analyzers. Analyzers possess moderate levels of both exploration and exploitation resources, and if they have a high level of OCIT, they may be likely to invest either set of resources in ENIT or ECIT. By contrast, if they have a low level of OCIT, they may be less likely to invest either set of resources in ENIT or ECIT because of the restructuring and opportunity costs mentioned above. Thus, firms with a higher leve of OCIT achieve better performance than firms with lower levels of OCIT, regardless of business strategy, ENIT, and ECIT. We do not expect any interaction effect (such as H1–H4) for Analyzers beyond this main effect of OCIT. Figure 2 depicts the research model, with Analyzers excluded for this reason.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Data

The data used for this paper were compiled from three secondary data sources: InformationWeek, Compustat, and Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP). InformationWeek<sup>5</sup> provides a yearly survey and ranking of the top 500 firms that are IT-intensive users across different industries. We use data from 2000 to 2007 as the basis for IT-specific information such as investments and financial budget ratios related to IT expenditures. Earlier data from InformationWeek have been used in other empirical studies (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 1999). We use an updated version of these data and supplement them with a variety of financial data on each firm-year observation from Compustat and CRSP.

We exclude (a) foreign firms (including foreignbased firms that are listed in the United States as com mon stock or American Depositary Receipts as well as those that are not listed in the United States), private firms, and subsidiaries or divisions of firms, for which specific stock market information is not available, to ensure the availability of key controls for the analysis; (b) financial firms (i.e., Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes 6000–6999) because the control variables used in this analysis operate differently for them (Fama and French 2001); and (c) firms for which segment data are not available within Compustat because of our use of market share and differences across industries in which our sample firms operate as control variables. These exclusions result in an initial sample of 657 firms across eight years, with 2,224 firm-year observations.

## 3.2. Measures

3.2.1. Dependent Variables. Firm performance is measured using Tobin’s q, a market-based measure of firm value, which has been extensively used as a performance measure in the business value of IT literature (Bharadwa et al. 1999, Chari et al. 2008, Havakhor et al. 2019). Tobin’s q is a forward-looking and risk-adjusted measure of firm performance that has been found to be less vulnerable to differing accounting practices and suitable for measuring the impact of IT investments (Chari et al. 2008). We measure Tobin’s q as in the prior literature (Chung and Pruitt 1994). A Tobin’s q over 1 indicates that the firm’s market value is greater than the replacement value of its assets, which indicates an unmeasured source of firm value (Bharadwaj et al. 1999)

3.2.2. Control Variables. Before describing our independent variables, we briefly discuss the control variables used in this study. Following the recommendations of prior work (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Mithas et al. 2012, Bardhan et al. 2013), we include both firm-level (Firm size, Advertising intensity, and Market share) and industry-level (Median Tobin’s q, Median capital intensity, Concentration ratio, and Environmental turbulence) controls that have been found to be correlated with the dependent variable. The inclusion of these control variables helps rule out alternative explanations by reducing unobserved variable bias and accounting for their variance in the model (Chari et al. 2008).

Firm size is included as a control because prior studies find it to be associated with performance (Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Mithas et al. 2012). Advertising intensity is shown to be associated with firm performance, especially market performance such as Tobin’s q (Aral and Weill 2007). Several theories, such as efficiency theory (Buzzell and Gale 1987), argue that Market share is associated with firm performance (Bharadwaj et al. 1999). Median Tobin’s q is used to capture the differences in performance across industries that may not be addressed by other controls (Dess et al. 1990, Bardhan et al. 2013). Median capital intensity is used because firms in industries with greater levels of capital may be better able to use the increased capital to make strategic moves. Concentration ratio is used to control for the influence of the top four firms in the industry (Montgomery and Wernerfelt 1988, Bharadwaj et al. 1999). Finally, Environmental turbulence is used to control for the effects that shifts in the environment may have on each firm (Xue et al. 2011, Havakhor et al. 2019, Sabherwal et al. 2019). Table 2 summarizes the operationalization of these control variables.

3.2.3. Independent Variables. We compute ENIT and ECIT using an aspect of the InformationWeek survey that has not received prior attention. Each year, InformationWeek provides a list of current and new IT and asks respondents to indicate the ones currently employed in their firms. The surveys from 2000 to 2007 include 41 unique technologies<sup>8</sup> (listed in Online Appendix A). In each year’s survey, there are 11–20 technologies, with several included in multiple years. To calculate ENIT and ECIT for a firm, we examine its responses for both years t <sup>−</sup> 1 and t to determine which technologies were adopted in both years t – 1 and t (i.e., continued investment in IT) and which were adopted in year t but not in year t – 1 (i.e., new investment in IT). Thus, to measure ENIT and ECIT for a firm in year t, the firm needs to be in the survey in both years t and t <sup>−</sup> 1.

Online Appendix B provides a road map of the measurement and analyses for our paper, including links to the tables and other appendices. For any IT to be considered in computing ECIT in year t, a firm must have adopted that IT in both years t and t <sup>−</sup> 1. If an IT was adopted in year t but not in year t – 1, it is considered within ENIT. To use a conservative estimate of ENIT and remain consistent with the consecutive-year requirement of ECIT, we consider an IT within ENIT only if it was included in the survey in both years t <sup>−</sup> 1 and t but was not adopted by the focal firm in year t <sup>−</sup> 1. This reflects the view (e.g., Dos Santos et al. 2012) that the same IT could constitute ENIT in one firm (which is adopting it for the first time in that year) and ECIT in another firm (which adopted it in a previous year). Online Appendix C provides detailed computations and sample estimations of ENIT and ECIT. After categorizing each IT in which a firm invests during year t into either ECIT or ENIT, we count the number of technologies in each category (i.e., ECIT or ENIT) for each firm-year to obtain continuous values of ECIT and ENIT. We control for changes in the survey—that is, the addition and removal of technologies—and consider alternative measures of ECIT and ENIT, as discussed in Section 4.2.

Recognizing that top managers both talk more about and spend more money on what is important to them, OCIT can be viewed in terms of either verbal or budgetary commitment (Preston et al. 2008). The verbal measure has support in the prior literature (e.g.,

Jarvenpaa and Ives 1990, Peslak 2005), suggesting that in organizations committed to IT for performing core activities, the annual report and the chairman’s letter to the shareholders will reflect that. For this measure, we examine the IT commitment for each sample firm via its public annual reports (i.e., Forms 10-K and 10-KSB). Consistent with the prior literature that uses a textanalysis approach to understand the strategic message of an annual report (e.g., Hynes and Bexley 2004, Rutherford 2005, Li 2008), we analyze a firm’s annua report to estimate its OCIT based on the verbal emphasis of IT within the reports. OCIT is calculated as the ratio of sentences in the firm’s annual report where words (or bigrams) such as “information technology,” “telecommunication,” “computer systems,” “digital,” “web,” and “online” and the specific technologies in the InformationWeek surveys are mentioned. We use a metasearch engine to index the keywords mentioned in the annual reports of our sample firms. On average the combination of a firm’s annual reports in each year includes 1,612 sentences, with 38 sentences mentioning IT-related keywords.<sup>9</sup> Online Appendix D presents a few examples of IT-related keywords in Black & Decker’s 2007 annual report. The verbal measure of OCIT is used for the primary analyses in this paper. The budgetary measure of OCIT, com puted as the ratio of the firm’s yearly IT budget allocation to its annual sales revenue, is used for robustness tests.

We use profile deviation analysis and Compustat data to identify the business strategy (i.e., Prospector, Defender, or Analyzer) (Miles and Snow 1978) for each firm-year observation in terms of patterns of resource allocation, with ideal profiles assessed based on the prior literature (Delery and Doty 1996, Sabherwal and Chan 2001, Sabherwal and Sabherwal 2007, Sabherwal et al 2019). Online Appendix E describes the procedure.<sup>10</sup> Online Appendix F shows how the key research variables (Business strategy, OCIT, ENIT, and ECIT) are computed for Black & Decker for 2007. As discussed earlier, we focus on the difference between exploration and exploitation. Accordingly, we represent business strategy using two dummy variables for Prospectors (high in exploration but low in exploitation) and Defenders (high in exploitation but low in exploration), with Analyzers being the reference group. Additionally, we provide results of split-sample analyses for all three strategies in the following sections to allow for a deeper examination of each strategy.

## 3.3. Analyses

Table 3 provides summary statistics and correlations for the key variables, using the verbal measure for OCIT. Before testing the research model, we standardize all variables and generate the interactions from these standardized variables (Cohen et al. 2013). The highest variance inflation factor is 2.72, well below the recommended threshold of 3.3, indicating that multicollinearity is not a serious concern (Cenfetelli and Bassellier 2009). As mentioned earlier, our sample consists of repeated observations of firms across multiple years, which leads to two potential econometric issues: heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation. The error terms related to each industry and firm observation are likely to fluctuate over time, resulting in panel-specific heteroskedasticity. A modified Wald test $( \chi ^ { \hat { 2 } } ( 2 4 7 ) = 1 . 9 \mathrm { { e } } + 3 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 \dot { 0 } )$ indicates the presence of heteroskedasticity. Next, to test for autocorrelation, we run the Wooldridge (2010) test $( F ( 1 , 1 2 6 ) = 7 . 3 2 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , which indicates the presence of autocorrelation. Because this autocorrelation could differ both within and across firms, we test for panel-specific autocorrelation. The results of these diagnostic tests indicate the need to address both heteroskedasticity and panel-specific autocorrelation. Therefore, we use feasible generalized least squares to estimate the models for hypothesis testing.

## 4.2. Results of the Robustness Tests

Before discussing the implications of the results, we conduct 28 robustness tests to address possible concerns regarding the estimation and measurement of the focal variables (including OCIT, ECIT, and ENIT) and a series of potential biases.<sup>11</sup> We summarize the potential concerns and the subsequent tests in Table 6 and provide the specific details and results of each test in Online Appendices H and I. Online Appendix H describes 15 robustness tests. One of those tests (specifically, in Model R7 in Table H2) uses the budgetary measure of OCIT, which is computed using InformationWeek 500 data as described in Online Appendix H. Moreover, we use the budgetary measure of OCIT to conduct 13 robustness tests that were originally conducted using the verbal measure of OCIT, as described in Online Appendix I. The results of these 28 robustness tests remain consistent and significant with our theoretical model across all tests, providing further support and confidence in our findings. Online Appendix J reports the results of tests of a potential sample-selection bias.

These tests do not provide any indication that the results of this study are specific to the sample firms. Thus, we find consistent support for the research model across a variety of independent and dependent variables, estimation techniques, and tests to address endogeneity, causality, and sample biases.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Key Findings

Senior business and IT executives regularly face decisions about allocating resources between the adoption of new IT and maintenance and refinement of existing IT. When considering their direct effects, both ENIT and ECIT contribute positively to the performance of Prospectors, Analyzers, and Defenders.<sup>12</sup> However, as we propose, firms are not homogeneous in the benefits they reap from ENIT and ECIT. Building on the literature on exploration/ exploitation and the resource-based view, we discuss and empirically show that a joint consideration of the core business strategy and OCIT provides insights into whether the necessary organizational resources are avail able and leveraged to effectively use ENIT or ECIT. For Prospectors (Defenders) investing in ENIT (ECIT), the marginal benefit increases with an increase in OCIT, and for Prospectors (Defenders) investing in ECIT (ENIT), the marginal benefit decreases with an increase in OCIT.<sup>13</sup> For Analyzers, the benefits reaped from ENIT and ECIT do not vary when OCIT changes, possibly because of the reduced restructuring or opportunity costs to access the resources associated with ENIT or ECIT as Analyzers possess balanced levels of both.

## 5.2. Limitations

The findings of this study should be examined in light of its limitations. First, in determining ENIT and ECIT, we are confined to the limited list of IT used in the InformationWeek surveys and by the fact that InformationWeek data were available only until 2007. We encourage future research to investigate alternative data sources and measures for ENIT and ECIT to examine the robustness of our findings. Second, the focus on publicly traded firms listed in the InformationWeek surveys limits the generalizability of our findings, as the listed firms are regarded as more successful in using IT (Bharadwaj 2000). Thus, caution is needed in generalizing their practices to other types of organizations. Although we do not find any empirical evidence to indicate differences between our sample and the larger set of firms in the Compustat database, future research should investigate the robustness of our findings in alternative samples of organizations (e.g., private, financial, small businesses). Finally, we used Miles and Snow’s (1978) typology of Prospectors, Analyzers, and Defenders to identify the business strategy and supporting resources that an organization possesses. Alternative methods should be used to analyze the general strategic approach of firms to extend the findings of this research to other strategic frameworks.

## 5.3. Implications for Research and Practice

Above, we have offered some suggestions for future research to address the study’s limitations. The study’s results have additional implications for future research that go beyond additional data collection or new samples. First, the results point to future research that can provide deeper insights into the effects of IT adoption by firms. This paper has focused on the performance consequences of the emphasis on new and current IT, but further research is needed to examine what affects ENIT and ECIT. For example, future research can examine whether the new IT investments are being pursued to investigate entirely new areas (implying more exploration) or to replace legacy systems (implying greater exploration as a result of the new IT as well as lesser exploitation as a result of the replacement of the legacy system). Similarly, another avenue for future research is to examine whether firms with greater turnover of business and IT executives exhibit greater ENIT, as may be expected based on the literature (e.g., March 1991).

Second, future research can provide insights into whether the effect of exploration or exploitation through IT depends on how the firm pursues it. For example, a firm could adopt a new IT (or continue an existing IT) while locating it internally and hiring employees with the related skills. Another firm could adopt (or continue) the same new IT through the cloud and outsourcing the needed roles to a vendor. These situations imply different levels of exploration (or exploitation) through IT and may have different performance implications. Further research is needed to understand these aspects.

Third, this study focuses on exploration and exploitation in terms of the focal firm’s adoption of new technologies and continuation with prior technologies. Exploration and exploitation could also be examined in terms of the use of the IT because some technologies may be used for either exploration or exploitation. Also, the results regarding a decrease in ECIT have potential implications for the discontinuation<sup>14</sup> of IT previously used in the firm. To reduce ECIT, as a Prospector should do, the firm should discontinue some of its legacy technologies. An inability to do so, which seems to be associated with a lower level of OCIT, hurts the Prospector firm’s performance. Thus, research on exploration and exploitation in terms of both use and discontinuation of IT may provide valuable insights.

Finally, we examine OCIT in terms of two aspects, using verbal commitment as the primary measure and budgetary commitment for robustness tests. The results of these analyses are consistent in the context of the research questions addressed in this study. However, future research on whether these two aspects of commitment differ in terms of their effects in other areas may be beneficial.

This paper also offers important practical implications. We believe that the results enable IT decision makers to appropriately emphasize new and current IT by examining the firm’s business strategy and the extent of OCIT. More specifically, the results indicate that Prospector (Defender) firms with high OCIT can derive greater benefits by allocating their resources to new (current) IT and using their resources that support exploration (exploitation). By contrast, Prospector (Defender) firms with high OCIT should allocate lower resources to the maintenance (adoption) of current (new) IT.

## 6. Contributions

This paper contributes to the literature on IT resource allocation by recognizing the explorative and exploitative nature of ENIT and ECIT, respectively, and examining the factors affecting the benefits from them. It shows that a joint consideration of business strategy (reflecting the presence of organizational resources supporting exploration or exploitation) and OCIT (reflecting the extent to which the organizational resources can be leveraged for IT) is needed to understand the benefits from ENIT and ECIT. Reaping benefits from investments in new and continuing IT requires distinct supporting resources, and a firm with greater OCIT dedicates the needed supporting resources to IT to a greater extent. The results also shed light on the conditions in which emphasizing IT can reduce the marginal benefits of the investments—that is, when a firm allocates its IT resources to aspects that do not match its business strategy. Thus, the paper extends the prior literature (e.g., Dos Santos et al. 2012) on the demand for new and continuing IT investments by (a) providing empirical evidence of the value they create for a firm and (b) identifying the firm-specific conditions that affect the extent of the value created. It also extends the prior theoretical literature (e.g., Wade and Hulland 2004) on how organizational resources enhance the value of IT investments by (a) showing that the mere presence of supporting resources may not suffice and (b) identifying an important link (i.e., OCIT) through which those resources are brought to bear on IT.

## 7. Conclusion

The IT market is continually changing, with frequent new releases, designs, and options. Such a dynamic market requires senior IT executives to look closely at investing in new technologies that may shape a firm’s future. However, they also need to pay careful attention to supporting the technologies the firm acquired earlier in order to derive long-term benefits from them. The allocation of IT resources to new or current technologies is an important issue, which is becoming even more prominent as IT budgets become more tightly controlled.

This study reveals that a joint consideration of a firm’s core business strategy and organizational commitment to IT provides insights into whether the firm should emphasize new IT or current IT when allocating IT investments. Firms pursuing Defender, Prospector, and Analyzer business strategies (Miles and Snow 1978) differ sharply in the benefits they receive from new IT and current IT. With an increase in organizational commitment to IT, Defenders obtain greater benefits from emphasizing current IT, whereas Prospectors obtain greater benefits from emphasizing new IT. For Analyzers, an increase in organizational commitment to IT does not influence the benefits obtained from emphasizing new IT or current IT. Thus, senior IT executives should examine their business strategy and organizational commitment to IT when prioritizing investment in new IT relative to investment in current IT.
