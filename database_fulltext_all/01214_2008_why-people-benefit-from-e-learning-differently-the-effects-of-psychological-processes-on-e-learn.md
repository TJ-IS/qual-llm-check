# Why people benefit from e-learning differently: The effects of psychological processes on e-learning outcomes

Zeying Wan \*, Yinglei Wang, Nicole Haggerty

Richard Ivey School of Business, The University of Western Ontario, London, ON N6A 3K7, Canada

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 27 August 2007 Received in revised form 25 July 2008 Accepted 12 August 2008 Available online 14 October 2008

Keywords: Social cognitive theory E-learning outcomes ICT experience Virtual competence

## A B S T R A C T

Using social cognitive theory, we opened up the black box of psychological processes in which e-learners engage. We believed that prior experience with ICT and virtual competence were two influential factors that affected e-learning and had a positive influence on its outcomes. We tested our hypotheses on a sample of 383 Chinese students participating in online courses. Our findings confirmed the effect of virtual competence and revealed a nuanced mechanism by which experiences with ICT affected elearning outcomes. We discussed the implications of this in e-learning practice.

## 1. Introduction

In the U.S., over 90% of all public institutions offered some form of e-learning courses in 2004 and 96.2% of them agreed that elearning was critical to the long-term strategies of their institutions [26]. Nearly 3.2 million students in the U.S. were taking at least one online course during the Fall 2005 term [4]. However, past failures have shown that investments in e-learning do not necessarily lead to financial returns and training outcomes. Understanding learners’ psychological processes is crucial for institutions to provide effective e-learning programs [2]. In response to this, we examined e-learners’ prior ICT experience and virtual competence, theorizing that they were major factors in the psychological processes that were of special importance to elearning.

We argued that e-learners’ psychological processes operate on the set of cognitive structures represented by virtual competence [28] when engaging in e-learning activities. We believed that prior experiences with ICT-related activities influenced e-learning outcomes through this competence. People who have prior experiences with ICT-related activities have developed ways to accomplish new learning tasks, resulting in better learning outcomes. We designed a study to test these effects.

Our goal was to contribute to the e-learning literature in two ways. We first identified and distinguished among types of individual experience with ICT as antecedents of e-learning outcomes. Secondly, we applied social cognitive theory (SCT) to explain the effects of different types of ICT experience on virtual competence and consequently on learning outcomes.

## 2. Research background

As described in Alavi and Leidner’s research framework, elearning is a virtual learning environment in which a learner’s interactions with materials, peers and/or instructors are mediated through information and communication technologies. It is different from the traditional environment because ICT are used as tools to support the learning process. Taking advantage of network infrastructures, learning can occur anywhere using many types of resources. This suggested that IT, instructional strategy, learners’ psychological processes, and contextual factors together influenced e-learning outcomes.

Many general technology features such as perceived flexibility, convenience, and availability have been shown to affect e-learning outcomes [6,10].

Instructional strategy includes teaching methods and models that help to present, sequence, and synthesize learning materials, which determines the content, sequence of different topics, and the relationships among them [23]. E-learning allows easy adoption of innovative strategies to enhance learners performance. For instance, learner control makes use of features such as hyperlinks that allow learners to navigate through text and multi-media content in their preferred sequence. Learners can skip familiar content and spend more time on topics of interest, as well as scheduling according to their own needs. Furthermore, new ways of presenting learning content can provide richer and more dynamic information display and animation [8].

In comparison, there is less understanding of learners’ internal factors. A few individual internal psychological states apparently affect e-learning outcomes, including learners’ prior computer experience, computer self-efficacy, motivation, computer anxiety, and communication apprehension [20,27]. We thus wished to contribute by linking individual psychological factors to learning outcomes and by considering the theoretical mechanisms at work.

SCT represents a broad approach to understanding human behavior and advanced capacities for learning [7]. According to SCT, learning is affected by experience. Individuals learn from the consequences of their own and others’ actions and develop new cognitive structures and behavioral routines. When actions produce positive outcomes, they tend to reinforce the cognitive structures and routines, but when past actions produce negative consequences, individuals adjust their cognition and routines to produce more positive outcomes. These produce rules of behavior that serve as guides in future situations. In this way, past experience serves to shape future actions via cognitive processes they engage when facing new contexts.

Following SCT, we focus on learners’ experience with ICT and virtual competence, which we felt were engaged in psychological processes during e-learning. While they contribute to the understanding of the determinants of e-learning outcomes, one may develop insight into the individual psychological factors important to e-learning.

Fig. 1 indicates the position of our research in Alavi and Leidner’s framework. In the context of e-learning, we were particularly interested in prior experience with ICT and the development of virtual competence to guide cognition and learning activities that enhanced learning outcomes. Therefore, we held external factors constant and tested the factors and relationships shown in the solid lines. Fig. 2 shows our research model with hypotheses.

## 3.1. Experience with ICT and e-learning outcomes

We investigated individual psychological processes with a focus on learning outcomes because they are the ultimate goals of people in learning environments. Such outcomes have generally been measured by an individual’s learning effectiveness and satisfaction; these outcomes are usually highly correlated, but not always. For example, people may learn effectively but be unsatisfied because the material did not achieve what they expected. Thus, we believed they tap into different aspects of learning outcomes and included both as dependent variables.

We highlighted a learner’s ICT related experience as important in influencing the outcomes due to the technology aspect of elearning. Learners with experience in Web-based courses are likely to embrace the learning process, thereby enhancing their learning outcomes [21]. On the other hand, a lack of ICT experience and skills may hinder e-learning; without some ICT experience, learners may suffer from anxiety and be unable to function properly [15]. Generally, individuals with more ICT experience tend to perform better.

Nevertheless, current conceptualization of ICT experience is oversimplified. It is generally self-rated, as the number of previous online courses taken or self-assessed degree of familiarity with computers. With such measures, only one dimension of ICT experience, quantity, is used. Different types of experience from different activities are ignored. From an SCT perspective, lack of rich information therefore interferes with our analysis of the impact of ICT experience on e-learning. In addition, with the ubiquity of computers and increased availability of the Internet, novices are less common, especially in the younger generation. Thus, comparing elearning outcomes of individuals with more or less ICT experience is not as meaningful as it once was. Therefore, a more nuanced and meaningful question must be asked: what kind of ICT experience is beneficial to an individual’s e-learning outcomes?

We considered three types of ICT experience: information seeking, computer mediated communication, and online socialization. At home, individuals use instant messaging (IM), and browse websites to seek information and interact with others. At work, they use search engines to find resources, communicate and collaborate with colleagues by e-mail, and form social circles. Information seeking are activities of individuals that use ICT as a one-way tool to retrieve information. Communication emphasizes interaction between individuals: E-mail and IM are both widely used. Socialization reflects the use of ICT to satisfy individuals’ social needs: e.g., online societies and game-playing. These three represent different activities conducted through ICT.

We first investigated experience with information seeking activities. ICT are used in e-learning programs primarily as a mechanism for transmitting content [9]. Learners experienced in information seeking via ICT are at an advantage because they may have developed favorable beliefs and attitudes toward ICT use and are willing to use them continuously [18]. In the same vein, learners who have engaged in information seeking activities via ICT in other contexts may develop favorable perceptions and attitudes to them for seeking information from course materials. For example, they may feel they have more control over the online resources after they have successfully acquired them. As a result, learners experienced in information seeking are likely to continue those activities at a higher level in e-learning environments than those without or with little experience. This improves learning effectiveness because more information sought means that learners are being exposed to and probably learning more course content.

In general, learners with more ICT experience may view elearning features as enablers rather than barriers, which then motivates them to put more effort into the learning processes and feel more satisfied with e-learning [19]. Moreover, perceived higher learner control over the content, an outcome of exercising information seeking via ICT, could also make learners feel more satisfied with e-learning [22]. Therefore, we hypothesized that:

H1a. Individuals who are more experienced in information seeking via ICT will learn more effectively in e-learning environments.

H1b. Individuals who are more experienced in information seeking via ICT will be more satisfied with e-learning environments.

The communication of e-learners is an important predictor of course effectiveness and learner satisfaction. As suggested by Piccoli et al., effective communication allows learners to evaluate their course progress and instructional needs. Prior experiences with ICT thus help in e-learning. According to media richness theory [13], experienced e-learners communicate more effectively because they can receive rich information via this medium and will be willing to continue using it, thus performing well. Furthermore, Marks et al. found frequent communication between instructors and learners can reduce psychological distance, increasing learners’ perceived satisfaction. Accordingly, we hypothesized that:

H2a. Individuals who are more experienced in communication via ICT will learn more effectively in e-learning environments.

H2b. Individuals who are more experienced in communication via ICT will be more satisfied with e-learning environments.

In e-learning environments, instructors and peers are important resources for learners [11]. However, there are different norms of behavior and different approaches to establishing relationships with others in online environments [29]. Individuals with experience of online socialization should approach these resources more effectively because they are familiar with the norms and approaches. For instance, a learner with experience in online socialization might obtain better response from an instructor by using emoticons because they animate a conversation. Such approaches help achieve favorable learning outcomes. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H3a. Individuals who are more experienced in socialization via ICT will learn more effectively in e-learning environments.

H3b. Individuals who are more experienced in socialization via ICT will be more satisfied with the e-learning environments.

## 3.2. Virtual competence and e-learning effectiveness

SCT suggests that there is a relationship between action and competence or capabilities. Individuals develop new competencies and understandings by observing others and through first hand experience gained while accomplishing a task. In the context of elearning, this implies that conducting activities via ICT may make an individual more capable of operating in virtual environments. We consequently assumed virtual competence (the knowledge, skills, and abilities that individuals possess to perform in virtual environments) to be important in e-learning.

Wang and Haggerty conceived virtual competence as an individual’s ability to leverage virtual settings to their maximum potential. This is an important antecedent of individual performance.

Three dimensions form virtual competence: virtual selfefficacy, virtual media skill, and virtual social skill. Thus virtual competence is a concept formed by these disparate dimensions rather than a theoretically derived latent construct underlying them all. Virtual self-efficacy is a measure of an individual’s confidence in his/her abilities to use ICT to undertake tasks remotely. It pertains to an individual’s motivation to make efforts in virtual settings. Virtual media skill is an individual’s ability to use features of ICT to their full potential. This involves the technical skills of individuals in virtual settings. Virtual social skill is the individual’s knowledge of and skills in building social relationships within virtual environments; it obviously is non-technical.

These three dimensions all affect an individual’s performance in e-learning in which students may feel uncomfortable and sometimes frustrated. Virtual self-efficacy indicates the degree of an individual’s willingness to make efforts and to persist in coping with the environment. Therefore, individuals who have high virtual self-efficacy are more likely to make an effort to be effective, thus achieving better learning outcomes. The potential advantages of virtual settings have been limited in the past by poor communication; this may have impaired the transfer of knowledge from instructors to learners in e-learning. Proficiency in virtual media skill has helped individuals maximize the flow of knowledge via ICT, enhancing learning outcomes. Also, e-learning often requires students to collaborate; good relationships with peers facilitate group processes, improving the effectiveness of collaborative learning activities, such as small discussion groups and peer teaching [17]. Thus, individuals who have better virtual social skill are more likely to achieve better learning outcomes. This lead to the hypothesis:

H4a. Individuals who have a higher level of virtual competence will learn more effectively in e-learning environments.

H4b. Individuals who have a higher level of virtual competence will be more satisfied with the e-learning environments.

In recent years, people have become more involved in ICTrelated activities both at work and home. According to SCT, these activities allow individuals to accumulate knowledge and enhance their skills in virtual settings. Experimenting with the technologies and receiving feedback increases individuals’ confidence and helps them find more effective ways to communicate and gain knowledge about the norms of online communication. As a result, individuals become confident in their ability to accomplish tasks through ICT (virtual self-efficacy), more capable of using ICT to transmit rich information (virtual media skill), and more skilful at teaming up with others (virtual social skill). Thus, experiencing ICT-related activities contributes to overall virtual competence. Therefore, we posited:

H5a. Individuals who are more experienced in information seeking via ICT will have a higher level of virtual competence.

H5b. Individuals who are more experienced in communication via ICT will have a higher level of virtual competence.

H5c. Individuals who are more experienced in socialization via ICT will have a higher level of virtual competence.

## 4. Research method

In order to keep information technology and instructional strategy constant, we sought a research site in a university setting that employed the same technology and instructional strategy across a wide variety of e-learning courses and individuals. We believed that this would allow us to provide the broadest possible test of our hypotheses. We collected data using a cross-sectional, self-reported survey to test the model.

## 4.1. Research setting

Data were collected at a large university located in East China. China had 162 million Internet users and 122 million broadband subscribers as of June 2007.<sup>1</sup> The city from which we collected data is a provincial capital with a well-established network infrastructure. The university is one of the first four to provide online courses to students in China. At this university, the e-learning program serves part-time students who work but want to obtain bachelor’s degrees. The program provides courses to students in a variety of majors, including computer science, business administration, electrical engineering, and English literature, etc.

At the beginning and the end of each semester, students have the opportunity to meet each other and the instructors. During the semester, the courses are provided online through an e-learning system developed by a local IT company. Besides downloading the course materials or viewing them online, students can interact with each other and the instructors by sending e-mail or at an online forum designed for each course. They can participate in class discussions by posting comments, questions, and responses at any time. For most students, this program is their first experience in participating at an e-learning program. Instructions and tutorials are provided at the beginning to familiarize students with the system. While there is variety in course content and individuals enrolled, all courses followed the same instructional design.

## 4.2. Data collection

A questionnaire was developed in English and then translated into Chinese, and back into English by other people than those who performed the initial translation. Several rounds of adjustment were made to ensure linguistic equivalence. This translation procedure is consistent with the guidelines established by Brislin regarding the equivalence of language translations [12].

The administrator of the e-learning program, who was blind to our research purpose, randomly selected 15 classes (Introduction to Business Administration, Foundation of Computer Science, and English Literature) with 786 students in total; questionnaires were distributed to them at the end of the Fall semester of 2006. The students all lived in the same city and vicinity and had similar Internet access and network infrastructure at home. They were informed that their participation was voluntary and anonymous. Three hundred and eighty-three questionnaires were returned for a response rate of 48.7%.

The average age of respondents was 27.8 years and the age range was from 20 to 58. Males represented 41% of the respondents. The respondents had 6.5 years working experience, on average. The sample represented 13 different majors. Most of the respondents (72.4%) were in their second year of study in this program; others fell within the first to the sixth year. Thus the respondents had a broader range of age and more working experience than undergraduate students. Hence, the respondents provided a better representation of the general population and were reflective of the population of elearning participants of interest to our study.

## 4.3. Measures

All the measures of constructs in this study were adopted or adapted from previous studies (see Appendix 1 for detailed items). Items were measured on a 7-point Likert scale when applicable.

We used material developed by Wang and Haggerty to measure virtual competence. The respondents’ ICT experience were measured by frequency and daily duration [25] and we measured virtual competence as a second order construct with measures shown in Appendix 1.

We used five items adapted from Alavi to measure individual learning effectiveness [1]. Because of the multi-instructor, multicourse, and multi-disciplinary nature of this study, we were unable to develop a common measure of actual learning. Therefore, we measured the students’ perceived learning effectiveness. We measured the other dependent variable, learners’ satisfaction with e-learning environments, by three items adopted from Alavi et al. [3].

We also included demographic information as control variables for the effects of gender, age, year in the program, and major in the study.

We chose PLS because it allowed us to include formative constructs, such as virtual competence and three types of ICT experience and also because it provides a method by which we can test the measurement and the theoretical models simultaneously. ## 5.2. Structural model

Research hypotheses were tested by examining the size and significance of structural paths in the PLS analysis and the percentage of variance explained. Fig. 3 demonstrates the results. None of the direct relationships between the three types of ICT experience and learning effectiveness was significant. Therefore, H1a, H2a and H3a were not supported by our data. Among the three types of ICT experience, only socialization directly affected learners’ satisfaction with the e-learning environment; however, the direction of this relationship was negative—the greater the prior experience with socialization, the lower the satisfaction with the e-learning course of the student. Thus H1b, H2b and H3b are not supported.

Individual virtual competence was found to be a significant antecedent of both perceived learning effectiveness and satisfaction, which supported H4a and H4b. Also, individuals’ experience with information seeking and communication was significantly related to virtual competence, while experience with socialization was not. These results provided support for H5a and H5b but not for H5c. In addition, the four control variables were not significant.

In sum, two types of ICT experience explained 30% of the variance in virtual competence, which in turn explained 12% of the variance in learning effectiveness. Virtual competence together with the learner’s experience with socialization via ICT explained 10% of the variance in satisfaction.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Summary of findings

The goal of our study was to attempt to understand how some psychological factors affect e-learning outcomes. We focused on three types of prior ICT experience and individual virtual competence. Prior research had suggested that the degree of learners’ ICT experience was instrumental to their learning outcomes. Our theoretical position indicated that different types of experience developed and reinforced different types of cognitions and cognitive processes. We pursued an understanding of this more nuanced view of the influence of prior ICT experience on e-learning outcomes. Moreover, we adopted virtual competence as an antecedent of learning outcomes. Further, we argued that learners’ ICT experience can indirectly affect learning outcomes by developing virtual competence. Our results bear out some of our theorizing.

Surprisingly, our results did not provide support for the direct positive effects of ICT experience on e-learning outcomes as hypothesized. Nevertheless, the results showed that experience with ICT may affect e-learning effectiveness indirectly through virtual competence. First, we found that students who were more experienced in seeking information and communicating via ICT had higher levels of virtual competence. Maybe frequent use of ICT for these kinds of activities helped individuals develop the capabilities to perform in virtual environments. Second, as expected, virtual competence was found to be an important antecedent of learning outcomes. Students with higher virtual competence learned more from their e-learning courses and were more satisfied with their elearning experience than others.

Prior research has well documented the direct effects of ICT experience on learning outcomes. By adding virtual competence as the intermediate step, we showed a clear process by which experience may affect learning outcomes. Our findings support the role of virtual competence in linking ICT experience to learning outcomes.

In our study there is no evidence that learners’ prior experience with socialization via ICT enhanced their virtual competence in elearning settings. Within our research site, the learning model and instructional strategy of the focal e-learning program were directly copied from the lecture method in traditional face-to-face classrooms. In such a learning environment, ICT were primarily used to automate content delivery. As noted by an IT staff member, students had very little social interaction with each other or even with instructors, though the ICT could provide such interaction. Thus the instructional strategy did not encourage interaction and students’ skills in socialization via ICT were not useful because they did not conduct social activities as part of their online learning. Furthermore, students who had socialized more in other virtual settings might feel less satisfied with our their social needs were not fulfilled.

The characteristics of the instructional design of the research site also provide plausible explanations for the non-significant direct effects of ICT experiences on e-learning outcomes. Under this type of instructional design, students did not need to make a lot of effort to seek information or content as it was readily available in the well organized course materials provided by instructors and they were not required to collaborate with others to accomplish tasks. As a result, it is possible that the lack of direct effects of students’ prior ICT experience in information seeking and communication on learning outcomes was due to the fact that the instructional design did not encourage them to draw upon those experiences.

Overall, we found that ICT experiences affect e-learning outcomes differently: information seeking and communication leads to effective learning by enabling learners to develop virtual competence. Prior ICT experience with socialization results in dissatisfaction with e-learning at our research site and has no influence on virtual competence. These findings provide a more fine-grained depiction of how the individual psychological factors affect learning outcomes.

## 6.2. Implications

For organizations and institutions that are considering growing their e-learning programs, our findings suggest that e-learning is not the best choice for everyone: learners with low virtual competence may face more difficulties than others in the learning process and achieve lower outcomes. Negative feelings may eventually increase the dropout rate or result in diminished learning effectiveness. Therefore, organizations and institutions need to take into account the special needs of learners who do not have adequate knowledge, skills, and abilities to perform in virtual environments (Internet beginners, individuals with low computer self-efficacy, and those with little technology background), the traditional classroom learning may be a better choice until they develop ICT experience and virtual competence.

Pretests could be designed to help learners assess their own levels of virtual competence, and thus make the proper choice in choosing their best option as well as understand their strengths and weaknesses in virtual environments encouraging them to develop the skills they need. A few training sessions about elearning technology in use and computer mediated communication skills could help learners master the environment and achieve better outcomes.

## 6.3. Limitations

There are several limitations of this study. First, we focused on only individual characteristics and intentionally held other factors constant. Important factors, such as instructional design and technology, were not included. In fact, as suggested by existing frameworks regarding e-learning, these factors may interact with individual characteristics to influence learning outcomes.

Second, virtual competence could also be an outcome of elearning processes. SCT states a reciprocal relationship between individuals’ cognitive properties and skills and their behaviors. In this vein, virtual competence facilitates e-learning and at the same time is reinforced during the e-learning process.

Third, we focused on virtual competence as a key antecedent of individual e-learning outcomes given its role in virtual environments. However, there are other individual cognitions such as motivation, that would be influenced by a variety of ICT experiences and that would eventually influence e-learning effectiveness.

## 7. Conclusions

Our study made several important contributions to knowledge of e-learning. First, we used SCT to provide a solid theoretical foundation and deepen our understanding of the individual psychological factors that are uniquely important in e-learning. Second, we distinguished between three types of ICT experience (information seeking, communication, and socialization) and empirically tested their effect on e-learning outcomes. This results in a more comprehensive account of the effects of ICT experience. Third, we disentangled learners’ psychological processes by introducing virtual competence, considering the psychological processes that link prior experience to learning outcomes, and then empirically testing virtual competence as an antecedent of the outcomes. Our findings revealed that experience with information seeking and communication via ICT helped individuals developed virtual competence, which in turn allowed them to learn effectively and feel satisfied with their experience.
