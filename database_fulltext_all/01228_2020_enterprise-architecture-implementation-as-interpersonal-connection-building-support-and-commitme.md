# Enterprise architecture implementation as interpersonal connection: Building support and commitment

Mark Dale<sup>1,2</sup> | Helana Scheepers<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Business Technology and Entrepreneurship, Swinburne University of Technology, Melbourne, Victoria, Australia <sup>2</sup>Faculty of ICT, Swinburne University of Technology, Melbourne, Victoria, Australia

## Abstract

Although architects are often made responsible for enterprise architecture implementation (EAI), they are dependent on business executives to fund the technology products and technology executives to provide staff and resources to support their implementation. Moreover, existing research into EAI focuses on the technical and social conditions associated with EAI success and failure and, to a lesser extent, on the influence of interpersonal interactions between architects and their stakeholders through which the EAI is enacted. Against that backdrop, we examine EAI from the perspective of the architects and their stakeholders with a focus on how architects can build commitment to and support for implementation of the selected technology products and programmes. Specifically, we rely on communities of practice (CoP) theory to investigate two cases of EAI with a focus on practices that enabled or prevented the architects from building communities with requisite commitment to and support for their efforts. We find that whilst the involved architects were required to liaise with business and technology stakeholders, their practices did not allow them to effectively build the necessary connections, and their EAI initiatives suffered accordingly. As a result, we theorize the permeability of community boundaries around architects as key to the success of EAI: When architects prioritize what they think is important over the views and concerns of business and technology stakeholders community, a dominant intra-CoP perspective will emerge, and boundaries around the community of architects will be reinforced, preventing stakeholders with legitimate interests in the EAI to influence the process; in contrast, when architects prioritize engaging with the viewpoints and concerns of business and technology stakeholders community, boundaries will become permeable, and an intercommunity CoP will emerge, enabling knowledge sharing and negotiations between everyone with legitimate interests in the EAI.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The successful completion of an enterprise architecture (EA) initiative is important for organizations, enabling the alignment of strategic and operational objectives and the Information Systems/Information Technology (IS/IT) port folio. However, many organizations are unable to implement the systems and platforms specified in their EA plans with severe financial and reputational consequences and lost opportunities to exploit the new platforms and systems (Roeleven, 2008). Despite nearly three decades of research and practice, EA implementation (EAI) remains a thorny challenge for organizations (Hope, Chew, & Sharma, 2017). Nearly a decade ago, 40% of programmes were predicte to shut down within 3 years (Sessions, 2009) and that “only a small percentage of companies did it well” (Ross, Weill & Robertson, 2006). Research companies such as Forrester and Gartner found that EA initiatives were often not accepted by business stakeholders and users and did not meet business objectives (Allega, 2010; DeGennaro, 2010) More recently, it seems the situation has not improved, with many organizations either unable to implement their EA plans or only able to partially implement them (Banaeianjahromi & Smolander, 2017; Hope et al., 2017; Nikpay, Ahmad, Rouhani, & Shamshirband, 2016).

Notwithstanding the continued failure of EAI initiatives, the need for EAI is well accepted, and many organizations undertake EAI initiatives to exploit opportunities afforded by new technologies, moving industry boundaries and an expanding and interconnected global economy (Bruls, van Steenbergen, Foothuis, Bos, & Brinkemper, 2010 Ross et al., 2006). Organizations undertake EAI initiatives to help them build adaptable and flexible systems and plat forms (Bruls et al., 2010; Ross et al., 2006), provide access to important customer data, and improve system and platform reuse (Ross et al., 2006). Several studies examine the benefits from business and technology platforms that are the result of successful EAI initiatives. Two characteristic claims are that an operating platform delivered via an EAI i likely to support the business strategy and operating model (Ross et al., 2006) and have a higher level of process and data standardization and integration (Boh & Yellin, 2006; Lankhorst, 2005)

Various causes for EAl failure have been proposed. As the platforms and systems specified in EAl plans are implemented through a series of technology and business improvement projects and programmes of work, author have applied project success factors such as cost management, change management, risk management, project management, and executive management support, amongst others (Boh & Yellin, 2006; Ross et al., 2006; Schmidt & Buxman, 2011; Weill & Ross, 2004). Authors have also examined the important role that technical architecture skill play in successful EAI outcomes, including knowledge of relevant technologies (Simon, Fischbach, & Schoder, 2014) modelling (Jonkers, Lankhorst, van Burren, Hoppenbrouwers, & Bonsangue, 2003), documentation (Schmidt &

Buxman, 2011), planning (Ross et al., 2006), problem solving (Abraham, 2013), and requirements gathering and analysis (Schmidt & Buxman, 2011).

For over two decades, researchers and practitioners have acknowledged the importance of social factors to the outcomes of an EAI and have made important contributions to EAI practice in laying out the different types of socia conditions associated with EAI failure and success. Spewak (1992) recommended that in the planning stage of an EAI, architects liaise with executive management to build awareness and support for the EAI, and Smolander and Rossi (2008), Aier (2014), and Faller and De Kinderen (2014) have examined the potentially negative effect of organi zational politics and culture on EAI outcomes. Faller and De Kinderen (2014) examined the impact of cultural diver sity on the effectiveness of architects, including their ability to communicate with their stakeholders. In their study, Niemietz, de Kinderen, and Constantinidis (2013) identified three types of architect-stakeholder communication defects: (a) lack of communication, (b) inappropriate communication, and (c) over-communication. Problematic interaction between architects and stakeholders is also considered to influence the effectiveness of architects and is a key success factor in coordinating EA activities (Abraham. Aier, & Winter. 2015)

The role that stakeholders play in the delivery of an EAI is an important theme in EAI research. Authors have examined the role of stakeholder acceptance (Gregor, Hart, & Martin, 2007), stakeholder communication (Poutanen 2012), the architect and stakeholder relationship (Smolander & Rossi, 2008), and how business stakeholders perceptions (Lange, 2012) can negatively influence EAI outcomes. Further stakeholder research has examined the different subgroups within the stakeholder community (Roth, Hauder, Zec, Utz, & Matthes, 2013) and the types of barriers that can exist between architects and these communities, including differences in the knowledge and background of architects and stakeholders (Abraham et al., 2015). Although many scholars have identified communication and col laboration between architects and their stakeholders as a major issue affecting the success and failure of EA initiatives, an explanation of the interpersonal forces that leads to such a breakdown in relations between architects and their stakeholders is incomplete and not grounded in theory (Banaeianjahromi & Smolander, 2017; Nikpay et al. 2016; Abraham, 2013;). Furthermore, much of the research and literature that investigates issues of shared understanding and communication between architects and their stakeholders is focused on the development stage of an EA, and very little attention has been given to the implementation stage (Banaeianjahromi & Smolander, 2017).

Whilst authors have established a substantial body of knowledge about the social challenges associated with EAI, EAI initiatives continue to experience social challenges, and this suggests a need for further research. Whilst existing EAI research acknowledges the importance of social factors to the outcomes of an EAI, the interpersona antecedents that may give rise to these factors has not attracted the attention of researchers to the same extent. This is significant since EAI research acknowledges the dependency of architects on the participation of stake holders (Donaldson, Blackburn, Blessner, & Olson, 2015), and, as it is characterized in the extant research, the relationship between architects and stakeholders is one of mutual dependence (Ahlemann, Mohan, & Schafczuk, 2012): Stakeholders need the technical expertise of architects to design and deploy platforms and processes that enable the organization to meet its strategic objectives, and architects are dependent on stakeholders to (a) help shape those platforms, systems, and processes and (b) to fund and provide specialist technology and business resources to deliver the specified systems and platforms into operation. Taking into consideration that EA initiatives do not always proceed as planned (Ahlemann & Arbi, 2012), that environmental changes can never be anticipated fully (Aier, 2014), and that reguirements are also volatile (Rosenkranz, Vranesic, & Holten. 2014) and not always known (Faller & De Kinderen, 2014), there is additional significance invested in the relationship between architects and their stakeholders. Though architects must build effective and collaborative relationships with their business and technology stakeholders, there are few, if any, theory generating empirical studies focused on the interpersona interactions between architects and their stakeholders that allow for a deeper explanation of an observed states of affairs and conseguently, this important area of EAl research is relatively undertheorized Whilst authors have identified the social conditions for EAl success, including, amongst others the ability of architects and their stake: holders to share knowledge (Foorthuis, van Steenbergen, Brinkkemper, & Bruls, 2016), there is a need for concepts and theories to help explain the interpersonal processes that enable architects to create the interpersona conditions conducive to these types of connections (Rosenkranz et al., 2014). To date, this aspect of the architect stakeholder relationship is viewed as a black box, and the contribution of this paper is to theorize these processe and to highlight the effect of the permeability of boundaries around architects, which can enable or prevent them from building support for and commitment to the selected technology products and programmes of technolog projects.

This research uses communities of practice (CoP) theory to theorize the social interactions between architect and their stakeholders. First, by using CoP theory, we aim to build an understanding of how the practices of archi tects may form boundaries, which make it difficult to form effective connections with stakeholders. Second, by using the CoP concepts of mutual engagement, joint enterprise, and shared repertoire, we investigate how architects can build effective connections with “others” from different CoPs and which enable them to bridge the boundarie between themselves and people belonging to other communities. Drawing on CoP theory, we adopted the following research question for this study: What impact do the practices of architects have on their ability to build interpersonal relationships that build support and commitment for an EAI? We undertook two in-depth EAI case studies in two larg Australian banks. Our findings indicate that to build commitment and support for the systems and platforms speci fied in the EAI plans, architects must be skilled at bridging the boundaries between themselves and their busines and technology stakeholders.

The contribution of this paper will be to complement the existing understanding of the social conditions of EA success with a focus on the interpersonal social processes that enable architects to establish effective connections with their stakeholders and build commitment and support for the technology products and programs of work the select. We identify the practices of architects that influence the permeability of the community boundary aroun them and which enable architects to establish a sense of mutual engagement and joint enterprise with their stakeholders. We also examine the implications for the training, education, and recruitment of architects, and we theoriz the social processes that enable individuals from one CoP to bridge the boundaries of their CoP and engage effec tively with everyone with legitimate interests in the EAI.

The paper is structured as follows. First, we discuss the approaches authors have taken to the social challenge of EAI and the CoP theoretical lens employed in this research. Next, we outline the research method and design adopted for this research. This is followed by a discussion of the findings from the data analysis. We then reinvok the theoretical lens to examine the practices of architects that can impact their ability to form effective relationship with their business and technology stakeholders and the approaches that architects may take to build more effectiv relationships. In the final section, we discuss the implications of this research for practitioners

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

In this section, we review the literature on the social factors related to EAI and develop the theoretical perspectiv for theorizing the practices of architects related to their interaction with their business and technology stakeholders by adopting CoP theory (Wenger, 1998).

## 2.1 | Enterprise architecture implementation

There is no universally agreed definition of EAI, and multiple meanings are ascribed to the term in both academic an practitioner literature (Bui, 2017; Simon et al., 2014). As the term is applied here, an EAI delivers into operation the systems and platforms specified in an organization's EA plans. An EAI initiative is divided into two phases: technology product selection and implementation planning. During an EAI, architects identify the specific hardware and software products to deliver the required systems and platforms and specify and coordinate the programmes of work to deliver those systems and platforms, into operation (Nikpay et al 2016). As. EAl initiatives, are funded by the organization rather than the architecture team, architects must build commitment and support for the selected tech nology products amongst senior executives who will fund the EAI.

It has for some time been an accepted fact within EAI research that many of the problems arising when undertak ing an EAI are social in nature (Lange, 2012; Spewak, 1992). The relationship between EAI failure and the inability of architects to build stakeholder commitment and support is well documented (Donaldson et al., 2015). According to Rouse (2005) “the people and organizational issues associated with transformational solutions are often the thorniest part of the road to success” (Rouse, 2005, p. 143). Authors have noted that EAI initiatives may fail because of (a) the reclusive working styles of architects (Poutanen, 2012; Van der Raadt, Schouten, & van Vliet, 2008), (b) the inability of architects to understand business values (Bui, 2017), (c) the inability of architects to take account of the different perspectives of their stakeholders (Smolander & Rossi, 2008), (d) the inability of architects to build effective knowledge sharing connections with their stakeholders (Van der Raadt et al., 2008), and (e) the inability of architects to obtain executive and general management support (Bui, 2017). EAI scholars have called for a greater partnershi between architects and stakeholders (Niemi & Pekkola. 2017) and greater stakeholder involvement in EA initiatives (Hope et al., 2017). Though the relationship between architects and their stakeholders is viewed as a major factor i the determination of organizational EAI outcomes, the lack of tools and methods for understanding these is viewed as a critical impediment to EAl success (Hope et al., 2017)

In spite of the well-documented social drivers of EAI failure and the ubiquity of EA frameworks that draw atten tion to these challenges, EAIs are plagued with difficulties, and organizations still have difficulty implementing their EA (Hope et al., 2017). Despite substantial research interest in this area, the widespread endorsement of stakeholder involvement and recognition of non-IT stakeholder involvement in IT decision making (Bui, 2017), actual engagement of stakeholders in EAI initiatives appears to have been less than successful. Researchers have taken steps to address this issue and have sought to clarify the stakeholder context of an EAI by addressing the stakeholder community and surfacing the different types of social groupings within that community (Roth et al., 2013). In their analysis of the stakeholder community, authors have adopted a functional approach differentiating stakeholders based on organizational location (Valorinta, 2011) and their position within the organizational hierarchy (Smolander & Rossi, 2008) Other authors have advanced our understanding of the architect-stakeholder relationship, identifying the knowledge boundaries between architects and their stakeholders (Abraham, 2013) and showing how, if architects are sensitiv to the differing information requirements of their stakeholders and adept in their use of technical artefacts, archi tects are able to bridge the knowledge differences between themselves and their stakeholders (Abraham, 2013 Niemi & Pekkola, 2017).

Despite our understanding of the stakeholder community and recognition of the need to mediate functional and knowledge boundaries between architects and their stakeholders, relations between architects and their stakeholders are still problematic. Schniederjans and Yadav (2013) found that trust issues still persist between architects and business stakeholders, adversely affecting EAI outcomes. Researchers have sought to explain the “gap” in th architect and stakeholder relationship in terms of differences in the motivations and viewpoints of architects and stakeholders (Faller & De Kinderen, 2014), organizational incentives and rewards (Foorthuis et al., 2016), conflicting assumptions and expectations associated with EAI activities (Aier, 2014), and the inability of architects to coordinat EAI activities across different functional groups (Espinosa, Armour, & Boh, 2010). Sessions (2009) describes the gap between architects and their stakeholders in terms of sensemaking and argues that stakeholders may have difficult engaging with the EAI and that architects must eliminate any barriers to stakeholder understanding: “sometimes there is an atmosphere of mystique around enterprise architecture that makes it unnecessarily complicated, resulting in lack of understanding by the people that need to understand it" (Sessions. 2009, p. 9)

Recently, authors have argued that the success of EAI initiatives maybe more dependent on the practices of architects rather than what is practised (Hope et al., 2017). Researchers have raised concerns about the relationship between the interactions between architects and their stakeholders and the outcomes of EAI initiatives (Bui, 2015; Malta & Sousa, 2012; Van der Raadt et al., 2008; Van der Raadt, Bonnet, Schouten, & van Vliet, 2010). Nakakawa, van Bommel, and Proper (2009) argue that collaboration between architects and stakeholders is the most critica factor in the outcome of an EAI and that the ability of architects to surface and reconcile requirements ambiguitie influences acceptance and adoption of the EAI. Hoyland (2012) stated that the ability of architects to gain acceptance for strategic-level solutions is likely to be influenced more by their ability to communicate and engage effec tively with stakeholders across and at different levels of the organization than using current EA modelling tools and techniques, which were proprietary, time consuming, and unable to fully represent the complex behaviours that are the basis of EAI problems. Personal organizational networks of architects are known to significantly contribute to EAI success (Aier & Schelp, 2010), as are stakeholder perceptions of architects (Aier & Schelp, 2010; Niemietz et al., 2013). The ability of architects to take into account the information requirements of their stakeholders when constructing the architectural solution is seen to reduce the sensemaking gap between what architects consider architecturally important and what matters to stakeholders (Niemi & Pekkola, 2017). This is significant as it has bee known for some time that an EAI represents design trade-offs amongst properties such as cost, usability, maintainability, and performance, and these represent the explicit commitment of a group towards an objective or course of action (Smolander & Rossi, 2008). EAI is the product of a process of decision making and negotiation for resources needed for building the systems, including personnel, expert skills, and funding (Bass, Clements, & Kazman, 2003). Likewise, a successful EAI can be considered as something that emerges from the cooperation amongst stakeholders whilst taking into consideration organizational constraints of monetary resources, legacy systems, available technol ogy, and the skills and experience of staff.

Authors' prescriptions for improving relations with stakeholders are varied and include enhanced management training of architects (Donaldson et al., 2015), increased business exposure for architects (Ross et al., 2006), more vigorous and rigorous use of structured methods (Aier & Schelp, 2010), and a variety of mechanisms for facilitating closer interaction between architects and stakeholders, such as methods for understanding organizational cultur and subcultures (Aier. 2014), governance processes (Mocker, Ross, & Hopkins. 2015), and interdisciplinary architec ture teams and liaison positions (Aier, Riege, & Winter, 2008; Strano & Rehmani, 2007). Some researchers have pro posed new methodologies specifically designed to facilitate more significant stakeholder involvement (Jahani, Reza Seyyed Javadein, & Abedi Jafari, 2010). Whilst these authors have made significant contributions to EAI researc and practice, the challenge of building more effective connections between architects and stakeholders persists, and this suggests that there is a need to examine the relationship between architects and their stakeholders at a deepe level, taking into account their interactions. Our research takes as its starting point the following question: Are th persistent difficulties in relations between architects and stakeholders rooted in more fundamental processes: th interpersonal processes between architects and their stakeholders?

## 2.2 | Communities of practice

To investigate the way in which architects interact with their various stakeholders, we adopt Wenger's (1998) CoP theory. Using CoP to conceptualize the behaviours of architects, light can be thrown on the practices that promot and inhibit collaboration and mutual engagement between architects and their stakeholders. A CoP is different to other types of communities or teams as it can be thought of as a group of individuals who come together around common endeavour and can share knowledge, experiences, tools, and practices to solve problems and build mutua engagement in that endeavour (Wenger, 1998). Over time, members of the community develop and agree on ways of doing things, ways of talking, shared beliefs, and values, and, in short, practices emerge during this shared endeav our (Lave & Wenger, 1991).

CoPs are conceptualized as voluntary communities of mutually satisfying relationships such as those engaged in by architects and their stakeholders during an EAI. However, CoPs may be brought into being by organizational need rather than something that emerges “naturally.” Cox (2005) suggests a community is often thought of as a “large self-conscious and externally recognized, tight knit, friendly, and geographically situated group.” However, a CoP may be a small group not always geographically collocated or in agreement. Whilst CoPs may not always be harmoni ous, a defining characteristic is that community members share knowledge and address issues of common concern (eg, face-to-face meetings, forums, and email) (Wenger, 1998). Where community members do not share knowledge, that community is not working effectively as a CoP (Wenger, McDermott, & Snyder, 2002)

Organizational CoPs are differentiated by their knowledge, shared perspective, and practices, and these repre sent boundaries between them. Wenger (1998) uses the concept of boundaries to delimit CoP (p. 129). Boundarie are not socially visible and denote discontinuities. Wenger describes boundaries as “lines of distinction between inside and outside, membership and non-membership, inclusion and exclusion” (p 120). He argues that an organiza tion, independent of its size, is not homogeneous in its practices, and, rather than being viewed as a single CoP, it is made up of a constellation of practices. Authors have extended the concept of boundary and have focused on professional boundaries (Amedore & Knoff, 1993; Edwards, 2012), perceptions and thoughts (Ankney & Curtin, 2002) and emotions (Bacharach, Bamberger, & McKinney, 2000). Within the CoP literature, there is an underlying assumption that boundaries depend on social interactions for their existence and that permeable boundaries that permit th flow of knowledge into and out of communities are conducive to effective relations between communities (Hernes 2003). In this research, we conceptualize the boundaries that differentiate one CoP from another in terms of the per: spectives, values, and expectations of their members, and we examine these differences empirically. We identif three CoPs in the context of an EAI: architects, technology stakeholders, and business stakeholders.

To accomplish tasks that require the involvement of people from multiple communities, members of a CoP must be able to bridge the boundaries between them and other communities (Wenger et al., 2002). The extent to whic members of one CoP are able to build reciprocal and collaborative relationships with members of other communitie (eg, inter-CoP connections) depends on the permeability (ie, “thickness”) of the boundary associated with that CoP The permeability of a boundary describes how easily knowledge and documentation can flow into and out from a CoP. “Thick” boundaries prevent connections with other communities and may arise, for example, because of percep tions and practices that prevent the sharing of knowledge and information with others outside of the CoP. Thick boundaries are related to a low level of relational competence where members of a CoP are unskilled in assimilating into their understanding of a task the views and concerns of others and are unable to align their own responses to that task with the enhanced interpretation (Edwards, 2012). In this research, we examine the way in which the per ceptions and practices associated with one CoP (architects) make it difficult to build reciprocal and collaborative relationships with other communities (business stakeholders and technology stakeholders) who have a legitimat interest in a common task.

Wenger's (1998) CoP theory provides a basis to theorize about the social processes that enable members of on CoP to form effective connections with other communities. Wenger conceptualizes the attributes that enable CoP to cohere, establish, and evolve as mutual engagement, joint enterprise, and shared repertoire (see Figure 1).

Through mutual engagement, members of a CoP jointly engage in discussion, negotiation, and exchange of infor mation by means of their physical colocation, mutually complementary roles, and shared goal orientation. Mutua engagement arises when individuals share common practices over a prolonged period in an enterprise that matters (Wenger, 1998). What is important or what something means is negotiated within the community. Joint enterpris implies that common interests, accountabilities, and shared goals hold the CoP together. Wenger (1998) explains that joint enterprise emerges through negotiations between community members. According to Wenger, mutual account ability arises when individuals negotiate what is important, what is not, and why it is important. The sense that mem bers of a CoP are accountable to others in the community for a deliverable or outcome is a force that animates the community. A shared repertoire of resources and practices suggests that community members develop a shared “toolkit” of resources (activities, symbols, and artefacts) through which they make sense of the world and which, in turn, become identified with the community. Significantly, Wenger argues that the presence of shared resources has a stabilizing effect on the community since these resources reflect shared beliefs (Wenger, 1998).

In the field of IS, scholars have used Wenger's (1998) CoP theory to address the challenges of intercommunity connections and interactions in a range of contexts. For example, Klein and Hirschheim (2008) used CoP theory to reveal the paradigmatic fragmentation within the IS discipline. Volkoff, Elmes, and Strong (2004) employed CoP the ory to understand the barriers to knowledge transfer between organizational CoPs, and, in their case study of requirements gathering practices in three organizations, Vashist, McKay, and Marshall (2010) used CoP theory to understand if business analysts could effectively bridge the user-IT gap, More broadly, IS researchers have used prac tice theory to explore the actions that people take and the structures of social life. Levina and Vaast (2008) used Bourdieu's practice theory to understand the challenges that arise in offshore-outsourced development projects du to different country and organizational contexts. ## 2.3 | Dysfunctional CoPs

An assumption within the CoP literature is that CoPs have a positive effect on the achievement of organizationa objectives. But, the practices of a CoP may also be counterproductive to the achievement of organizational objec tives (Wenger, 1998). This occurs when a community is dysfunctional and its practices lead to knowledge monopo lies and insularity, behaviours that prevent effective relationships and knowledge transfer with other communities (Wenger et al., 2002). Research into dysfunctional CoPs categorizes community disorder into two types: internally dysfunctional and internal/externally dysfunctional (Mládková, 2015; Wenger, 1998). In the first type, there is no mutual engagement and joint enterprise amongst members of a community, and, therefore, the community does not function effectively as a mechanism in which knowledge is created and shared (Mládková, 2015). The second kind of disorder is subtler and arises when the community is ostensibly functioning too well. The very attributes that make a CoP an environment conducive for learning are the same qualities that can influence it to focus on its own accomplishments and objectives and not on its relationships with others outside of the community (Wenger et al., 2002) For example, members of a CoP engaged in technically complex, interrelated, and time-constrained tasks may focus their efforts on task coordination and knowledge creation within their own community, ignoring the legitimate con cerns and relevant expertise of stakeholders outside of that community.

Within the literature, three factors are recognized as leading to dysfunctional CoPs: (a) a sense of domain owner ship, (b) strong relationships amongst members of a community and to a domain, and (c) knowledge monopolie (Mládková, 2015). Sometimes, the enthusiasm for a domain leads to domain possessiveness or zealousness on th part of members. This can lead to arrogance as community members believe their knowledge of a domain is mor important than others such that they believe no one else has a legitimate understanding of that domain. A strong relationship to a domain and to each other makes a CoP behave like a clique (Mládková, 2015). Members of commu nity perceive themselves as an elite and adopt reclusive practices that create thick boundaries, which make it difficult for knowledge to flow into and out of the community. Elitism may lead to knowledge monopolies where a commu nity does not share its knowledge with the rest of organization, even when there are practical reasons for doing so (Mládková, 2015; Wenger et al., 2002).

The social dimensions of Wenger's (1998) CoP theory (joint enterprise, mutual engagement, and shared repertoire) are used to inform the empirical work for understanding the practices of architects and how the practices of architects may create boundaries that make it difficult for them to effectively manage the relationships with thei business and technology stakeholders.

## 3 | METHODOLOGY

We adopted a case study methodology as we wanted to examine the social complexity of an EAl and the behaviours that can lead to architects successfully engaging with their stakeholders. Eisenhardt (1989) and Yin (1989) argue that case study research is an appropriate research approach for investigating complex phenomena within their actua setting. Benbasat, Goldstein, and Mead (1987) indicate that case study method allows a researcher to ask how and why questions to understand the nature and complexity of the processes taking place (p. 370). The selection of case is based upon theoretical and pragmatic considerations. The selection of the Australian banking industry was made on practical grounds. In general, banks have a high level of experience with EA (Schmidt & Buxman, 2011), and, in a recent study of European, North American, and Australian banks, a large number of participants considered EA important (Schmidt & Buxman, 2011). Though there is considerable research into EAI, research from the perspective of the architects and their stakeholders about their interactions is limited. The professional EAI experience of one of the authors of this paper for over 20 years also indicates that for many architects, building effective collaborative relationships with stakeholders is problematic

We analysed and interpreted data from two in-depth case studies of EAI in the Australian banking industry. Th primary data collection method was semi-structured interviews. Twenty-five interviews of 45- to 60-minutes dura tion were recorded and transcribed and included architects and business and technology stakeholders from differen organizational levels, including deputy CEO, executive and general managers, and operational and project level staff A summary of interview participants is provided in Tables A1 and A2. In both cases, we had access to architectur documentation and models, team meetings, and field observations, and, following the recommendation of Walsham (2006), we also wrote down impressions and ideas that emerged from interviews and observations and used thes to inform our analysis of interview data. Triangulation using different data sources provided contextual richness fo our research and was used to verify the interactions and relationships between the architects and their stakeholders

Although the concepts of CoP and boundary practice were used to frame the research perspective and inform the empirical work, during the initial data analysis, we attempted to remain open to field data and tried not to be constrained by any predefined theoretical codes in our data analysis strategy. However, as implied by Walsham (2006) we were inevitably sensitized to various notions and concepts because of our acquaintance with CoP perspective and literature. In formulating the data analysis strategy, we were also sensitive to how the professional industr experience of one of the authors and the potential for a priori assumptions based on professional experience coul bias our interpretation of the data. As Glaser and Strauss (1967) argue, the researcher does not approach reality as a tabula rasa (blank slate). The danger according to Glaser (1992) is that researchers with a priori experiential and theo retical knowledge are at risk of consciously or unconsciously applying existing theoretical schemes to their data Glaser and Strauss (1967) say that the researcher can be guided by a comprehension of existing theories and that this may help the researcher “see relevant data and abstract significant categories from his/her scrutiny of the data” (p. 3).

To remain open to the field data and leverage but not be constrained by a priori learning and experience, the dat analysis strategy used in this research is grounded theory. We adopted the coding and categorization techniques dis cussed in Charmaz (2006). Our purpose is to see the world as architects and their stakeholders do—“from the inside” (Charmaz, 2006, p. 14). Nevertheless, it is acknowledged that a grounded theory analysis of the roles and practice of architects involved in an EAI offers an interpretive portrayal of the studied phenomena and is not an exact pictur of it (Charmaz, 2006, p. 10).

The data analysis occurred in two phases: firstly, a within-case analysis of each of the two cases and secondly, a cross-case analysis considering the findings across both cases. The within-case analysis approach follows the approach recommended by Charmaz (2006) and is described in Figure 2.

## 3.1 | Within-case analysis

We derived 56 theoretical codes from which to begin the within-case analysis, drawing out patterns of social behav iour, as prescribed by Glaser (1992) (See Figure 3). Following this principle, we adopted the practice recommended by Goulding (2002) of generalizing theoretical codes and adopted the social dimensions joint enterprise, mutua engagement, and shared repertoire as superordinate categories to help make clear the connection between the results that emerge from the inductive coding and the social dimensions of a CoP. This additional process of abstrac tion and generalization is consistent with the “scaling up” approach adopted by Urquhart et al. (2010, p. 372) and is in keeping with the philosophical basis of interpretive field research as defined by Klein and Myers (1999). As we are concerned with identifying the practices that enable architects to build effective connections with stakeholders (inter-CoP perspective) and those practices that inhibit effective connections with stakeholders (intracommunity perspective), we have subcategorized the theoretical codes into inter-CoP and intra-CoP perspectives

The findings presented in this paper are organized according to the three social dimensions of a CoP: joint enter prise, mutual engagement, and shared repertoire. Each dimension is examined with representative theoretica categories, samples of interview text, and field observations. Our approach is to establish a clear connection between the results that emerge from within the two within-case analyses and the social dimensions of a CoP.

The objective of our cross-case analysis is not simply to make a judgmental comparison of the architects' prac tices in each case. Rather, our intention is to seek out significant patterns and insights relevant to the social pro cesses associated with the interactions between architects and their stakeholders in both cases and which can explain the interpersonal forces that influence the ability of the architects to establish and maintain effective connec tions with their stakeholders. The three social dimensions, joint enterprise, mutual engagement, and shared repertoire, served as the starting point for reflecting on the similarities and differences across both cases. During the cross-case analysis, notes were taken to record any new insights and areas that might reveal new patterns and insights between the cases. This allowed us to refine our list of theoretical categories and to re-evaluate the rele vance of the social dimensions. Describing the context of each case in detail and engaging with the empirical dat meant that we became intimately familiar with the case and the practices of the architects, thus enabling recon textualization, that is, the development of emerging interpretations (Staehr, Shanks, & Seddon, 2012). Through con stant interaction with the data and reference to our theoretical categories, similar categories began to emerge as we engaged and re-engaged with the case data. We continued with this process until we reached a point of saturation.

## 4 | CASE DESCRIPTIONS

## 4.1 | Bank1

Bank1 has over 48,000 employees, operates in Australia, New Zealand, the Pacific, Europe, United Arab Emirates and the United States of America, and has over 8 million customers worldwide. At the beginning of 2011, the CEO of Bank1 announced a new 5-year strategic plan that required radical change to the business model and structure of the organization and would see it operating in new international markets. This change had significant ramifications for the existing technology portfolio of the organization. Many of the technology management and operational sup port functions were to be disbanded, and many of the technology systems and environments they managed and maintained were to be upgraded or decommissioned.

The architects allowed themselves 12 months to complete the EA plans and begin implementing the new systems. This ambitious time frame arose from a desire of the architects to be seen to be proactive in supporting th new strategy.

Once the strategy was announced, we moved fast. If we did not, we would not be visible. We developed the architecture, leveraging earlier architecture designs and we got approval from the executive leadership Then we defined the technology assets [i.e. products] and implementation plans. We made the decision on which systems the businesses needed to execute the strategy. The business wasn't involved … the architec ture and roadmaps [implementation plans] were completed in twelve months. (B1-A2)

The architects met several times over 6 weeks with the executives of the business divisions to understand theil requirements, and for the next 4<sup>1</sup> months (until June 2011), the architects developed an integrated set of EA models that described the desired systems and platforms, including the data, application, infrastructure, integration, storage and security specifications required to support the business divisions. In June/July of 2011, the architects presente the EA plans to the senior executives of the various business divisions, who approved them.

The architects allowed themselves 6 months (June to December 2011) to complete the selection of the technol ogy components, the implementation plans, and other technical documentation. During this period, the architects were involved in two significant activities. Firstly, they selected the new software and hardware products to build the systems and platforms specified in the EA plans. This task, which began in June/July 2011, was expected to be finished by December 2011. In December 2011, the architects presented the plans for the selected hardware and software products and the programmes of work to the senior executives of the business divisions for approval. How ever, these executives were reluctant to commit to funding the hardware and software products and the programmes of work specified by the architects, and, by the end of 2011, the implementation of the EA had stalled.

The second significant activity that the architects were involved with was the Architecture Review Board (ARB) In June 2011, the CIO established the ARB to review the hardware and software changes proposed by existing an new technology projects at Bank1. Senior members of the architecture team staffed the ARB, and the ARB became an important approval step in the funding of technology projects at Bank1. Approval from the ARB also gave projects access to critical implementation facilities like the development, testing, and production environments. Existing tech nology projects that were already underway, as well as new technology projects, were required to submit their pro posed hardware and software changes to the ARB for approval. From its inception, the architects used the ARB as an instrument to stabilize the technology environment of Bank1, thus ensuring the relevance of the EA plans and the selected technology products and implementation plans. The architects effectively used the ARB to stop new tech nology projects from beginning and existing technology projects from implementing.

## 4.2 | Bank2

Bank2 has more than 40,000 employees and more than 12 million customers worldwide and operates in Australia New Zealand, England, North America, and Asia. In 2010, the CEO of Bank2 announced a new strategy to modernize the bank, digitizing its business operations, upgrading the core banking platform, and launching an online bank. The new strategy radically changed the EA of Bank2, forcing the retirement of many legacy systems and the introduction of shared platforms and new platforms to support the new online bank and radically changing the way the ban interacted with its customers. The architects liaised with the senior executives of the different business divisions and lower level staff from the business and technology to understand the requirements of the divisions and also the constraints of existing business processes and technology systems. They developed a new EA and, in conjunction with the technology staff, selected new technology components and developed implementation plans to operationaliz those components. The new technology components and implementation plans were accepted by the business, and the first projects associated with the EAI began in 2011.

In 2017, the EA is deployed to nearly 1,000 branches, 10,000 employees, and 10 million customers. It ha reduced the approval process for all retail loans to less than 5 minutes, provides industry leading self-service capabil ity to customers, and has also replaced the legacy customer master database, allowing managers to see a retail cus tomer's entire relationship with the bank on one screen. At the completion of our research, more specialized service were also under development or running in the test environment of Bank1.

## 4.3 | Ownership of the EA in Bank1 and Bank2

In both cases, the EAI process was owned by the architects as they were responsible for the selection of the technology products and for building support and commitment for those products amongst their business and technology stakeholders. At both Bank1 and Bank2, the EA is funded by the individual businesses rather than the organization Whilst technology functions at both Bank1 and Bank2 do not provide actual funding for the acquisition of the selected products, key areas within those respective technology functions provide implementation and specialist technology staff as well as access to preproduction and production environments for testing and operationalizing the selected products. In both cases, it was critical that the architects build connections with stakeholders from both the business and technology to build support for and commitment to the selected technology products an programmes of technology projects to deliver those technologies into operation.

In this paper, we define EAI failure as the inability of architects to build support for and commitment to th selected technology products required to deliver into operation the systems and platforms specified in the EA plans In corollary, we define EAI success as the ability of architects to build support for and commitment to the selected technology products.

## 5 | FINDINGS

To ensure the anonymity of the interview participants, we adopted an alphanumerical code. Bank1 and Bank2 are referred to as B1 and B2, respectively, and architects have the prefix “A,” business participants “B,” and technology participants “T.” Thus, interview participants are referred to as B1-A1, referring to the organization and their role For a full list of the interview participants, see Tables A1 and A2. The theoretical categories are related to each of the concepts of CoP (see Figure 3) and will be selectively discussed to illustrate the concepts

## 5.1 | Joint enterprise

Joint enterprise implies that common interests, accountabilities, and shared goals hold the CoP together. Wenger (1998) explains that joint enterprise emerges through negotiations between community members and mutua accountability (ie, mutual engagement). Comments categorized under this dimension describe the attitude of the architects towards the participation of stakeholders in the EAI. We also include comments of stakeholders regardin the practices of architects and the extent to which they enabled stakeholders to advocate and negotiate for their interest in the EAI. The strength of the comments from business and technology stakeholders in Bank1 about their dealings with the architects could be interpreted as a symptom of a relationship that needed careful attention. At Bank2, the comments of business and technology stakeholders focus on the steps that the architects took to build a sense of shared endeavour and how this in turn helped to build support for and commitment to fund the new systems and programmes of technology projects associated with the EAI. In general, comments from business and technology participants indicate a very positive working relationship with the architects. In the next section, the selection of the system and platforms in Bank1 and Bank2 is discussed, highlighting their contrasting approaches.

## 5.1.1 | Not considering the business

At Bank1 and Bank2, the architects selected the new platforms and systems and developed implementation plans for the new hardware and software. However, the architects in both organizations differed in their approach to th selection of the new hardware and software. At Bank1. the architects did not make efforts to build a sense of join: enterprise with their business and technology stakeholders and seemed to assume that it was not important to do so

Our role is first and all, to define the technology components and the implementation plans. That's all we do … Irrespective of what the business wants, we build technology capabilities. (B1-A1)

## 5.1.2 | Building a shared understanding

At Bank2, the architects worked closely with business and technology stakeholders to build a sense of joint enterprise. To help them select the appropriate systems, the architects collaborated with senior executives and other rep resentatives from each business division to develop strategic business plans for future products, customer services and business processes. These plans were the basis of the architects' technology product selection and tools that enabled the architects and business stakeholders to build a shared understanding about the future direction and objectives of the business. One architect described the architects as enabling business stakeholders to participate in the selection of technology products and also helping them to understand the connection between the selected sys tems and the organizational capabilities, products, and services they wanted to develop.

Business strategic planning definitely rings a bell with executives. What is the business strategy, what are the business capabilities and how do we design those capabilities into the organization? Then we ask what technology is required to achieve those capabilities. When we link business strategy, business capability business process and technology, business and technology executives understand it and buy into it. (B2-A8)

B2-A8 said that the strategic planning tools adopted by the architects incorporated language and concepts that are familiar to business staff and were more accessible than technical modelling methods

UML and wiring diagrams – no one in the business understands them. Would you buy anything based on them? (B2-A8)

## 5.1.3 | Bank2: Building relationships

The architects at Bank2 were sensitive to the importance of the participation of business and technology stake holders and gave attention to the concerns of business and technology stakeholders. Communication tools such as online feedback surveys allowed business and technology staff to voice their criticisms of the architects' practice and led to face-to-face discussion to understand and resolve those concerns. These tools helped architects to sur face emerging problems and maintain positive relationships with staff

We have a regular quarterly performance feedback process. All our stakeholders, on a consistent set of measures, assess the team and individual architects. We then have to respond to that feedback, in person This is critical since we rely on our relationship with the business and technology to get projects done. (B2-A7)

The architects focused on building strong ongoing relationships with business and technology stakeholders. We asked one of the architects how they were able to build support for and commitment to fund the technology components, and he said that it was “the relationships we build with our business and technology stakeholders” (B2-A9). This attitude is also reflected in the comments of technology participants.

It's a collaborative relationship. The business and technology are making sure they align to the architecture. If the business needs to make changes to the programs of work, they will put those changes forward to the architects and discuss them. If a proposed change does not align with the overall direction of the architecture, the architects with help from technology staff will put forward alternative solutions. The solutions are discussed by the architects, business and technology staff and a preferred solution is agreed. (B2-T6

The architects characterized their relationship with business and technology stakeholders as based on practica considerations. Although they described the organizational culture of Bank2 as valuing and rewarding collaboratio between staff, the architects said that over a period of time, business and technology staff had come to trust th advice of the architects. The architects also made themselves accessible to business and technology staff

It's about the quality of what you have to say and being available. There is no point going to see someone unless you have done the analysis and you have the time to take them on the journey. If you provide them with sound, practical and sensible analysis and support, which they see as valuable, then over time the relationship develops. (B2-A9)

## 5.1.4 | Bank1: Wanting management control over the implementation plans

At Bank1, the architects seemed to conceptualize EAI as something that is “done” to stakeholders rather than “with” them. They were more interested in stabilizing the IS/IT portfolio and ensuring the relevance of their technology product selection than building a sense of joint enterprise with the business. For example, they used their control over the ARB to effectively stop all technology projects. At Bank1, projects usually originated in the business from a business need or opportunity, and, once validated, business stakeholders engaged technol ogy specialists to flesh out the scope and specifications of the solution and its costs, benefits, and risks. Once approved, the solution design document was submitted to the ARB for approval. B1-A2 said that the architects used the ARB to “shut everything down” and to “kill projects, big and small,” as the head of the architecture team had wanted. One architect described the ARB as behaving like “control freaks” (B1-A3). B1-A5 acknowl edged that the architects effectively prioritized the EA over the commercial needs of the business but said that it was important to keep the technology portfolio stable.

The practice of the architects at Bank1 to not release the EAI plans and the specified technology products to stakeholders reflected a mindset that prioritized what was important to the architecture over what was important to the business. The head of the architecture team, who directed the architecture team to act in this way, had firm views about the working relationship between architects and the business and the delimitation of their respectiv expertise.

[We are] technology specialists and understand technology better than the business …. We focus on strategy and architecture, not project delivery. The businesses and technology should play a bigger role and take ownership of the technology projects. They cannot hide. They own the architecture. (B1-A1)

This approach seems to reflect a worldview that the EAI belonged to the architects rather than something that was shared with the business and technology

A concern of the architects was that, once the EAI plans were operationalized as programmes of work, how busi ness and technology staff could be made to implement the technology products as they were specified. In one team meeting, the architects discussed this matter at length, and it seems there was considerable distrust amongst th architects towards the business and technology.

There is always a trade-off between following the architects' plans and delivering the project on time and within budget. The first thing to be dropped is the architecture. The business and technology go for a quick and dirty solution. We see that in projects all the time. (B1-A4)

Whilst it is reasonable that the architects as the “authors” of the new EA would want to have some oversight over how their plans are executed, the architects seem to see the success of the EAI as dependent on how effec tively they can control the business and technology rather than working collaboratively with them. Alternative approaches did not appear to have been considered.

## 5.1.5 | Contrasting approaches to joint enterprise

The architects' conceptualization of their EAI role demonstrates a contrast in EAI approach. In selecting th new systems, the architects at Bank1 focused their efforts internally within the architecture team—completing the required technical architecture artefacts, liaising with other architects, and using the ARB to control pro jects. During the EAI, they focused on what they thought was important from an architectural perspective rather than establishing a sense of joint enterprise with their stakeholders. At Bank2, the architects worked closely with business and technology staff to produce a business strategy plan that situated the new systems in a business context, linking them with the strategic objectives of the individual business divisions and their vari ous products, services, and business processes. When selecting the new systems, the architects also worke closely with technology staff to understand the constraints of the existing technology portfolio and to ensure that the selected software and hardware was appropriate. This approach, which is based around discussion and negotiation of what is important to architects and business and technology stakeholders, is viewed as critical to building support for and commitment to fund the new systems as it is seen to bring architects closer to busi ness and technology staff.

## 5.2 | Mutual engagement

Through mutual engagement, members of a CoP jointly engage in discussion, negotiation, and exchange of information through their interaction, complementary roles, and shared goal orientation. Mutual engagement arises when individuals are able to negotiate and agree what is important (Wenger, 1998).

Comments included here emphasize the architects' sense of accountability towards their stakeholders and the extent to which the practices of architects enable stakeholders to jointly engage in discussion, negotiation, and exchange of information. In both cases, there were differences in the architects' awareness and acceptance of the commercial realities of the organization in which they worked. In Bank1, the architects seemed to prioritize the archi tecture over the commercial and pragmatic concerns of the organization. In contrast, the architects at Bank2 under stood the commercial reality of the business and the need of the business to pursue emerging market opportunitie or respond to regulatory changes as they arise. Rather than try to prevent the business from pursuing technolog projects, these architects accommodated the changing priorities of the business, and, together with the technolog staff, they proposed alternative systems that satisfied the needs of the business. The willingness of architects to deviate from the selected systems helped to foster a sense of mutual engagement with the business since, through their actions, the architects demonstrated that they did not privilege the architecture over the needs of the busines but adopted a practical approach that was supportive of the needs of the business to manage their costs, make profit, and respond to regulatory changes.

## 5.2.1 | Bank1: Working independently from the business and technology

At Bank1, the architects exhibited a stronger sense of accountability to each other than they did to their stake holders. In selecting the systems and platforms, the architects developed detailed hardware and software evaluatio tools and methods that incorporated architectural objectives (eg, scalability, simplification, and reduced carbon foot print), risk and market analysis, and implementation references. They worked collaboratively amongst themselve and demonstrated high levels of mutual accountability to each other but seemed unconcerned that the tools used to evaluate the appropriateness of technology products were not developed in collaboration with business and technol ogy stakeholders. Our field note records the following

They attended product demonstrations locally and internationally, reviewed product documentation, and talked to people in reference organizations where a particular software and hardware product had been implemented. They also talked to and took advice from other architects in their team and discussed poten tial hardware and software solutions with them. In team meetings, the architects discuss and compare the suitability of particular products. On the basis of their interactions with vendors and colleagues, the architects selected the hardware and software products they thought would deliver the new strategy. Business and technology were not involved in the selection of any of these products and they were not involved in discussions with vendors or product demonstrations.

## 5.2.2 | Bank1: Not appreciating the commercial reality

The architects at Bank1 were viewed as prioritizing architectural objectives over the commercial reality of the orga nization and expensive systems over more pragmatic ones, which satisficed the requirements of business stake holders. One senior business executive felt that the architects were pursuing idealistic and “Rolls Royce” solution whereas they should be focusing on practical outcomes.

We have to draw a balance between architectural perfection and real-life constraints. No, they cannot have 9 billion dollars to rebuild the bank. Is it really necessary to have the Rolls Royce version of such and such a solution when the Band-Aid version would do fine. (B1-B2)

Another senior business executive commented that the architects pursued idealistic technology solutions that cost “three times more than other solutions” at a time when the Australian economy was being affected by th Global Financial Crisis (GFC) and Australian banks were being asked to contain their spending in order to have suffi cient cash reserves.

Even though it may be building foundational capability for the organization, it was impossible because of the GFC. (B1-B4)

Some interview participants specifically characterized the architects as being in an “ivory tower.” A senior tech nology executive commented that the solutions proposed by the architects “were not based in reality and could not be delivered.” They said the architects had selected software that was untried in other organizations and would be very difficult to implement at Bank1 because technology staff did not have the appropriate skills. Other technolog stakeholders (including B1-T2), who had also worked with the architects prior to the EAI, made similar comments

The architects looked at brochure-ware and some of the demos of the vendors and thought the software would absolutely meet our functional requirements and was right for us. But, when it came to the reality there were no people in the bank or in the region who knew how to integrate those products with our systems. The projects just failed miserably. (B1-T2)

## 5.2.3 | Bank1: Not communicating

At Bank1, technology stakeholders commented on the practice of the architects to not release the specified technol ogy products and programmes of work and how this impacted technology staff working on projects. A senior technology executive commented that because the architects would not release the selected technology components, technology project teams were in effect hoping that their proposed software and hardware changes were consistent with the technology components specified by the architects. A technology manager said that the architects wer accountable for explaining and communicating their decisions, “but they don't live up to it.”

The architects do not understand the importance of communication. They will not publish and communicate the implementation plans. They will not meet and talk to people. I've seen the architecture implemen tation plans hanging on the walls where the architects are, but the architects have not discussed them with us and will not discuss them with us. (B1-T4)

## 5.2.4 | Bank2: Appreciating the commercial reality

In contrast, the architects at Bank2 were sensitive to the commercial reality of the bank and the need of the individ: ual businesses to pursue emerging market opportunities or respond to regulatory changes as they arose. Rather tha try to prevent the business from pursuing technology projects, these architects accommodated the changing priori ties of the business, and, together with the technology staff, they proposed alternative products that satisfied th immediate needs of the business as well as the EAI

Working with the architects, we will frame the technology options, cost them out, decide on a preferred solution and together with the architects we will go back to business and discuss the different solution options. It really comes down to an agreement between the architect, business and technology staff about what we are going to do. (B2-T5)

## 5.2.5 | Bank2: Being inclusive

At Bank2, the architects' willingness to deviate from the selected systems and platforms was appreciated by business participants. Through their actions, the architects demonstrated that they did not privilege the architecture over th needs of the business but adopted an inclusive approach that was supportive of the needs of the business to manag their costs, make a profit, and respond to regulatory changes.

The architects are involved with the business and meet with them regularly. They are in touch with the business, they are aware of the business's direction, what the market is doing, what the banking industry is doing and they speak the same language as the business. The architects have a very close and open relationship with the business. (B2-T5)

## 5.2.6 | Bank2: Interacting with business and technology staff

At Bank2, the architects interacted closely with business and technology stakeholders, demonstrating a strong sense of accountability to both business and technology stakeholders. They worked closely with business executives and accepted that business requirements and priorities could change as the regulatory and market environment of th bank could change. The architects adopted a flexible approach to the hardware and software products they selecte and made changes, as required, when business requirements and priorities changed.

Some you win some you lose. Unfortunately, a lot of the technologies associated with the new strategy cannot be implemented straightaway because the organization's current regulatory requirements have overtaken the implementation of the architecture. (B2-A7)

The architects are also pragmatic in their approach, and, whilst they seek to promote certain architectural goals such as shared platforms, they also recognize that these goals may not be possible or desirable for all businesses

The portfolio of businesses that I look after is diverse: Finance, Risk, Treasury, Business Services, Technology, Financial Crime, Human Resources, Marketing, Communications, Group Governance and Legal. There are ten different strategies and with some businesses you have to have some stand-alone systems. (B2-A9)

When selecting the new technology products, the architects also worked closely with technology staff to under stand the constraints of the existing technology portfolio and to ensure that the selected software and hardwar was appropriate.

We actually work quite intimately with the business to align the business and the technology selection. We plan the business model, the customer channels, the types of business products and services, and then with technology we will develop the technology plan and specify the hardware and software products we want to use. (B2-A8)

The architects saw technology staff as having a legitimate interest in the selection of hardware and software products as they viewed technology staff as having a superior understanding of the existing IS/IT portfolio at th system level.

If we did not talk to the guys in technology, that just creates trouble. What's the point? We're in this together and we need their expertise. They know the environment better than we do. (B2-A7)

## 5.2.7 | Contrasting approaches to mutual engagement

Practices inclusive of business and technology stakeholders lead to good relations with them. The architects at Bank1 wanted to complete the selection of the technology components and implementation plans within 6 months and this pressure, it can be argued, made it difficult for them to engage with and take account of others' perspective and why their approach had a distinctly architecture focus. It is also evident from comments of business and technol ogy stakeholders at Bank1 that they expected the architects to liaise closely with business and technology staff Some technology stakeholders commented strongly that the architects had a responsibility to share the technology selection and implementation plans as this would enable projects to align their solution designs to the EA and b approved by the ARB. Aside from a solution design and implementation planning role, the architects were expected to fulfil a number of roles, including (a) understanding the “external world” of the business, in particular, the accountability of the organization to its external stakeholders, and (b) understanding the “internal world” of the organization and how they could leverage the expertise and experience of others. Comments from business and technology staff suggest a link between the failure of the architects to build support for and commitment to fund the EAI and thei isolationist approach. The accountable and pragmatic approach of the architects at Bank2, on the other hand, can be linked to a worldview that accepts business requirements are emergent and accepts the experience and expertise of others. By accommodating the need of the business to make change and recognizing the experience and expertise of technology staff, the architects were effectively able to build mutual engagement with their stakeholders.

## 5.3 | Shared repertoire

A shared repertoire of resources and practices invokes the shared “toolkit” of resources (activities, symbols, and artefacts) through which members of a CoP make sense of the world and reconcile differences in perspectives and values between themselves and other members of the CoP and which, in turn, become identified with the communit (Wenger, 1998). Comments included in this category emphasize the steps that architects took to bridge the differ ences in perspective, experience, and expertise between themselves and their stakeholders. Architects comment on the importance of their influencing role and how they consciously adapted their language and tools when dealing with business staff. They emphasized the importance of talking the same language as the business and framing the selection of software and hardware components in terms that are meaningful to business staff. It is also apparent that when architects are adept at modifying their language and tools to accommodate the backgrounds and view points of their stakeholders, they are able to build effective and collaborative connections with those stakeholders.

## 5.3.1 | Bank2: Speaking two languages

At Bank2, the architects appreciated that business and technology staff inhabited different “worlds” and reflected on the potential of architecturally complex language and models to confuse and alienate. They saw that their relation ships with business and technology staff depended on their ability to use language that promoted shared under standing with their business and technology stakeholders and referred to this as “business speak” and “technolog speak.”

We have to be able to communicate to the business in business-speak and not technology-speak. If we communicate in technology speak, we will lose the business. It takes a long time to build that communication skill. (B2-A8)

## 5.3.2 | Bank2: Building common tools

At Bank2, the architects, with the involvement of business and technology staff, conjointly developed many of the tools, including documentation templates, used in the selection of technology components and implementation plan ning. The architects and technology staff, for example, codeveloped several documentation templates, including spreadsheets, used for various tasks related to the EAI, including cataloguing the existing application portfolio and mapping the strategic objectives and desired capabilities of the business to prospective new systems. These tools enabled the architects and technology staff, for example, to share their knowledge and adopt an agreed approach and solution to a particular problem.

The architects sit down with the business to understand their required business and process capabilities. They then work with technology to understand the capability of the existing technology portfolio. Both the architects and technology are guided by what the business want to see and they tailor the technology around that. (B2-T6)

Business and technology participants commented on how the development of these tools fostered a collabora tive relationship between the architects and business and technology staff. In particular, they reflected on how col laborating on one task could “snowball” into collaborations in other activities, suggesting that the tools developed in the course of the EAI helped foster an ongoing collaboration between the architects and business and technolog staff.

We not only developed the strategic business plan together, selected the technology components and the implementation plans, but we also defined and agreed the project metrics and the reporting deliverables (B2-BU6)

There was a strong sense of appreciation in the comments of some participants that the tools developed jointl between the architects and technology staff made life easier for technology staff.

We worked with the architects to build the application inventory and define what the inventory looked like in terms of its layout. There's been some good work to document the application landscape and that work has enabled project staff to understand how their project is impacting system ‘x and what the strategy is for that system. It has really made a significant difference to those staff. (B2-T6)

## 5.3.3 | Bank1: Having a technical architecture focus

At Bank1, the modelling templates the architects used in their work revealed a strong technical architecture empha sis. Many were rich in technical detail and included architectural models that combined data flows, system interfaces and infrastructure processes for entire countries. Some of the architectural model documentation printed on singl sheets of paper measured more than 6 ft high and at least 4 ft wide. These diagrams and other architectural models and documentation were kept in a locked room accessible only to the architects. When we asked one architect about the large architectural diagrams and who was likely to use this work and what might it be used for, he commenteo that it was primarily for the architects' use. There seemed to be little concern on the part of the architects that some of the documentation they produced might not have much meaning for business and technology stakeholders. Ou field observations include the following comments.

Some of the architecture diagrams are highly detailed and technically complex. They are notated in UML and reflect the format of activity state diagrams. On one wall on very large single sheets of paper (approxi mately 6 ft. tall and 4 ft wide) with very small font (possibly 8 or 9 size) there are activity state diagrams for the technology portfolios for particular countries. These models map all of the systems, the processes and data flows that are involved for the different ways in which a customer can interact with the bank (branch, telephone, internet, call centre, hardcopy mail) in a particular country. They are technically very impressive to observe, represent a significant effort to produce and integrate highly specialized knowledge of the bank's systems, processes, infrastructure and data, but we are not quite sure how they might be of practical use. They seem symbolic rather than significant in the sense they represent the technical knowledge and modelling expertise within the architecture team, but they are not made available to technology staff and contain a lot more information than is required for the implementation plans and do not have a clear problem or solution focus.

Technology stakeholders wanted the architects to explain the connection between the selected technology products and business processes and services. In particular, they wanted the architects to explain how diagrams such as th type described immediately above explained the business improvements associated with the EAI.

They have to make a connection between the platforms they have designed and how the functionality of those platforms will be consumed by the businesses. ## 5.3.5 | Contrasting approaches to shared repertoire

At Bank2, the architects emphasized the importance of building a common understanding of the EAl with their stakeholders, and, consequently, they modified their modelling tools and language to facilitate closer interaction wit their stakeholders. Documentation templates were codeveloped with business staff, and architecture tools such as the application inventory tool were codeveloped with technology staff. The information contained in these tools was shared with technology staff, and the architects viewed this as helping technology staff in their work. The architects also viewed the codevelopment of templates and the use of a common language as important to the exchange of knowledge between themselves and business and technology stakeholders. At Bank1, the architects focused on completing their EAI plans within the allotted 6 months, and their practices, documentation, and tools were technically oriented and did not involve the participation of stakeholders, and this may explain the technical detail of theil models as well as the attention they gave to management control approaches to achieve their objectives rather than pursue alternative approaches.

## 6 | DISCUSSION

In this section, we discuss the findings from the data analysis to understand the practices that enable or inhibit archi tects to build effective interpersonal connections with their business and technology stakeholders. The EAI initiatives at Bank1 and Bank2 ultimately achieved different outcomes. At Bank 1, the architects were unable to obtain busi ness funding for the technology products they selected, and, ultimately, the business divisions developed and implemented their own architectures, thus ignoring the architects. At Bank2, on the other hand, the EAI initiativ was successful since the architects built support and commitment for the technology products they selected, and the programmes of work associated with the EAI are still continuing.

The findings across the two cases contrast two different approaches to EAI, which can be characterized as an iso lationist and “intra-CoP perspective” and a collaborative and “inter-CoP perspective.” The challenges associated wit an intra-CoP approach (Bank1) appear to be more acute than those challenges that arise from a more collaborativ and inter-CoP approach (Bank2). One can also infer from the nature of concerns associated with the intra-CoP approach that these concerns could arise from differences in assumptions about the roles and practices of architects The architects at Bank1 viewed their EAI role in technical terms and their objectives were to select new systems and develop implementation plans to deliver those systems into operation. However, the architects did not involve busi ness and technology stakeholders in the selection of the technology products but instead focused on how the busi ness could potentially be made to implement those products. In selecting the technology products, the architects at Bank1 demonstrated that they felt a stronger sense of accountability to each other than they did to their business and technology stakeholders. The hardware and software evaluation tools and method were not shared artefacts in the sense that they were not negotiated and developed in collaboration with business and technology stakeholders Rather, they reflected what was important and mattered to the architects. The architects at Bank2 similarly viewed their role as selecting new systems; however, they worked closely with business staff to understand their long-term and emergent needs and worked closely with technology staff when selecting systems to satisfy the changing priori ties of the business. In Bank2. there is a stronger sense of joint enterprise and mutual engagement between the architects and their stakeholders since what is important and matters in the EAI is negotiated between the architects and their business and technology stakeholders. The architects accepted that business priorities and requirement may change and accordingly worked with business and technology stakeholders to find suitable technology solu tions. The architects collaborated with business stakeholders to build tools that enabled them to capture the strategic objectives of a business as well as understand the envisaged future products and services. They also collaborated with technology stakeholders to develop technology plans for those envisaged products and services. Through this process, the architects and their business and technology stakeholders worked together to build artefacts that pro moted a shared understanding and a sense of joint enterprise. These artefacts helped the architects to understand the strategic requirements of the business and also enabled business stakeholders to understand how the EAI woul enable the delivery of those requirements.

The different approaches can also be linked to a difference in worldview and willingness to recognize the experi ence and expertise of others. Architects who adopt an inter-CoP and collaborative approach accept that business pri orities may change and are willing to modify their technology selection and implementation plans as required. Whilst they may question the motivation of the business to make change, they factor the motivation and objectives of th business into their system selection. To understand the constraints of the existing environment and the implication for their decision making, the architects must also liaise with technology staff. Whilst architects possess deep archi tecture knowledge across several areas, they are dependent on the experience and expertise of technology staff who have a deeper understanding of the technology portfolio and how the constraints of the portfolio may influence system choices.

By accommodating the need of the business to make change and recognizing the experience and expertise of technology staff, the architects at Bank2 were able to build relationships and mutual engagement with their stake holders. Architects unwilling to accept that the external environment of the business may change and that the priori ties of the business may also change are also unwilling to accept that the selected technology components an implementation plans must change accordingly. They view the system selection and implementation plans as one-off occurrences and resist changes required by the business and technology. They seek to “protect” the selected sys tems, and this approach can heighten tension between them and business and technology staff.

Another characteristic associated with the intra-CoP and inter-CoP approaches is the relative importance given to the concerns and objectives of architects over those of business and technology staff. Many interviewee com ments left us with an impression that the architects' prioritization of what matters to them over the requirements of business and technology staff may have a role to play in some issues. In Bank1, the architects monopolized knowledge of the selected technology products and implementation plans so as to stop all technology projects. They did this because they prioritized the stability of the IS/IT portfolio over the needs of the business and also the information requirements of technology projects. Whilst the architects worked effectively within their own community to develop the hardware and software evaluation tools, they exhibited characteristics of a dysfunctional CoP in the sense that they focused on their own needs and objectives and not on their relationships with their stakeholders. At Bank2, many comments from architects and business and technology staff suggest that architects worked collabora tively with business and technology staff and deferred to the needs and priorities of the business. When business pri orities changed, the architects and technology staff worked together to develop alternative architectural solutions.

The tendency of architects in Bank1 to prioritize what is important to them relative to the views and concerns of business and technology staff helps to make sense of some of the differences observed across the two cases. In Bank1, the architects seemed to believe they could impose their technology selection and implementation plans on business and technology staff. They effectively used the ARB to stop projects and prevent the business divisions from pursuing their own technology projects. The situation in Bank2 was different. The architects appeared to us their influence to build support for and commitment to fund the systems they selected. They adopted a commercially pragmatic and collaborative approach and made themselves accessible to business and technology staff. They exchanged knowledge and shared information with them and recognized and incorporated into their decision making the views and concerns of business and technology staff. In short, the architects at Bank2 showed a strong inclination to reduce the boundaries between them and business and technology staff. Figure 4 describes the different ori entations of the architects in both cases.

As indicated by the findings in both cases, the practices of architects effectively created a boundary around them. The boundaries around the communities of architects in each case existed in different states of perme ability: thick and thin. The practices of the architects at Bank1 are indicative of a thick boundary as the prevented connections with others who have a legitimate interest in the EAI. The architects prioritized what they thought was important over the views and concerns of business and technology stakeholders. They priori tized architectural objectives over business objectives and did not take into consideration the commercial con straints and profit imperative of the organization when selecting new systems. They perceived that it was not important to engage with stakeholders, yet business and technology participants expected the architects to explain the connections between the selected technology components and desired business processes, products and services. The architects felt that there was sufficient knowledge and experience within the architectur team to select the technology products, but technology staff perceived that insufficient recognition was given to the experience and expertise of technology staff.

The practices of the architects at Bank2, on the other hand, were indicative of a thin permeable boundary as they allowed connections with others. The architects at Bank2 engaged with business and technology stakeholders considered their needs, and were careful to use language and models that were meaningful to both business an technology stakeholders. They perceived that technology staff have experience and knowledge that is beneficial to the EAI, and technology participants expected architects provide opportunities for technology staff to share thei knowledge and experience. The architects also involved technology staff in the selection of technology products as this was crucial to building support for those components, and technology participants expected architects to be available to discuss matters related to the selection of technology products. The architects perceived that develop ing documentation templates and other tools with technology staff will build connections with them, and technology staff perceived a strong sense of collaboration with the architects

## 7 | COP AND THE EAI PRACTICES OF ARCHITECTS

## 7.1 | Mutual engagement

The mutual engagement dimension suggests that the actions of individuals become meaningful because of engage ment amongst individuals in the same community (Wenger, 1998). However, in an EAI, the social configuration that matters is the one formed through inter-CoP interactions with business stakeholders, on the one hand and with technology stakeholders on the other. To build a sense of mutual engagement with stakeholders, architects must consider measures that enable effective inter-CoP connections, reducing boundaries between themselves and thei stakeholders and promoting closer working relationships with stakeholders. For example, architects should reflect on the different types of technical knowledge relevant to the selection of technology products. Such an approac may be useful since architects may fail to understand problems associated with available commercial software and hardware products and the potential pitfalls of deploying some products into the technology environment. Archi tects should also reflect on the dynamic and emergent nature of the business environment and how business priori ties and constraints may change, and this may make it necessary to make changes to the selected technology components and implementation plans.

## 7.2 | Joint enterprise

Wenger (1998) describes joint enterprise as a source of social cohesion because it is the result of a collective pro cess of negotiation amongst members of the CoP about what is important to the group (p. 77). Further, joint enter prise does not simply mean a stated goal but represents the development of mutual accountability amongst individuals in a CoP and is seen to arise from mutual engagement. As “being involved in what is important in the architects” work is focused in their connections with business and technology stakeholders, the mutual accountabil ity that is important in the architects' EAI work also appears to be focused on the inter-CoP connections with busi ness and technology stakeholders. When architects adopt reclusive practices and do not share information with technology project teams, this can create a thick and impermeable boundary around the architects' CoP (intra-CoP perspective) and prevent connections between the architects and technology stakeholders. Adopting an inter-CoP approach may lead to thin permeable boundaries that allow information to flow between the architects and tech nology stakeholders.

## 7.3 | Shared repertoire

Wenger (1998) argues that over time, individuals engage in a joint endeavour that matters and develop resources for negotiating meaning between them (p. 82). They develop a shared repertoire of resources, including activities tools, and methods, that reflect a shared worldview. The findings of this research suggest that in an EAI, the critica dimensions of engagement and enterprise are located at the connections between architects and business stake holders and architects and technology stakeholders. The shared repertoire of these connections would arguably be more effective if it were customized for each engagement (eg, architects-business and architects-technology). Th tools, methods, and other artefacts that architects use need to help them understand what is important within each connection and enable mutual accountability to be developed and sustained at each connection. This implies the need to consider different “repertoires” at the connections between architects and business stakeholders and archi tects and technology stakeholders. The detailed technical architecture models and architecture evaluation criteria used by the architects seem to be disconnected from the requirements of the connections where architects engage with other communities. This raises concerns as to whether the shared repertoire of the architects, their tools, methods, and artefacts, whilst supporting their need for detailed architectural information, also supports their need to “live in multiple worlds.” Evidence from this research suggests that some of the difficulties that architects have in establishing effective inter-CoP connections in an EAI are due to the lack of this capability. For example, whe designing software and hardware evaluation models, architects may prioritize architectural goals (eg, flexibility and scalability) (Bank1) and not incorporate the values and concerns of business and technology stakeholders.

## 7.4 | Theorizing the EAI connections of architects

Existing EA theory has not adeguately theorized the interpersonal forces that enable architects to build effective connections with their stakeholders. Authors have examined EAI as a technical architecture activity and have also examined the social conditions of EAI success and failure, but authors have not given adequate attention to the important antecedent role that the practices of architects play in the interaction with stakeholders and how this impacts an outcome of an EAI initiative. Wenger's (1998) conceptual dimensions of mutual engagement, joint enterprise, and shared repertoire emphasize that what matters and is important to a CoP is worked out within the community itself and is a cohesive force within that community. However, in an EAI, being involved in what matters and is important is not so much a matter of engagement amongst architects themselves as it is a matter of engagement between architects and their business and technology stakeholders. When architects adopt an inter-CoP perspective, building a shared sense of joint enterprise and mutual engagement with individuals from other CoPs and modifying tools and methods to enable a shared perspective and objectives, an intercommunit CoP emerges. This shift in emphasis away from the architects' CoP as the loci of social cohesion and learning to an intercommunity CoP between architects and their stakeholders has implications for Wenger's CoP theory as a framework for conceptualizing the connections that architects must build with their stakeholders (See Figure 5)

First, given that “being included in what matters is a requirement for being engaged” (Wenger, 1998, p. 74) and what matters in the architects' EAI work is their engagement with their business and technology stakeholders, th concept of mutual engagement needs to be reconceptualised as intercommunity engagement. Given the tendency towards individual liaison work and the potential amount of time spent in intercommunity work during an EAI, archi tects must be skilled in developing shared understandings and objectives with individuals from other CoPs. Thi engagement we refer to as intercommunity engagement. Second, considering Wenger's (1998) suggestion that engagement results in a joint enterprise (relations of mutual accountability), this focuses our attention on relations of mutual accountability between architects and stakeholders. As joint enterprise arises from a shared sense of accountability that individuals involved in a CoP have for other members of the CoP, we need to consider whether joint enterprise should likewise be considered intercommunity enterprise. Third, the extent to which the repertoir of architects can enable them to “live in multiple worlds” may depend on the extent to which tools, methods, and artefacts developed by architects promote knowledge sharing and cocreation with stakeholders from different areas and levels of the organization. The detailed technical architecture models and methods used by the architects seem to be disconnected from the requirements of the intercommunity interactions between architects and their stakeholders. The shared repertoire required at the connections between architects and their stakeholders (architects business and architects-technology) should be customized to support the need for knowledge sharing and insights at those connections. This implies the need to consider different repertoires at the connections between architects and business stakeholders and architects and technology stakeholders and which considers the different perspectives and priorities of business stakeholders and technology stakeholders. Consequently, the important role that shared repertoire plays in an EAI reconciling the different perspectives and priorities between architects and their stake holders makes it necessary to reconceptualize shared repertoire as intercommunity repertoire.

As discussed above, existing CoP theory does not adeguately characterize the social processes that enable connections across community boundaries. As they are described in this paper, effective connections between individuals from different CoPs are enabled by thin boundaries around their respective CoPs, which allow for the emergence of joint enterprise, mutual engagement, and shared repertoire between them. In this sense, thin boundaries between CoPs are, in effect, instance-specific intercommunity CoPs. Whilst efficient knowledge sharing and the development of new knowledge are salient characteristics of these CoPs, the role of such social configurations in an EAI is much more complex. Intercommunity CoPs allow individuals from different CoPs to negotiate for their interests and develop mutual accountability and a sense of shared enterprise as they work on a task that is relevant to them. These individuals develop tools that facilitate the reconciliation of differences in perspectives and backgrounds and promote a shared worldview. It also should be noted that the focus of our intercommunity lens has been directed towards architects and business stakeholders and architects and technology stakeholders and does not include the intercommunity CoP that may arise between technology and business stakeholders. Whilst this can be an important social configuration in an EAI, our focus is the practices of architects and how they impact the ability of architects to build effective connections with business and technology stakeholders. The reader is directed to Vashist et al. (2010) for a CoP analysis of business and technology inter action. A conceptualization of the intercommunity CoP concept is provided in Figure 5.

Theorizing the connections that form between different CoPs has implications for EAI practice and how we conceptualize the role of architects in an EAI. Whilst descriptions of architects' role in an EAI emphasize a technical perspective, findings from this research indicate that where architects are required to seek the involvement of business and technology stakeholders, architects must be skilled intercommunity agents. Whilst architects involved in intercommunity connections have episodic and task-focused contact with business and technology stakeholders, they need to maintain ongoing relationships with business and technology stakeholders in order to maintain continuity of support for the EAI. As there are differences in perspectives between business and technology stakeholders, architects may be more effective in viewing business and technology stakeholders as representing different practices. As intercommunity agents, architects must play dual roles as technical experts and also reflective practitioners sensitive to how their practices and tools enable or inhibit connections with others Thick boundaries around the EAI CoP adversely affect the outcome of the EAI since architects do not enable business and technology stakeholders who have legitimate interests in the EAI to participate. Thin boundaries enable business and technology stakeholders with legitimate interests to participate in the EAI and make it easie for architects to maintain ongoing support for the EAI. The important role that architects play in enabling thin boundaries demands that we reconsider the skills required of architects during an EAI. The multidimensiona nature of an EAI CoP suggests that there is a sharper research question than the one we originally proposed: Is there a significant practice emerging? In some organizations, EAIs require not only deep technical expertise across multiple technology and business domains, but also, as our data demonstrates, relationally competent individuals skilled in developing a sense of mutual engagement and joint enterprise across different communities and able to use different methods and tools to enable knowledge exchange, shared understandings, and goals. Whereas EA CoPs have been narrowly defined in the past, our research shows that in some organizations, architects play dua roles as technically and relationally skilled practitioners. ## 8 | CONCLUSION

The objective of this paper is to shed light on an under researched aspect of the architect and stakeholder relationship and to theorize how architects may build commitment and support for an EAI. This study, drawing on evidence from two actual EAIs in their organizational context and the interaction between the architects an business and technology stakeholders, offers insights into the practices and interpersonal forces that may influ ence the outcome of an EAI initiative. This research shows that if architects are unable to bridge the bound aries that arise between themselves and their business and technology stakeholders, this may make it difficult to build support for and commitment to the selected technologies and the implementation plans.

A key finding of this research is therefore that an EAI may fail if architects do not build effective connections with business and technology stakeholders. Our research suggests that technical expertise may be somewhat les important than a high level of relational competency. Whilst not every architect involved in an EAI may need this, if it is absent, then failure would be the likely outcome. Secondly, it may be sufficient if there were a spe cialized “stakeholder liaison” role “perspective spanner” as part of the architecture team and the team, especiall the leader, understood its importance. This has implications for the education of architects and the emphasi given to technical architecture skills and the little consideration given by tertiary institutions and professiona organizations to the relational challenges associated with an EAI.

In terms of the research question (What impact do the practices of architects have on their ability to build stake holder support and commitment for an EAI?), which was focused on understanding how architects can build effectiv connections with their stakeholders, the major findings are as follows.

1. In order to bridge the boundaries between themselves and their stakeholders, architects must develop relationa competencies that allow them to assimilate the perspectives and concerns of their stakeholders in their own responses to a particular architectural task or problem.

2. The extent to which architects are able to build mutual engagement with their stakeholders in an EAI is dependent on their relational competencies. The potential for ambiguity and variety in an EAI means that architects must be skilful in adapting their relational competencies to reconcile different perspectives, concerns, and objec tives associated with the EAl in order to establish effective connections with different stakeholders

3. The intercommunity connection perspective highlights the nature of the interactions between architects and their stakeholders during an EAI. Though architects need to maintain ongoing relationships with business and technology stakeholders, they have direct engagement with them on an as-needs basis for short-term task specific activities, and once the objectives of their engagement are satisfied, they can then disengage from that interaction.

Several interesting research themes have emerged through the course of this research. Specific to EA, our research reveals that technical architecture expertise may not be the only requirement to be an effective architect as technical knowledge and skills may prevent a thorough contextual understanding of stakeholder requirements and perspectives associated with technology selection and implementation planning. An important research direction therefore would be one that explores how the analysis and planning practices of architects could support a thorough analysis of and question the assumptions and worldview that relate to stakeholder requirements and priorities Moreover, whilst our case analysis focused on an EAI implementation in two Australian banks, we feel that our con tribution is not limited to EAI specifically and that our theoretical perspective and research methodology may provid useful insights into the interpersonal conditions that affect the outcomes of other multidisciplinary initiatives

This research has implications for the extent to which technical specialization is required in the architecture role It appears that whilst architects are required to have specialized technical skills for undertaking architecture work the findings of this research indicate the importance of hybrid roles (roles that have knowledge of two areas), whic are needed to build effective connections with business and technology stakeholders. These findings may provide an improved understanding of whether architects need to be technical specialists as well as competent, perspective takers or can architecture subject matter expertise alone make individuals suitable for the architecture role

However, the research is not without its limitations. Firstly, our research findings are based on an examination of two cases that involved 25 interviews. Our research was primarily concerned with making changes to the EAI prac tices of architects, and, from the results, we proposed ways in which EAI practice in general can be improved. How ever, our research remains limited as we have not “tested” our proposed improvements to the EAI practices of architects. This would improve the generalizability of our results and provide practical outcomes for the EAI practices of architects.

## AUTHOR BIOGRAPHIES

Mark Dale has more than 27 years of experience in executive leadership roles, enterprise architecture, data architecture and strategy, storage strategy, security architecture, and process analysis and solution design in the financial services, education, and health services industries. He has been the CIO for large pharmaceutical com panies and has led IT functions for superannuation companies. He has been responsible for application, infrastructure, and desktop support teams and the day-to-day operational management of technology teams and vendors and has led data warehouse strategy, architecture, and data architecture projects in the design and delivery of identity and access management and cash management solutions.
