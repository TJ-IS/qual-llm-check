# Investigating the Relationship Between Perceived Risks in Communication and ICT-enabled Communicative Behaviors

## Abstract

We examine employees’ perceptions of communication problems in the workplace and their active communication behavioral responses when multiple ICTs are available for use. Through the lens of the situational theory of publics, we shed light on how employees adapt to increasing communication demands. We uncovered active technology-mediated behavior such as information gaining, action, and social interaction seeking, and their association with perceived risk (i.e. message, information and action). Findings suggest that technology-mediated communication behaviors are not only enabled by the use of ICTs but behavior also involves appropriate structuring of single and multiple ICTs to manage problems encountered during communication.

Keywords: ICT use; perceived risk; organization communication; structuring mechanisms; situational theory of publics; distributed work; use of multiple ICTs

## 1. Introduction

The proliferation of information communication technologies (ICTs) has changed communication behaviors in organizations. Employees collaborate and communicate across temporal, spatial, cultural, and organizational boundaries [9, 79]. They may use multiple ICTs during a single interaction because a variety of technologies are available [40, 70, 79], which can intensify the complexity of organizational communication. Similarly, increased perceptions of risk are associated with an ICT-mediated communication environment [59]. In sum, prior research suggests ICT use is associated with problems for employees as well as opportunities for achieving goals [7, 8, 39, 48].

#

We examine this dichotomy with a study of ICT-mediated communication in a global IT consulting company. To study how single as well as multiple ICTs are used in a workplace where multiple ICTs are available for use, we adopt the notion of ICT structuring mechanisms [49] which refers to the process through which users manipulate and reshape their technologies to accomplish a desired outcome [50, 87]. We investigate structuring mechanisms and their relationship to perceived risks in communication mediated by ICT. We draw from the situational theory of publics (STP) that considers the extent to which people are aware of a problem and the extent to which they do something about it [21]. Employees perceive a problem that requires a response from them, and subsequently, engage in deliberate communication behaviors to improve the situation. Our study seeks to shed light on the following overarching question: How and why does perceived risk influence communication behavior in an environment where multiple ICT are available for use?

This study will examine organizational communication where employees are active problem solvers and they communicate to solve a potential problem. We examine both employees perceptions of communication problems and their active communication behavioral responses when multiple ICTs are available for use. Through the lens of STP, we advance our understanding of perceptions of employees when they encounter communication problems and the social processes created by their communication behaviors. Our research sheds light on how employees adapt to increasing communication demands (e.g. virtual teams, complex ICT-enabled interaction, increased task complexity) in the workplace. It also extends STP to include ICT usage communication behaviors in business organizations.

## 2. Background

2.1 Communication Problems and Multiple ICT Use

#

In line with many studies of organizational communication, we view communication behavior as goal-driven where organizational members communicate to achieve intended results [12, 35, 51]. Further, accomplishing communication goals is necessary for the success of the individuals as well as the organization [52]. We expect employees to actively work towards accomplishing goals in communication goals in an environment where multiple ICTs are available for use. Specifically, we examine organizational communication problems and their associated perceived risks as well as the communication behavioral responses to the risks perceived.

The use of ICTs plays a crucial role in the social construction of risk in organizations [11] since ICTs are often relied upon in the workplace to provide ubiquitous access to content and people, allow fast transfer or exchange of content, enable more sophisticated processing of data, and facilitate communication across time and space [e.g. 33, 40, 70, and 79]. There are risks in such heavy reliance on ICT. Robert et al. [59] found that individuals perceived higher risk in ICTmediated communication than face-to-face communication, but little work has been done to examine how individuals adjust their communication behaviors in response to the greater risk.

We suggest that an employee who perceives a potential communication problem (e.g., makes a risk assessment) responds by selecting from the available set of ICT to support an active communication behavior to resolve the problem. The employee first decides whether to use one or multiple ICTs (e.g., telephone conference call or email and conference call). If multiple ICT are used, the employee can use different structuring mechanisms to combine the media [5]. The ability to effectively use single as well as multiple ICTs to accomplish communication goals during the communication process is a critical skill in organizations today [71]. Woerner, et al. [85] found that organizational members used both single ICT and multiple ICTs to work with colleagues who were both collocated and distributed across distant sites to accomplish different communication goals. In terms of combinatorial use of ICTs, three distinct ICT structuring mechanisms have been cited in the literature: concurrent, sequential, and repetitive. We now look at these options in more detail.

A concurrent structuring mechanism is the combinatorial use of ICTs in parallel [43]. An example is the use of instant messaging while participating in a telephone conference call. Past research on ICT use in organizations has shown that employees observed that participants deployed multiple ICTs simultaneously to complete an organizational task [40, 79].

The combinatorial use of ICTs in sequence or the use of one ICT followed by another successively is another structuring mechanism. For example, sending an instant message after posting meeting minutes to a repository to let others know the minutes are available. Specifically, Massey and Montoya-Weiss [40] observed that people switched from one ICT to another if one ICT was deemed to be inappropriate. A sequential structure of ICT use has also been adopted under time pressure [81] or when people encounter complexity during communication [43]. A sequential structuring mechanism includes use of multiple ICTs, with employees switching from one to another consecutively until they accomplish their communication goals.

The third mechanism is the repetitive use of the same structure of ICTs to accomplish communication goals over time. Except for a handful of studies [e.g. 70], research on how people use ICTs over time (e.g. as follow-up) to accomplish communication goals is very limited. In particular, Stephens [70] argued that a follow-up attempt to use an ICT is likely dependent on the initial ICT chosen and the related outcome. A repetitive structuring mechanism refers to situations where employees use the same ICT structure repeatedly until the communication goals are accomplished (e.g., repeated emails [38]).

Thus, there is significant evidence in the literature that people are using different combinations of media to reduce perceived communication risks. However, most of this research has been descriptive, identifying distinct combinatorial structures as the focus of the study [e.g., 5] or as an ad hoc finding [e.g., 40]. There is little theoretical basis for these findings (a recent exception is [38]). We now introduce the situational theory of publics to provide a theoretical grounding for our study.

## 2.2 Situational Theory of Publics

The situational theory of publics (STP) is a foundational theory in communication research that considers how people perceive a situation and the resultant communication behaviors [21]. Originally used in the field of public relations to examine conditions that lead people to respond and communicate when they perceive a problem in the external environment, it has also been used to explain communication associated with consumer behavior, political activism, and the influence of perceived shared risk in crisis communication [2, 3].

Three factors affect communication behavior according to STP. Problem recognition is the perception of a problem or issue that needs to be addressed. A goal-driven individual will attempt to devise communication strategies to ensure effective organization communication that addresses the problem [51, 73]. Constraint recognition is the extent to which one’s behavior is limited by factors that cannot be controlled, either psychological (e.g., self efficacy) or physical (e.g., access to an ICT). Level of involvement is the extent to which a problem or issue is relevant to an individual. For example, an IT consultant committed to providing superior software products will be more engaged and committed to understanding a customer’s business problems.

Risks arise out of the actions and activities of individuals and society through conscious decision-making [4]. Scholars suggest that risks can be perceived, and the study of risks should examine issues surrounding the problem such as processes leading to the problem and subjective beliefs, values and contextual interpretation of the problem [63, 65]. STP has been used by researchers to investigate communication responses to problems under conditions of risk in public arenas such as threats posed by bioterrorism or other crises [2, 41]. Aldoory and colleagues [3] found that greater perceptions of risk were associated with increased information gaining behaviors.

The focus of our study is to examine the influence of risk perception on ICT usage behavior in organizational communication. Adapting STP to this context, problem recognition is triggered when an individual perceives some type of risk in the communication process. Constraint recognition refers to the available media that individuals have access to in the organizational context. In other words, the individual recognizes the media available to be used will shape their response behavior. The perception of risk leads to heightened awareness and attention to the situation. Specifically, under increased perception of risk of communication problems, individuals become more involved in the situation. STP suggests that these three factors will influence the resulting communication behavior of the individuals.

Further, STP suggests that individuals adjust behavior in an effort to address the type of problem identified. The use of ICT is known to introduce different types of risk into the communication process [59]. We next briefly review previous research examining different types of perceived risks associated with ICT use.

## 2.3 Perceived Risks in ICT-Mediated Organizational Communication

Perceived risk measures beliefs of the uncertainty regarding possible negative consequences. It develops when people identify adverse consequences or uncertainties that may pose potential problems to achieve favorable outcomes [11]. Specifically, perceived risks in communication refer to the perceived problems that may influence one’s ability to accomplish communication goals. To

#

investigate risks associated with the use of ICT, or computer-mediated communication, we adopt Weaver [80]’s typology of communication problems to identify potential risks. The first type, the technical aspect of communication concerns the accuracy of the message transmitted between the sender and receiver. The second category, the semantic aspect deals with the meaning of the information to the receiver. Finally, the effectiveness category of risk deals with the influence of information on the receiver, or action the sender expects the receiver to take. We reviewed past studies of computer-mediated communication that exhibited evidence of technical, semantic or effectiveness communication problems and summarized them in Table 1. Because of the dramatic changes and advancements in communication technology, the review was intentionally limited to research published since 1990. Our review shows that effectiveness communication problems appeared to be less frequently cited in past studies than technical and semantic communication problems. This could be due to the fact that the majority of these studies did not focus on organizational communication. For organizational communication, all three categories are equally critical. Overall, our review shows that these three categories of communication problems are linked to the different stages of the communication process. Based on our review, we identified risk factors associated with each type of perceived communication risk and the ways these risks might be manifest when ICTs are used.

We found that technical communication problems are primarily found when ICTs are perceived to fail to effectively support the faithful generation, transmission, or reception of the intended message between the sender and receiver. At the semantic level, the focus is on understanding of the message content and a different set of factors contributes to potential communication problems. First, a number of studies focus on the lack of understanding of the message content due to cognitive or affective limitations of individuals involved in the communication process. Other

#

studies took a relational perspective, examining problems in understanding the message due to lack of a shared background between the sender and receiver. At the effectiveness level, factors leading to communication problems include the lack of action or action taken by the receiver that were not intended by the sender.

In summary, we have identified different perceived risks of communication problems across the different aspects of the computer-mediated communication process, i.e. message, information and action. There is some evidence that combining ICTs can help manage perceived risks that may occur in the message, information, and action components [e.g., 56, 45, 30, 32]. For example, past studies have suggested that sequential switching from one ICT to other ICTs ensures that messages are received [e.g., 56, 45]. Also, concurrent and sequential structuring of ICTs may address problems in the information component by providing explicit context in the form of external representation to develop or reconstruct an individual’s understanding process [30, 46]. A synchronous conversation improves one’s ability to correct misunderstandings [e.g., 1, 32, 81]. Furthermore, redundancy appears to be an effective way to ensure that the receiver is able to act upon the message [70]. Individuals may send the same message repeatedly using the same ICT(s) to reinforce the message and pressure their communication partners to act upon the message when risks are perceived in the action component.

Thus, we propose the following research question:

RQ1: How is type of perceived risk, i.e., message, information, or action, and level of perceived risk, i.e., high or low, associated with use of ICT structuring mechanisms, i.e., sequential, concurrent, or repetitive?

## 2.4 Communication Behaviors in ICT-Mediated Organizational Communication

STP suggests that when an individual recognizes a problem in a public arena that is relevant and within his/her ability to resolve, the individual will respond by invoking communication behaviors with the intent of gaining information [3]. Our literature review suggests that communication behavior in organizations is similarly employed with the intent of gaining information to reduce the risk of perceived failure. One of the major communication purposes for using ICT is to seek additional information so as to gain more insight into a problem [20]. Such information gaining communication behaviors are likely to be important in the workplace as the information gained from different ICT helps to reduce miscommunication [45]. However, this may lead to increased complexity [85] and create information overload due to increased information communicated [27]. Recent research on organizational communication has suggested appropriate infusion of informal communication in the workplace is necessary to enhance team bonding among members [45]. Increasingly, employees in the workplace are also relying on ICT to facilitate informal communication or social interaction [36]. Additionally, some ICT (e.g. email) has been shown to be effective to pressure receivers to take action in the workplace [67]. Collectively, past research suggests that different types of communication behaviors (e.g. information gaining, social interaction seeking or action seeking) are likely to occur in the workplace depending on the types and levels of perceived risks in the communication process.

Thus we pose the following question:

RQ2: What communication behaviors are associated with active ICT usage in response to different types of perceived risk, i.e., message, information, and action, and levels of perceived risk, i.e., high or low?

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Research Setting

Past research has indicated that analyzing risk requires insight into the real-life context to understand its implications for the organization. Thus, we conducted our exploratory study in the Mid-west (U.S.) division of a global IT consulting company headquartered in India, which we will call ITServ. Employees in ITServ are geographically and temporally distributed with some employees located at the headquarters and other employees located at different clients’ sites in the U.S. The core business of ITServ is in IT consulting and custom software development. Employees are involved in many different aspects of software development work, such as managing software development projects, coordinating business and functional requirements, developing customized software for various clients, providing IT consultation, and providing help-desk and end-user support.

The ITServ employees located at client sites have to coordinate business and functional requirements, while the employees located in India have to develop software for their clients. It is vital for employees in both the U.S. and India to coordinate their work schedules so they can provide 24-hour “round-the-clock” service to their worldwide clients. To gather contextual insights, the research team had several meetings with different senior managers of the company before the formal data collection. The purpose of these meetings was to understand the business

#

environment, organizational hierarchy, work functions of the employees, and the types of ICT used by employees. This background information enabled the research team to design and customize the interview protocols using specific language and technical terms familiar to the respondents. In addition, one member of the research team attended several project meetings as a passive observer to gather more contextual information about development projects.

Two client sites from the mid-west U.S., M-Org and N-Org, were identified by a senior manager of ITServ to be appropriate for this study. The two client teams were similar in terms of team structures, work procedures and distribution, organization policies, and employees’ skills. The managers in charge of the two sites were instructed to choose a pool of employees from different hierarchical levels within the two sites such that the research sample would be representative of the employee population. We obtained access to these employees through their managers. The employees were informed that participation in the study was voluntary, they could opt out at any time, and that all collected data would be kept confidential. Employees selected to participate in the research were all involved in computer software development and were full-time project members of one of the project teams working either at M-Org (20 ITServ employees) or N-Org (27 ITSErv employees). The team members in the U.S. were located at the appropriate client’s site, while team members in India were located at the main offices of ITServ. Demographics of respondents are shown in Table 2.

## 3.2 Data Collection

Data for the study was collected in two phases: (1) a one-day communication diary and (2) a follow-up semi-structured interview. All the forty-seven respondents participated in both phases. The instruments (communication diary and the interview protocols) were pilot-tested with four employees (two from each project group) before they were administered to all the respondents of the study. The pilot-test results showed that only minor wording changes to the communication diary forms were required. After the pilot test, an email explaining the purpose of the study was sent to all respondents to invite them to participate in the study. When a respondent accepted the request, an Excel spreadsheet was sent to him or her to log all the communication activities he or she was involved with in a typical workday.

The diary method of data collection requires respondents to keep logs of various communication activities. Respondents were asked to create a one-day diary of all communication activities [42]. They recorded communication activities over different time periods of the day: Before 8 AM, 8-10 AM, 10AM-12 PM, 12-2 PM, 2-4 PM, 4-6 PM, and After 6 PM. For each communication activity, respondents indicated the time, duration, goal of communication, communication partner(s), communication technology or communication technologies used, and the risks perceived about the communication. A copy of the communication diary for the “Before 8AM” time period is in the Appendix.

Interviews were conducted after the respondents had completed their communication diaries. They took place via telephone, lasted about an hour, were held at a time specified by the respondent, and were recorded with the permission of the respondent. A sample of the interview protocol is shown in the Appendix.    Demographic and job information (i.e. job title, gender, and company tenure) of the respondent was obtained during the interviews. The respondents were asked to clarify and elaborate every entry in their communication diary during the interview. In particular, we sought to uncover what and how ICTs were structured and used. Respondents were not directly prompted for answers but were allowed to give spontaneous reasons for the communication technology used and their perceptions of communication problems. As the unit of analysis in the study was the communication event, communication activities for each respondent that shared the same purpose and communication partner(s) were linked to the same communication events. ## 3.3 Constructs

## 3.3.1 ICTs and Structuring Mechanisms

We define ICTs broadly to include telephone, and all forms of electronic communications that support communication and information sharing in the workplace [40, 70]. We examined the ICTs available to all the respondents at ITServ for use in their work environment, and also documented faceto-face communication.

 Instant Messaging – Allows synchronous written communication. An important feature is that it displays availability of other potential communication partners online through a “buddy list”.

 Email: Allows messages to be stored, manipulated and processed. Messages can be edited and forwarded to other parties. In addition to the text body of an email, attachments allow other types of data to be sent. Email attachments might include software programs and video clips.

 NetMeeting™: Allows remote access to shared work objects such as software application systems and PowerPoint presentations.

 Telephone/Teleconferencing: Allows audio synchronous communication. Includes one-on-one telephone communication and teleconferencing with multiple partners located at different locations.

Software project management system: Allows the storage, retrieval, and update of software project-related information in a centralized location.

 Shared electronic repository: Allows sharing of electronic files among project team members. Implemented as a project website or a shared file server.

Problem Reporting Application: Facilitates the reporting, tracking, and fixing of software application problems. Can be connected to the email system. For example, to facilitate monitoring urgent problems, individuals can set up email connections to the application such that they will be notified once there is a change in status of the problem. The Problem Reporting Application at the two sites are different even though the functionalities are the same. All the respondents have access to the application system.

 Face-to-face: Face-to-face communication.

In addition to capturing the ICTs used, we captured the structuring of ICTs for each communication event. Three binary variables were used to denote the use of sequential, concurrent, or repetitive structuring mechanisms. The value “1” indicated the particular structuring mechanism was being used while “0” indicated the absence of that structuring mechanism.

## 3.3.2 Perceived Risks in Communication

Our review of past studies in Table 1 provided the basis for how we measured the three perceived risks constructs. The perceived risk in the message, information, and action components for all the communication activities were self-reported by the respondents in the communication diary (refer to communication diary template in Appendix). Perceived risk in the message component (RM) was measured using the mean of three items relating to the generation, transmission, and reception of the message to rate the degree of technical problems. Perceived risk in the information component (RI) was

#

measured using the mean of four items to assess understanding of the information in the message. Respondents were required to rate the degree of cognitive difficulty encountered by the communication partner(s) to comprehend the message, the importance of the visual and verbal cues exhibited by the communication partner(s) to understand the message, and the importance of the shared background between the sender and receiver(s) to understand the message. Perceived risk in the action component (RA) was measured using the mean of three items relating to the action required by the receiver(s) of the message. Specifically, respondents were required to rate the degree of importance for the communication partners to take action.

A median split of each of the three risk variables was done to divide the communication events into either high-perceived risk or low-perceived risk for each of the three risk scales. For instance, a communication event was classified as having high-perceived RM if the value of the perceived RM for that communication event was higher than the median of the perceived RM. The communication event would be classified as having low perceived RM if the value of the perceived RM was lower than the median. The same applied to perceived RI and perceived RA.

## 5. Discussion

In this study, we first examined communication problems in the workplace and conceptualized perceived risks in communication based on problems encountered by identifying the underlying components of perceived risks in communication from an organizational perspective. Next, we examined perceived risks and their associated active technology-mediated communication behaviors from the lens of STP. We extend STP by moving beyond the public arena and apply it to technologymediated communication behaviors in a business organization. The original conceptualization of STP adopted a narrow perspective of active communication behaviors comprised mainly information acquisition [31]. However, besides information gaining, we also uncovered other types of active technology-mediated communication behaviors with the goal of influencing action and engaging in social interaction as well as their associations with perceived risks in the workplace. Our overall findings suggest that technology-mediated communication behaviors are not only enabled by the use of ICTs but such behaviors also involve appropriate structuring of single as well as multiple ICTs to manage the problems encountered during communication. Indeed, effective and efficient technologymediated communication behaviors in the workplace may depend on how single and multiple ICTs are structured and used in response to perceived risks.

#

Information gaining and action seeking behaviors occurred during situations with high perceived risks. Specifically, we found that information gaining behaviors were evident during high perceived risks in the message and information component. This is not surprising as looking for information is an important communicative function of most ICTs [20]. For high perceived risks in the message component, respondents used multiple ICTs sequentially to seek additional information, provide acknowledgement of message reception, and determine recipients’ availability. The most common ICT combination (telephone/teleconferencing and email) was useful to manage perceived risks in the message component. Used sequentially, email provided detailed information and a followed-up phone call to ask the recipient to check for email ensured that the email was received. For high perceived risks in the information component, information gaining behaviors using concurrent structuring of ICTs were more prevalent. Seeking additional contextual and visual information helped to manage perceived risk. Hence, the information need to acquire contextual information (e.g. background knowledge, acknowledgement) drives information seeking behaviors [30]. For instance, telephone or teleconferencing was combined with other ICTs such as NetMeeting™ or email to provide background or contextual information as a concurrent structural mechanism.

Interestingly, we found that sometimes the information gaining behaviors came in the form of supplying additional information. These were responses to the needs of communication partners for more information rather than their own information needs. This communicative act of supplying additional information is consistent with perspective taking, which refers to the act of taking another person’s viewpoint by adjusting behavior to suit the other person [17]. It is an important organizational communication practice [53]. Even though some ICTs can be structured in a way to enable many concurrent transmissions, such concurrent transmission of information can potentially lead to information overload [27]. We extend past research and show that perspective taking is an important part of active communication behaviors in response to perceived risks in communication. We also show that appropriate structuring of ICTs plays an important role and enables behaviors to facilitate knowledge integration, which is crucial for organizational learning and innovation.

Next, we found that action seeking behavior was often in response to high perceived risks in the action component. Action seeking behavior involved using repetitive structuring mechanisms, such as the repetitive use of a single ICT (e.g. sending an email repeatedly) to manage perceived risks by pressuring the receiver to take relevant required action [60] or to complete organization tasks. Straub & Karahanna [72] found that knowledge workers in organizations are driven to use ICTs to complete tasks. Over time, these individuals develop beliefs about the usefulness of the ICTs as they gain experience with them [37, 78]. This means that employees may use the same structure of ICT use repeatedly (repetitive structuring) to seek task closure [40].

The affordance of the ICT, in particular the persistence of the message, appears to be an important consideration for action seeking individuals during communication. For instance, the permanence of email is an important resource to manage perceived risk in the action component. Email not only allows people to create a written record that helps them avoid blame and disputes [70], but it also enables copies to be sent to third parties (e.g. team leader or manager) [67]. It is apparent that the social context (i.e. software development work setting) of our study plays an important role in the development of such communication behaviors since software development problems and issues typically need to be documented for future reference. This refines STP theory and highlights that social contexts affect problem recognition in the workplace, which ultimately influence technology-mediated communication behaviors.

There are interesting findings for situations with low perceived risks that need to be discussed. First, respondents used fewer ICTs during low perceived risk situations. Specifically, employees prefer

#

to use a single ICT in situations with low risk perceptions since it required less effort compared to using multiple ICTs. We extend Clark and Brennan [8] (1990)’s argument that individuals tend to use the least amount of effort in their choice of media as long as the chances of accomplishing communication goals are not compromised (i.e. low perceived risk situations). This means that when high risk is perceived and chances of accomplishing communication goals are compromised, employees will be goaldriven and willing to expend additional effort required to use multiple ICTs during the communication. Second, very interestingly, our results reveal that FTF communication (without using any ICT) appears to be more prevalent during situations with low risk perceptions. We found that complete FTF communication was seldom used during high perceived risk situations. This could be due to the negative features of FTF communication in the workplace as elaborated by Nardi and Whittaker [46]. They reported that employees tended to avoid FTF communication because such communication was perceived as expensive, had low-productivity, and was not effective in work situations that require documentation. Third, social interaction-seeking behaviors often occur during low perceived risk situations.

Our research highlighted the frequent occurrence and importance of such non-task related interactions in organizations as such behaviors help to forge informal bonds which are essential in the workplace [26]. In addition, the use of instant messaging was important to enable social interaction seeking behavior as it allowed employees to remain in contact outside office hours. This is consistent with recent research by Vrocharidou and Efthymiou [75], who found that instant messaging was perceived by college students to be the most useful application for social purposes. Indeed, the use of technologies to solidfy the feeling of shared values and interests in the workplace, regardless of whether they are worked related, has received more attention lately [26]. With the increasing pervasiveness of social technologies used in the workplace, such social interaction seeking behaviors are likely to become more widespread.

##

The focus of this research is on goal-oriented communication in the workplace and hence we mainly examined active communication behaviors. However, our findings also suggest that passive communication behaviors occur rather frequently in organization communication. In its original formulation, the STP theory considers passive communication behaviors to be situations where communicating members will process information that comes to them randomly but will not actively look for information. We found that employees participated in technology-mediated knowledge sharing sessions as “listeners.” Knowledge sharing sessions were held weekly among members in different locations to share project information with all team members. Employees were not actively seeking information, but the information gained from such sessions was stored in employees’ information inventory for future use. More interestingly, we found that employees were also multitasking when they passively engaged in such interactions. Respondents might participate in a knowledge sharing session as listeners and at the same time they were working on another separate task with other team member(s) on instant messaging [9]. It is clear that the resources afforded by ICT play critical roles to enable such concurrent passive communication behaviors that help employees to attend to different communication needs in parallel. Such behaviors are likely to trigger concerns about whether they will lead to productive and effective outcomes. Research has shown that task complexity increases as people try to multitask, subsequently elevating employees’ stress levels [53].

## 6. Study Limitations and Implications

We realize that the generalizability of our results is limited as this research was conducted in one company. However, since this is one of the first studies aimed at exploring the relationship between perceived risk and use of ICTs to accomplish communication goals, our objective was to eliminate the influence of as many extraneous variables as possible. In addition, we collected data from two sites

#

within the same company. Such a research design allowed us to investigate the communication practices and perceptions of multiple individuals working across different sites but under similar team structures, work procedures, organization policies, and with similar skills. Furthermore, other strengths of our research design such as the real nature of the communication events and the variety of different types of data collected in the communication diary and interviews offsets this limitation. A second limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design. Ideally, such research should be conducted by using longitudinal designs with time lags appropriate to the variables involved to better capture the contextual elements of ICT usage behaviors and perception of the communication [33]. This provides better understanding of the complete communication practices in actual work settings. Lastly, this study did not make use of published scales to measure the perceived communication risks variables because available scales were not applicable to the context of perceived risks in this research. However, when developing the scales, we paid careful attention to existing theories and related constructs reviewed in past studies (as shown in Table 1). Moreover, we tested the validity and reliability of the perceived risks scales and they exhibited acceptable psychometric values.

As the use of ICTs has become more prevalent in organizations, improving organizational communication is extremely valuable [7]. Hence, this research has important implications for managers and workers in the workplace. First, understanding technology mediated communication behaviors will help managers better understand the nature of ICT use to enable organizational communication behavior. Although there is an abundance of research on single ICT selection and use in organizations, very little research has examined how employees cope with multiple ICTs in the workplace. By focusing on the plethora of ubiquitous ICT in a distributed workplace, this study provides insights on how organization members use single and multiple ICTs to manage communication problems. The use of STP allowed us to focus on the communication behaviors employed in response to different types of

#

risk. We chose to investigate a setting where communication issues are crucial to success and there is a significant amount of communication risk, i.e., successful development of software where employees of a customer organization in the US determine the requirements but the software is actually built by employees of a vendor organization in India. Thus, employees faced high levels of risk in all components of the communication process. However, in other organizations, some aspects of risk may be more salient than others. Our study provides the basis for managers to diagnose a given situation and organize communication strategies to minimize identified risks. For example, when the situation is perceived to have a high risk of failure at the information level, managers may want to provide employees with communication strategies and access to technologies that can provide visual and contextual information. These technologies and the development of such strategies may be less important in a situation where there is little risk in understanding but high risk at the message component.

Second, this study enhances our understanding of active communication behaviors (i.e. information gaining, action seeking, and social interaction seeking) in organizations and in terms of how ICT can be used to enable these communication behaviors. This will help managers formulate a strategic view of the choices of technology for communication so that firms can invest in advanced communication technologies as well as providing employees with suitable strategies for using these technologies in combination. Next, past research has emphasized that employees should be equipped with “ICT-based” communication competence during ICT-mediated communication [e.g. 69]. ICT-mediated communication in the workplace requires unique communication competence that is essential for ICTenabled communication behaviors. Specifically, to accomplish communication goals in an environment where multiple ICTs are available for use, the ability to perceive different types of risk in the different communication components (message, information, action) and adapt appropriate communication behaviors in response to the risks perceived can be viewed as communication strategies that can be practiced and mastered. Organizations have to ensure relevant training to help employees develop their perceptual ability and adaptive communication competence so that communication failures can be minimized.
