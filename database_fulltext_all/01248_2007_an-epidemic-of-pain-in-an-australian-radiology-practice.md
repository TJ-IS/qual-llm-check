# An epidemic of pain in an Australian radiology practice

Jeanette Van Akkeren<sup>1</sup> and Bruce Rowlands<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of the Sunshine Coast, Queensland, Australia; <sup>2</sup>Griffith University, Brisbane, Australia

Correspondence: Jeanette Van Akkeren, University of the Sunshine Coast, Queensland, Australia. Tel: þ 6 175 499 7226; E-mail: jvanakke@usc.edu.au

## Abstract

This paper presents findings from a study of an organisationally mandated assimilation process of an enterprise-wide information system in a radiology practice in Australia. A number of interviews with radiologists, radiographers and administrative staff are used to explore the impact of institutional structures on the assimilation process. The case study develops an argument that culture within and outside the Australian Radiology Practice (ARP), social structures within the ARP and organisational-level management mandates have impacted on the assimilation process. The study develops a theoretical framework that integrates elements of social actor theory (Lamb & Kling, 2003) to provide a more fine-grained analysis concentrating on the relationships among the radiology practitioners, the technology (an enterprise-wide Health Information System), and a larger social milieu surrounding its use. This study offers several theoretical and practical implications for technology assimilation in the health and radiology industry regarding the important roles social interactions, individual self-perceptions, organisational mandates and policies can play in assimilating new ICTs. ## Introduction

Health Informatics is a relatively under-researched area, needing in-depth analysis and reporting given that the healthcare sector has lagged in IT adoption (Wilson & Sloane, 2007). In a recent empirical study on the adoption of electronic medical records (EMRs) by physicians in the U.S.A., Audet et al. (2004) found that most physicians do not use EMRs and related technologies and report that adoption is uneven. This paper addresses barriers to health information system (HIS) use and an existing lack of research in healthcare organisations by studying the Information and Communication Technology (ICT) assimilation process in a large geographically dispersed Australian radiology practice – hereafter known as ARP. The assimilation process described is one of non-voluntary adoption where authorities made the initial decision to adopt a new HIS and mandated its use. Targeted users (in this case radiology practitioners) had few alternatives but to adopt the innovation and make the necessary adjustments for using it to perform their jobs. Theories of technology assimilation (Gallivan, 2001) suggest that a variety of institutional, social, and political factors blend together in influencing the extent to which complex ICTs are actually assimilated in practice. This research addresses a significant issue: what social and institutional forces influence the assimilation of information systems (IS) in the healthcare industry? Given that healthcare provider organisations have long been lagging adopters of ICTs, this is an important issue.

In this paper we define ICTs as encompassing all those technologies that enable the handling of information and facilitate different forms of communication among human actors, between human actors and electronic systems, and among electronic systems. We rely on the definition of assimilation as the extent to which the use of the technology diffuses across the organisation, including work processes, and becomes routinised in the activities of those processes (Purvis et al., 2001). We then extend this definition of assimilation to refer to the success achieved by firms in utilising the capabilities of ICTs to enhance business performance. Not only does assimilation refer to the extent to which an ICT has been infused into specific business activities, but also how effectively the ICT is in enabling the conduct of those activities relative to rivals (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999). In terms of the ARP in this research, the enhancement of business performance as well as trying to remain competitive were particularly crucial.

Findings based on in-depth interviews with radiology practitioners are reported that demonstrate both the enablers and inhibitors to the assimilation process. According to Klecun & Cronford (2005), many contemporary HIS are complex and aim to be both technologically and organisationally innovative, involving a large number of different stakeholders, spanning institutions and professions, and having the potential to change the way people and organisations work and how services are delivered and experienced. Our research supports that view with the aims of providing both theoretical and practical contributions to the ICT assimilation process and identifying the issues that have enabled and/or hindered the process and subsequent use of a complex HIS. This paper reports how radiology practitioners assimilate an HIS, with a focus on describing how assimilation is bound up in everyday social and organisational structures.

Previous research has tended to focus on the features of the ICT and individuals’ behaviours while under-emphasising the role of context and institutional structures, for example, see Davis (1989), Ajzen (1991) and Taylor & Todd (1995). Among the many studies of ICT assimilation in the health literature, few pay attention to the role of context, social, institutional and organisational structures embedded in the ICT (with the notable exceptions of Davidson & Heslinga, 2007; Jensen & Aanestad, 2007; and Sallas et al., 2007). In this paper, we will argue that it is the policies and practices embedded in the HIS that constitute the structural exercise of authority. Also, it is the constraints based around the accepted, contested and everyday use of the IS by radiology workers that obviate the need for more overt or direct forms of authority.

The healthcare industry has begun to make major investments in IT, and although expectations and enthusiasm may be high, there are well-recognised economic, institutional, and technical impediments to widespread and effective use of ICT in healthcare that remain poorly understood (Garabaldi, 1998; Bodenheimer & Grumbach, 2003; Davidson & Heslinga, 2007). In addition, little is known about why some medical practices have adopted and implemented HISs successfully, whereas many others have not (Miller, 2003). Kaplan (1998) posits that for evaluating the assimilation of ICTs to support a learning process, and in order to improve future outcomes in HIS, it is not sufficient to evaluate around isolated criteria but rather it is necessary to study the complex processes that contribute to outcomes and to consider relationships between system characteristics, individual characteristics, organisational characteristics, and interactions among these characteristics. Such approaches incorporate wider, macro-level institutional contexts that surround an organisation and explain how that may impact on the HIS assimilation process. As Klecun & Cronford (2005) suggest, there still exists a clear division between the formalist or rational functionalist tradition in both the IS and health informatics disciplines. Further, there appears to be little direct engagement between those doing more traditional evaluation of HIS assimilation and those developing the new approaches. This research seeks to bridge that divide and provide a broader, more holistic approach to understanding the effects of enabling and constraining constructs and contexts on the HIS assimilation process.

Orlikowski & Iacono (2001) argue that most IS literature treats IS as separable from the social and organisational contexts in which they are instantiated, appropriated, and enacted. When ICTs are viewed as technical artefacts that are relatively stable, discrete and fixed, it is easy to view the use of an ICT as an independent event. This view encourages the belief that institutional structures have little to do with the assimilation of an ICT in the workplace. Technocentric views of the systems assimilation processes omit the role of human agents and the existing organisational and social contexts in the assimilation of ICT artefacts. However, Walsham (1993) argues that political, cultural and organisational aspects must be seen as necessarily playing a major role in shaping the evolution activity of the assimilation process. Our research supports Walsham’s view that it is the roles of the human actors, the interplay between the organisation, the ICT, the social and political contexts and the broader institutional factors that will ultimately impact on the ICT assimilation process.

Issues of alignment with business goals and institutional interests, understanding of existing work practices in formal and informal senses, and the diverse power bases and competing stakeholder groups and their information needs, have all been given attention in the IS field (Barley, 1986; Symons, 1991; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998). Our research supports this view and further suggests that it is the social, cultural and organisational contexts that will ultimately have the greatest effect on the acceptance and use of an IS. Previous research in HIS has not had this focus and we argue, therefore, that an institutional focus is a preferred lens to study assimilation as it takes a more holistic approach by looking at the organisation and the environment surrounding the organisation.

The case study reported in this paper develops the argument that institutional forces embedded within HIS are active forces in the systems assimilation process. The influence of the radiography systems occurs both through the material constraints such as intranet technology, prescriptions of process such as patient validation mandated by the system, and through the experiences and learning from previous use of the system that shapes workers’ approaches to using the ICT in their workplace. This case study also attempts to contribute to our understanding of ICT assimilation by exploring the role of institutional structures such as government requirements and industry pressures in the assimilation of health ICTs by radiology practitioners.

As already reported, the assimilation of HIS is a topic of significant research attention within IS at the organisational level and the importance of the success or otherwise of the assimilation process persists as an issue of concern in HIS (Heeks, 2006; Wilson & Sloane, 2007). Heeks (2006) posits that both the conceptualisation and the evidence base for HIS success and failure are weak, adding that the failure rates for HIS are in line with general IS surveys. Further, Heeks (2006) argues that there is a need for the conceptualisation of a model that future researchers may use to gain a greater understanding of the HIS phenomena. The significance of this research is that it further develops a framework to understand the enablers and constrainers of the ICT assimilation process in the HIS industry. Lamb & Kling’s (2003) social actor model is the chosen framework for this research as it takes into account the role of the social actor and the contexts and constructs at various levels (individual, workplace, organisational, and industry) that enable or inhibit assimilation and further use of an ICT.

This paper addresses two research questions: (1) What are the enablers and inhibitors in the assimilation of a new ICT in radiology practices; and (2) Do the four dimensions of the social actor model illuminate the micro- and macro-level constructs and contexts that impact the assimilation process in the healthcare industry? Insight with respect to these questions will be derived from an interpretive case study of the assimilation of a HIS in a large-sized Australian company that encompasses numerous metropolitan and provincial radiology practice units. Each practice unit consists of between 10 and 35 radiology workers. The unit of analysis for the case study were radiology workers at various levels including radiologists, radiographers, practice managers, senior executives and administrative staff, all of whom had at least 2 years experience in the company. A total of 23 interviews were conducted with informants at varying levels within the organisation.

This research is important for the following three reasons. First, it takes a more holistic approach than previously applied by incorporating institutional, social and individual level analyses to gain a greater understanding of the reasons behind the often high failure rate of ICT assimilation (Delone & McLean, 1992; Raheb, 1992; Fichman, 2000; Zhu & Kraemer, 2005). Second, it provides an alternative approach to previous research that has tended to focus on technical issues, largely ignoring other factors that can influence acceptance of a new IS. Raheb (1992) and Martinsons & Chong (1999) argue that more research into ICT assimilation and use is needed to provide a better understanding of the contexts that work for or against actors and the organisation during the ICT assimilation process. Third, it provides an alternative approach to the broader IS acceptance literature, which is dominated by individualistic theories of human behaviour, for example, Diffusion of Innovation Theory (Rogers, 1995), the Theory of Reasoned Action (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980), the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis et al., 1989), and the Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen, 1991; Taylor & Todd, 1995). In contrast, this case adopted an analytical perspective (Kling et al., 2005) in that the intent was to contribute towards developing socio-organisational theory about ICTs in institutional and cultural contexts. The approach to theory building, with an emphasis on concept development and operationalisation made use of the social actor model to illuminate an under-researched aspect of HIS practice – the context of HIS assimilation within a large ARP. Based on the findings of a detailed case analysis, this paper details a fresh approach for understanding HIS assimilation across multiple levels by conceptualising the radiology practitioner as a social actor who engages in multiple uses of the HIS in an organisational context.

The research approach makes use of the social actor model by illuminating an aspect of IS practice – HIS assimilation – at four levels: individual, project, organisational, and industry. This novel theoretical approach, when combined with in-depth empirical research, extends our current understanding of contemporary ICT assimilation. The paper also operationalises the social actor model as first demonstrated in Rowlands (2006a) in terms of mapping of the case interview text to the model’s 16 constructs. These are important findings as the social actor model is relatively new. The research provides empirical support for modelling radiology practitioners, not as users of a HIS, but as social beings who are engaging uses of the HIS, while embedded within an enabling and constraining social context.

The following section overviews the case and describes the conduct of the study, followed by the case findings and analysis. The remaining sections provide a closing discussion and conclusion.

## Theoretical framework

Our attempt to understand the assimilation of an inhouse developed HIS in a large-scale organisation made us aware of a need for theory and analytical framework that addresses issues of the technological artefact, the role that actors play in enacting the technology, and at different levels of analysis. Social informatics research (Kling et al., 2005) has devoted attention to how information technologies enter into action. One of the most prominent lines of work is research on conceptualising the user as a social actor (Lamb & Kling, 2003; Lamb, 2006). Drawing on the work of Scott (1995), Lamb (2006) describes how the social actor concept has been theoretically supported by institutionalist approaches, whereby institutions provide a framing context within which social actors make constrained choices about ICT use, particularly when they are situated in organisations. Scott (1995, p. 60) describes how institutional elements are carried or reproduced through culture, social structures, routines, rules, regulations and perhaps also by technologies. According to the social actor model, people’s individual autonomy and their behaviours are shaped by the social norms, institutional forces (routine work practices), and the social and physical structures that surround them. This approach reflects what Orlikowski & Iacono (2001) identify as the ‘ensemble view’ of technology where ICTs are components of a more complex sociotechnical ensemble that includes people, work practices, and institutional and cultural factors.

## Social actor mode

The case analysis applied the social actor model as a conceptual lens to understand HIS assimilation within a single organisational setting. The primary strength of this model is that it is context-centred, integrates people’s interactions, their information environments, and their technologies as the basic unit of research analysis (Lamb, 2006). In terms of our case study, social actors can be professional individuals holding university qualifications (radiologists, e.g., are highly specialised physicians performing the role of diagnosis, intervening radiology, etc.), radiographers and sonographers (operators of highly technical imaging equipment), groups of firm members acting in concert in administrative and/or management roles, or organisations, such as ARP, interacting with industry regulators or industry standards (Medicare Australia and the Australian and New Zealand Radiology Institute).

To illuminate the cultural, social, organisational and institutional contexts that impact on the ICT assimilation process, we provide Lamb’s (2003, p. 14) general description of social actor model definitions. Affiliations of social actors are networked, exchange-related, multiple and changing. Environments are technical, institutional, ICTenhanced, and expansive. Interactions are (mostly) legitimate, action-enabling, constructed and role-based. And finally, and perhaps most important to our study of ICT assimilation, social actors continually improvise their uses of ICT and reconfigure their organisational roles (identities) to reconstruct and represent themselves as competent, ICT-savvy social actors. In sum, social actors are not just ‘users’ of ICTs – they effectively redesign ICTs in use (Lamb, 2005, p. 288).

## The research site

The research study was carried out in a large ARP. The health services sector was chosen because of the extremely important role that ICT plays in the operation and success of businesses in this industry, and the ARP selected has extensive experience and use in practice of an enterprise-wide HIS. Importantly, the radiology industry is highly technical, highly competitive, as well as being regulated and institutionalised. Therefore the ARP provides a rich case with which to examine key components of the social actor model. The selection of the case site was based on a combination of accessibility to the company’s owner(s) and practice units, and interestingness – in the sense that the ARP is one of Queensland’s top three radiology firms, and its IT department is considered to be a leading player in providing state-of-the-art HIS solutions to patients.

## Data source

The unit of analysis for the case study comprised radiologists, radiographers, administrative workers and management. The period spent in the field totalled 20 months and used multiple techniques of data collection. However, in the researcher’s role as a non-participant outside observer, the semi-structured face-to-face interview was chosen as the primary data source with a variety of practice employees. All semi-structured interviews were guided by an interview protocol with slight changes made to each occupational group as needed. Interviews with participants dealt with the following issues: reasons or motives for using the HIS; the conditions that shaped their use; major issues with the HIS; the assimilation process itself; and the nature of their relationships with persons whom they work with across the organisation. The average length of each interview was approximately 1 h. Interviews were taped. A total of 23 interviews were conducted across eight different sites and at varying levels within the organisation. Four of the sites were metropolitan; the other four were provincial practices. Nine interviews were carried out in the first round in July 2006 and a further 14 interviews in the second round in November and December 2006.

## Data analysis

The multi-dimensional concept of the social actor model was used as a theoretical lens to draw meaning from the interview data and guided our data analysis. It helped make sense of what occurred in the field, provided a set of constructs to be investigated, and guided the interpretation and focus. For example, it was used both to organise and classify the data, but also to identify the assumptions and perceptions of the technology that the different stakeholders had of the HIS under investigation. Our interpretation and analysis of the data then enabled us to identify the issues that best described the HIS assimilation process in radiology practices, highlighting those factors that enabled and those that inhibited the assimilation process.

The analysis of transcripts involved three phases and five steps. The analytical techniques adopted during the research design are explained below, and follow an approach to analysis reported in Rowlands (2006b). Phase 1 Early Steps in Analysis involved steps 1 and 2. Phase 2 Within Case Analysis involved steps 3 and 4. Phase 3 Cross-Case Analysis involved step 5, combining the qualitative responses into narratives or decision ‘stories’ in answer to the research questions.

Step 1: Interpretive translation of the original model. In this step, the 16 characteristics and behaviours of connected and situated individuals (as described in column 2 of Tables 2–5) were interpreted by the authors and translated to an equivalent meaning in terms of the case scenario (as described in column 3 of Tables 2–5). Through a process of iteration and revision, we agreed on an interpretation taken from the interview transcripts that we thought best fitted the meaning of the original social actor construct. This process of interpretation and translation of the original constructs to a match in the transcripts continued for the entire 16 behaviours of the connected and situated individual.

Step 2: Assigning codes. Based on definitions from the social actor model, codes were assigned or operationalised to match the examples from the case (c.f. Table 2, column 1). By operationalisation, we mean that the social actor model was expressed in coded elements that could be identified in textual analysis. In this paper, a code is defined as an abbreviation or symbol applied to a segment of words (e.g. a sentence or paragraph) that captures the description of the phenomenon.

Step 3: Coding. A systematic way of handling the volumes of rich text was through coding. Once data were collected, each of the interviews were imported into Nvivo7 – qualitative text analysis software, and coded accordingly. Initially, 16 codes (described in column 1 of Table 1) were drawn from the social actor model. However, as data analysis continued, a new code emerged under the identities dimension that did not fit neatly into the original social actor model. Consequently, [IDN-DEMO] was added to describe the demographics of individual practice workers, including age, geographic location of practice units, and gender.

Step 4: Managing the data. Data were recorded in an Nvivo7 database, which was then used to assist subsequent analysis. Procedures included importing transcribed interviews, coding, adding comments and reflections, sorting the interpretations by code, and retrieving text of selected instances into the body of the research report. This same format was carried through the entire 23 interviews.

Step 5: Writing the narrative. The last step in analysis involves presenting a narrative that integrates interviewee accounts taken from the entire case study database, guided by the theoretical lens offered by the social actor model. In this narrative (the cross-case analysis to follow), we present informed perspectives in a coherent and convincing story. In our interpretation it was not our intention to find ‘one true voice’ but rather to understand the processes and patterns that manifest themselves within multiple interpretations.

## Cross-case analysis

In this section, we first describe the institutional context surrounding the assimilation of the HIS, and a description of the HIS itself. Then we describe in turn the four dimensions of the social actor model with illustrative quotations from the ARP personnel.

## Institutional context

ARP is large in terms of Australian company size. It comprises two major business arms: radiology practices and aged-care facilities. The company is publicly listed and answerable to its shareholders. The Board of Directors is made up of radiologists, company executives and senior accountants. In addition to the radiology practices and aged care facilities, the company also has a centralised IT department. There are 32 practice units in Queensland alone, and a further 40 practice units throughout Australia and the Asia-Pacific region. ARP is head quartered in a major Australian capital city and practice units are spread throughout a number of metropolitan suburbs and in provincial (rural) areas.

The role of workers is highly status oriented, indicating a hierarchical culture within the company. For example, the radiologists are held in high esteem by most others in each of the practice units. Many employees have been with ARP for over 10 years, and have a long history with the company. However, there is a growing shortage of fully trained and experienced radiologists, radiographers and administrative staff, and competition among the industry for these skills is growing – a clear example of macro-level, environmental factors impacting at the organisation level.

The type of HIS under focus can be categorised as an enterprise-wide application. The system captures patient transactions from the moment they walk into a practice unit, until their procedure is completed and the account settled. The HIS was also designed to meet requirements of corporate accounting, statutory reporting requirements, as well as local applications such as reporting on films completed at each practice, and staff rosters. All practice units are linked so that radiologists are able to source patient histories regardless of where a previous procedure was completed. Development time for the new system was approximately 2 years, and the implementation of the new system was spread over a 6-month period across all practices. The system was designed and developed by external software developers specifically for the ARP. At the time of interviews, most practice units had been using the HIS for one-and-a-half to two years. The HIS was implemented as a straight cut-over. That is, on Friday, the old legacy system ceased operation to enable the new HIS to be in operation throughout practice units the following Monday morning. Most radiology administrative workers were only trained for a few hours over the weekend, radiographers were given one evenings’ training the previous week, and radiologists were trained during the first week of operation while continuing to diagnose patient images.

## The analysis

The social actor model involves four dimensions: affiliations, environment, interactions and identities. We propose that each dimension characterises organisational members and their HIS assimilation context. According to Lamb (2006), affiliations and environments relate people to their organisation, and to the industries and environments of those organisations. The other two dimensions – interactions and identities – relate organisationally situated individuals to others and to the technologies they use to interact with and present themselves to others. We use this multi-dimensional conceptualisation of the social actor to guide our qualitative analysis of interview transcripts.

Affiliations According to Lamb (2006), networks are a basic configuration for organising social, economic and political exchanges. The affiliations dimension in Table 2 drew attention to the interaction practices migrating within ARP and across organisations. On a network level, as relationships change and new social actors are enrolled, interaction practices migrate within and across organisations. For example, radiology staff regularly work with, or report to, external organisations when an aspect of their work is mandated to move outside the organisation, such as reporting to Medicare Australia, a Federal Government agency that provides financial assistance to patients. Radiology workers also liaise within the parent organisation and with external business clients (referring GPs and patients). These interactions bring about change such as patient reports being sent directly and more efficiently to referring GPs [AFF-RELAT]. Thus, interactions shaped how the HIS was assimilated and, in particular, how the system evolved over time. The differences in work practices that were introduced into ARP, such as doctors having to edit their own patient reports or administrators being able to work from home [AFF-NETWORKS], created challenges and opportunities for radiology workers. As two administrators explained:

When it first happened I had people clambering ‘when can I type from home, when can I type from home?’ Well everybody wants to type at home but we have to have some people here! [AFF-NETWORKS].

That’s another advantage [of the system], the typing of reports. I sit at home and type reports sometimes, via the internet. People who work at the branch are logged in and have access to all the branches [AFF-NETWORKS].

However, many affiliate organisations employed an HIS that was not necessarily compatible with the new system, and at times this resulted in problems in the practice units. Two administrators and two radiologists provide examples of the types of issues and challenges that were introduced with the new HIS. Some changes to work practices and affiliate relationships inhibited the process; others helped to enable the process [AFF-RELAT].

As I see it [the system] is actually more of a pain in the bum, because it won’t highlight when you put in illegal entries so it’s certainly not helping us comply with Medicare regulations in that respect [AFF-NETWORKS].

It is accessible for the governmentyMedicare forms are all entered on here – electronically downloaded to Medicare so they can check and see what’s going on which makes it easier all round [AFF-EXCH].

It’s a frustrating thing y it’s more frustration than anything, especially in a practice like this that we’re trying to build up and we’re finding that GPs are sending elsewhere because we can’t get their [patient] reports through [AFF-EXCH and AFF-MULTI].

There are a lot of problems with the report that comes off this system. The reports are meant to be automatically emailed to the doctor three times a dayybig problems with thatythey’re not getting anything. They disappear into the ether! [AFF-EXCH].

The affiliation dimension of the framework highlighted substantial changes to work practices with clients (GPs and patients) and with government organisations that both enabled and inhibited the assimilation process. In particular, although the automatic emailing of reports to doctors was seen as a positive aspect of the new HIS, the unresolved technical problem of reports not getting through from time to time created negative attitudes to the HIS in four of the eight practice units. Similarly, interactions with government affiliates were also improved through online access, yet faults with the system caused headaches for administrative workers.

Environment By focussing outside the organisation, the environment dimension in Table 3 draws attention to stabilised and institutionalised practices that take place within ARP. According to the social actor model, the environment exerts technical and institutional practices (standards) on radiology workers and on each individual practice. Lamb (2006) asserts that the nature of the environment an organisation operates in greatly affects assimilation of ICTs, and cites examples like quality management practices that can be coerced through regulatory agencies, professional codes of ethics, laws, and industry-wide practices. For example, for ARP to gain quality standard certification and industry acceptance, it would need to demonstrate that certain standard processes [ENV-MAND] were in place such as reporting correctly to Australian government bodies, such as Medicare. Also, referring GPs requires fast and accurate patient reports from the radiology practices, and puts pressure on practices to provide this service. In fact, if a practice is not providing reports in a timely manner, GPs are very quick to refer patients to competitors. Also, patients do not like to be kept waiting as many are unwell, in pain, or have attended ARP during work hours and are under their own time constraints. Hence, external bodies and expectations by industry partners and clients exert pressure on individual practices to comply with their needs [ENV-MAND]. These environmental pressures can provide enabling contexts for the assimilation of an HIS that provides the technological innovations required to meet these needs. However, for the cases in this research, technological problems, infrastructural issues [ENV-INFRA] and lack of training [ENV-CAPITAL] have all contributed to inhibiting the assimilation of the HIS.

Resistance [to the system] has been very high as we were given lousy computers. We were given the old ones. I’ve got another patient waiting for me and the bottom line is the patients have to be processed as quickly as we can [ENV-CAPTIAL].

There are a few issues of [the system’s] design that are slow for us, the radiologists, but I think one of the major issues is our network bandwidth into our practices which makes us slower [ENV-INFRA].

The only thing that’s wrong with [the system] is the Internet-based part as when it slows down it really irritates the doctors and everybody because you can’t do anything [ENV-INFRA].

The training was inadequate for staff and we were getting into situations where we had to try and sort problems and we were even taught the wrong way at times [by the external developers] [ENV-CAPITAL].

The code [ENV-MAND] was by far the most commonly noted in Table 3, indicating that all interviewees understood that the adoption of the HIS was mandatory, provided standard processes for patient visits and the collection of invoicing data required by ARP. The majority of participants viewed the HIS as a coordinating, controlling business process mechanism by the controlling body – senior executives of ARP. Therefore, although adopting the HIS was mandatory, the assimilation of the new system was determined by the social actors in each practice unit and impacted by the context and many constructs that worked either for or against the acceptance of the HIS. All business processes within each of the practice units had to adhere to the new system, and the HIS coordinates activities, regulates outcomes, and imposes standards [ENV-MAND] in each practice, and for each individual. According to the social actor model, the environment and adherence to industry-wide work practices exert technological and institutional standards on each of practice units and played a significant guiding role in having the system developed in the first place. As a radiographer explained:

Basically, I think it [the system] gave us a little bit more movement in the corporate world so that we could actually play on a level field with the opposition because they were linked a long time ago [ENV-MAND].

In addition to linking practices and having an online presence, practices were also provided with up-to-date technologies that were more in line with competitors and external technological innovations.

[The new HIS] uses bar codes and all those things, and I think it probably has made us more efficient [ENV-INFRA].

The parent organisation also requires individual practices to convey a professional and ‘slick’ image to referring GPs and patients [ENV-POLICY], and part of the reason for developing the system was to improve that image and remain competitive for referrals and attractive to patients. In this sense, the implementation of the HIS has met the needs of ARP, as one senior administrator observed:

If a good referrer suddenly drops off, [the HIS] rings an alarm bell, so off [the senior radiographer] goes and visits and takes a little mud cake or something and has a chat and oh, you know ‘I notice you haven’t been referring, what’s been the problem?’. Yeah, here’s the mud cake you know, it’s not a bribe, it’s morning tea [ENV-POLICY].

However, participants also recognised that meeting policy guidelines of the parent organisation and the expectations of industry colleagues could only work if the technology supported them. For this reason, this construct also became an inhibitor in half of the practice units. As a radiologist explained:

You should speak to [name given] as he goes around and deals with the doctors and a lot of them were referring elsewhere because the reports just weren’t going through [ENV-POLICY].

The environment dimension of the framework illuminated contexts and constructs that both enabled and inhibited the assimilation process in each of the cases. Without exception, the most inhibiting constructs were the lack of education and training by the parent organisation [ENV-CAPITAL] and the lack of infrastructural richness of the system [ENV-INFRA]. Enabling constructs included the industry standards expected in radiology practices [ENV-MAND] and the requirements by the parent organisation and other external bodies for practices to meet those standards [ENV-POLICY].

Interactions According to the social actor model, individuals are involved in networks that take shape within and among organisations. Networking refers to the interactions where organisational members work and interact with others (affiliates) using an HIS (and other media) in support of their interactions.

At senior management level, the HIS is seen as a vehicle to bring together radiology workers and coordinate their tasks when interacting with clients, industry bodies and government agencies. The interaction dimension was a prominent context raised in the interviews (see Table 4) as it describes radiology workers in their day-to-day work role networking and relating themselves to others and to the HIS they use to interact with and present themselves to others (their Identities – see Table 5).

According to the social actor model, organisational members seek to communicate in legitimate ways. In its most visible form, the HIS mandates validation throughout all phases of the patient process and requires all levels of radiology workers to complete their tasks and then ‘validate’ the patient before the patient can be processed at the next level [INT\_PROCESS]. The HIS in this sense is seen to facilitate patient coordination and is a mechanism to review progress (how long does a patient have to complete their visit), as expressed by a practice manager. However, this process is often stymied as the streamlining purpose of the HIS was never explained to the radiology workers, as one senior administrator and one middlelevel administrator described:

My biggest beef is lack of training. When you have a patient and they [radiographers, radiologists] aren’t ‘validating’ at the end of their process, the girls out the front can’t do an account or anything until the radiographer or radiologist completes. Again, it comes back to [lack of] training [INT-CONNECT] [INT-PROCESS].

The problem is that there has not been enough crosspollination between the technical people and the office staff – they [technical people] don’t fully understand the impact if they don’t complete the validation [INT-CONNECT] [INT-PROCESS].

In terms of producing documentation and validation processes, participants at every practice commented that the HIS was extremely unreliable – either it was ‘freezing’, ‘going slow’ or not printing the required documentation to complete the process or allow them to do their job properly. Interactions with the parent organisation to fix these problems were generally ignored, and the one IT person who was responsible for maintaining all systems across 22 practices simply could not keep up. One radiologist said that doctors would often forget to validate their diagnosis resulting in a patient not receiving their report and films. Also, at three of the eight practices, administrators commented that data residing in the system was unavailable to them [INT-PROCESS], which impacted negatively on their particular role [INT-PERCEP], and consequently impacted negatively on assimilation.

It’s working for me – for the things I need to do, but I would like to do more. I just want better access to that data [INT-PROCESS].

Yeah, it would crash 3–4 times a day. We had to reboot the computers and everything would stop and freeze and we couldn’t access or report stuff. Everything came to a great big grinding halt [INT-PROCESS].

However, there were also positive aspects to the system that helped improve acceptance of the HIS by many radiology workers, in particular, the administrative staff in six of the eight practice units had a more positive attitude to the new system [INT-CONNECT]. As a senior administrator explained:

I can access all branches without having to physically go there. I can just see what their workload is, what’s going on. Also, at the end of the week I can download a financial report that tells me exactly what branch did what. In this way, [the HIS is] brilliant, brilliant, they are fantastic [INT-CONNECT].

Interactions, like identities, were prominent issues raised throughout the assimilation process and provided valuable insights into many negative issues that inhibited the process for radiology workers. All participants, with the exception of one radiographer, recognised the value of the system in providing a more streamlined approach to patient care and reporting, and most administrators reported improvements in accessing information. However, a great deal of negativity was prominent in the views of all participants in relation to the loss of identity (appearing incompetent) and a lessening of quality interactions due to inherent management, cultural and technological issues that were not dealt with by management. The assimilation process also highlighted an organisational level cultural issue where different user groups within a practice were treated differently in terms of training on the system and on their requests for change to the system. As one radiographer perceived, ‘They [the parent organisation] just don’t care’. The following two participants support this perception (radiologist and a radiographer).

Every month we asked for changes [to the system] and then after a certain period of time those changes stopped happening so we were left with this half completed product [INT-SOCIAL].

We wanted changes to be made and we just got stonewalled and they never got done [INT-SOCIAL].

In brief, the attitude of a majority of participants (mentioned at six of the eight practices) was that the parent organisation conveyed a lack of trust in radiology employees to work to their greatest capacity, and in addition, a perception that the parent organisation was not concerned with the many technological issues and the insufficient training that many practices were crying out for [INT-SOCIAL]. The following two quotes are examples of perceptions of radiology workers; the first is a radiographer and the second an administrator.

A lot of people said to me that it was [designed] so that Big Brother could see exactly what we were doing [INT-SOCIAL].

It was introduced for streamlining [processes] but also for checking up on us of course. They can now trace who’s doing what, when and where. There’s a lot of that and I keep getting told ‘Why aren’t you doing this, or why haven’t you done that’. It’s like Big Brother is watching us [INT-SOCIAL].

Overall, the perception of being watched had a substantial negative impact on the acceptance of the system as radiology workers felt that they were not trusted and in some cases, not important enough to help in dealing with technical problems. The ability to interact within ARP and externally with affiliates was often problematic, and that, coupled with a belief that there was a lack of care from the parent organisation, resulted in very negative perceptions of the HIS. This will be discussed further in the Conclusion section.

Identities The social actor model holds that identities are among the ways social actors perceive themselves and present themselves to others. Table 5 demonstrates the codes that were applied to the identity constructs and provides a brief description of how these related to radiology workers, including the new code that emerged from the data – [IDN-DEMO].

The HIS conveys a sense of the social actors’ and the firm’s technological competency and helps to convey perceptions within and outside their environment and with their affiliates, such as referring general practitioners (GPs) and patients. Within practice units it became quickly apparent that the identities of the various radiology workers were closely linked to the HIS during the assimilation process, continuing to the present day. In the majority of interviews, feelings of inadequacy and frustration were described by participants due to the difficulty in using the system. Because of their inability to work with the new system, radiology workers suffered from feelings of incompetence which manifested in frustration and ultimately, anger, as one radiologist reported:

[The radiologists] kicked walls, putting holes in the walls. There was the expense of sending out a tradesman to fill the holes in the wall. There have been two of those. Um cursing, swearing, there’s probably frustration and anger, we haven’t had any examples of where it has affected reporting, I think, but I am sure that it probably could potentially, but basically grumpiness of the radiologists was a big issue. The administrative staff just cried [with frustration at using the new system] [IDN-PERCEP].

In addition, the frustration led to radiographers and administrative staff at seven of the eight practices leaving and never returning. As one radiographer explained:

It initially caused a lot of grief with people because of not understanding the systemyand you know, rather than adapting to the system a lot of people attempted to adapt the system to them. When that didn’t work, they left! [IDN-PERCEP].

Another component of identities is that of changing roles of the participant (IDN-ROLES). Administrators and radiologists all reported changes to their work practices and roles due to the implementation of the system – roles that, without exception, participants were not prepared for. As one administrator stated emphatically:

[It makes me feely]Frustrated y because basically I have to help other regions as well. I’m tutoring people on the system, chasing radiographers to complete their part, fixing problems and I still have to do my [administrative] work [IDN-ROLES].

Radiographers were particularly annoyed at having to complete administrative tasks when the new system was implemented – tasks that they were not previously expected to do [IDN-ROLES]. Since the introduction of the system, many radiographers still continue to resist completing these tasks, as they feel it should not be their responsibility to do so [IDN-USER]. The new tasks impact on all other radiology workers within a practice unit as it interrupts the work flow, delaying patients from moving speedily through the radiology process and allowing the doctors to efficiently complete their diagnosis [IDN\_PER-CEP]. All but one of the radiologists had similar attitudes to their changing job role due to the introduction of the HIS, as a radiologist and an administrator commented:

We shouldn’t have to deal with this crap. Our role is to diagnose patients, not secretarial work where we have to edit the typists’ work, or validate patients. Conservatively, our productivity dropped by about 50% during the implementation and for months after [IDN-USER, IDN-PERCEP].

They [radiologists] felt that they were suddenly becoming typists because they had to make their own corrections [IDN-ROLES].

A key aspect of identities during the assimilation process was the negative self-perceptions from actors’ use of the technology in terms of task competency and their changing job roles. The level of frustration appeared to be dependent on the level of IT experience of the respondents, their age, their interactions with others at work, and the culture within a practice – whether it was one of cooperation or one of ‘sink and swim on your own’. In particular, age was raised in five of the eight practice units by radiology workers as an inhibiting factor during the assimilation process and was a new code that emerged from the data analysis for the Identity dimension.

Again, I’m not trying to be ageist, but people are set in their ways, they’re comfortable with what they do. New technology causes them griefyreally; the best ones are my young radiographers or my 16–17 year old junior office girls [IDN-DEMO].

Look this probably sounds terribly discriminatory, but the young ones are fine, it’s the older ones around 40 or 50 who had a bit of a problem getting their head around the new system [IDN-DEMO].

Maybe I shouldn’t say this, but I’m younger than all the others [radiologists]. I’m more computer savvy. I like to try new things so I’ve taught myself a lot of this stuff [IDN-DEMO].

In addition to age, the culture of ARP impacted on HIS assimilation. All of the provincial practices had a culture of cooperation and attempted to work together to overcome problems. However, their metropolitan counterparts were less inclined to do so. As an administrator in a provincial practice explained:

It’s a bit like a family situation, once something changes, it’s amazing how a group of people can come together like we didy.human naturey.‘ohhhh it’s changed, help, what’s going on’ [IDN-PERCEP].

Another source of frustration was the perception by respondents from the provincial practices that they were provided with insufficient preparation and training compared with their metropolitan counterparts. That frustration ultimately impacted on their ability to properly assimilate the HIS.

The identities dimension of the framework illuminated the many-faceted perceptions, attitudes, social networks and differing cultural attitudes that ultimately enabled or inhibited the HIS assimilation process.

To summarise the case findings, we have illustrated how radiology workers enacted the HIS, and how they were constrained by organisational-level cultural and social contexts, and how the intra-organisational affiliations between the referrers and patients and the radiology practices strongly shaped HIS development and assimilation. We demonstrated how radiology workers attempted to enact these interactions, and how the HIS become part of the routine way of doing things in ARP, although fraught with problems throughout the assimilation process. Evidence was presented of how radiology workers identify with the HIS they use, and how the assimilation process acted as an inhibitor in many respects in defining themselves as competent and legitimate social actors. We also showed how external influences such as government agencies, referrers, and patients shape work practices inside ARP therefore providing insight into how and why the HIS was partially assimilated.

## Findings

The first research question asked: What are the enablers and inhibitors in the assimilation of a new ICT in radiology practices? To address the first research question, we drew on field study data and Lamb and Kling’s theorising about how a social actor engages in multiple uses of an HIS focusing on the relationships among people, ICTs and an environment that infuses meaning and purpose surrounding its use. The analysis provided an indication that the four constructs (representing behaviours of the social actor) within each of the four dimensions of the framework enabled us to better understand the assimilation process. The 16 behaviours of the connected and situated actor drew attention not only to the individual, but more importantly to their work relationships, the technology, and the wider environment of the work place. The dimensions of the model that have emerged as most prominent in terms of inhibiting or enabling the HIS assimilation process are those of identity, interactions and affiliations. The participants’ comments suggest these dimensions had the greatest impact on their ability to assimilate the new HIS. However, the importance of the environment dimension is also present, particularly in relation to the technological and institutional practices or standards external agencies impose on members within each of practice units [ENV-MAND] [ENV-POL-ICY]. In addition, it was reported that contextual factors such as the competitiveness of the radiology industry generally and, more specifically, the shortage of skilled radiology workers in the Australian work force all impacted on the HIS assimilation process [ENV-MAND]. Finally, the lack of capital expenditure on education, training and system support was perceived as an inhibitor to the HIS assimilation process by participants [ENV-CAPTIAL].

One of the most prominent inhibitors during the assimilation process belonged to the identity dimension as participants felt, without exception, that the assimilation process was very problematic. The consequences were frustration, anxiety, depression, and in some cases, workers resigning [IDN-USER] [IDN-DEFINE] [IDN-PER-CEP]. Job roles were also altered with the assimilation of the HIS, and although some embraced the change (such as administrative workers being able to work from home), others were not happy with the new tasks they were expected to complete, particularly the radiologists and radiographers [IDN-ROLES]. The demographics of participants also emerged as an inhibitor during the initial stages of the assimilation process and this construct has been added under the identities dimension [IDN-DEMO].

Cultural rules and attitudes within ARP also influenced the assimilation process, as many administrative and radiography workers mentioned the cultural divide between the various roles of radiology staff and this was also observed by the researcher. For example, the radiologists were treated with reverence by most within ARP, and many administrative staff and radiographers expressed feeling of a lack of support during the assimilation process from the radiologists and, at times, radiographers. Much frustration was expressed by administrative staff in particular that they were expected to take on additional work that the new system required the radiologists and radiographers to do [IDN-ROLES] [INT-PROCESS]. This construct was drawn out in two different dimensions – that of ‘Roles’ in the identities dimension and ‘Process’ within the interactions dimension. In particular, the radiologists did not perceive that it was their role to edit reports or validate patient processes, two new requirements introduced with the new system, and expected administrative staff to complete those tasks for them. There are three distinct groups in each practice (administrators, radiographers and radiologists), and each has its own, unique social system and cultural attitudes. Although the three groups work together well as a team in ensuring patient care, there are clear ‘demarcation’ lines between the three groups. The extent of this divide is greater in the metropolitan practices compared with the provincial ones; however, demarcation exists in all. The impact is felt most negatively by the administrative staff who were expected to cope with the new HIS themselves while dealing with very angry and sometimes abusive radiologists, who expected them to drop whatever they were doing and solve their problems immediately [IDN-PERCEP].

Another major finding of the analysis was the impact of interactions on the assimilation process. Interactions were identified as local (within practice units), across units, and with external affiliations such as referring GPs, patients and government agencies. There were both enabling and constraining interactions – in particular, the improvements in processing a patient during his/her visit [INT-PROCESS], compared with the lack of cooperation by radiologists and radiographers in validating patients throughout the process [INT-CONNECT]. The potential of the HIS was also recognised by most participants, especially in relation to the data available in the HIS [INT-COMM]. Reporting and patient histories was much improved with the new system. The only frustration was that much of the data was inaccessible to higher level management and, more importantly, the parent organisation was not interested in helping middle-level managers obtain the data they needed [INT-PROCESS] [INT-SOCIAL].

There were many enabling factors for HIS assimilation for the affiliation dimension, in particular, the ability to exchange information online [AFF-NETWORKS], and improved networking and information sharing with GPs [AFF-EXCH]. Technical hitches reduced some of the benefits, particularly with regard to email sent to GPs but not arriving; and the inability of the HIS to ‘flag’ illegal entries that were then sent onto Medicare and consequently rejected [AFF-EXCH]. The level of impact in regard to this dimension was somewhat dependent on the reliability of the technological infrastructure the system was built around. At times, an enabling construct of the affiliations dimension moved towards inhibiting the assimilation process due to system slowdowns and freezing issues [AFF-RELAT], or affiliates such as GPs not receiving the information they required in a timely manner [AFF-MULTI].

A major finding at the organisation level related to enhancing business performance and ultimately the competitiveness of the organisation. With respect to the goal of streamlining business processes, providing online access to employees and meeting the needs of affiliates (such as timely reports to GPs), the new HIS has been successful. However, due to the lack of technological infrastructure, and the perception of social actors that expenditure on training and systems enhancements was inadequate, the assimilation of the new HIS across all practices was diminished.

Our second research question asked: How useful are the four dimensions of the social actor model in illuminating the micro- and macro-level constructs and context that impact on the assimilation process in one practice in the healthcare industry? We argued that the social actor model provided a useful lens to identify factors across a number of levels that had the greatest impact on the assimilation process with some constructs within each dimension appearing more prominently than others. This was to be expected. However, additional constructs that the model does not specifically mention did appear to influence the assimilation process. The demographics of age and gender of the social actors, and the geographic location of practice units (metropolitan or provincial) impact on system use. We therefore argue that the addition of [IDN-DEMO] would extend the Lamb and Kling model to more holistically account for the range of issues that impacted on the HIS assimilation process. Overall, the framework illuminated a vast range of issues, contexts, constructs and social actions that ultimately benefited or worked against the assimilation of the HIS assimilation process.

## Discussion and conclusion

The case study illustrates the active role of social institutions such as established work practices and culture in the assimilation of a HIS and the utility of the concept of user as a social actor. The case shows that pre-existing structures such as organisational policy, mandates and practice culture play an active role in constraining and enabling human agency in the use of an ICT, particularly in relation to the cultural and social systems within each practice, coupled with the expectations of individual actors. This finding supports Walsham (1995) and Orlikowski & Iacono’s (2001) view that social, political, cultural and organisational context, and aspects and the role of human agents, play a major role in shaping the evolution activity of ICT assimilation. Social systems within individual practices of this radiology organisation have evolved around the value individuals, society and the parent organisation place on the various roles of radiology workers within a practice. For example, the radiologists are hierarchically at the top of the social spectrum and are aware of this. The radiographers fall in behind the radiologists and are respectful yet cynical about the radiologists. The administrative staff are at the lower end of the social spectrum, and there appears to be an expectation that they should attempt to solve all other participants’ problems as they arise, and do so expediently. Consequently, administrative workers are the most competent users of the HIS and due to this are more accepting of the system as a whole.

In terms of prior theory, Kaplan (1998) called for further investigation on the technical, individual and organisational context that contributes to ICT assimilation outcomes. This research has addressed these levels, and further extends Kaplan’s work by illuminating a key organisational construct – an organisational-level mandate to adopt the innovation that impacted on human agents, ICT use and the attitudes of stakeholders to the organisation. The transcripts indicated that organisational level mandates given without the supporting infrastructure to promote individual use of the system played an important role in forming the negative attitudes and beliefs of the social actors. This finding also supports Gallivan’s (2001) framework to incorporate organisational-level and institutional contexts when ICT use is mandated at a higher level. The mandates required by the company and other affiliates (GPs, Patients, Government agencies) also helped to mould the assimilation and evolution of the HIS over time. Most importantly, analysis of interview transcripts and observations when visiting each practice indicate a strong resentment towards management in relation to their handling of the assimilation process. Two years later, the resentment is still strongly felt. It would be useful if future researchers could extend this work and further investigate the depth of use (i.e.) how extensively other HISs are used under conditions of mandated use in other healthcare industry sectors.

In terms of practical contributions, it was reported in every practice we visited that the mandates enforced on the individual practices by the parent organisation to assimilate the HIS – although enabling the assimilation process to begin – inhibited the further use of the system quite drastically. The perceived lack of consultation, lack of training, the inability or unwillingness to correct system faults and the constant ignoring of requests for change provided very negative outcomes, not only for the individual radiology workers, but also for each practice, for patients and for referring GPs. Without exception, all interviewees felt that further use and acceptance of the HIS would have been improved had the parent organisation, and the external IT developers consulted more with the targeted users.

All research designs have limitations. In our paper we have only examined one HIS and thus we cannot generalise beyond it to others, although other HISs would likely mirror the same institutional contexts of systems assimilation as described in this case because, as we argue, assimilation is tied to structures of culture, work practices and norms. However, the findings do not constitute a ‘test’ of the framework. Rather, the case succeeds in establishing the plausibility of the Lamb and Kling model by providing evidence of its capacity to provide meaningful analytical codes to better understand the assimilation process. As argued previously, our approach provides a fresh perspective on contextual issues surrounding HIS assimilation than reported on before. Our research presents an example of how institutional structures of ARP provide an overarching, framing context within which radiology workers often made constrained choices about systems use. The model illustrates the constraining and enabling aspects of the IS itself and the influence of the organisation on system use. We concur with Lamb and Kling that their contextual model offers relevance in helping IS researchers to better understand ICT-related phenomena.

In terms of future research, it is hoped that future IS researchers will extend our work across other health practices and in other industries and thereby provide further extensions to the model’s dimensions. For example, the professional identities of radiology workers were implicitly linked to the new HIS and transformed their roles within each of the practice units. Are similar transformations occurring in other health industries as well? As Lamb & Davidson (2005) suggest, further research on how professional identities change over time due to the pervasive use of ICTs is clearly needed. Further research could also provide valuable insight into the various cultural contexts that exist within individuals, within a practice, at the parent organisational level and/ or at the broader national level. Perhaps an interdisciplinary approach that includes the individual, organisation and institutional-level cultural contexts could provide further valuable insights into HIS assimilation and further use?

To conclude the case, we claim firstly that the four dimensions accord with social informatics literature (Kling et al., 2005, p. 26), where ARP’s HIS was conceptualised as part of a larger socio-technical ensemble. The 16 coded behaviours drew focus on the way the HIS was enacted. An ensemble view, according to Sawyer & Crowston (2004, p. 52) is one where specific artefacts and people are interdependently connected through roles, uses of information, and actions. This conceptualisation of ICT and the various examples in the analysis section provides a good illustration of the so-called web models of IS in which people, their roles, their uses of a need for information, and the computing elements (HIS) are connected together and embedded in a larger social milieu.
