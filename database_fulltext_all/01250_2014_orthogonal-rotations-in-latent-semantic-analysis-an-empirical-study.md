## a b s t r a c t

The Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA) literature has recently started to address the issue of interpretability of the extracted dimensions. On the software implementation front, recent versions of SAS Text Miner ® started incorporating Varimax rotations. Considering open source software such as R, when it comes to rotation procedures the user has many more options. However, there is a little work in providing guidance for selecting an appropriate rotation procedure. In this paper we further previous research on LSA rotations by introducing two wellknown orthogonal rotations, namely Quartimax and Equamax, and comparing them to Varimax. We present a study that empirically tests the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the chosen orthogonal rotations on the extraction and interpretation of LSA factors. Our results indicate that, in most cases, Varimax and Equamax produce factors with similar interpretation, while Quartimax tends to produce a single factor. We conclude with recommendations on how these rotation procedures should be used and suggestions for future research. We note that orthogonal rotations can be used to improve the interpretability of other SVD-based models, such as COALS.

## 1. Introduction

In the last two decades, text mining [14] became an ubiquitous research area aiming at facilitating the comprehension of large quantities of unstructured text in order to support different types of business decisions with consequences for both academia and practitioners [45,35]. Originally conceptualized by H.P. Luhn, text mining evolved from simple abstracted principles, de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and postulates [43], to non-fully automated systems [40] and semi-fully automated systems [20].

Multiple approaches were developed and proposed in order to respond to different needs of utilization of text mining. Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA) is a frequently used text mining method for concept extraction [6,8]. Other well-known concept extraction methods include Probabilistic Topic Modeling [3], Probabilistic Latent Semantic Analysis [18] and Non-negative Matrix Factorization [27]. The concept-based dimensions produced by LSA were found, in their raw format, hard to interpret [25,2]. In more recent years, research started to better address the interpretability issue [41,12] using Varimax rotations on the extracted factors. On the practitioner's side, recent implementations of SAS® Text Miner, beginning with the version 4.2 available in 2010, offer Varimax rotations that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve the analyst's ability to interpret the LSA dimensions [37]. In this paper we further previous research on LSA rotations by introducing two other well-known orthogonal rotations, namely Quartimax and Equamax (also known as Equimax), and presenting an empirical study that compares the resulting rotated factors to those obtained through Varimax.

The paper is structured as follows. We start with a short introduction to LSA. We then present the principle of simplicity, which is the main idea underlying rotation in factor analysis. Subsequently we conduct an empirical comparative study on three orthogonal rotations and report the results. We conclude with a number of observations and recommendations related to the use of orthogonal rotations as part of LSA.

## 2. Latent semantic analysis

Latent semantic analysis [24], sometimes also referred to as latent semantic indexing [46], has recently gained increased attention in the academic community. A search using “Latent Semantic Analysis” or “Latent Semantic Indexing” terms in eight electronic databases, including Business Source Complete, Academic Search Complete, Library & Information Science Source, MEDLINE, Psychology & Behavioral Sciences Collection, Science & Technology Collection, Humanities Full Text, and SOCIndex Full Text, reveals the increasing trend in research interest shown in Fig. 1, where, for each year since 1989, we have counted all the articles that use LSA/LSI in their titles, abstracts, or keywords. LSA operates on textual data, which uses words as the most fundamental measurement unit. Building upon words, with the help of syntactic and grammatical rules, sentences are created. Combined sentences result in paragraphs that incorporate related ideas, concepts or topics. Multiple paragraphs create sections or chapters that eventually become documents. These documents are the most widely used forms in which ideas, or concepts, or latent topics, are encountered. Finally, a collection of documents is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the information retrieval/text mining language as a corpus. This represents the space in which text mining methods are applied.

The rationale for using LSA, the mathematical foundations behind the method, and a series of examples in which the method was successfully applied, can be found in an edited volume published in 2007 [25]. The main motivation for using LSA is that the algorithm was found able to model human understanding of word meaning and perform humanlike activities such as: evaluation of multiple-choice tests results, modeling of paragraph to paragraph coherence, and improvement of information retrieval. In addition to being a computational algorithm, LSA is also perceived as a theory of meaning in which words and documents represent vectors in the LSA space. Thus, LSA is able to match similar ideas by mapping them on a system of coordinates of reduced dimensionality.

The foundation of LSA is the vector space model (VSM), which treats every document as a bag-of-words, eliminating the syntactic and grammatical structure of the text. In VSM, documents are represented as mathematical vectors in a multi-dimensional space where each unique term that appears in the corpus is a dimension. Comparisons between documents can be performed by computing the cosine of the angle formed by the respective vectors, also known as document similarity. When the cosine between two vectors is 0 it is said that the vectors are orthogonal therefore the documents have nothing in common, whereas if the cosine value is 1 the documents include identical sets of terms. A more detailed presentation on the vector similarities in the VSM can be found in the work of Salton and his colleagues [36]. The VSM was shown to have several limitations in dealing with the length of the documents, the order of the words in a document, the synonymy, and the polysemy of the words [11,26]. To cope with these limitations, various corrective approaches were introduced. These include the reduction of the space of words by eliminating very common terms (stop words), the stemming of terms to their roots, and the establishing of a cosine threshold for retrieving similar documents [17]. When LSA was introduced, its authors proposed it as a solution to address the synonymy and polysemy issues existing in VSM [11]. More recent studies continue this endeavor [49,16].

LSA starts with the creation of the VSM, represented by a typically large term-by-document matrix in which, initially, the frequency of a word appearing in all documents is inserted at the intersection of a row and a column. Most often, the resulting term-by-document matrix is sparse. That is, it has a big number of “zero” values, indicating that the average document has a small fraction of terms appearing in it. To cope with the sparsity and keep the size of the term-by-document matrix manageable, a series of adjusting measures are taken. For example, a stop-word list is used to eliminate very common terms (“a”, “of”, “but”, etc.), which tend to appear in a large number of documents and therefore play a very small part in the formation of language use patterns. Next, a stemming procedure further reduces the number of words that enter the <sup>fi</sup>nal analysis. After applying the stop-words and stemming, the term frequencies are weighted using local information from each document as well as global information from the entire collection of documents. One of the most commonly used weighting scheme is TF–IDF, where terms frequencies are locally not weighted (“TF”), but globally weighted using inverse document frequency (“IDF”). Another widely used weighting scheme is the Log-Entropy transformation.

In order to describe the mathematical operations of LSA, let us denote the term frequency matrix that results from the aforementioned steps as matrix A. Using singular value decomposition (SVD), matrix A is decomposed into term eigenvectors U, document eigenvectors V, and singular values Σ (see Fig. 2). The main premise of LSA is the option to reconstruct A by multiplying the matrices on the right-hand side of Fig. 2, without using all the available dimensions (factors), but by keeping only a few top dimensions. The number of dimensions retained for further analysis can be established based on threshold values for their corresponding eigenvalues, or manually selected by the researcher. The extracted dimensions can be associated with terms and documents through eigenvector matrices U and V, respectively, but, as previously mentioned in this paper, the dimensions may not be easily interpretable through this association.

To facilitate the labeling of LSA factors, Varimax orthogonal rotation, traditionally used in exploratory factor analysis, was introduced as an additional step following SVD [41]. However, there is a little research on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence that various types of orthogonal rotation may have on the resulting factors. In addition to Varimax rotation, this study includes Equamax and Quartimax rotations. The main purpose of the study is to clarify the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the aforementioned orthogonal rotations on the resulting rotated LSA factors. We begin our investigation by presenting some related principles in the next section.

## 3. Simplicity and orthogonal factor rotations

## 3.1. The principle of simplicity

Invented more than 100 years ago to describe the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of an unobservable factor called g (for general intelligence) on examinee's test scores on several domains, the single factor model (SFM) assumes that items form a homogenous set and measure just one common attribute [42]. The single factor model was later improved to cope with the functions of multiple latent variables and became known as common factor model (CFM) [44]. Based on the common factor model assumptions, factor analysis differentiates between exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA). While EFA seeks to uncover the number of existing factor as well as the variable–factor relationship, CFA seeks to validate the factor structure which is a priori assumed to exist in a set of data, and evaluate the relationship between each factor. More on the philosophy of EFA can be found in Mulaik [30] and on the philosophy of CFA can be found in Jöreskog [21]. For the purposes of this paper, we observe that LSA is similar to EFA, as it attempts to uncover (explore) latent factors in unstructured textual data.

Several estimation methods generally used in EFA include the principal component method (PCA), unweighted least squares (ULS), generalized least squares (GLS), maximum likelihood (ML), principal axis factoring (PAF), alpha factoring (AF) and image factoring (IF). Nearly most of the time the extracted factors are orthogonal being ordered based on the amount of variance in the original data set explained by each factor [1].

To facilitate the interpretation of extracted factors that describe a subspace of the original data space (i.e., explaining less variance), two main types of rotations are traditionally used: orthogonal rotations and oblique rotations. The rationale for the use of orthogonal rotations is based on the principle of simple structure as proposed by Thurstone [44]. This principle is condensed in <sup>fi</sup>ve rules that ensure easiness over determination and stability for the factor loadings con<sup>fi</sup>guration [5]. Given the structure of a factor loadings matrix, the matrix is considered simple if:

a) Each row of the matrix contains at least one zero

b) Each column of the matrix contains at least as many zeros as the number of the columns in the matrix

c) For every pair of columns of the matrix the difference between two corresponding loadings is maximal

d) For every pair of columns of the matrix there is a substantial ratio of zeros

e) For every pair of columns of the matrix only a few rows with large loading exist.

For the case of a factor loadings matrix $\Lambda _ { \mathrm { ( p x m ) } } ,$ , the complexity of the matrix is simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed by multiplying the loadings by an orthogonal matrix $\mathrm { T } _ { \mathrm { ( m x m ) } } ,$ , resulting in $\mathsf { a } \Lambda ^ { * } { } _ { ( \mathrm { p x m } ) }$ matrix that minimizes a continuous function $f ( \Lambda ^ { * } )$ of the coresponding factor loadings. As the multiplication of two orthogonal matrices results in an orthogonal matrix, the newly obtained factor loadings remain orthogonal:

$$
\Lambda^ {*} = \Lambda \mathrm{T}.
$$

Based on the concept of complexity [7], Crawford and Ferguson [9] proposed a family of continuous functions f(Λ\*), weighted by a parameter k, given by

$$
\begin{array}{c} f \big (\Lambda^ {*} \big) = (1 - k) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} c (s _ {i.}) + k \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} c \Big (s _ {. j} \Big) \\ = (1 - k) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} \sum_ {l \neq j} ^ {m} \lambda_ {i j} ^ {2} \lambda_ {i l} ^ {2} + k \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {k \neq j} ^ {p} \lambda_ {i j} ^ {2} \lambda_ {k j} ^ {2} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

in which $k \in ( 0 , 1 ) , \lambda _ { i j }$ are the elements in $\Lambda ^ { * } , s _ { i j } = \lambda _ { i j } ^ { 2 } , i = 1 , . . . , p ,$ and $j = 1 , . . . , m .$ . Complexity is then minimized by minimizing f(Λ\*). Utilizing the k and (1 − k) weights, the right-hand side of the complexity function $f ( \Lambda ^ { * } )$ can exclude one of the two components or include both of them depending on the values of k. The <sup>fi</sup>rst right-hand side component represents row (variable) complexity and the second represents column (factor) complexity. The result is a continuum of orthogonal family rotations that includes Quartimax for k = 0, Varimax for $k = 1 / p ,$ and Equamax for k = m / 2p. This study focuses on these three orthogonal rotations, details of which are presented in the following section.

## 3.2. Orthogonal rotations

## 3.2.1. Varimax rotation

Invented in 1955, the Varimax rotation gained rapid popularity. It represents an implementation of Thurstone's principle of simplicity applied to the columns of a factor loadings matrix [22]. Technically, the Varimax rotation seeks to maximize the variance of the factor matrix loadings by forcing the variables to have either high loadings or low loadings (near-zero) on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c factor. This results in the facilitation of the factors' interpretation as the initial variables tend to be linked to a smaller number of factors and vice versa. However, this approach comes with the drawback of redistributing the variances of the most important factors among the less important factors. As such, for situations in which the constructs under investigation are represented by a general overall factor, this factor may not be detected after a Varimax rotation [23]. This observation is important, especially for research that seeks to evaluate the common method bias. While checking for common method bias, one should use the unrotated factor solution and not perform Varimax rotations.

## 3.2.2. Quartimax rotation

Quartimax rotation [48] also seeks to maximize the variance of the factor loadings, but it does by forcing high factor loadings or low factor loadings (near-zero) on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c variable (row of the factor loadings matrix), thus spreading the variance of the variable across all factors. This time the large loadings are in<sup>fl</sup>ated and the small loadings are de-<sup>fl</sup>ated so that each factor has only few variables with large loadings. Independently proposed by Carroll [7], Saunders [38], Neuhaus [31], and Ferguson [15], the Quartimax method has the disadvantage of being more likely to yield a general common factor.

## 3.2.3. Equamax rotation

The Equamax procedure was initially proposed [39] as a compromise between Varimax and Quartimax rotation. As such, the procedure seeks the maximization of the variance of the square loadings for both factors and variables. However, the Equamax procedure was shown to yield unreliable solutions, especially when the number of the extracted factors is unknown [23], therefore the use of the procedure is not recommended for exploratory analysis.

The orthogonal rotations presented in 3.1.1–3.1.3 were originally applied in traditional factor analysis as the <sup>fi</sup>nal step after the decomposition of the correlation matrix. Research shows that factor rotations can also be applied as a <sup>fi</sup>nal step of post-LSA quantitative analysis. For example, Hu and colleagues [19] show that, by transforming the entire LSA space to the new base, it may be possible to <sup>fi</sup>nd a new base with meaningful dimensions. Along similar lines, Evangelopoulos and colleagues [13] make use of an orthonormal rotation matrix M, such as the one produced by Varimax, and show that (i) the loading matrices UΣM and VΣM are not unique, and (ii) rotation of the latent semantic space has no effect on document comparison. In order to keep the representation of both terms and documents in the factor space consistent, term loadings UΣM, as well as document loadings VΣM, are both rotated using the same rotation matrix M, produced by simplifying the term loadings. In conclusion, the application of an orthogonal rotation to an initial latent semantic space extracted by SVD extends the understanding of the latent space without affecting term or document comparisons.

## 4. Comparative study

The main purpose of this study is to empirically test the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the chosen orthogonal rotations on the extraction and interpretation of factors obtained through LSA. To achieve this goal, we perform the analysis on three textual datasets; one of the datasets contains short text massages (SMS), the second contains medium-sized email messages, and the third contains large size news stories. These datasets will be referred from now on as short, medium and long messages. We perform our tests on various numbers of extracted factors (i.e. 3 factors, 5 factors, 7 factors, and 9 factors). We developed the testing code in the statistical programming language R [33] based on Fridolin's “demo/ lsa\_landauer.r” example [47]. Table 1 shows the parameter settings we applied in our experiments. The parameters listed in the table rows were kept <sup>fi</sup>xed for all three types of orthogonal rotation, to ensure that any observed differences in the extracted factor loadings are due only to the rotation type. All rotation types used the same stop list of 544 terms, the same stemming procedure, and used a log-entropy term weighting scheme.

The interpretation of each factor is usually performed considering the high-loading variables. As such, variables with a higher loading are more important in de<sup>fi</sup>ning a certain factor than variables with a low loading. In addition, factors composed of just a few variables are easier to interpret than factors composed of more variables. Here we adopt George Miller's position suggesting that humans processing information perform well when dealing with no more than seven, plus minus two, pieces of information [28]. Therefore, we believe that a number of 5 to 9 extracted variables per factor are suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to label and interpret a speci<sup>fi</sup>c factor. However, for demonstrative purpose we kept for the analysis all the extracted words for each factor. In order to compare the differences in rotated factors produced by the three aforementioned orthogonal rotations, we used two ranks coef<sup>fi</sup>- cient correlations that are frequently used in information retrieval text extraction comparisons [4]: Kendall Tau and Spearman.

## 5. Results of factor comparison

The results of factor comparison for each dataset are shown in Appendix B. The <sup>fi</sup>rst column of a table showing the results represents the compared factors. For instance, V1Q1 refers to the comparison of the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor extracted using Varimax rotation (V1) with the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor extracted using Quartimax rotation (Q1), V1E1 refers to the comparison of the <sup>fi</sup>rst extracted factor using Varimax rotation (V1) with the <sup>fi</sup>rst extracted factor using Equamax rotation (E1), and E1Q1 represents the comparison between the <sup>fi</sup>rst extracted factor using Equamax rotation with the <sup>fi</sup>rst extracted factor using Quartimax rotation. The next column represents the value of the correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient using the Spearman and Kendal Tau approaches. Each coef<sup>fi</sup>cient is accompanied by its p-value as described in the header of each table. The last column for each factor comparison is a stability index measure that we introduce in this study, aiming to capture the extent to which the structure of each extracted factor is stable, regardless of the type of rotation. This index was computed by dividing the number of pair loadings from compared factors by the total number of loadings per extracted factor. For example, Table 3a corresponds to a stability index of 1 because Varimax rotation (column V1) as well as Quartimax rotation (column Q1), both produce high loadings on all three listed terms, producing three out of three row-wise loading pairs that are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. In contrast, Table 3b shows high loadings in two out of three pairs, producing a stability index of 0.66. The stability index in Table 3c, where no term shows high loadings for both rotations, is equal to 0.

For each data set, the 3-factor (3F) solution yielded 9 possible pairs of loadings (Varimax vs. Quartimax, Quartimax vs, Equamax, and Varimax vs. Equamax), the 5-factor (5F) solution 15 yielded pairs, the 7-factor (7F) solution 21 pairs, and the 9-factor (9F) solution 27 pairs. The 72 pairs of loadings were evaluated using both the Spearman rank correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient and the Kendall Tau rank correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient, producing 144 comparisons, or a total of 432 comparisons for all three data sets.

For the short messages dataset, for the 3F solution using the Spearman rank correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient, the comparison of V2Q2 and E2Q2 shows that the structures of obtained factors after performing the orthogonal rotations result in factors that are not correlated. Similarly, for the 5F solution the comparison of V2Q2 and E2Q2 shows that obtained factors are not correlated. For the 7F solution only E4Q6 comparison shows that the rotated factors are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different. For the 9F solution, all the comparisons imply that the rotated factors are correlated. However, starting with the 7F solution, we observe that the correlated factors have a different position depending on the type of rotation performed. For instance, for the 9F solution V1 is similar to E3 but not to E1. Also, V1 is similar to Q4 and not to Q1. We also observe that Varimax and Equamax rotations appear to be more correlated than Varimax with Quartimax or Equamax with Quartimax. This observation is supported by the fact that for 3F and 5F solutions, factors produced using Varimax are more often correlated with the corresponding factors from Equamax (i.e. V1E1, V2E2, V3E3 etc.) Starting with the 7F and 9F solutions, even though the listing order of the factors changes, Varimax and Equamax rotations still produce factors that have the highest correlations.

## 6. Discussions and implications

Our study sheds light on the similarities between three orthogonal rotations for text mining analysis, and, depending on the attributes of the dataset, suggests possible methodological choices in the analysis of textual data. Hence, this section proceeds with the listing of a series of observations and recommendations.

Observation 1: Regardless of the type of message (short, medium, and long), when extracting a relatively big number of factors (such as 9 in our study), all three orthogonal rotations (Varimax, Equamax, and Quartimax) appear to produce similar factors. The interpretation of the factors looks stable for most of the rotated factors. However, for certain solutions (such as 9F in our study) factor order may change, but without affecting the interpretation of the rotated factors.

Recommendation 1: When a relatively large number of factors are extracted, any of the three types of orthogonal rotation (Varimax, Quartimax, and Equamax) will typically produce similar results, regardless of the type of the message.

Observation 2: Varimax and Equamax orthogonal rotations, regardless of the number of factors extracted, appear to result in more similar (correlated) rotated factors (see Appendix B) than the factors produced using Varimax and Quartimax, or Equamax and Quartimax. When extracting a small number of factors (3 and 5 in our example), it appears that the <sup>fi</sup>nal order of rotated factors is similar for both Varimax and Equamax rotation for short and long messages. In addition, differences in the order of the rotated factors were noticed for medium messages. In order to understand this irregularity for medium messages we looked at the sparsity of the initial matrices for all the datasets (Table 4). However, the sparsity of the initial matrices for short, medium and long messages matrices appears to be similar.

We further investigated this issue and we noticed that the total variance explained by the extracted factors was larger for the medium messages compared to the variance explained in short and long messages (see Fig. 3 and Table 5, last row).

The larger variance indicates that the factors extracted from the medium messages have larger loadings (since variance explained is equal to the sum of squared loadings). This situation can occur when the data set exhibits stronger patterns, i.e., when larger numbers of documents revolve around more consistent topics. In order to further understand the possible differences among the extracted factors from the three datasets, we have altered the medium messages dataset and performed the same analysis on a subset of 848 messages out of the initial 1481 medium messages. The truncated dataset of the medium messages resulted in extracted factors explaining variance that is lower than the variance explained by the factors in the short and long message datasets (see Table 6, last row). Due to space limitations, in Appendix B we present the extracted factors corresponding to the initial 1481 medium messages and not the ones extracted from the truncated dataset. The same 1481 messages are used in all the analyses presented in this manuscript.

Comparison of rotations using the truncated medium messages dataset indicated that the previously observed differences were eliminated. As such, the order of the rotated factors was preserved for relatively small numbers of extracted factors (3 and 5 in our example) and even 7 factors, regardless of the orthogonal rotation used, but the order started to change for a number of extracted factors equal to 9. It is possible that the order of the rotated factors is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the amount of explained variance. This issue may be worth further investigation in a future study.

Our results indicate that Varimax and Equamax produced similar rotated factors. As such, both methods can be used to check the stability (reliability) of the rotated factors. However, it is important to note here that the order at which a factor appears after rotation may be an important indicator of the factor's semantic content. If, for example, a factor that appears to be related to “saving paper” is listed <sup>fi</sup>rst after Varimax rotation and listed third after Equamax rotation, this may be an indication that the Varimax factor talks about “saving paper” in the more general context of “saving energy”, whereas the Equamax factor has a narrower interpretation of “saving paper”, and that's why it explains less variance. Researchers are, therefore, warned of possible misleading factor interpretations when the rotated factor order is not identical across different rotation methods.

Recommendation 2a: Use Varimax and Equamax rotations to check the reliability of the rotated factors, as these two rotations often produce similar results.

Recommendation 2b: Use caution in interpreting similarity of the rotated factors if their listing order differs by the type of rotated method (Varimax, Equamax, Quartimax).

Observation 3: When a small number of factors (such as 3 and 5 in our example) are rotated using Quartimax rotation, most of the terms load on the <sup>fi</sup>rst rotated factor whereas only one or two terms load on the remaining factors for both short and medium messages. The <sup>fi</sup>rst rotated factor then appears to describe the entire dataset, whereas the remaining factors may be only partially interpretable (see Appendix A). The typical situation is that the terms with the highest loadings on the <sup>fi</sup>rst rotated factor using Quartimax have also the highest loadings on the <sup>fi</sup>rst rotated factor using Varimax or Equamax. In a manner that is reminiscent of Harman's test in factor analysis [32], Quartimax rotation tends to produce one general factor that accounts for the majority of variance when extracting a small number of factors (such as 3 and 5 in our example). However, with larger numbers of extracted factors (such as 7 or 9 in our example), Quartimax produces similar results to Varimax and Equamax even though the order of the extracted factors may differ. Therefore, when a large number of factors is extracted (such as 7 and 9 in our example), Quartimax can also be used to check the stability (reliability) of the rotated factors.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this manuscript can be extended to other algorithms that use SVD as their <sup>fi</sup>nal step. One such algorithm is a variant of the Correlated Occurrence Analogue to Lexical Semantic, or COALS, known as COALS–SVD [34,29,10]. Similar to LSA, COALS–SVD also relies on SVD to reduce the dimensionality of a similarity matrix. Unlike LSA, which operates on a term-by-document transformed term frequency matrix, COALS–SVD operates on a term-by-term transformed correlation matrix. COALS starts by compiling an initial co-occurrence matrix with a ramped, 4-word window and continues by transforming the correlations of the raw term co-occurrence matrix, with negative correlations set to 0 and positive correlations square-rooted. The transformed matrix X is subjected to Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) as follows: $\mathsf { X } = \mathsf { U } \Sigma \mathsf { V } ^ { \mathrm { T } }$ . Using these matrices, term loadings UΣ can be compiled. It can be shown that UΣ are term loadings as follows: Since X contains the square roots of term correlations, ${ \bf \boldsymbol { X } } { \bar { \bf \boldsymbol { X } } } ^ { \mathrm { T } } = { \bf \boldsymbol { X } } { \bf \boldsymbol { X } }$ contains term correlations. But $\mathrm { X X ^ { \mathrm { { T } } } } = \mathrm { U } \Sigma \mathsf { V } ^ { \mathrm { { T } } } ( \mathrm { U } \Sigma \mathsf { V } ^ { \mathrm { { T } } } ) ^ { \mathrm { { T } } } = \mathrm { U } \Sigma \mathsf { V } ^ { \mathrm { { T } } } \mathsf { V } ( \mathrm { U } \Sigma ) ^ { \mathrm { { T } } } = \mathrm { U } \Sigma ( \mathrm { U } \Sigma ) ^ { \mathrm { { T } } }$ , which means that UΣ can “reproduce” the term correlation matrix XX<sup>T</sup>, therefore UΣ are term loadings. Just as term covariances are unaffected by rotations in LSA, term correlations in COALS are also unaffected by rotations. This can be shown as follows: ${ \tt X } { \tt X } ^ { \mathrm { { T } } }$ represent term-to-term correlations in COALS and term-to-term covariance in LSA. For both models, $\mathrm { X X ^ { \mathrm { T } } } = \mathrm { U } \Sigma ( \mathrm { U } \Sigma ) ^ { \mathrm { T } }$ , but exploiting the orthonormality property of the rotation matrix M, by which $\mathrm { ~  ~ \Gamma ~ } = \mathrm { ~  ~ M ~ } { \sf M } ^ { \mathrm { T } } = \mathrm { ~  ~ I ~ } , \mathrm { ~  ~ { \sf U } \Sigma ( { \sf U } \Sigma ) ^ { \mathrm { T } } ~ } =$ $\mathrm { U } \mathrm { \Sigma M M ^ { T } ( U \Sigma ) ^ { T } = U \Sigma M ( U \Sigma M ) ^ { T } }$ , therefore UΣM are term loadings of the same term correlation matrix XX<sup>T</sup> for COALS (and the same covariance matrix for LSA). This implies that term loading rotations have no effect in term-to-term correlation/covariance results. They have, however, the potential of producing interpretable dimensions. This is something that the COALS–SVD literature has not yet explored.

## 7. Conclusions and further research

The increased amount of available textual data generated by diversi-<sup>fi</sup>ed sources of information that have potential business value such as tweets, blogs, company reports, service representative notes, assessor notes, and textual customer feedback, presents a challenge to text mining and text analytic techniques. Mirroring traditional numerical factor analysis, LSA has mostly used Varimax rotation for simplifying the interpretation of the extracted factors. To our knowledge, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to compare three types of orthogonal rotations (Varimax, Quartimax, and Equamax), within the LSA context.

There are certain aspects of this study that should be treated with caution. First, the number of extracted factors presented here varies within a relatively small range that extends from 3 to 9. This choice was primarily dictated by space restrictions, given our intention to offer enough detail to make the reader aware of certain critical details and offer useful insights. Our study suggests that rotated factor interpretation becomes more stable for larger numbers of extracted factors. Even though our detailed presentations include up to 9 extracted factors, solutions with even larger numbers (such as 25) exhibit rotation results that agree with our general conclusions and recommendations. Still, future studies may want to expand this line of investigation to include larger numbers of factors. As a second limitation, this study examined only three popular types of orthogonal rotations. Other types of rotations [5] may be worth investigating in future studies.

He received his Ph.D. in Information Systems from the University of North Texas and his M.S. in Databases from the Academy of Economic Studies, Bucharest, Romania. His research interests include Text Mining, Web Design, and Quantitative Methods. His publications include articles appearing in the Communications of the ACM and ACM Inroads.

Nicholas Evangelopoulos is an Associate Professor of Decision Sciences at the University of North Texas and a Fellow of the Texas Center for Digital Knowledge. He received his Ph.D. in Decision Sciences from Washington State University and his M.S. in Computer Science from the University of Kansas. His research interests include statistics and text mining. His publications include articles appearing in MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, Communications in Statistics, and Computational Statistics & Data Analysis.
