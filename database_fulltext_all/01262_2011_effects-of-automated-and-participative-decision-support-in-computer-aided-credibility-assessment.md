Ab stract: Historically, inaccurate credibility assessments have resulted in tremendous costs to businesses and to society. Recent research offers unobtrusive credibility assessment aids as a solution; however, the accuracy of these decision aids is inadequate, and users often resist accepting the aids’ recommendations. We follow the principles of signal detection theory to improve the accuracy of recommendations in computer-aided credibility assessment by combining automated and participatory decision support. We also leverage participation in decision-making theory to explain and predict an increased acceptance of assessment aid recommendations when perceptual cues are elicited from users. Based on these two theories, we design and test a hybrid decision aid to perform automated linguistic analysis and to elicit and analyze perceptual cues from an observer. Results from a laboratory experiment indicate that decision aids that use linguistic and perceptual cues offer more accurate recommendations than aids that use only one type of cue. Automatic analysis of linguistic cues improved both the decision aid’s recommendations and the users’ credibility assessment accuracy. Challenging the generalizability of past findings, the elicitation of perceptual cues did not improve the decision aid’s recommendations or the users’ assessment accuracy. Elicitation of perceptual cues, however, did improve user acceptance of the decision aid’s recommendations. These findings provide guidance for future development of credibility assessment decision aids.

Key words and p hrases: credibility assessment, decision support systems, indirect cues elicitation, linguistic analysis, signal detection theory.

It is difficul t for p eopl e to assess credib il ity correctl y in face-to-face interactions (e.g., [3]), but this capability is critical to effective communication and decision making [11, 46]. Accurately judging the credibility of others has important applications in business contexts such as auditing [32], negotiation [46], and other contexts in which motivations to mislead can be high and information is unevenly distributed. Modern business history is replete with examples of organizations being harmed or collapsing because of skillful deception—often causing stakeholders to lose billions of dollars. Today, deception is at the heart of many illicit activities that threaten organizations and society as a whole. Efforts to maintain global security have brought credibility assessment to the forefront of public interest and interdisciplinary research (e.g., [26]).

Credibility is the believability of a source due to the message recipients’ perceptions of the source’s trustworthiness and expertise [27, 41]. Credibility is influenced by receiver characteristics, source characteristics, message characteristics, and cognitiveprocessing routes [11, 27]. Estimates of others’ degree of credibility are often misplaced [3]. The primary goal of credibility assessment is to ensure that credibility is properly attributed—meaning that the recipient perceives a source as credible when the source’s message is true and accurate. Although credibility assessment may cover mistaken and ill-informed messages, we focus solely on distinguishing between truthful messages—those messages the sender believes to be true—and deceptive messages— those messages the sender believes to be false [5]. Specifically, we examine the accurate attribution of credibility—that is, judging a message to be credible when truthful or noncredible when deceptive.

Despite the importance of accurate credibility assessment, research repeatedly shows that most people are too trusting when evaluating incoming messages [34]. A recent meta-analysis investigating human-assessment ability demonstrates that when people are faced with equal numbers of truthful or deceptive messages, they can distinguish truthful messages from deceptive ones at an accuracy rate of 54 percent—a rate only marginally better than chance [3]. Identifying deceptive messages becomes even more difficult as the frequency of deception decreases [35].

To help address these issues, researchers have tried to improve credibility assessments by using decision aids (e.g., [2]), such as polygraphs, that are designed to detect deception through monitoring physiological indicators of stress and arousal. However, despite their ability to assess credibility, these kinds of decision aids are not feasible in all situations due to their intrusive nature [40]. Recent research has investigated new unobtrusive methods of assessing credibility (e.g., [23, 31, 33, 58]), such as automated language processing and analysis tools [59, 60, 61], that rely on observable behaviors to detect cues that are normally difficult for humans to detect. These types of decision aids are typically 70 percent to 80 percent accurate in detecting deception [62].

Although these reported accuracy rates exceed those of unaided humans, unobtrusive decision aids for credibility assessment are inadequate in many situations as a result of two notable limitations. First, users often do not accept a decision aid’s recommendation despite the aid’s potential to improve accuracy [30]. Second, diagnostic perceptual measures of credibility that have been shown to improve credibility assessment accuracy [45, 46] have not been incorporated into decision aids. These measures are currently identifiable only by humans and thus have received little attention in designing decision aids despite their potential to improve assessment accuracy.

To address these issues, this study uses signal detection theory (SDT) and participation in decision-making (PDM) theory to guide the design of a hybrid expert system that analyzes the structure and content of messages (i.e., linguistic cues) and elicits perceptual information from an interaction observer (i.e., indirect cues), which information is difficult for a decision aid to approximate. Our hybrid expert system is the first decision aid to consider both linguistic and indirect cues in assessing credibility. Our system provides the user fully automated analysis of linguistic cues but requires participation from the user to collect and analyze indirect cues. Building on SDT, we posit that considering both linguistic and indirect cues will result in higher recommendation accuracy from credibility assessment aids. For example, a system implemented at a screening checkpoint to detect deception could increase accuracy not only by analyzing the entrants’ statements (via linguistic cues) but also by eliciting perceptual information (via indirect cues) from an observer. Further, drawing upon PDM research, we posit that eliciting indirect cues (e.g., perceptions of cognitive load) from users will increase acceptance of decision-aid recommendations. Finally, with more accurate decision-aid recommendations accepted by users, computer-aided credibility assessment accuracy should improve.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. First, we provide an overview of human- and computer-based credibility assessment methods. Second, we explain SDT and the theoretical foundation it offers for improving recommendation accuracy. Third, we operationalize hypotheses to test the influence of automated (based on linguistic cues) and participatory (based on indirect cues) decision support on the accuracy of credibility assessment recommendations made by decision aids, acceptance of those recommendations, and assessment accuracy of aid users. Finally, we analyze and discuss the results as well as provide recommendations for future research, note limitations of the study, and present conclusions.

## Background Literature

## Human Assessment Capabilities

Hum ans face a numb er of difficul ties when assessing the credibility of a source. First, in making assessments, people tend to rely on behaviors that are not diagnostic of deception. For example, recent research shows widespread belief that deceivers avert their eyes during deception [24]. Yet systematic research has shown no link between gaze aversion and deception [15]. Although numerous behaviors have been correlated with deception, no perfectly reliable diagnostic cues exist in credibility assessment; thus, behavioral cues to deception are only probabilistically linked to actual deception [15]. This gap is problematic for manual assessment because message receivers and interaction observers frequently focus on a few characteristics, such as gaze aversion, which they believe to be diagnostic and exclude other potentially diagnostic characteristics. Credibility assessment training can improve assessment accuracy; however, improvements are generally minimal [22].

Moreover, people typically adopt a heuristic-based approach for judging credibility [4, 8]. For example, message receivers are often predisposed to label the majority of messages as truthful and believable. This phenomenon is termed truth bias [39]. Many researchers believe that truth bias is an availability heuristic cemented by prior experience [43] because most messages that people receive each day are truthful. Accordingly, to preserve cognitive effort, people often initially label all incoming messages as “truthful.” Research on deception frequency shows that even though most incoming messages are truthful, people regularly lie to others; yet they do not expect to receive deceptive messages [14].

In contrast, a minority of people use an opposite heuristic to the truth bias because they are exposed more frequently to lies than to truth. This phenomenon, called the Othello error or lie bias, is seen among individuals who frequently encounter noncredible sources (e.g., law enforcement personnel). These individuals are more likely to label incoming messages as “deceptive” [17]. Such mislabeling of credibility undermines the ability to detect possible deception.

Finally, people are limited in their information-processing capabilities [42] and typically focus on small subsets of nondiagnostic cues. People simply do not have the cognitive capacity to monitor multiple channels of communication (e.g., verbal and nonverbal) in order to note deviations in behavior that might raise suspicion while simultaneously maintaining an interaction. The vigilance of an observer slowly fades over time in complex computer-aided tasks [44]. The resulting faded attention and diminished cognitive resources induce assessment based on heuristics. Whereas heuristic-based decision rules can suffice in some complex decision-making contexts, heuristics are rarely appropriate for credibility assessment [8].

Despite humans’ difficulty with credibility assessment, there is reason to believe that they can effectively contribute to a hybrid human–computer system of credibility assessment. Perceptual measures are among the strongest cues to deception that humans are uniquely capable of evaluating. A recent meta-analysis linked decreased verbal and vocal immediacy, implausibility, illogical structure, and ambivalence to deception [15]. Such perceptual cues are generated by manual behavioral coding where trained coders observe an interaction and record their perceptions about what they observed (e.g., [6]). These perceptual cues are difficult to automatically approximate because they represent global assessments of a whole interaction, span verbal and nonverbal channels, and require semantic understanding of verbal messages.

Building on behavior coders’ ability to successfully identify perceptual cues highly correlated with truthful and deceptive messages (e.g., [15, 50]), Vrij et al. [51, 52] developed and successfully tested methods for rapidly eliciting information from interaction observers. They term this elicitation a collection of “indirect cues” and hypothesize that assessing credibility indirectly would improve assessment accuracy. They suggest that individuals are somewhat reluctant to characterize a message as deceptive or a sender as deceitful, but through indirect cue elicitation, observers are “subtly directed to more valid cues of deception” [52, p. 285] and are encouraged to implicitly form veracity judgments [52]. As illustration, by encouraging people to attend to the number of visual, sound, and special details, they form decisions based on diagnostic credibility cues and are less subject to explicit, heuristic-based judgments. Results from this method of manual credibility assessment have shown promise by yielding approximately 75 percent accuracy [51, 52]. An additional benefit of using indirect cues is that the elicitation process can be quickly accomplished with limited training and is completely unobtrusive.

## Computer-Based Assessment Capabilities

Recent credibility assessment research has developed new, unobtrusive assessment methods based on observable behavior [31]. These new methods are a significant departure from past attempts at machine-aided credibility assessment that have consistently targeted physiological indicators of stress and arousal [10]. Instead, these new, unobtrusive methods focus on observable behavior (e.g., kinesics, linguistics, vocalics) and could operate nearly in real time.

One unobtrusive deception detection technique of note is automated language processing and analysis [60, 61]. In most interactions, language is the mechanism through which deceptive messages are exchanged. Researchers have long sought to identify cues that deceivers exhibit or strategies they use so that deception can be identified.

Several manual credibility assessment methods have been developed as a result—such as criteria-based content analysis and reality monitoring—but these methods require trained analysts to meticulously examine suspected statements for extended periods of time [50]. The nuances of language can be difficult for an observer to monitor in real time, especially if the observer must also maintain an understanding of the semantic meaning of the interactants’ statements. Observers simply lack the cognitive ability to count parts of speech and monitor the status of an interaction simultaneously.

Various researchers have tried to construct computer-based decision aids to capture and analyze message characteristics and present recommendations concerning the credibility of the message (e.g., [23, 28, 62]). These aids have attempted to approximate manual credibility assessment methods in an automated setting, and thus generally focus on categories of credibility cues such as passive voice, self-reference, negative statements, generalizations, uncertainties, temporal details, spatial details, and affective details [61]. In contrast to more gestalt indirect cues that are determined from considering an interview in its entirety, linguistic cues are highly granular (i.e., finely detailed) in nature and require significant processing capability to monitor. For example, at a screening checkpoint, an observer would have a difficult time parsing out parts of speech and analyzing these parts of speech (e.g., calculating means and ratios) to assess credibility while carrying on a conversation. An automated system, however, can record, transcribe, parse, and analyze a conversation in nearly real time. Decision aids that utilize linguistic cues have consistently exceeded the assessment capabilities typically seen among unaided observers (e.g., [62]) and can significantly extend the capabilities of users. The decision aids do not suffer from the same biasbased approach that many people use in assessing credibility and maintain constant vigilance and attention. However, both novice and professional credibility assessors frequently reject decision aids’ recommendations in favor of their own assessments— often to the detriment of assessment accuracy [30].

## Theory and Hypotheses

Whereas b oth hum an- and comp uter-b ased m ethods of credibility assessment have shown promise in past research, little is known about the effects of joining both approaches to improve assessment accuracy and user acceptance of recommendations. We leverage SDT [25, 47] to merge the more gestalt, human-based approach with the more granular, computer-based approach to produce more accurate recommendations and assessments. We also leverage PDM research to explain how recommendations from a decision aid might be more frequently accepted.

## Detecting Signals of Deception

SDT is applied to scenarios where an individual is given a sensory stimulus and then tries to discern signal (i.e., deception in our context) from noise (i.e., truth in our context). SDT provides two prescriptive mechanisms to improve the detection of a particular signal. Our approach in merging human- and computer-based approaches to credibility assessment takes advantage of both mechanisms. Prior to describing these mechanisms, however, we provide a short description of SDT.

Our credibility context follows the most traditional SDT task type, called the yes/no task. A yes/no task involves a series of two kinds of trials: signal trials, which contain signals the observer is seeking, and noise trials, which present noise that the observer may mistakenly equate with a signal. A credibility assessment thus may result in four possible outcomes for each trial: (1) hits, (2) false alarms (or false positives), (3) misses, or (4) correct rejections. A hit occurs when the observer concludes that deception has occurred and when deception is actually present. A false alarm, or false positive, occurs when the observer concludes that deception has occurred when deception is not actually present. A miss occurs when the observer concludes that no deception is present when deception actually is present. Finally, a correct rejection occurs when the observer determines that no deception is present when deception actually is not present. These four potential outcomes are summarized in Table 1.

A key component of SDT is that individuals have a decision variable by which they determine whether a signal exists. Applied to our context, this decision variable would be something akin to suspicion. Many different contributors to suspicion may exist. For example, various behavioral cues (e.g., lack of visual, sound, and spatial details) may heighten suspicion. SDT represents the distribution of the decision variable values for the signal trials as the signal distribution, and the distribution of the decision variable values for the noise trials as the noise distribution. In a simple yes/ no task, the signal and noise distributions are assumed to be of equal size and equal variance, though nonparametric data can also be used [1, 45].

In addition to the decision variable, each assessor has a threshold for the decision variable that, if exceeded, indicates deception is present. This threshold is termed the criterion, and in the case of credibility assessment, the criterion determines when suspicion (the decision variable in our context) is sufficiently high to warrant an assessment of deception. Clearly, formulating a decision variable and where the criterion is placed are perceptual and subjective. As a result, a key assumption of SDT is that “if the subject is using an appropriate decision variable, and if the subject is capable of distinguishing between signals and noise, the decision variable will be affected by the stimuli that are presented” [47, p. 138]. As previously mentioned, SDT proposes two mechanisms whereby decisions may be improved, and through these mechanisms credibility assessments may be enhanced through a decision aid that incorporates automatic and participatory approaches. As illustrated in Figure 1, the first mechanism proposed by SDT to improve decisions is to create more separation between the signal distribution and the noise distribution. In our context, this is accomplished by increasing either the diagnostic ability of existing features or the number of cues that provide unique diagnostic information.

To explain how to implement either approach, we turn to research on deception and credibility (e.g., [15, 50]). No characteristic or cue of deception is completely diagnostic and reliable, but some are more diagnostic and reliable than others. Several linguistic cues to deception have consistently shown diagnostic ability [60, 61]. Yet attending to these cues (e.g., parts of speech) may be beyond the cognitive ability of unaided observers. Attending to linguistic cues requires a significant amount of processing capability that an unaided human may lack. Furthermore, past research has found perceptual measures—such as cognitive effort and level of detail—to also be diagnostic indicators of deception [51, 52]. However, unaided individuals may not be aware of these cues or cannot effectively incorporate such perceptual cues in their assessments. Conversely, a decision aid with a high level of processing ability and focus on diagnostic cues should be able to create more separation between the signal distribution (i.e., deception) and noise distribution (i.e., truth).

The second mechanism by which SDT may improve assessment accuracy is through proper placement of the criterion. This proper placement is particularly salient in credibility assessment because of the widespread tendency toward a truth bias. A conservative criterion (that exists with a truth bias) prevents actual deceptive messages from being classified as deception even though the receiver has some level of suspicion. Proper placement of the criterion maximizes overall accuracy and helps minimize misses and false alarms.<sup>1</sup> Placement of the criterion is accomplished by examining past values of the decision variable for occurrences of known deception and truth and then setting the criterion so that false negatives and false positives are minimized. In Figure 1, this is illustrated by moving the criterion value to the point where the signal distribution and noise distribution intersect. The calculations for accomplishing optimal placement of the criterion are straightforward [48]; yet altering one’s own criterion during unaided credibility assessment can be difficult because of the criterion’s subjective nature. Conversely, establishing an appropriate criterion for the decision aid is simply a matter of changing numeric values in the aid’s decision model.

In summary, because a decision aid has a strict focus on diagnostic linguistic and perceptual cues, a greater ability to process those cues, and an ability to set the criterion, a decision aid’s recommendation accuracy should exceed an unaided observer’s accuracy. Therefore,

## Hypothesis 1: A decision aid that implements both linguistic analysis and indirect cue elicitation will produce recommendations that will be more accurate than the judgments of an unaided observer.

With both linguistic and indirect cues stemming from observable behavior, the potential exists for overlap between the sets of cues. If much overlap between the sets exists, SDT suggests that accuracy improvements would be minimal because the cues would not be contributing unique, diagnostic information to the assessment. We posit that whereas some overlap between linguistic and indirect cues may exist, the cues are largely complementary because each set provides unique, diagnostic information to the assessment. The two sets are collected at different levels (granular versus gestalt) and draw on differing observable characteristics. Linguistic cues are extracted solely from the message; however, indirect cues may capture not only the message but also the characteristics of the message source and message delivery. Further, indirect cues take context into account, which is largely ignored by the linguistic cues set. Because linguistic and indirect cues are captured at different levels of the interaction and cover different characteristics of the interaction, they both should provide unique information that is diagnostic of credibility. This unique information should increase the distance between the mean of the signal (i.e., deception) distribution and the mean of the noise (i.e., truthful) distribution—thus making the recommendations based on both sets of cues more accurate. Therefore,

Hypothesis 2: A decision aid that implements both linguistic analysis and indirect cue elicitation will produce more accurate recommendations than a decision aid that implements only one of these components.

## Accepting Credibility Assessment Recommendations

User acceptance of recommendations from decision aids has attracted a great deal of attention in recent information systems (IS) literature (e.g., [49, 55]) because if users do not trust a decision aid’s recommendation, the decision aid cannot assist the user. The same is true for decision aids supporting credibility assessment. Although we anticipate that the automated analysis of linguistic cues will increase accuracy by augmenting the user’s cognitive abilities, the increased accuracy may be partially negated if the user is reluctant to accept the decision aid’s recommendations. This reluctance has been a significant area of concern noted in past research on aided credibility assessment [30]. In answer to this concern, we posit that an additional benefit provided by eliciting indirect cues from the user is that the user will be more likely to accept the aid’s recommendation.

To further explain the link between user participation through indirect cue elicitation and acceptance, we turn to research on PDM. The central premise of PDM is that participation in the decision-making process increases the acceptance of decision outcomes. PDM theory suggests two mechanisms that may influence acceptance of decision outcomes [37]. First, participation serves a cognitive function by allowing the participant to consider information that is used in the decision-making process. Accordingly, those who participate in the decision are at least partially aware of what information was considered and how that information influenced the decision outcome. Second, participation serves a motivational function by increasing commitment to the decision outcome. This motivational function stems from perceptions of control over and ownership of the decision outcome [18]. For example, a participant in a decisionmaking task will be more committed to the decision outcome to which he or she has contributed, and this greater degree of commitment will be manifest by acceptance of the outcome. In practice, PDM has been applied in interpersonal interactions to overcome resistance to change [13] and increase goal acceptance [19, 53]. Researchers have noted that PDM has had a variable effect on performance gains [37].

IS researchers have long known that participation in the development of a decision aid increases the likelihood that the decision aid will be used [12]. However, the notion of participation increasing the acceptance of a single recommendation is a concept that has only recently attracted attention (for a notable exception, see [38]). Dealing with the broad category of recommendation agents, researchers have suggested direct links between the amount of information the user provides (e.g., user preferences on which recommendations are based) and acceptance of the aid’s recommendation [56]. This proposition has garnered empirical support in an e-commerce setting as customers who contributed information to recommendation agents perceived the recommendation to be individually customized [36] and were more likely to accept the agent’s recommendation [54].

When PDM is applied to the computer-assisted task of credibility assessment, participation is anticipated to have an effect similar to that observed in recommendation systems in e-commerce. However, rather than considering which product to buy, users are evaluating the credibility of another person. The method of collecting linguistic cues is fully automated and—despite the provision of explanations—the basis of recommendations may be opaque. The elicitation of indirect cues, conversely, invites the user to contribute to the aid’s recommendation. Such elicitation makes clear the information that is being considered by the decision aid and increases the level of commitment the user feels toward the recommendation. Through the cognitive and motivational mechanisms suggested by PDM, users will be more likely to accept the aid’s recommendations. Therefore,

Hypothesis 3: A user will accept the recommendation of the decision aid more frequently when the aid implements indirect cue elicitation.

## Increasing Assessment Accuracy

Decision aids that automatically gather and analyze linguistic cues augment users cognitive capacities. The automated analysis of linguistic cues focuses on theoretically sound, diagnostic cues of deception and produces unbiased recommendations. Unaided credibility assessors would not have the cognitive capacity to track these diagnostic cues in real time—let alone analyze them without bias. For example, a decision aid deployed at a security gate would aid an observer in detecting deception by performing complex, linguistic analyses that would normally not be available to the observer. Further, the decision aid can present this analysis to the observer in an interpretable recommendation, increasing the likelihood that the recommendation will be understood and considered during the assessment. This should increase the user’s ability to distinguish between deception and truth. Past research has shown that users who have had access to unobtrusive decision aids that present recommendations based on linguistic cues have significant improvement in their credibility assessment accuracy [29]. Therefore,

## Hypothesis 4: When using a decision aid implementing linguistic analysis, a user will be more accurate in credibility assessment than will an unaided observer.

Unique to this study is the implementation of indirect cue elicitation in a decision aid. In past work on manual credibility assessment, indirect cues have been diagnostic of credibility [52]. Although the danger exists that observers will perpetuate their biases and suspicions through their indirect cues scoring, indirect cue elicitation has been proposed as a valid method to collect diagnostic, perceptual measures from interaction observers. In contrast with unaided assessment, indirect cue elicitation helps observers determine the linkage between observed cues and the level of credibility. Furthermore, unaided observers frequently utilize nondiagnostic cues to determine credibility—a practice that undermines assessment accuracy. A decision aid can elicit diagnostic, indirect cues based on perceptions of the source and message; evaluate the cues reliably; and present the user with an interpretable recommendation. The provision of an interpretable recommendation should positively influence a user’s credibility assessment accuracy by increasing the user’s ability to distinguish between truth and deception. Therefore,

Hypothesis 5: When using a decision aid implementing indirect cue elicitation, a user will be more accurate in credibility assessment than will an unaided observer.

We posit that recommendations based on both indirect and linguistic cues will improve user assessment accuracy more than will recommendations based on only one group of cues. Building on our previous hypotheses, using both types of cues should provide the most accurate recommendations. Linguistic and indirect cues cover both source and message characteristics, are taken from granular and gestalt levels, and have been shown to be diagnostic in past research [29, 31, 51, 52]. According to SDT, considering unique, diagnostic cues makes discrimination between deception and truth clearer. Thus, the recommendations produced by this aid should positively influence the user’s assessment accuracy the most. This conclusion tests the possibility of a human–computer system for credibility assessment. Therefore,

Hypothesis 6: When using a decision aid implementing linguistic analysis and indirect cue elicitation, a user will be more accurate in credibility assessment than will a user of an aid that implements only one component.

## Method

A control ed l ab oratory exp erim ent was conducted to test the hypotheses. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four treatment groups and were asked to watch ten randomly ordered, prerecorded interviews. Participants then provided a credibility assessment for each interview.

## Participants

The experiment involved 167 participants recruited from an upper-division business course at a large southwestern university. The mean age of the participants was 21.4, the mean years of secondary education were 3.3, and the gender distribution of the participants was 45 percent female and 55 percent male. Participants received extra credit for participating in the experiment and those who scored in the top 10 percent on correct assessments were given a cash award of \$10.

To ensure that participants who had access to the decision aids were comfortable using them, we queried participants regarding ease of use on a two-item Likert-type scale. Participants found the decision aids easy to use (mean [M] = 5.95; standard deviation [SD] = 0.83; 1 = “strongly disagree,” 7 = “strongly agree”) and found it easy to get the system to do what they wanted it to do (M = 5.08; SD = 1.32; 1 = “strongly disagree,” 7 = “strongly agree”).

## Stimulus Material

The stimulus materials for this study came from a previous experiment that collected high-stakes, unsanctioned deceptive and truthful interactions during interviews [35], and the materials have been successfully used in prior observer studies (e.g., [30]). The context for the stimulus materials was an Exline scenario [20], in which some interviewees opted to cheat while responding to a series of obscure trivia questions. The interviewees were all promised a financial reward for answering the trivia questions correctly, and the interviewer was called out of the interview room, leaving the answers in the room with the interviewee. Some interviewees opted to cheat and then lie about their cheating. The data set contains 22 interviews that were used in the experiment (7 deceptive, 15 truthful). All of the interactions were used for training the decision aid, and 10 interviews—5 truthful and 5 deceptive—were selected for stimulus materials during the experiment. Of the 5 guilty interviewees, 2 were male and 3 were female. Of the 5 truthful interviewees, 3 were male and 2 were female.

## Design

Upon arriving at the lab, participants were seated at a computer and randomly assigned to one of four conditions: unaided, indirect cues only (IDC-only), linguistic analysis only (LA-only), and both indirect cues and linguistic analysis (IDC-LA). The IDC-LA condition tested the full functionality of the human–computer assessment system. Each participant viewed an orientation video that provided a brief description of the decision aid and reported accuracy rates of past validation efforts of linguistic analysis. All participants were told that the decision aid’s accuracy rate is between 60 percent and 80 percent, a rate consistent with our pilot tests and past research in linguistic analysis [62]. Following the orientation, the participants viewed 10 randomly ordered interviews. After viewing an interview, the participant had access to the decision aid, if applicable. Before receiving a recommendation from the decision aid, participants in the IDC-only and IDC-LA conditions responded to indirect cue elicitation questions, which the system analyzed in forming its recommendation. Participants in the LA-only condition received a recommendation from the decision aid automatically. Participants then provided a credibility assessment consisting of a judgment of being guilty or not guilty of cheating.

To be consistent with actual assessment interviews, where actual truth is rarely known immediately following an interview, feedback regarding an interviewee’s actual truthfulness was not revealed until after the experiment had concluded. After viewing and assessing all 10 interactions, participants were questioned in a final survey about their perceptions and attitudes toward the decision aid and computer-aided credibility assessment.

## Decision Aid

This work presents a partially functional decision aid called the hybrid-behavioral analysis system (H‑ BAS), which has two major components. The first component presents linguistic analysis results from message feature mining. While all of the steps in message feature mining are automated, the transcription of the interaction to be analyzed is not. Therefore, for this experiment, transcriptions were manually created and message feature mining was then applied. While linguistic analysis has produced raw accuracy rates that exceed 80 percent [62], the recommendations provided by the linguistic analysis component during this experiment characterized 6 out of the 10 interviews correctly. The decision variable was formed using lexical diversity, verb quantity, affect ratio, group references, and activation as per Zhou et al. [61]. A more detailed description of message feature mining is provided in Appendix A .

It should be noted that while 60 percent accuracy may seem to be a small improvement over what is expected without computer assistance (approximately 54 percent [3]), the improvement represents a meaningful increment that is difficult for even trained observers to attain [22]. Further, the accuracy rate is similar to other rates demonstrated by Zhou et al. [62] using linguistic analysis. Moreover, the training data sets were highly limited, but more data should improve the accuracy of the decision aid. Finally, the moderate accuracy of the decision aid avoided range restriction problems and allowed for measurable performance improvement through the incorporation of indirect cues.

The second component of the H-BAS collects and analyzes indirect credibility cues from the user. This component was piloted twice using the stimulus material and was reviewed by credibility assessment experts to validate the method. The first pilot test consisted of untrained behavior coders who used the elicitation items in assessing the stimulus interviews. During the first pilot test, it was noted that participants would initially assess the credibility of the interviewee and then conform their responses to their assessments during indirect cue elicitation. This practice defeated the purpose of indirect cues, and consequently all items mentioning guilt and deceptiveness of the interviewee were removed. Further, at the suggestion of the participants, elicitation items were reworded for clarity. The final set of items elicits cues in the following categories: complexity, engagement, plausibility, uncertainty, cooperativeness, anxiety, affect, and suspicion. To ensure proper function of the elicitation items, a second pilot was conducted with trained behavior coders. Using the final items and accompanying scoring mechanism, the trained coders achieved a mean accuracy level of 62 percent. A description of indirect cue items and their validation is provided in Appendix A .

Both components also provide explanations about the functionality, credibility cues, decision models, and recommendations they present. The orientation on the decision aid and the provided explanations were intended to familiarize the users with the operations of the decision aid and to provide the users a mechanism whereby they could evaluate the performance of the decision aid. The decision aid was only partially functional; transcriptions were created by hand, and message feature mining was performed prior to the experiment. However, the decision aid is conservatively representative of the capabilities provided by an unobtrusive credibility assessment decision aid (see [29, 31, 61]). The decision aid prototype is shown in Figures 2 and 3.

## Post Hoc Analysis: Judgment Errors

## Discussion

The p urp ose of this study was to devel op theory-based hypotheses and to test them with an innovative decision aid in experimentation. Using predictions based on SDT and PDM, we tested the influence of linguistic cues and indirect cues on the accuracy of recommendations made by decision aids, how often users accepted those recommendations, and how accurate those assessments were.

The results suggest that, at least in our context, a system offers more accurate recommendations when both indirect cue elicitation and linguistic analysis are instantiated in the decision aid. The decision aid is more accurate with both components than with only one component—although the improvement from indirect cue elicitation is marginal—and the accuracy of the decision aid exceeds that of the unaided observer (H1, H2). Further, users accept recommendations more often when the decision implements the indirect elicitation component (H3). Our results confirm that recommendations based on linguistic analysis improve user accuracy in assessing credibility (H4). However, our results challenge the generalizability of past research that suggests indirect cues alone will improve user accuracy in credibility assessment. Users did not improve in credibility assessment accuracy when using a decision aid designed to elicit and analyze indirect cues (H5). Even when combining indirect cues with the linguistic analysis (H6), users did not experience a significant improvement in assessment accuracy.

In summary, linguistic analysis techniques improve recommendation accuracy and indirect cues add some marginal benefit. Elicitation of indirect cues improves the likelihood that users will accept the recommendations; however, the elicitation of indirect cues does not increase user assessment accuracy. The primary increase in user accuracy stems from the use of a decision aid implementing linguistic analysis. This is a possible indication that the two techniques (LA and IDC) act in fundamentally different theoretical ways.

The lack of support for H6 merits additional discussion. Our findings suggest that although the recommendations produced by the decision aid improve in accuracy by considering indirect cues, this benefit does not transfer to the system’s users. Further, users tend to accept recommendations that incorporate indirect cues more than recommendations based on linguistic cues. Thus, our research exposes a potentially dangerous scenario in which users are more likely to accept the recommendations of a decision aid, but the decision aid’s recommendations do not improve their assessment accuracy. The reasons for the poor user performance in utilizing indirect cues may stem from deficiencies in the system and in the user. We elaborate on these two potential sources of poor user performance, as follows.

## Source 1: System Deficiencies

The first possible explanation behind indirect cue failure is that the questions eliciting the indirect cues were somehow faulty or not diagnostic. This conclusion contradicts what has been shown in past research: complexity, engagement, plausibility, uncertainty, cooperativeness, anxiety, and affect have all been shown to be highly diagnostic across varying conditions [9, 15, 50, 63]. Such indirect cue elicitation techniques have been used previously with significant success [52], and during pilot tests for the H‑ BAS, the elicitation questions and scoring method produced promising accuracy rates.

The users in our context may not have found the recommendation helpful, indicating weakness in the interface design or the content. However, this explanation for the poor performance is difficult to support for two reasons. First, our findings show that the recommendations were accepted by the users in the large majority of judgments, thus indicating that they either believed the system’s recommendation or came to the same conclusion independently. Second, users rated the system’s perceived ease of use fairly high.

## Source 2: User Limitations

A number of plausible explanations exist for the poor performance of the IDC-only condition that stem from the user. First, the users may not have properly understood the questions eliciting the indirect cues. This misunderstanding is likely, given that wide variation in indirect cue scores occurred for the same interviewee (see Appendix B). For example, some participants thought an interview contained many details, while other participants thought that the same interview contained few details. This variation indicates potential reliability problems with participants’ responses and is problematic because the variation in responses results in variation in the decision aid’s recommendation.

This issue was likely due to the perceptual nature of the questions; that is, what one person believes to be a large number of details may be small for another. The provision of explanations was an attempt at attenuating this effect by encouraging common definitions of key terms during elicitation. However, this attempt was largely ineffective. Vrij et al. [52], in past work exploring indirect cues, used trained behavior coders. The participants in the second pilot tests for this work were also all trained behavioral coders. It is important to note that in our pilots, the coders were not trained to specifically identify the indirect cues elicited by the decision aid; however, they did have prior experience in carefully observing behavior.

An additional difficulty that the users faced was separating their judgments from the indirect cues that they observed. This difficulty was observed during the selection and first pilot testing of the indirect cue items, where participants would make an assessment and then ensure that all of their responses to the indirect cue questions matched their assessment. It may be unreasonable to expect that observers are capable of scoring indirect cues in an unbiased, balanced fashion when they are also required to provide a veracity judgment of what they observe. To avoid cognitive dissonance (e.g., [21]), the users may be ensuring that the recommendation the system produces is consistent with their unstated credibility belief. In terms of PDM, this is equivalent to having complete rather than partial control over the recommendation supplied by the aid. In such cases, indirect cues simply echo what the users already think, and thus they do not have the opportunity to consider a conflicting recommendation.

## Application to Research

In this paper, we build on a foundation started by other researchers (e.g., [16, 31]) who posit that the way to improve the accuracy of credibility assessment is to consider more cues that have been associated with deception. Credibility assessment is a complex task that may benefit from examining many different sets of cues. Using SDT, we investigated two sets—linguistic cues and perceptual cues. This research presents a mechanism for joining these vastly different sets of cues so that they can be analyzed and fused into a single recommendation regarding the credibility of an interviewee. This mechanism may be useful in other complex tasks that require significant information-processing capabilities, but also rely on perceptual information produced by a user or observer in coming to a solution. The results of our study present a hybrid approach that may be a useful model for systems intended to support complex tasks; however, determining the best method for merging perceptual information with objective information remains an open question.

Our findings also support the notion that participation in forming recommendations results in a greater tendency for contributors to accept the recommendations. While this is consistent with PDM, so too is the finding that participation in forming recommendations produces an inconsistent effect when measured by performance gains [37]. Evidence of inconsistent performance gains is an important caveat as IS researchers continue to probe the potential role of the user of recommendation systems. In contexts such as e-commerce, user participation in recommendation formation may be a boon. However, our research suggests that in complex tasks such as credibility assessment, user participation may hamper instead of enhance performance.

Finally, our study challenges the generalizability of past studies (e.g., [52]) that found using indirect cues alone can improve credibility assessment accuracy. We found in our context that indirect cues did not improve users’ accuracy in assessing credibility when used alone or in conjunction with linguistic analysis. This finding has serious implications for deception researchers and suggests that efforts in developing computer-based decision aids to assist in credibility assessment may best be expended by focusing on extending the capabilities of an observer via the provision of automated tools. The place of indirect cues in the process of credibility assessment merits further research to explain the contradictory findings in the literature, and we revisit this topic in the limitations and future research section.

## Application to Practice

Our study’s results, when juxtaposed against previous related studies, underscore the difficulties of conducting effective and accurate credibility assessment in research and practice. In general, deceivers usually have an advantage in most deceptive interactions, and many interaction observers would benefit from knowing that they cannot assess credibility as well as they think they can. The introduction of a decision aid to assist in correctly attributing credibility may appear on the surface to be a trivial exercise, when in fact it is not. The introduction of a decision aid may have many unexpected consequences, not the least of which is the lack of improvement in assessment accuracy, as was shown with the introduction of indirect cue–only elicitation in this study. Despite the complexity of introducing a decision aid into the assessment process, a number of important findings of this study apply to practice.

First, users tend to have the highest credibility assessment accuracy when given a recommendation based on automated cue collection and analysis. In our study, the most significant improvement in user accuracy came from using an automated decision aid that extended the abilities of the observer by providing a recommendation based on a linguistic analysis. In our context, the user filled a supervisory role and approved or dismissed the recommendation of the decision aid.

Second, at present, little gain is likely from including indirect cues in the decision aid’s recommendation formulation to increase credibility assessment accuracy. Incorporating indirect cues improves recommendation accuracy, but only by a small—yet significant—amount, and that increase in accuracy does not transfer to users’ assessments. Further, significant time cost occurs as the users respond to the cue elicitation. However, we are not prepared to dismiss indirect cues outright. Situations may exist in which credibility assessment based on indirect cues is desirable (e.g., for rapid screening and for evaluating short interactions [52]). If indirect cues are to be used, it may be important to segregate the judgment role from the indirect cue–collection role. Segregation of these roles may limit the effect seen in our study, where participants mold their indirect cue judgments to match their assessments of credibility. Further, explicit training of those who intend to provide IDCs may be crucial. The provision of explanations that observers may access does not seem to provide the reliability necessary for credibility assessment using indirect cues. Relying on the observers to recognize when their knowledge about an indirect cue is deficient presents the problem of many observers assuming that they are skilled enough to perform the task when they are not.

Finally, our study suggests that one can improve adoption of a decision aid’s recommendation through eliciting indirect cues from the user. If user acceptance of the aid’s recommendation is of high priority in a specific context, it may be helpful to incorporate functionality, such as indirect cue elicitation.

## Limitations and Future Research

A key limitation of our study was that participants were asked to make credibility assessments based on a prerecorded video and did not have an opportunity to interact with the source. In many real-life credibility assessment situations, participants have the opportunity to exchange information and ask follow-up questions during interviews. Consequently, future research should examine the benefit of using hybrid decision aids in interactive communication exchanges to assess credibility.

Further, we currently conceptualize indirect cues as gestalt cues that are to be judged holistically across an interaction. This is just one conceptualization of indirect cues, among potentially many. For example, indirect cue elicitation may be specific to a particular behavior and may be constrained to a specific time. Whereas our conceptualization of indirect cues did not produce a compelling increase in assessment accuracy, it is possible that another conceptualization might. However, other conceptualizations will need to balance specificity in indirect cue elicitation with information overload. This is an issue not addressed in this paper that future research should address.

In addition, this study used a student sample, and participants did not have experience with the decision aid prior to the experiment and likely did not have formal training in carefully observing the behavior of others. However, the students were given an orientation about the use of the decision aid and were offered explanations about the function of the decision aid, and none of the participants appeared to have difficulty using it. Future research should test the generalizability of our results to other populations aside from students. Other populations that may react differently to the decision aid may include trained behavior coders (similar to those used in our pilot) and law enforcement professionals. These groups may be more sensitive to and better able to gauge indirect cues, and samples drawn from these groups may reveal to a greater degree where potential problems exist with indirect cue elicitation.

Finally, no serious consequences were associated with incorrectly assessing credibility. Testing real-life consequences clearly violates human-subject protections, and thus is infeasible for experimentation; however, future experimental research could at least raise the stakes involved to better emulate real-life contexts where credibility assessments are performed.

## Conclusion

This study addresses the growing need to accurately assess credibility. Doing so has great potential to save businesses and society considerable resources and to prevent substantial harm. Using a theoretical approach grounded in SDT and PDM, this work explores methods intended to increase the credibility assessment accuracy of decision aids and credibility assessment accuracy of users. A new hybrid decision-aid system was designed to test the influence of indirect cue elicitation on credibility assessment performance. The collection of both perceptual and automatically extracted cues significantly improved the accuracy of recommendations produced by decision aids. Although the collection of perceptual cues improved user acceptance of the aid’s recommendation, it did not improve user assessment accuracy. These findings should inform the design of future systems intended to support credibility assessment and have important implications for future systems that may require a hybrid design.

## Appendix A: Components of the H‑ BAS

## Message Feature Mining

The p rototyp e system that was devel op ed for this work presents the results of a type of linguistic analysis called message feature mining. This method was developed by Zhou et al. [61, 62] and is an extension of previously existing manual methods of credibility assessment, namely, criteria-based content analysis (CBCA), reality monitoring (RM), and scientific content analysis (SCA) [50]. The process was originally developed for use with persistent conversations (e.g., chat, e-mail). However, the method has been extended to face-to-face encounters [7, 31].

Message feature mining is a fully automated method of credibility assessment that requires sample data for training. However, message feature mining requires transcripts of conversations as input. The prototype does not currently offer automatic transcription of face-to-face interactions (although such functionality is feasible). The transcriptions of the interactions used in this work were performed manually. Once the transcriptions are complete, lexical features are extracted from the text. These features fall into categories of quantity, complexity, uncertainty, nonimmediacy, expressivity, and diversity, informality, specificity, and affect. The potential features are extracted from the training data, and because of the large number of potential features a feature-pruning step is adopted. The pruned feature set is then used in a classification algorithm. For this work, logistic regression was selected, although other classification algorithms could certainly be used (e.g., decision trees, neural networks, support vector machines).

Once message feature mining is complete, the H-BAS presents the results to the user. The H‑ BAS presents two types of information to the user: numeric and explanatory. The numeric scores represent the raw score of the classification algorithm (probability of credible response), dichotomous judgment, and level of confidence (strength of classification). The H‑ BAS also provides natural language explanations that detail the message feature mining process, each of the numeric scores, the features used in classification, individual feature levels, and a general justification for the recommendation offered. These explanations are roughly analogous to definitional, rule-trace, and justification explanations in other expert systems (e.g., [57]).

## Indirect Cue Elicitation

Indirect cue elicitation is accomplished through posing questions to the user of the H‑ BAS (who has also observed the face-to-face interaction to be examined) and retrieving perception-based judgments concerning the interaction as a whole. Of critical importance is selecting which questions are most diagnostic and not overburdening the user with too many questions. Therefore, an extensive literature search was performed to select diagnostic perceptual cues. Among the research reviewed were a series of meta-analyses detailing diagnostic cues [9, 15, 50, 63]. The review produced a list of candidate diagnostic cues. Items were collected that captured these diagnostic cues, and the items fell into general categories of complexity, engagement, plausibility, uncertainty, cooperativeness, anxiety, affect, and direct measures (e.g., suspicion). With the exception of direct measures, all categories demonstrated large effect sizes (d > | 0.20 |). To ensure that the items properly captured the intended cue, the items were reviewed by credibility assessment experts and were piloted with the experimental stimuli. As a result of the pilot tests and subsequent expert review, the wording of some of the items was adjusted and one question was dropped. (The dropped question was an item that asked observers if they thought the person they were viewing was guilty of what they were accused.) It was noted during the pilot tests and in the ensuing discussion that participants would fill out the question concerning guilt first and then complete the rest of the items, conforming their answers to correspond to their estimation of guilt. This practice defeated the purpose of indirect cue elicitation; therefore, the item was removed. The items were piloted again with the stimuli by five trained behavior coders. The resulting items are shown and reliabilities across coders are reported in Table A 1. All items are scored on a seven-point scale with “strongly disagree” and “strongly agree” as end points.

Indirect cue elicitation is incorporated in the H-BAS by posing each question item to users after they observe an interaction. After a user responds to each item, the H‑ BAS tabulates the results and presents a recommendation. Consistent with other manual credibility scoring methods (e.g., RM and CBCA), the H‑ BAS equally weights each item in arriving at its recommendation. Using this scoring method, the five trained coders mentioned previously achieved an accuracy rate of 62 percent.

The H‑ BAS also provides detailed explanations that offer definitions for key terms and clarifies the relationship between the items and credibility. The prototype also gives scoring results for each category and dynamically generated justifications supporting the recommendations (again in line with definitional, rule-trace, and justification explanations).

The results from both indirect cue elicitation and message feature mining are then combined to produce a single recommendation (although the scores from both indirect cues and linguistic cues are displayed to the user). This combination is accomplished through averaging the two scores. Sample explanations provided by the H‑ BAS are shown in Figure A1. These explanations are offered to users if they click on the Define Terms or Explain buttons beside the first indirect cue question. (The interviewee provided many details.)
