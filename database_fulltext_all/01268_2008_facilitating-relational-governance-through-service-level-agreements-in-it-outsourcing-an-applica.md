# Facilitating relational governance through service level agreements in IT outsourcing: An application of the commitment–trust theory

Jahyun Goo ⁎, C. Derrick Huang

Information Technology and Operations Management, Barry Kaye College of Business, Florida Atlantic University, 777 Glades Road, Boca Raton, FL 33431-0991, United States

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 18 June 2007 Received in revised form 9 June 2008 Accepted 20 June 2008 Available online 25 June 2008

Keywords: Outsourcing management IT SLAs Relational governance Trust–commitment theory

## a b s t r a c t

Firms increasingly rely on outsourcing for strategic IT decisions, and the many sophisticated forms of outsourcing require signi<sup>fi</sup>cant management attention to ensure their success. Two forms of interorganizational governance–formal control and relational–have been used to examine the management of IT outsourcing relationships. Contrary to the conventional substitution view recent studies have found that these two governance modes are complementary; however, the dynamics of their interactions remain unexplored. Based on the commitment–trust theory, this paper focuses on how the formal control mechanism can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the relational governance in an outsourcing engagement. Using service level agreements (SLAs) as a proxy for formal control, this study <sup>fi</sup>nds that eleven contractual elements, characterized as foundation, governance, and change management variables in an SLA, are positively related to the trust and relationship commitment among the parties. Trust and commitment, in turn, positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence relational outcomes that we theorize would contribute to outsourcing success. Both research and practical implications of the results are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

As information technology (IT) becomes an important tool in a dynamically changing business environment, <sup>fi</sup>rms are increasingly exploring outsourcing options to support strategic IT decisions. Recent outsourcing practices, such as business process outsourcing [66], offshore outsourcing [82,86], and capability outsourcing<sup>1</sup> [32], place a heavy emphasis on nurturing a high level of trust and commitment using hybrid governing skills such as formal and relational contracting throughout the course of the engagement [16,51]. As such, the skill sets for managing the relationship between the service recipient (SR) and the service provider (SP) have become a new strategic asset [18]. Similar to marriages or friendships based on trust and commitment, however, not all outsourcing relationships work well. A deteriorating relationship can result in higher costs, operational disruption, and lost business opportunities [31], leaving the parties wishing that they had planned better in addition to relying exclusively on trust and commitment. Therefore, greater understanding of how to manage IT outsourcing relationships to create and sustain strategic value, particularly for the client <sup>fi</sup>rm, is highly desired.

Prior research has suggested two distinct modes of governance for managing interorganizational relationship (IOR): formal controls–driven by written contracts and other management-initiated mechanisms designed to guide behavior toward desired objectives–and relational governance– driven by unwritten, practice-based mechanisms designed to in<sup>fl</sup>uence interorganizational behavior. It is widely observed that an increasing range of outsourcing activities is organized through complex contracts [20,50,84], while other studies <sup>fi</sup>nd that IT outsourcing arrangements employ various relational governance mechanisms [34,54,62]. This is understandable, because effective collaboration is likely to require alignment of both interests and actions between <sup>fi</sup>rms [36,66]. While a contract attempts to align incentives and interests by providing an administrative architecture, within which an outsourcing partnership proceeds [37], relational governance addresses how the information exchange between the SR and the SP promotes both a shared understanding of the task environment and mutual adjustments to align actions to ensure success of IT outsourcing relationship [78,79,102,103].

Consistent with the above observations, recent studies have shown that contracts could be used to effectively manage various dimensions of relational exchange in IT outsourcing [66,67,95] and that formal and relational governance function are complementary, not substitutive [7,78]. However, there has been relatively little understanding of how contracts could build and improve relationship quality in outsourcing. Given the positive association between relationship quality and IT outsourcing success [34,62], identifying contractual provisions that could foster favorable relational quality helps management to strategically use contracts in promoting successful outsourcing relationship. Against this backdrop, this study attempts to reconcile these two governance modes by exploring the complementarity of service level agreements (SLAs), a formal control mechanism, in the development of relational governance in IT outsourcing arrangements (cf, [78,83]).

The rest of paper is organized as follows. First, we review the literature on the commitment–trust theory and its various relationships in relational governance. We then provide an overview of SLAs in IT outsourcing as a form of formal contracts. This is followed by the research model and hypotheses and, subsequently, a section on research methods, including data collection and statistical analysis. The results are presented, followed by a discussion of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The paper concludes with the limitations of this study and proposed future research directions.

## 2. Trust and commitment in relational governance

The centrality of the relationship has led researchers to identify key attributes that ensure successful relational exchange [19,24,27,41,49,60,68,74,99,102]. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant research effort has also been directed to explaining how these necessary relationship attributes are developed. Among them, Morgan and Hunt [74] theorize that trust and relationship commitment play key mediating roles in the process of relationship development and relationship performance. When both trust and relationship commitment–“not just one or the other”–are present, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, productivity, and effectiveness of relational outcomes can be produced [74]. They posit that (1) relationship bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and termination costs relationship in<sup>fl</sup>uence commitment, (2) shared values in<sup>fl</sup>uence both commitment and trust, and (3) communication and opportunistic behavior directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust (and, through trust, indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uence commitment). Although relationship commitment and trust are highly desirable “qualitative outcomes” themselves as components of the relationship development process [73], Morgan and Hunt propose <sup>fi</sup>ve additional qualitative outcomes and theorize that these outcomes promote relationship success. “Acquiescence” and “propensity to leave” directly <sup>fl</sup>ow from relationship commitment. “Functional con<sup>fl</sup>ict” and “uncertainty” are the direct results of trust. And “Cooperation” arises directly from both relationship commitment and trust. Their model is shown in Fig. 1.

Trust and relationship commitment have also been found as key factors in the context of managing IT outsourcing [13,83]. In such an engagement, two organizations cooperate toward sometimes ambiguous outcomes involving the exchange of invisible assets, as compared to de<sup>fi</sup>nable outcomes over the exchange of tangible assets in common transactional relationships [45]. Although these two attributes can exist at the outset among partners who have had prior transactional relationships or social ties [23], many interorganizational relationships are formed among parties who are initially unfamiliar with each other. Under this circumstance, some type of initial trust building mechanism might be required, because parties have not worked together long enough to develop an interaction history [69]. The initial trust situations are becoming more common due to the increasing number of temporary task teams and project engagements [71]. In such cases, trust and relationship commitment in interorganizational relationships could begin with legal recourse such as contracts and promises [69,87,103], a developmental process consistent with the premise of institutional trust that views the formal contracting as an initial trust building mechanism in interorganizational trust [25,43,69,103]. This informs the research question we address in this paper: How can contracts be used to foster attributes of relational governance that mediate success of IT outsourcing engagements?

Consistent with Morgan and Hunt's [74] trust–commitment theory, we theorize that the presence of trust and relationship commitment is central to successful IT outsourcing relationship. In particular, we incorporate the two governance perspectives and propose that formal contracts promote the development of relational governance in IT outsourcing arrangements [78]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this study focuses on the direction from SLAs as formal contracts to trust and commitment as key relational attributes, and posits that a well-developed and well-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed SLA can promote trusting exchange relationships and the relationship commitment in IT outsourcing engagements. We also note that this complementary relationship between formal contracts and relational governance is reciprocal; that is, trust and relationship commitment in relational governance could re<sup>fi</sup>ne SLA that further support greater relational exchanges [78,79].

## 3. SLA: formal contract in IT outsourcing arrangements

An SLA is a formal written agreement, often developed jointly by SR and SP, that speci<sup>fi</sup>es products or services to be provided at a certain level in an outsourcing arrangement. Most often, after the overall outsourcing deal is negotiated by lawyers, SLAs are added as an addendum to the contract to provide project-speci<sup>fi</sup>c terms and conditions. The inclusion of SLAs results in a range of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Some claim that the key to managing successful IT outsourcing relationships is related to the quality of the SLAs terms [72], and it is widely observed that an increasing range of exchange activities is governed by well-structured SLAs [47,66,88]. Prior studies have indicated that SLAs help to ensure that the early, more vulnerable stages of exchange are managed successfully [88].

To study the dynamics of SLAs and outsourcing deals, Goo et al. [30] identify eleven contractual elements in SLAs that could be important in managing IT outsourcing relationships. Each of these eleven elements refers to a set of interrelated formal contract clauses re<sup>fl</sup>ecting substantive goals or themes to be realized. They further categorize these contractual elements into three areas: change management, governance, and foundation characteristics (Table $1 ) . { } ^ { 2 { \overset { \cdot } { } } }$ Foundation characteristics in SLAs deal with the ground rules [91] to build a spirit of agreement and ownership of the functional exchange among those entities involved in its development [88]. They help decision makers understand and share the intent of the creation of the relationship, as well as set clear standards of conduct by de<sup>fi</sup>ning what the SR and SP are obligated to deliver and at what cost [79]. Governance characteristics harmonize the relationship through a clear statement of measurements, communication channels and methods, con-<sup>fl</sup>ict arbitration, and penalty and rewards. These are important, because to ensure that the relationship remains on course requires continually assessing the values that the relationship is creating for the various stakeholders [9]. Lastly, change management characteristics address how uncertainties can be handled. Because the rapidly changing IT environment and business conditions often require a fast response by SPs to deliver new services or modify current one, a change management plan is a critical dimension in SLAs. They ensure that the SP will deliver valuable inputs to the SR and strengthen the SR/SP relationship when constructing contractual and service level de<sup>fi</sup>nitions in situations of increasing uncertainty [85].

Using Goo's [28] operational constructs for the structure of SLAs while extending prior models for trust and commitment, this study examines the effects of speci<sup>fi</sup>c contractual elements (as antecedents) on the cultivation of trust and relationship commitment (as key relational attributes of relational governance), and further assesses their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the qualitative relationship outcomes in IT outsourcing. We argue that cooperative behaviors, the basis for companion working spirit in IT outsourcing arrangements, are emanated from psychological attributes such as trust and commitment, similar to other relational exchanges [55]. Trust and commitment in outsourcing relationships can be nurtured over time through joint development of SLA as well as ongoing exchanges guided by the elements of SLA. For example, outsourcing relationships generally begin with negotiations over joint expectations, risk and trust through formal bargaining or informal sense making [79]. Then the SR and the SP reach an agreement on the obligations and rules for future action in the relationship. At this point, the terms and governance structure of the relationship are established and codi<sup>fi</sup>ed in a formal relationship-based contract as a commitment to the engagements [67,95] and informally understood in a “psychological contract” during the contracting process [54]. The parties might then use their perceptions to develop a view of how the partner will act in the future, thus forming their trust in the partner. In the next section, we describe the research model and its associated hypotheses.

## 4. Model and hypotheses

To examine the connections between SLAs and trust and commitment, we extend Morgan and Hunt [74] model (as shown in Fig. 1) to the case of IT outsourcing. The relationship commitment, which increases with the perception of the other party's commitment [2], is conceptualized in terms of the SR's perception on parties' intention to stay in the relationship and willingness to put in effort to maintain the relationship. Morgan and Hunt [74] suggest that relationship commitment depends on relationship bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, termination costs, shared values and trust. And we base the measure of trust on a conceptualization of McKnight et al. [69], re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the SR's expectation that the SP will ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the requirements speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the SLAs in future actions.<sup>3</sup> Individuals interact, experience, and observe the actions of a relationship partner (e.g., how they have communicated, whether they have kept commitments or been honest, etc.) and then use these perceptions to develop a view of how the partner will act in the future (i.e., their trust in the partner). We believe that SLA contract clauses encode many of these precursors and thus reinforce an increase in the commitment of both parties in the context of IT outsourcing. Using Goo's [28] eleven SLA elements, we posit that (1) foundation characteristics (re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the relationship bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and shared values as in Morgan and Hunt model) and governance characteristics (re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the relationship termination costs and communication <sup>fl</sup>ow in Morgan and Hunt model) directly and positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence both commitment and trust; (2) change management characteristics (specifying to mitigate opportunistic behavior originated from requirement uncertainty) directly and positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust; and (3) trust directly and positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences commitment. Our model is shown in Fig. 2.

In addition, similar to Morgan and Hunt [74], we argue that these attributes of trust and commitment in relationships become source of qualitative relationship outcomes in functional exchanges of an outsourcing relationship. While relationship commitment and trust themselves are highly desirable “qualitative outcomes ”[73], we model <sup>fi</sup>ve relationship outcomes that have been commonly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as in IT outsourcing literatures [27,49,52,62,66,98] and theorize that these outcomes promote relationship success in IT outsourcing arrangements. First, “Acquiescence” and “Relationship Durability” directly <sup>fl</sup>ow from Relationship Commitment. Second, “Harmonious Con<sup>fl</sup>ict Resolution” and “Active Information Exchange” are the direct results of trust. Third, “Cooperation” arises directly from both relationship commitment and trust. In the following sections, we detail the development of each of the hypotheses in our model (Fig. 2).

4.1. Precursors of trust and relationship commitment: SLA characteristics

## 4.1.1. Foundation characteristics

The SLA elements related to foundation characteristics can impact parties' relationship commitment in many ways. Service Level Objectives specify common goals and general commitment, and Process Ownership Plan sets clear standards of conduct by de<sup>fi</sup>ning roles and responsibilities of the parties involved in the outsourcing arrangement. Such provisions allow the SR and the SP to share common expectations and capabilities. As the organizational commitment literature suggests, when partners share values they indeed will be more committed to their relationships [2,38,46,74]. In addition, being more knowledgeable about each other's business through Service Level Contents allows parties to more effectively cooperate and, for the SP, to carry out services that the SR depends upon. As such, the cultivation of these mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial IORs can be “institutionalized” through foundation characteristics that encourage a mindset of continuous commitment to the relationship [69]. Moreover, since the intention of the relationship is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in these foundation elements of SLAs, the objectives and motivations are at least partially understood and shared by the individuals who may inherit the responsibility for carrying out the terms of the SLA [54,79], thus making the relationship stable over time. And such elements re<sup>fl</sup>ect the relationship's consistency and thereby reinforce the perception that both parties intentions to remain in the relationship prevail [1]. Therefore:

H1. Foundation characteristics of the SLA positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence relationship commitment of both parties to IT outsourcing relationships.

Shared values–the extent to which partners have common beliefs about what behaviors, goals, and policies are important and appropriate–are a direct precursor of both trust and relationship commitment [19,40,74], and those SLA elements of foundation characteristics are explicating common beliefs between outsourcing partners. Joint development of expectations about the deliverables to be included in the SLAs would likely reinforce a sense of structural assurance and situational normality, thus positively informing trusting beliefs [69]. Furthermore, process ownership and level of service to be delivered identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in SLAs formalize the roles and the responsibilities of the SP and the SR, and, as such, enhance trust in the outsourcing relationships [5,83]. Finally, trust emerges when outsourcing relationships are characterized by bilateral convergence of interests [56]. SLA elements of foundation characteristics provide grounds for building and sustaining trusting beliefs of the SR, where their interest convergence with those of the SP can be assured. Therefore:

H2. Foundation characteristics of the SLA positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust of SR in SP.

## 4.1.2. Governance characteristics

The governance characteristics of SLAs aim to maintain relationships through a clear statement of the performance measurements, con<sup>fl</sup>ict arbitration, penalty and rewards, exit policy, and communication plan. SLA provisions underlying governance characteristics can build commitment in the IT outsourcing relationship. First, the Communication Plan sustains information to foster con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the continuity of the relationship [73]. Second, the Enforcement Plan supports the motivation to continue a relationship, because it requires the parties to take into account the legal and economic consequences of violating explicit terms of the contract. The prospect of termination costs leads the parties to view the relationship as important and reinforce commitment [46]. For example, the Enforcement Plan codi<sup>fi</sup>es the penalties associated with opportunistic behaviors or termination without due cause to protect the SP's investment for performing the outsourcing relationship. When the SP perceives that its investments are secured because of the terms of the plan, the SP becomes more committed to the relationship. This, in turn, increases the SR's commitment as a result of the better perception of the SP's commitment [2]. Third, the Measurement Charter sets the benchmarks and assesses whether the SP is meeting the target performance and accomplishing stipulated terms in the SLA, and <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely commit themselves to establishing, developing, and maintaining relationships with such partners [92]. The Measurement Charter can also reduce the uncertainty about behaviors and outcomes by providing formal rules and procedures to govern the relationship. Such clear guidelines specifying the rights and obligations of both parties can improve coordination and increase both parties' commitment. Therefore:

H3. Governance characteristics of the SLA positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence relationship commitment of both parties to IT outsourcing relationships.

Trust in IT outsourcing relationships is largely associated with creating con<sup>fi</sup>dence of the SR in the SP as a result of that party's ability to meet the target performance and accomplish the stipulated terms in the SLA [49]. And the contractual elements associated with the governance characteristics can enhance trust in multiple ways. For instance, the Enforcement Plan serves as “carrot and stick” to ensure that the SP satis<sup>fi</sup>es the provisions in the SLA and, thus, supports the SR's trust in the SP [90]. In addition, a mutually agreed method of Conflict Arbitration Plan engenders feelings of procedural justice between the two parties. These perceptions encourage positive beliefs about the SP's integrity by suggesting that there can be harmonious outcomes to con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution and tends to reduce the scope, intensity, and frequency of opportunistic behavior of the SP, which, in turn, will lead to mutual trusting beliefs [101]. Moreover, specifying communication processes such as the frequency, relevance and timeliness of interactions facilitates consistent knowledge exchange [74] that reinforces positive SR perceptions about the faithfulness of the SP [17]. Such integrative method of governing the exchanges speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the SLA should lead to the development of trusting beliefs of the SR in the SP. Therefore:

H4. Governance characteristics of the SLA positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust of SR in SP.

## 4.1.3. Change management characteristics

Transaction-cost economics prescribes that IORs incorporate mechanisms that facilitate joint adaptation to problems raised from unforeseen changes into the contract. Mayer and Argyres [67] <sup>fi</sup>nd that changes to the structure of contracts are made to re<sup>fl</sup>ect learning over time. Most notable contract modi<sup>fi</sup>cations involve inclusion of planning for contingencies and processes for incorporating feedback. Thus the purpose of change management elements in SLAs is to establish ground rules and procedures for dealing with future uncertainties, because IT environments evolve rapidly and business conditions often require fast response from the SP to modify current services or deliver new ones. These characteristics also seek to ensure that the SP continues to deliver valuable input to the SR and that the SR/SP relationship remains close even when precise speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of services and service levels may not be possible in highly uncertain situations.

Four contractual elements underlie change management characteristics (see Table 1). The Future Demand Management Plan speci<sup>fi</sup>es the processes that will be used by the SR and the SP to de<sup>fi</sup>ne requirements, prioritize initiatives, prepare and approve schedules, and manage the implementation of new or modi<sup>fi</sup>ed services. The Anticipated Change Plan ensures that the right processes, people, and tools are in place to enable the SR and SP to stay on top of changes and ensure effective decisionmaking. The Innovation Plan, often synchronized with Enforcement Plans, identi<sup>fi</sup>es the structure and processes for introducing new innovations. The Feedback Plan documents the feedback process and provides the road map for ef<sup>fi</sup>cient adjustments by identifying all affected areas and resources.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, change management characteristics in the SLA help the SR build trust in the SP by serving as repositories for knowledge about how to govern future collaborations [67]. The change management elements including the aforementioned four plans provide the ground rules and procedures for future contingencies, which would likely lead to desired outcomes if followed. These plans are often open-ended provisions that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the expectations that both parties are willing to adapt as circumstances change, which reinforces mutual perceptions of benevolence [66]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, these plans seek to ensure that the SP will continue to deliver valuable inputs to the SR and that the relationship will remain close even though service level de<sup>fi</sup>nitions may be unrealistic in times of greater uncertainty. And such provisions in the SLA support a sense of situational normality with the implication that the SP in the contingent situation would still act normally and can be trusted. Therefore:

H5. Change management characteristics of the SLA positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust of the SR in the SP.

## 4.1.4. SR trust in the SP

SR trust in the SP refers to the SR's beliefs about the favorable characteristics of the SP such as benevolence and integrity/honesty. The trusting beliefs re<sup>fl</sup>ect the SR's expectation that the SP will ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the requirements speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the SLAs in future actions, and are distinct from trusting intentions, which is the willingness to depend on the SP.

Trust is a trait embedded in every exchange relationship and a necessary condition for relational governance [69,74,101]. Once exchange partners are granted “trustworthy” status, they are expected to behave accordingly in the future. Social exchange theory supports this causal relationship through the principle of generalized reciprocity, which holds that the SR trusts whenever the SP has been trusted in prior exchanges and defects in response to defection [60]. And economists emphasize that such rational calculation with respect to the expectations of future exchanges prompt trust in the present [41,69]. Therefore, SR's perceptions and its SP's intent to trust in the relationship re<sup>fl</sup>ect the relationship's stability, and greater trust leads to the higher level of commitment of IORs [74,101]. Relationships imbued with trust are often valued as “the cornerstone of the strategic partnership,” and parties are likely to commit themselves to such relationships [68]. Therefore:

H6. Trust positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences commitment of both parties to IT outsourcing relationships.

## 4.2. Outcomes of trust and relationship commitment

Following Morgan and Hunt [74], we posit that trust and relationship commitment become source of qualitative relationship outcomes in functional exchanges of an outsourcing relationship. Five qualitative outcomes are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the context of IT outsourcing arrangements: Acquiescence, Relationship Durability, Harmonious Con<sup>fl</sup>ict Resolution, Active Information Exchange, and Cooperation.

## 4.2.1. Acquiescence and Relationship Durability

Drawing on marketing literature [57,74], we de<sup>fi</sup>ne Acquiescence as the degree to which SP (SR) accepts or adheres to SR's (SP's) speci<sup>fi</sup>c requests or policies. Acquiescence is reactive and associated with the performance outcome of compliance [57]. Further, relationship commitment is related to relationship termination cost, which is positively related to Acquiescence [97]. For example, the SR might <sup>fi</sup>nd itself locked in the services delivered by the SP even though the SR has the ultimate power to terminate the contract at any time, but switching costs tend to prevent that option. On the other hand, the SP may <sup>fi</sup>nd itself dependent on the SR for remuneration for services rendered and return on relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets invested. As such Acquiescence between the SR and the SP can be positively in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by relationship commitment [74]. Therefore:

H7. Relationship commitment positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences Acquiescence of both parties in IT outsourcing relationships.

We use a construct labeled “Relationship Durability,” replacing the “propensity to leave” in Morgan and Hunt model to make all hypothesized relationships positive. Relationship Durability is the perception of SR that SR and SP will likely continue the relationship in the (reasonably) near future [29]. We posit that the strong positive connection between relationship commitment and stability of the company– customer relationship [74] also holds at the interorganizational level of IT outsourcing relationship. Just as excessive employee turnover is costly for employers, partnership instability is costly. As such, durability is a desirable performance outcome [57] that can be achieved through fostering commitment. Therefore:

H8. Relationship Commitment positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences Relationship Durability in IT outsourcing relationships.

## 4.2.2. Cooperation

Cooperation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the spirit of working together by partners on complementary activities with the objective of achieving mutual goals [4,27]. In this de<sup>fi</sup>nition, Cooperation involves more than just adhering to requirements; instead, it is a process of working together to identify and implement the best possible solutions. We posit that both relationship commitment and trust directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree of Cooperation between the SR and the SP. A partner committed to the relationship will cooperate with another member because of a desire to make the relationship work [74]. For example, with time, misunderstanding, con<sup>fl</sup>icts, and changing expectations between SR and SP are inevitable because each participant has its own objectives, and these factors can become cause for rethinking the terms of the relationship [80]. However, SR and SP might continue to cooperate because both parties' relationship commitment is high, and each participant gives up a certain degree of autonomy in favor of achieving those objectives [21]. Therefore:

H9. Relationship commitment positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences Cooperation in IT outsourcing relationships.

Both theory and empirical evidence indicate that trust also leads to Cooperation [3,19]. Through prisoner's dilemma experiments in game theory [75], it is suggested that the initiation of Cooperation requires trust and that a party will undertake highrisk, coordinated behaviors if trust exists. Thus, trust in the SP enables not only the SR following SP's IT practices or processes but also proactively suggesting better IT practices or processes. Once trust is established, <sup>fi</sup>rms learn that coordinated, joint efforts will lead to outcomes that exceed what the <sup>fi</sup>rm would achieve if it acted solely in its own best interests [8]. In addition, within the context of a trusting relationship, <sup>fi</sup>rms sometimes are willing to owe each other's favor [4]. Therefore:

H10. Trust positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences Cooperation in IT outsourcing relationships.

4.2.3. Harmonious Conflict Resolution and Active Information Exchange

Con<sup>fl</sup>ict in an outsourcing arrangement is inevitable, given the complexity of technology, dynamic and fast-paced business environment, and the disparate goals of the parties. It often leads to the destructive consequences: hostility and bitterness. Thus, one of the most crucial skills required to manage ongoing relationships with the SP is the ability to resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icts [48,62]. Harmonious Con<sup>fl</sup>ict Resolution refers to the extent to which the SR and the SP achieve mutually satisfying resolutions of their con<sup>fl</sup>icts to eventually replace disagreements with agreement and consensus [65,81]. When con<sup>fl</sup>icts are resolved amicably, such disagreements are often referred to as functional conflict, because they prevent stagnation, stimulate interest and curiosity, and provide a medium through which problems can be aired and solutions arrived at [17,19]. It is known that trust often leads a partner to perceive that future con<sup>fl</sup>icting episodes will be functional, and trusting parties accept some level of con<sup>fl</sup>ict as being just another part of doing business [4]. We therefore argue that the SR that has developed strong trust in the SP is more likely to work out their disagreements harmoniously.

H11. Trust positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences Harmonious Con<sup>fl</sup>ict Resolution in IT outsourcing relationships.

We also view trust as a prerequisite for Active Information Exchange, often considered a critical determinant of outsourcing success [61]. Active Information Exchange refers to the formal and informal sharing or exchange of meaningful and timely information between <sup>fi</sup>rms [4]. Perhaps the most straightforward way for encouraging the information exchange in an IOR is to sign a legal document de<sup>fi</sup>ning the scope of information sharing. However, the establishment of trust can preserve and deepen the information sharing relationship that comes from contractual agreements such as SLAs. Most notably, the SR and the SP who do not trust one another are likely to fail to exchange information for the fear of exploitation [44]. In addition, from the transaction-cost perspective, self-enforcing safeguards such as trust contribute to a freer and greater exchange of information between committed exchange partners, because decision makers do not believe that it is necessary to protect themselves from others' opportunistic behaviors [14]. Therefore:

H12. Trust positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences Active Information Exchange in IT outsourcing relationships.

## 4.3. Control variables

The model incorporates three control variables: length of association, type of IT activity outsourced, and extent of substitution. Besides the effect of SLA (i.e., formal contracts), the length of association between SRs and SPs that generates interactions over time provides a foundation for interorganizational trust by constraining opportunism in an interorganizational exchange. For example, past interactions allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to select those partners who have demonstrated lower propensity to engage in opportunistic behavior [35]. The effect of length of association on the development of trust has received considerable support by research on social networks [15,35,67,93]. The type of outsourced IT activity represents an important control variable that could affect the level of relational outcomes. For instance, outsourcing engagements for comprehensive services, such as company-speci<sup>fi</sup>c application development (e.g., American Airline-Sabre), generally invoke conscious attention of the SR to the process of developing relationships between the SR and the SP over time. On the other hand, a low-level activity such as outsourcing desktop support is unlikely to require signi<sup>fi</sup>cant efforts of the SR towards developing commitment and enduring relationship. The extent of substitution, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the proportion of total IT budget spent on outsourcing, may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the realm of the SR/SP relationships [52]. This is understandable, because the SR may tend to actively develop positive relational outcomes such as commitment towards the relationship when a large portion of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's IT budget is “locked in” an outsourcing contract.

## 5. Research methodology

## 5.1. Data collection

For data collection, the current study utilizes “key informant” methodology, which relies on a selected set of members to provide information about a social setting [96]. In such survey research, targeted respondents assume the role of a key informant and provide information on an aggregated unit of analysis–a single contract in this study–by reporting on group or organizational properties rather than personal attitudes and perceptions. For this study, it was important to identify organizations that were actively engaged in IT outsourcing using SLAs with an IT vendor as well as respondents within those organizations who were responsible for and/or knowledgeable about the arrangement. We obtained the attendee list of a national outsourcing conference in South Korea as potential respondents. We then determined that business and IT executives, speci<sup>fi</sup>c titles including President, CIO/Vice President of IT, Director/Assistant Vice President of IT, IT Managers, Contract Of<sup>fi</sup>cers, Heads of IT Sourcing Teams, as the most appropriate sources of organizational information regarding IT sourcing decisions. And we made pre-recruiting calls to those businesses and IT professionals on that attendee list. The sampling frame was further reduced by eliminating individuals whose <sup>fi</sup>rms had not been involved in any IT outsourcing engagement within the past <sup>fi</sup>ve years. As a result, one hundred <sup>fi</sup>fty professionals from the revised list agreed to participate in the survey or directed us to key informants involved in IT outsourcing arrangements using SLAs. E-mails containing the URL for the web-based online survey instrument were sent to those key informants. Of the 150 participants who agreed, 92 (61.3%) completed responses.

To assess potential threats of nonresponse bias, the respondent and nonrespondent <sup>fi</sup>rms were compared with respect to sales and the number of employees. No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences were found at the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level of 0.05. Further, the distribution of responses across industries was examined. While the manufacturing industry was found to be slightly over represented and the public/government sector slightly under represented, the sample included numerous cases from a variety of industries (Table 2). Demographic information about the respondents showed that about 46.8% were senior IT executives and 41% were IT managers. Although some preliminary steps were taken to ensure appropriate selection of key informants, a formal check was administered as part of the questionnaire [58]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, two items regarding key informant quality were used to assess the informant's knowledge about the SLA chosen and his or her involvement with IT outsourcing arrangements on a seven-point scale. The mean score for informant quality for each item was 5.60 (SD=1.04) and 5.80 (SD=1.08) out of 7, respectively, indicating that respondents chosen were appropriate and, thus, all responses were retained.

## 5.3. Data analysis and results

The assessment and estimation of the structural model were conducted using the statistical technique of partial least squares (PLS). This technique is especially suited for this study, because it allows latent constructs to be modeled as formative or re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators. Re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs are formed with the indicators directed in an outward fashion from the latent variable items representing the effects of the construct under study and, therefore, “re<sup>fl</sup>ect” the construct of interest. All <sup>fi</sup>rstorder constructs as well as relationship outcomes constructs in this study are re<sup>fl</sup>ective. In contrast, a formative construct is formed with the indicators directed in an inward fashion toward the latent variable if the indicators are conceptualized as the aggregation of variables that in combination lead to the formation of the latent variable. Thus formative indicators are often used to construct super ordinate constructs, where the individual indicators are weighed according to their relative importance in forming the constructs [11]. In our model, three SLA characteristics were assessed as formative constructs, where eleven SLA elements (i.e., <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs) served as indicators of the second order constructs. Consistent with the view of Goo [28], the theoretical justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for this modeling is the anticipation that the elements were not necessarily correlated with each other. However, sets of different elements could be aggregated to visualize the same underlying characteristics. For instance, the presence of governance characteristics is the result of a collection of legal provisions involving multiple contractual elements in an SLA. But the fact that governance characteristics exist does not necessarily ensure that, say, a communication plan exists, and vice versa.

Each one of the constructs in the structural model exhibits both convergent validity and discriminant validity. Convergent validity is adequate when constructs have an Average Variance Extracted (AVE) of at least 0.5 and when items load highly (loading N0.5) on their associated factors as well. Table 3 shows that the re<sup>fl</sup>ective measures have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant loadings that are much higher than the suggested threshold. For satisfactory discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE should be greater than the variance shared between the target construct and other constructs in the model [11]. ## 6. Discussion

Our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that the characteristics of SLAs are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to trust between the SR and the SP. In addition, foundation and governance SLA characteristics– re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the parties' expected relationship bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, shared values, and termination costs–encourage social investments that carry strong expectations of relationship commitment, which may be a departure from pure economic motives [33]. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that a formal contract such as a wellstructured SLA is critical in making the “shadow of the future” [41,46] by safeguarding future exchanges in the IT outsourcing relationship.

The association between the SLA characteristics and the SRs trust in the SPs is worth exploring. Consistent with the literature, trusting beliefs in the context of IT outsourcing relationships form largely with cognitive cues [89] based on interactions and institutional support [103]. And according to Morgan and Hunt [74], trust is formed with the existence of three variables in the relationship: shared values, communication, and opportunistic behavior. Our results show that the contractual provisions, which re<sup>fl</sup>ect those three variables, also positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the formation of trust in IT outsourcing relationships. Further, as structural assurance, SLA induces the trusting beliefs of the SR in the SP, because it provides security of the arrangement and supports the SR's beliefs about the maintenance and integrity of future exchanges with the SP. This also suggests that those “secured” precursors of trust contained in written SLA elements re<sup>fl</sup>ect a state of relational “embeddedness” [63,94] between SRs and SPs, in which outsourcing exchanges are improved through economies of time, integrative agreements, improvements in allocative ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and complex adaptation. Such improvements assume that the SR and the SP interact within the framework of the provisions speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in that SLA. Thus, the SR's experience and observations of the SP's actions–for instance, how they have communicated, whether they have kept the commitments, and if they have been honest about the services–inform perceptions about how the SP will act in the future, which in turns in<sup>fl</sup>uence the SR's trust in the SP. In this fashion, to the SR, an SLA acts as the necessary “impersonal” structures in place to enable the SP to maintain a successful future endeavor. As such, this study contributes to the growing evidence that effective institutional structures engender trust in impersonal contexts of IT use (e.g., [25,76]).

The role of initial trust building in IT outsourcing has become increasingly important because the number of new task teams in IT outsourcing arrangements continues to rise [86] and trust is more essential between strangers than when parties have interacted repeatedly [42,59]. Institution-based trust is often considered as an initial trust building mechanism in interorganizational relationships, particularly in situations where new work relationship is formed [69]. Institutional trust is especially salient in such IT-enabled outsourcing activities as application service and offshore outsourcing, where <sup>fi</sup>rms and individuals often interact with new and/or unknown entities with minimal prior experience of dealing with each other. This study suggests institutionbased trust as a trust building mechanism, as well as <sup>fi</sup>nds support for speci<sup>fi</sup>c forms of structural assurance in building interorganizational trust and commitment in the context of IT outsourcing. As an extension to the current study, future research can test the effect of structural assurance and situational normality (the perception that the environment is in order, the situation is normal, appropriate, well-ordered, and favorable for assuring a transaction) on initial institutional trust building in various IORs.

For practitioners, this study offers insights into the structure of an effective SLA. Quite often, SLAs contain clauses and metrics dealing with only the most rudimentary service elements but ignore important issues pertaining to governance–such as communication mechanisms, joint decisionmaking mechanisms, and con<sup>fl</sup>ict management–as well as those pertaining to past experience and the client's changing business needs. Such underspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed SLAs ignore the intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that can be derived from them related to building effective SP–SR relationships [50]. We believe that extensively developed and well-structured SLAs can be used to effectively gauge and manage relationships and activities associated with IT outsourcing for the life of the contract [20,47]. To that end, this study utilizes the framework, in which eleven contractual elements of an SLA are categorized into three substantive dimensions of foundation, change management, and governance, and shows the association of those SLA structure characteristics with trust and commitment building. In so doing, this study not only provides a parsimonious structure for rationalizing and re<sup>fi</sup>ning SLA but also provides meaningful insights into which characteristics of SLA are particularly effective for enhancing speci<sup>fi</sup>c relational attributes. In practice, managers may customize their SLAs within the diverse contexts of outsourcing arrangements by composing the different sets of the elements in SLAs, and an SLA that takes on certain characteristics could be used to nurture speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of relationships. For instance, SLAs structured to emphasize both foundation characteristics and change management characteristics can effectively foster trust between the SR and the SP to facilitate the development of relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c systems and applications over the life of the outsourcing contract. This mechanism can be regarded as an incentive for relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments, an inherently risky activity for the SP.

Our results also suggest that, in practice, parties should establish well-structured SLAs at the initiation of the engagement or re<sup>fi</sup>ne the SLA re<sup>fl</sup>ecting learning from prior working experiences. Too often, outsourcing engagements are largely based on mutual trust without details of written terms of the arrangement, perhaps due to either the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of anticipating or specifying all possible contingencies or the concerns of signaling distrust. As Fitzgerald and Willcocks [20] have noted, partnership issues in IT outsourcing must be considered in conjunction with the contractual arrangements. This is consistent with Sabherwal's recommendation to balance structural control and trust to improve performance in outsourced IS development projects in light of his observation that excessive focus on either structure or trust hurts performance [83]. The current study <sup>fi</sup>nds that well-structured SLAs–effectively a legal “safety net” in lieu of exclusive reliance on mutual trust–can serve to build such social elements as trust and commitment. This suggests that the combination of and positive interaction between the two modes of governance–formal control (via SLAs) and relational governance (via trust and commitment)– can be a powerful mechanism for generating favorable relationship outcomes that we believe would successfully manage IT outsourcing relationship.

Theorizing trust and commitment as key variables that mediate relationship outcomes, we examine six hypothesized relationships between trust/commitment and <sup>fi</sup>ve relationship outcomes in the context of IT outsourcing, and <sup>fi</sup>nd that all hypothesized relationships are strongly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and high in magnitude. In particular, both trust and commitment explain a substantial amount of the variances in <sup>fi</sup>ve outcomes, including almost half of the variance of Acquiescence, Relationship Durability and Harmonious Con<sup>fl</sup>ict Resolution, and more than 30% of the variance of Cooperation and Active Information Exchange in outsourcing engagements. Theorizing that those relationship outcomes are important factors for outsourcing success, our results suggest that well-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed and comprehensive SLAs facilitate the trust and commitment building between the SR and the SP, which, in turn, are keys to the relationship development process of IT outsourcing. This connection between the formal control and relational governance in outsourcing success is the fundamental proposition that we tested in this study.

## 7. Limitations and future research directions

Our study has several limitations. We utilize a single respondent–the SR–for each outsourcing contract. This limits our results to re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the perspectives of the SRs of the SR–SP dyad. But one party's perceptions of the other partner's commitment are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the latter's actual commitment, because both partners presumably reveal their true perceptions, intentions, and actions to each other during interactions over time [2]. We therefore submit that this approach possesses certain level of accuracy in IT outsourcing relationships, as the SR's perception of the SP is shaped by the latter's behavior, which is in turn a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the SP's attitude. Nevertheless, an analysis of the relationship between SLA and trust and commitment and their relational outcomes from the SP's perspective can be used to validate the result of this paper, as well as a useful extension to a better understanding of highquality IT outsourcing relationships over time.

We are also concerned that the single respondent for each outsourcing contract may introduce common methods bias. We performed Harman's single factor test by loading all of the items in this study into an exploratory factor analysis although the possibility of common method bias problem could be minimal because the items of SLAs constructs measured the factual nature of the contractual clause [77]. The results generated a large number of factors with one of them accounting for 18% of covariance among the measures at maximum, evidence that there is not a substantial amount of common method variance present.

Another limitation is the cross-sectional design employed in this research. Although the results provide strong support for the hypotheses, our model could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a longitudinal research design, which can provide strong inferences for causality relationships. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a number of relationships in our model, including those between the three SLA characteristics and commitment and trust, are potentially cyclical. We performed an ex post analysis using the Hausman endogeneity test [39], and the result con<sup>fi</sup>rms the possibility of such endogeneity relationships. This is consistent with our expectation that such relationships can be reciprocal, and our results are still valid in that SLAs and commitment and trust lead to the six relational outcomes. As a natural extension to the current study, further research is called for articulating such bi-directional relationships among these variables and examining the reverse effect of relational governance on contract re<sup>fi</sup>nements through a longitudinal study to better understand the virtuous (vicious) cycle of outsourcing relationships in nature [83].

Lastly, a potential limitation concerns the nature of the sample utilized in this analysis. The survey in this study was conducted at organizations that implemented IT outsourcing through SLAs. Although such a sampling frame, which contains organizations participating in the activity of interest with senior executives with possible vested interest, is widely used in IS research, the inherent self-selection may result in biased and inconsistent estimates when applied generally. While the sample shows good representation of <sup>fi</sup>rms by size and industry, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings can only be generalized to <sup>fi</sup>rms within the sampling frame — i.e., outsourcing engagements implemented with SLAs. No claim of external validity for this study's <sup>fi</sup>ndings, particularly in cases where no SLAs are implemented in outsourcing arrangements, can be made. It is also worth noting that the sample was composed of domestic Korean organizations, selected from the attendee list of an outsourcing conference. We anticipate that there could be systematic differences among countries in many aspects from contract drafting to interorganizational trust, which makes generalization of the results to other settings dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. However, we believe that a future study that compares the current <sup>fi</sup>ndings to an analysis of data from another country– in particular a developed country such as the U.S.–could yield insight into how the structure of SLAs and the trust and commitment differ in different cultural and legal settings.

## 8. Conclusion

The contribution of this study is two-fold. First, the paper shifts the focus from relationship quality itself–the major stream of IT outsourcing research–to the fostering and management of the partnership attributes that promote relationship quality. Prior studies on IT outsourcing relationships provide insight into partnership factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence effectiveness of IT outsourcing and do not examine how to cultivate those factors to garner outsourcing success. As an extension to past studies which emphasize the importance of the contract [49,84], we view SLAs as a foundation of the outsourcing relationship and a road map for outsourcing success. This paper applies the commitment–trust theory from relationship marketing to IT outsourcing arrangements and extends the theory by examining the role of formal contracts as potential precursors of trust and commitment. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that the formal SLA elements are related to the development of trust and commitment, which play a mediating role in enlarging “self-enforcing range” of relational governance [53].

Second, our study contributes to the emerging stream of research on IOR governance [25,36,66,78] by providing a bridge between the two modes of governance–formal contracts and relational governance–that are usually investigated separately. It is worth noting that the preponderance of empirical work has focused mainly on social mechanisms that foster trust and commitment, where formal mechanisms have been regarded as a signal of distrust [10,26,64]. This study assesses the relationship between formal contracts (i.e., SLA characteristics) and relational governance (i.e., trust and commitment) and shows, in the context of IT outsourcing, the complementary relationship between two governing modes.

In summary, the intent of this study is to understand how formal contractual characteristics contribute to developing relational attributes such as trust and relationship commitment in IT outsourcing practices. Using cross-sectional data, the results indicate that well-structured SLAs play an important role in cultivating favorable relationships in the course of outsourcing engagements, which, in turn, are related to successful relational outcomes of IT outsourcing. As a conclusion, this study suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms strategically use SLA to successfully manage IT outsourcing relationship [6,67].

Jahyun Goo is an assistant professor of MIS in the Barry Kaye College of Business at Florida Atlantic University. He received his Ph.D. and MBA degrees in MIS from the School of Management at the State University of New York at Buffalo. His active research areas are information systems (IS) sourcing, IT management and strategy, interorganizational relationships, and IS Security. His papers have been published or accepted for publication in MIS Quarterly, Decision Sciences, Decision Support Systems, Information Systems Journal, Information Systems Frontier, and IEEE IT Professional, among others. Jahyun has presented his research at ICIS, HICSS, AMCIS, ECIS, INFORS, etc. He received a best paper award at HICSS 2007 and was nominated for a best paper award at ECIS 2006. He is a guest associate editor of MISQ special issue on Cybersecurity.

C. Derrick Huang is an assistant professor in the Department of Information Technology and Operations Management in the Barry Kaye College of Business at Florida Atlantic University. Previously, as a practitioner, he held executive-level positions in the area of marketing and strategic planning in a number of high-tech companies. Dr. Huang's research interest lies in the business value and strategic impact of information technology in organizations, and his current focus is on the economics of information security investments and the strategic management of information systems outsourcing. He holds a Ph.D. from Harvard University.
