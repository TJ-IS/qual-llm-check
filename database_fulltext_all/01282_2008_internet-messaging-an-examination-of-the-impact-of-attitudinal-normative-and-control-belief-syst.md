# Internet messaging: An examination of the impact of attitudinal, normative, and control belief systems

G. Premkumar <sup>a,1</sup>, K. Ramamurthy <sup>b,</sup>\*, Hsin-Nan Liu <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business, Iowa State University, Ames, IA 50011-2063, United States

<sup>b</sup> Sheldon B. Lubar School of Business, University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, Milwaukee, WI 53201, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received in revised form 6 January 2008 Accepted 6 June 2008 Available online 19 August 2008

Keywords: Instant messaging Innovation adoption Diffusion of innovation TAM TPB Control beliefs Hedonic beliefs Utilitarian beliefs

## A B S T R A C T

While instant messaging (IM) has been very popular among teenagers and college students for social and recreation communication, its use in corporate settings has been limited. We examined the factors that influence the intention to use and actual use of IM. A research model was developed based on diffusion of innovation and Socio-psychological theories. The model examined the influence of seven variables in three belief structures (the attitudinal, normative, and control). The model was empirically validated using a field survey of 349 respondents. The results indicated that five of the seven variables explained about 41% of the variance in the intention to use IM. The five significant variables were: utilitarian beliefs, hedonic beliefs, subjective norms, critical mass, and ease of use. Affiliation motivation was not significant and social belief had a negative impact on intention. As expected, intention to use had a significant influence on actual usage.

## 1. Introduction

Unlike E-mail, which is asynchronous, instant messaging (IM) provides real time communication. The user has control over his or her communication since he or she can decide when to receive the communication (offline, away, or online, etc.) as well as deciding with whom they wish to communicate (buddy list, friends, contacts, etc.). Control is a significant benefit because people seem to have no control over their inbound communications, such as unwanted phone calls from tele-marketers, or flooding of E-mail with spam and other junk.

Companies have been reluctant to accept IM for various reasons: First, they are concerned about its impact on productivity. E-mail sensitized many companies and led them to ban IM rather than allowing its use—assuming that productivity losses outweigh its benefits. Second, network externalities affect the utility of a communications technology, negating the isolated implementation of IM in a few pockets of the organization; for example, it may be a good technology for the help-desk staff, but all users would then need it to contact the desk, thereby limiting control over its use. Third, security concerns have made some organizations ban IM. Fourth, doubts have been raised about its utility. However, IM allows rapid communication and some firms have used it effectively.

IT research has extensively studied the adoption of IT innovations using diffusion of innovation (DOI) theory [21], and socio-psychological theories such as TRA and TPB. The predominant premise has been that individuals will adopt and use an innovation if they perceive that it is useful. Recent studies have pointed to the need for including additional variables related to human and social change processes [13]. The diffusion of computers to the home and the use of the Internet have resulted in their becoming devices that satisfy social interaction and provide entertainment. A recent study [33] extended TAM into the context of t-commerce (e-commerce with interactive television) considering the effect of seven factors – perceived ease of use (EOU), perceived usefulness (PU), perceived enjoyment, trust, attitude, normative belief of family and friends, and subjective norm (SN), i.e., attitudes of friends or colleagues – on whether he or she should perform the behavior. Perceived enjoyment was the most important factor affecting attitude and behavioral intention (BI) toward t-commerce adoption. IT applications have evolved to systems providing social and hedonic outcomes. However, there has been little examination of their impact. We decided to investigate this gap. The primary objectives of our study were to:

1. Develop a model of IT usage that incorporated a broader perspective of usage.

2. Validate this model empirically using data from a field study.

3. Examine the differences in usage among user segments and thus understand the factors that affect them.

## 2. Theory and research model

Our research model was based on two theoretical foundations – adoption and diffusion of innovation and socio-psychological theory on beliefs and individual traits.

## 2.1. Diffusion of innovation

New IT has been considered as innovation, and its adoption and use has been grounded on diffusion of innovation (DOI) research. One line of this research stream has looked at various factors influencing IT adoption and use: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, cost, image, result demonstrability, and visibility. Tornatzky and Klein [24] in a meta-analysis of these studies found that relative advantage, compatibility, and complexity were consistently related to adoption. Some researchers have drawn parallels between TAM and DOI research by comparing the variables used in the two streams—PU with relative advantage and PEU with complexity.

## 2.2. Socio-psychological theories

Not all communication is for utilitarian outcomes; some of it is for social enjoyment or outcome. Need for attachment/affiliation, a sense of need to belong, has been shown to be important for all human beings [6]. Researchers have explored whether it is an individual trait or context dependent; e.g., in an office, an individual may have less need for attachment but exhibit a different and much higher need in a family or social setting.

The TRA [2,5] formed the theoretical foundation for developing TAM. In this, PU and PEU were found to be significant predictors of attitude and BI.

The TPB [3] extended TRA to reflect complexities in a world where the individuals did not have complete control over their behavior. TPB posited that behavior was a direct function of BI and perceived behavioral control (PBC). BI was formed by attitude (reflecting favorable or unfavorable feelings towards performing a behavior), SN and PBC (constraints in performing the behavior). The results of a quantitative meta-analysis of previous research on TAM/TAM 2 indicated a significant influence of SN on PU and BI to use [22]. Two studies [29,31] provided a summary of research on TAM.

## 3. Research model

We proposed a research model that incorporated the various belief structures that we expected to be relevant in a comprehensive assessment of intention and usage. The model with the three belief structures and the relevant variables is shown in Fig. 1. Since IM is used for utilitarian as well as social purposes, we needed to capture a broader set of outcome beliefs. An important extension was thus the inclusion of social, hedonic, and utilitarian outcomes.

Beliefs on utilitarian outcomes, captured through PU, have been found to be a key predictor of intention and usage [28]. The role of pleasure and enjoyment has been less investigated, but a few studies, e.g. [32], have incorporated hedonic outcomes (i.e., pleasure derived from the product, such as playing games and chatting with friends). A recent study [14] indicated that perceived playfulness and PU both contributed significantly to users’ intent to reuse a web site. Social outcomes also arise from public recognition and increase in status by using an innovation; this is considered a social outcome of adoption [18].

Motivation theory also supports the attitudinal belief structures [25,26,30]. Extrinsic motivation pertains to achieving valuable goals and outcomes, while intrinsic motivation pertains to other outcomes, such as for fun, enjoyment, and pleasure, which result from performing the activity. While utilitarian beliefs map to extrinsic motivation, the others reflect intrinsic motivation.

According to TPB, BI is determined by Attitude toward behavior, SN, and PBC. Attitude is determined by the individual’s evaluation of behavior providing favorable or unfavorable outcomes. PBC reflects perceptions of internal factors that facilitate or inhibit the behavior. Each of these three determinants is reflected in the underlying belief structure (attitudinal, normative, and control beliefs). In our study we examined attitudinal beliefs on three outcomes—utilitarian, hedonic and social. Control beliefs comprised beliefs about inhibitors and facilitators in performing the behavior. In our study we examine three aspects of control beliefs: PEU, perceived critical mass of the group, and affiliation motivation, since all of these are within the general control of the individual.

## 3.1. Attitudinal belief structure

Attitudinal or behavioral beliefs involve the favorable outcomes of the behavior. In our study we examined three: utilitarian, hedonic, and social belief outcomes.

We expected that attitudinal beliefs would play a significant role in influencing use intentions. The importance of beliefs on hedonic outcomes is dependent on the context in which the technology is used, thus for multi-use technologies it was essential we capture hedonic beliefs.

Hence, we posited that:

H1. Utilitarian outcome beliefs will have a positive influence on intention to use IM.

H2. Hedonic outcome beliefs will have a positive influence on intention to use IM.

H3. Social outcome beliefs will have a positive influence on intention to use IM.

## 3.2. Normative belief structure

Normative beliefs may be subtle or direct and their influence could vary with the context of the study. Prior studies in marketing have clearly established the importance of social influence in determining behavior [4]. But while some IT studies have not found it to be important [16], others have found a significant relationship between them [10]. Very often in workplace settings adoption and use is not voluntary and is dictated by the PU of the innovation and not by social influence. In our study, the use of IM was voluntary and thus, the social network was expected to have a significant influence. Hence, we posited that:

H4. Subjective norm will have a positive influence on intention to use IM.

## 3.3. Control belief structure

Since the technology under study was IM, which is extensively used in social settings, the individual’s interest in inter-personal interaction is a motivating factor and an important determinant to the use of IM. Two factors have been found to influence the use of a communications tool in a community setting: interest of the individual in participating in inter-personal interaction and a requirement of the individual’s community that he or she participates. Studies in innovation adoption have highlighted the role of critical mass and network externalities for adoption of innovation.

## 3.3.1. Affiliation motivation

Affiliation motivation (AM) is an individual characteristic that reflects his or her desire to interact [12]. Generally, people with high AM are found to be comfortable in social situations and seek social networks for information and help [17]. They value interaction with, and feel valued by, others. However, individuals with low AM feel isolated, associate themselves with little self value, and are unable to contribute to the community; they withdraw from social interaction. IM enables creation of a virtual social network through buddy lists and allows individuals to have two-way synchronous interaction with others on the network. Based on past research, we expected that individuals with high AM and associated strong motivation for social interactions would be more likely to use IM than those with low AM. Hence we posited that:

H5. Affiliation motivation will have a positive influence on intention to use IM.

## 3.3.2. Perceived critical mass (PCM)

While AM captures an individual’s interest in inter-personal interaction, PCM captures the community’s readiness. For an innovation to be accepted it must have a sufficient number of adopters for it to be perceived as valuable and thus induce new members to adopt it. The long gestation period for adoption of email was attributed to a lack of members in the community with access to the technology. Once a threshold was reached the number of adopters increased almost exponentially. In the IM context, its value to a user increases as more members become part of the social network. PCM can be expected to have a significant effect on IT adoption. Hence we posited:

H6. Perceived critical mass will have a positive influence on intention to use IM.

## 3.3.3. PEU

For a new innovation, this is a critical determinant in the initial stages, since the vendor would like the adopter to have a positive experience with the product, though it has been found to be less important in later stages. In general, various studies on TAM have validated the importance of this variable [7]. Hence we posited that:

H7. Ease of use will have a positive influence on intention to use IM.

## 3.4. From intentions to actual use

We added the following hypothesis for the sake of completeness.

H8. Intention to use IM will have a positive influence on the actual use of IM.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Measurement

We used a questionnaire to determine all research variables using multiple-item scales, primarily adapted from prior studies with minor wording changes to tailor them to an IM context. The scales for utilitarian beliefs (PU, PEU) and BI were measured using items adapted from TAM studies [8]. The social outcome beliefs were based on [27]. The items for hedonic outcome beliefs (Perceived Enjoyment) were derived from [1]. The scale for SN was adapted from [23] and affiliation motivation from [11]. The items to assess perceived critical mass were based on [15].

The survey had three major sections. The first section contained 33 items measuring the determinants of BI. All items in this section were coded on 7-point Likert Scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). The second section contained information about adopters’ actual usage, such as the number of people in their buddy lists and how often they logged onto IM. The third section contained demographic information about the respondents, including age, gender, student status, and experience with computer and IM.

The survey was first pilot tested with 10 students. They provided feedback on the content as well as the comprehensibility and clarity of the questions; this was used to modify and improve the clarity of the items and reduce the time to complete the survey.

## 4.2. Data collection

The data was collected with a field survey; we needed a context that used IM for both utilitarian and non-utilitarian purposes. An undergraduate student population fit this requirement, as it uses IM for (assignments, class interactions, and part-time work) as well as for social purposes (chat rooms, voice communication, etc.). Study of IM in a corporate setting could be problematic because use is often dictated by corporate policies and does not reflect the individual’s beliefs.

Undergraduate students taking a large core business class and an MIS class in the College of Business at a large public university completed the survey. A letter explained the objectives of the study and promised confidentiality of all responses. Participation in the survey was voluntary. 349 students completed the survey. The demographics of respondents are given in Table 1.

The sample was representative of the student population.

Non-response bias was not an issue as most students in the class willingly participated in the study; some 15 students could not participate for personal reasons. There was a possibility of

## 4.3. Validity and reliability

The constructs were tested for validity and reliability to make sure that the measurement was accurate and sound. Validity was assessed by determining content, convergent, and discriminant validity. Content validity was established through the use of items validated in prior research instruments. Convergent and discriminant validity were evaluated using factor analysis; their validity and reliability are reported in Table 2.

As shown there, all items loaded on appropriate constructs and there were no significant cross loadings (>0.4). The reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and all constructs had values greater than 0.6, thereby assuring adequate reliability [19]. Based on these assessments we claimed that the scales exhibited adequate validity and reliability.

## 5.1. Discussion

All independent variables, except AM, were significant in predicting intention. We expected that people with a high desire to interact with others would be more intent on using IM. Although AM was positively correlated with intention, other factors played a more dominant role in influencing intention to use IM.

The results supported the basic TAM model, with both PU and PEU being significant predictors. PEU was less significant compared to PU. Given the simple nature of the software and easy user interface this is understandable. However, the lack of easy inter-operability among IM software may have frustrated some users leading to lower scores on PEU.

We found perceived enjoyment to be one of the most significant determinants of intention. Since the major use of IM was for interaction with friends, hedonic beliefs were salient. Respondents spent less than 20% of their time for utilitarian purposes. Social outcome beliefs had a negative coefficient, but this was significant at $p < 0 . 0 5$ level. An analysis of the correlation matrix indicated that it was positively correlated with intention and other factors were better able to explain the variance in intention to use IM.

## 6. Conclusion

We examined factors that influence the intention to use and actually use IM. A model was developed to examine the influence of seven variables in three different belief structures. The model was empirically validated using a field survey of 349 respondents. The results indicated that five of the seven variables explain about 42% of the variance in intention.

## 6.1. Implications and limitations

Perceived critical mass and hedonic outcome beliefs had the most significant influence on intention to use IM. The lack of significance of Affiliation motivation was puzzling. If IM technology does not meet the needs for social interaction then it raises some interesting issues regarding the importance of face-to-face interactions notwithstanding newer forms of communicationa innovations.

Opportunities (or lack of them) may color beliefs in the use of technologies. As technologies mature and new applications are identified, beliefs may change about the current fashion. The extensive use of IM technology for social interaction does raise some concerns for companies who deploy IM. A decline in productivity from misuse of the technology could occur. While the unobtrusive nature of the technology may shield misuse, the ability to monitor usage could be a detriment to misuse. Specific task contexts and choice of IM as an alternative communication tool should be examined to decide whether, and under what conditions, use of IM would be desirable.

The study had one major limitation: the sample consisted only of college students in a relatively homogeneous age group; thus it is not generalizable to other groups.

## Appendix A. Measurement items

All items used a Likert type scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7).

1. Utilitarian beliefs (PU)

(1) Using IM enables me to accomplish tasks more quickly.

(2) Using IM improves my performance.

(3) Using IM increases my productivity.

(4) Using IM enhances my effectiveness.

2. Hedonic beliefs (PE)

(1) I have fun interacting with IM.

(2) Using IM provides me with a lot of enjoyment.

(3) I enjoy using IM.

(4) Using IM bores me (reverse coded).

3. Social beliefs (SO)

(1) Using IM helps me to improve my status.

(2) Using IM helps me to be part of the group I would like to belong.

4. Subjective norm (SN)

(1) People who influence my behavior think that I should use IM.

(2) People who are important to me think that I should use IM.

(3) My superiors think that I should use IM.

(4) My friends think that I should use IM.

5. Perceived critical mass (PCM)

(1) Many of my friends use IM.

(2) Of the people I interact regularly, many use IM.

(3) A large percentage of my friends use IM

6. EOU—reverse coded

(1) It is difficult to learn how to use IM.

(2) Instructions for using IM are hard to follow.

7. Affiliation motivation (AM)

(1) I like to be around people for emotional support in good or bad times.

(2) I have a strong desire to get people I am around to notice and appreciate me

(3) I think being close to others and relating to them on a oneon-one level is one of my favorite and most satisfying pastimes.
