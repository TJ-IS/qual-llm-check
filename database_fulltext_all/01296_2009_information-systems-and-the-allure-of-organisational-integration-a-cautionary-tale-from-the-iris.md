# Information systems and the allure of organisational integration: a cautionary tale from the Irish financial services sector

Anita Mangan and Se´amas Kelly

Centre for Innovation, Technology & Organisation (CITO), UCD School of Business, University College Dublin, Ireland

Correspondence: Anita Mangan, Q239, Quinn School, UCD School of Business, University College Dublin, Belfield, Dublin 4, Ireland. Tel: þ 353 1 716 4776; E-mail: Anita.Mangan@ucd.ie

## Abstract

In this paper we draw on evidence from an in-depth, longitudinal, interpretive study of an ambitious attempt to implement a large-scale information systems (IS) infrastructure (the ILCUTECH Standard Information System (ISIS) project) within the Irish credit union movement, to explore some of the difficulties and contradictions associated with information and communication technology (ICT)-enabled organisational integration. We argue that, in their enthusiasm for facilitating IS innovation, the leadership of the movement neglected more deep-seated organisational issues that were, to that point, largely unarticulated. In an attempt to get to the heart of these issues, we synthesise a distinctive theoretical perspective that draws mainly on institutional theory. This helps untangle the complex institutional heritage of the credit union movement, which resulted in the emergence of an interesting hybridity of institutional logics and latent contradictions. These contradictions provoked the emergence of a particular kind of institutional entrepreneurship, which was deeply implicated in reshaping institutionalised ways of thinking and doing. We emphasise the political nature of the change process and argue that the institutional arrangements for governing the ISIS project, which also underpinned the governance of the broader movement, were not well suited to manage the risk associated with such an enterprise. We conclude by suggesting that the case described might be an example of the workings of a broader kind of change dynamics around ICT. Specifically, the scale and scope of the change project acted as a trigger that brought tensions underlying existing competing institutional logics to a head, thus provoking institutional reform. ## Introduction

The idea that technology has the capacity to instigate significant organisational change surfaces with almost every new technological development. Some of the early literature on information and communication technologies (ICT), for example, argued that ICT could facilitate novel and more flexible ways of organising business activities (Davidow & Malone, 1992; Byrne, 1993; Ferioli & Migliarese, 1996) by allowing organisations to achieve higher levels of global integration while retaining significant local autonomy and flexibility (Lipnack & Stamps, 1996). This was an alluring vision of organisational transformation because it posited

ICT-enabled change (IS innovation) as a relatively straightforward process, suggesting that organisations could reap the benefits of integration without surrendering flexibility. A key difficulty with such assumptions, however, is the emphasis on generalised features of these technologies to explain change in a teleological manner, at the expense of attending to the formative role of the institutional contexts in which they are embedded (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2000).

For many years now, a section of the IS community has been developing analytical perspectives to understand the role of the social/political context in shaping the process of IS innovation (cf. Walsham, 1993; Ciborra & Lanzara, 1994; Avgerou, 2001; Avgerou et al., 2004). One criticism of such approaches, however, is that in attending to the situated nature of social action in the immediate setting, the role of broader contextual influences (or what Pettigrew (1990) terms the ‘outer context’) in shaping action can be overlooked (Walsham, 2001; Avgerou & Madon, 2004; Kallinikos, 2004). In this paper we draw on institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1991; Scott, 2001) as a means of addressing such concerns. This approach has the advantage of situating organisations within a broader institutional field (or, as we illustrate below, a set of institutional fields), which shapes its structures and practices in important ways. From such a perspective, organisational change is conceptualised as a dual process of the de-institutionalisation of established practices and the institutionalisation of new, modified ones (cf. Robey & Boudreau, 1999; Avgerou, 2000).

Specifically, the paper develops an institutionalist approach to examine a complex process of organisational change in the Irish credit union movement, where a ‘disastrous’ attempt to develop a shared ICT infrastructure ultimately proved to be very consequential from the point of view of institutional reform. The ILCUTECH Standard Information System (ISIS) project was an attempt to deploy IS as a response to the perceived need to modernise the movement. The aim of ISIS was to provide a centralised, standard banking system that would enable all credit unions to offer an expanded range of financial services to their members. The project never progressed beyond pilot testing and was abandoned in 2001, following severe budget and schedule overruns.

Although research into large-scale IS implementation failures is common (cf. Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1987; Keil, 1995; Drummond, 1996; Mitev, 1996; Wilson & Howcroft, 2005), what is unusual about this case is the unprecedented upheaval to the movement in the aftermath of the project’s failure. Credit unions are non-profit organisations and the failure of ISIS meant a loss of €35 million of members’ savings. While the perceived need for a large-scale network solution still remains, confidence in the movement’s ability to implement a largescale project has been eroded. Furthermore, after years of favourable coverage, the media has become consistently critical of the movement. Finally, in the immediate aftermath there was a threat of a schism in the movement and the Irish League of Credit Unions (ILCU) were forced to implement significant structural changes, which included making radical alterations to their governance structure and hiring a full-time CEO.

In our analysis of the ISIS project, we argue that one of the key problems for the movement was that their enthusiasm for facilitating organisational transformation through the deployment of IS led them to neglect more deep-seated issues that were previously largely ignored or unarticulated. Thus, what was initially seen primarily as a technical problem turned out to be much more fundamental and deeply rooted. We illustrate the usefulness of institutional theory for untangling the complex institutional heritage of the credit union movement, which resulted in the emergence of an interesting hybridity of institutional logics and latent contradictions. This helps us understand the circumstances that facilitated the emergence of a particular kind of institutional entrepreneurship, and how this in turn was implicated in reshaping institutionalised ways of thinking and doing. We emphasise the political nature of the change process and argue that the institutional arrangements for governing the ISIS project, which also underpinned the governance of the broader movement, were not well suited to managing the risk associated with such an enterprise. We conclude by suggesting that the case described might be an example of the workings of a broader kind of change dynamics around ICT. Specifically, the scale and scope of the change project acted as a trigger that brought tensions underlying existing competing institutional logics to a head, thus provoking institutional reform.

The paper is structured as follows. In the next section we sketch the theoretical ideas about institutional theory that informed our analysis, before subsequently going on to outline our research approach and methods. We then describe the historical context of the Irish credit union movement and narrate the story of the ISIS project. In the penultimate section, we offer an analysis of the ISIS case, based on the theoretical ideas introduced earlier. We conclude by summarising some important conclusions and implications of the work.

## Information infrastructure projects, risk, institutional theory, institutional entrepreneurship and organisational change

In recent years, a very interesting literature has emerged that examines the complexities associated with managing the development of large-scale information infrastructures that provide ways of seamlessly connecting (and transforming) practices across multiple locales (Hanseth et al., 1996; Star & Ruhleder, 1996; Hanseth & Braa, 1998; Ciborra & Associates, 2000). This work illustrates the many challenges involved in successfully integrating a patchwork of diverse practices and technologies, and illuminates the piecemeal and tentative process through which such formations typically emerge. In so doing, it emphasises the central role of historical trajectories of development; by pointing out that there is rarely such thing as a green field site, the importance of path dependence, improvisation and bricolage is vividly underscored. In particular, by providing rich empirical descriptions of such projects as they unfold over time, the accounts of rational, planned and controlled processes that predominate in the mainstream managerialist literature (e.g. Weill & Broadbent, 1998) are challenged by a characterisation that emphasises chance, emergence and ‘drift’ (see especially Ciborra & Associates, 2000). Thus conceived, infrastructure projects are open-ended endeavours, with discrepancies between the initial goals and actual outcomes, and which often seem to be out of control (Ciborra & Associates, 2000). Implementation is theorised as a process that is founded upon a series of small, ongoing mutual adjustments.

While this literature is refreshing in the manner in which it explores the uncertain, tentative emergence of such infrastructural formations, it has less to say, however, about the significance of specific kinds of institutional arrangements that might prevail and about ways in which collective action might be mobilised to shape such processes in meaningful ways. The question is, what kinds of institutional arrangements are more or less amenable to the successful emergence of robust and flexible infrastructures? In this regard, the work of Flyvbjerg et al. (2003) on ‘megaprojects’ and risk is helpful. This research points to the repeated failure of large-scale infrastructural projects and argues that much of the difficulties with such efforts could be ameliorated by ‘reforming the institutional arrangements that form the context of megaproject decision making’ (Flyvbjerg et al., 2003, p. 7). In particular, they argue that:

y good decision making is not only a question of better and more rational information and communication, but also of institutional arrangements that promote accountability, and especially accountability towards risk. We see accountability as being a question not just about periodic elections, but also about a continuing dialogue between civil society and policy makers and about institutions holding each other accountable through appropriate checks and balances. Thus we replace the conventional decisionistic approach to megaproject development with a more current institutionalistic one centred on the practices and rules that comprise risk and accountability. (Flyvbjerg et al., 2003, p. 7)

Here, then, there is a shift in focus to the institutional arrangements that prevail in a given setting, with a particular emphasis on the importance of practices that promote accountability towards risk. As a means of developing the notion of the importance of such institutional arrangements, we now turn our attention to the body of literature concerned with institutional theory.

Institutional theory (often referred to as new institutional theory or neoinstitutional theory to distinguish it from earlier attempts to account for environmental influences on organisations by writers such as Selznick, Parsons and Simon – see Scott, 2001) emerged as a ‘corrective’ (DiMaggio, 1988) to overly calculative and rational accounts of human behaviour in organisational settings. Instead, this approach emphasises the importance of taken-for-granted assumptions and routinised social behaviour in the ongoing reproduction of organisational life within a given institutional context. For institutionalists, organisations occupy positions within organisational fields (see also Bourdieu, 1990) that are structured and stabilised by shared institutional logics. Institutional logics are ‘y taken-for-granted, resilient social prescriptions, sometimes encoded in laws, specifying the boundaries of a field, its rules of membership, and the role identities and appropriate organizational forms of its constituent communities’ (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006, p. 28). Consequently, as fields mature, organisational forms (‘archetypal configurations of structures and practices given coherence by underlying values regarded as being appropriate within an institutional context’ Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006, p. 30) converge isomorphically towards increasingly taken-for-granted templates (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). Thus, we may talk of the embeddedness of organisational actors, or of embedded agency, underscoring the manner in which the institutional logic associated with a given field or organizational form shapes action.

As outlined above, however, institutional theory has a conservative bias, as institutions are typically viewed as ‘the source of stability and order’ (Scott, 2001, p. 181). Moreover, this brings with it the danger of structural determinism, which Seo & Creed (2002, p. 226) term the ‘paradox of embedded agency’; that is, if organisational action is deeply embedded, this implies a diminished role for agency and a difficulty in explaining change. In other words, if ‘behavior is substantially shaped by takenfor-granted institutional prescriptions, how can actors envision and enact changes to the contexts in which they are embedded?’ (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006, p. 27). One of the most prominent ways of explaining change has been with reference to the disruptive impact of exogenous ‘jolts’ (Meyer, 1982), which can take the form of ‘social upheaval, technological disruptions, competitive discontinuities, and regulatory change’ (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006, p. 28). Such jolts may disrupt institutionalised practices within a field by provoking a reconsideration of extant logics. In this way, change becomes possible in such ‘fields in crisis’ (Fligstein, 1997).

More recently, however, attention has been given to the notion of institutional entrepreneurship (DiMaggio, 1988; Greenwood et al., 2002; Maguire et al., 2004; Munir & Phillips, 2005; Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006) as an alternative, more endogenous, mechanism of change. This concept focuses attention on the ‘activities of actors who have an interest in particular institutional arrangements and who leverage resources to create new institutions or to transform existing ones’ (Maguire et al., 2004, p. 657). Consequently, then, the challenge is to ‘explain how, and which, embedded actors are able to envision, then impose, alternative futures’ (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006, p. 29), as institutional change is ‘a political process that reflects the power and interests of organized actors’ (Maguire et al., 2004, p. 658). Institutional entrepreneurs ‘lead efforts to identify political opportunities, frame issues and problems, and mobilize constituencies y and spearhead collective attempts to infuse new beliefs, norms, and values into social structures’ (Rao et al., 2000, p. 240). This implies that these change agents have to be powerful and politically adept – skilled at aligning their change programme to the conditions that prevail within the field in question. In this sense, institutional entrepreneurship might be conceived of as a process of ‘institutional bricolage’ (Maguire et al., 2004, p. 674), bearing in mind that outcomes often escape intentions (Giddens, 1984).

In their attempts to develop a theory of institutional entrepreneurship in mature fields, Greenwood & Suddaby (2006) point to two important circumstances under which actors might be motivated to consider change. First, the location of actors within a field will influence their relative embeddedness, where this is ‘indicated by awareness of alternatives, openness to alternatives, and motivation to change’ (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006, p. 29) – that is, an embedded actor is ‘neither motivated to change, nor aware of or open to alternatives’ (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006, p. 29). Second, latent contradictions within a field can also create the circumstances that precipitate change. Seo & Creed (2002) identified a number of distinctive types of field-level contradictions that can provoke praxis – where actors move ‘from unreflective participation in institutional reproduction to imaginative critique of existing arrangements to practical action for change’ (Seo & Creed, 2002, p. 231).

Interestingly, Avgerou (2000) makes the case that IS innovation should be viewed as an institution in and of itself. Specifically, she argues that the ‘continuous introduction of information technologies in organizations is, to a large extent, sustained by its own actors and processes’ (Avgerou, 2000, p. 234) and that the ‘ubiquitous spread of IT is sustained not necessarily because the rational arguments on its value are convincing, but because IT has captured the hopes and fears of people in their professional roles as well as in their personal lives y the wisdom of expanding computer uses in ever more organizational activities is not seriously challenged. IT applications y have become a ‘‘rational myth’’ ’ (Avgerou, 2000, pp. 237–238). Following this line of reasoning, then, we might say that IS innovation has an institutional logic of its own, which can interact with the logics of other institutional fields to provoke praxis.

The institutional perspective, of course, is not without its critics, and in our appropriation of these ideas we are sensitive to two particularly important shortcomings;

namely, the lack of attention to the mechanisms by which specific logics are made durable and ‘travel’ across tracts of time-space, and the ways in which the practices associated with institutional fields are implicated in producing distinctive kinds of ‘actorhood’ or subjectivity (see Foucault, 1980; Miller & O’Leary, 1987; Knights & Willmott, 1989; Rose, 1999; Hasselbladh & Kallinikos, 2000). The former criticism pertains to the concrete mechanism by which ideas and practices become institutionalised. Hasselbladh & Kallinikos (2000) point out that the empirical programme of institutionalism, which has been dominated by cross-sectional studies, is not very helpful for illuminating such processes as they unfold over time, preferring instead to explain things in terms of the relatively unproblematic and uninsightful notion of ‘diffusion’. To address this shortcoming, they offer an interesting analytical approach to tracing the ways in which ideas can become ‘objectified’ and transformed into ‘durable social artefacts’ (Hasselbladh & Kallinikos, 2000, p. 699).

While the former criticism addresses issues of objectification, the latter one emphasises processes of subjectification. Specifically, the argument is that institutionalists neglect 'how the embeddedness of instrumental ideas and orientations shape organizational tasks in ways that help to create distinctive forms of actorhood’ (Hasselbladh & Kallinikos, 2000, p. 699). Therefore, there is a need to consider how specific ideas, practices and technologies create the conditions for the emergence of distinctive forms of agency, thus constraining the openness of situated action (see Kallinikos, 2004). Such a perspective would suggest that the emergence of forms of institutional entrepreneurship is not a spurious process, but is very much enabled and constrained by the institutional logics at play in a given context and the forms of actorhood that they produce. As such, forms of human agency cannot be uncoupled from the practices and technologies that constitute them.

In conclusion, then, we believe that the institutionalist perspective outlined above provides a rich means of conceptualising processes of institutional change in which ICT is implicated. Crucially, it offers a way of understanding the complexities of the institutional logics that prevail in particular settings, and how these create the conditions for (and are reshaped by) the emergence of particular forms of institutional entrepreneurship (agency). In what follows, we will attempt to apply these ideas to make sense of the role of the ISIS project in institutional change in the Irish credit union movement.

## Research approach and methods

The research formed part of an interpretive, longitudinal study (1999–2006) into the use of technology within the ILCU, with particular attention given to the failed ISIS project (1998–2001). Primary data were collected from unstructured interviews with a range of people involved in the credit union movement. The sampling strategy involved interviewing as wide a range of stakeholders as possible (Miles & Huberman, 1994; Silverman, 2001), including ordinary members, full time and voluntary staff, officers and managers of individual credit unions, the President and Vice-President of the ILCU, the Chairman of ILCUTECH, members of the ISIS project team (including the project manager), the Credit Union Advisory Committee (which reports to the Irish Government), the Chief Executive of the Irish Financial Services Authority, as well as two of the external IT suppliers. In total, 62 respondents from 42 institutions were interviewed during the course of the research. Ten of those interviewed had connections with several credit union organisations, and 16 people were interviewed multiple times. The respondents were evenly split between the opponents and proponents of the project. In addition to the interviews, time was also spent observing the daily routines in three credit unions in order to gain an understanding of quotidian social practices (Silverman, 1998).

Sources of secondary data included media reports, public and confidential internal documents (including minutes of meetings, annual reports, accounts and specially commissioned consultants reports), Government white papers, debates and legislation. While credit union personnel provided some of this documentation, the majority of the secondary data were collected independently.

Following Watson’s (1994) image of research as a craft, constructing the analysis was an iterative process that involved developing and discarding themes through numerous discussions and rewrites. Re-reading the data regularly was an important part of the analysis (Wolcott, 1994) and the ISIS case history was re-written numerous times in order to tease out themes and gaps in the data that were identified during discussions. The analysis began early in the data collection process (Silverman, 2000). After the initial interviews, we compiled a list of key words that cropped up relatively frequently (including: ethos, professionalism, volunteer, amalgamation, large, small, amateur, members and service). In tandem to this, we began reading around the debates about ICT and organisational transformation. Institutional theory and, in particular, the concept of institutional entrepreneurship, offered a way of theorising the contradictions we identified in the case. From the initial list of key words, we developed the idea of two contrasting organisational forms within the credit union movement, both of which had been present from the movement’s inception, but which had evolved differently.

## The Irish credit union movement and the ISIS project

## The Irish credit union movement

The Irish credit union movement originated in Dublin during the 1950s. At a time of high interest rates in Ireland, the founders were interested in offering an alternative to hire-purchase credit and illegal moneylenders (Culloty, 1990; Quinn, 1999). The first two credit unions opened in 1958 and enthusiasm for the movement grew rapidly. Based on the Credit Union Acts, 1966 and 1997, each credit union is independently run on a non-profit basis and can offer savings and loan facilities to its members. Since its inception, the movement has played an important role in Irish society, achieving widespread recognition and popularity. The most recent figures show that for the year ending December 2007, there were 521 credit unions affiliated to the ILCU, with 2.9 million members, savings of approximately h13.4 billion and loans of h7.3 billion, representing annual growth rates of 9 and 6%, respectively (ILCU, 2008).

In addition to the legally distinct credit unions, the founders also created a centralised administrative organisation that operates alongside the individual credit unions, offering training and support services for its affiliated members. This organisation, now known as the ILCU, was created in 1960 and the first set of standard rules for credit unions were adopted at its inaugural Annual General Meeting (AGM) in 1961. Like the individual credit unions, this body was organised on a voluntary basis from the outset; the first secretary of the league performed and paid for all the administrative work and also allowed her home to serve as the league’s office until 1969, when the credit union headquarters moved to its own premises.

From these modest beginnings, the ILCU has grown alongside the credit unions. The ILCU is a non-statutory body with full-time administrative and technical staff. The Board of Directors, however, is comprised solely of voluntary officers. Membership of the ILCU is voluntary and credit unions become affiliated members when they accept and adopt the standard rules for credit unions (ILCU, 1998). Key to the ILCU’s understanding of cooperative principles are the ‘central values of equality, equity and mutual self-help’ (ILCU, 1998, p. 96). In practice, this organisational form is manifested as a notfor-profit ethos that values democratic control, social inclusion, education and a broad understanding of community. In terms of governance structure, credit unions operate on the principle of democratic control whereby members enjoy equal rights to vote. Thus, individual credit unions allow one vote per member, irrespective of the amount of their savings, while each affiliated credit union is entitled to send two voting delegates to ILCU meetings.

Traditionally the movement was organised by small groups of volunteers who ran the credit union offices in a variety of borrowed locations such as parish halls and school buildings. None of the officers received payment for this work and many of the credit unions only opened for a couple of hours per week. More recently, however, with the continued expansion and growth of the movement, many of the credit unions have begun to organise themselves on a more professional basis. Responding to the changing needs and expectations of an increasingly diverse membership base, there has been a move to build bespoke credit union premises, full-time counter staff and managers have been hired, business hours are kept, and, although the Boards of Directors are still comprised of voluntary officers, more emphasis is being placed on the professional management of each credit union. IT has also come to be seen as a key driver for modernisation and change within the movement. Interest in the perceived need for IT-enabled change reached its apotheosis during Ireland’s electronic commerce-fuelled ‘Celtic Tiger’ boom years, in the late 1990s, when technology came to be regarded as the solution to the problem of organisational transformation. As will be outlined below, the ISIS project came to be understood as the ‘silver bullet’ that would ensure that the movement continued to be successful in the future.

## IS and organisational change

In the credit unions’ early years, administrative work was done manually; membership details and accounts were all kept in handwritten ledgers. As membership grew, there was additional work for the voluntary officers and they frequently spent extra hours double-checking each other’s calculations. Adding machines were the first technological innovation adopted in the 1970s, and in the 1980s many offices began to install computers and to automate their processes. In the late 1990s, the ILCU turned to IT in an attempt to dramatically transform the organisational structure of the league. They wished to maintain the credit unions’ autonomous, decentralised status while simultaneously reaping the benefits of increased integration. Owing to the fractured nature of the credit union movement, the IS selection process was highly decentralised, with each credit union responsible for sourcing their own hardware and software. By the late 1990s, there were approximately 450 computer installations, comprised of at least 33 different software systems serviced by 26 suppliers, as well as several smaller credit unions that updated their accounts manually (ILCUTECH, 2000). It was felt that because there was no common interface between the systems, this produced operational inefficiencies making economies of scale impossible. Furthermore, the credit unions had become overly dependent on individual suppliers; in 1997 and 1998 two significant suppliers withdrew their services from credit unions.

Additional impetus to adopt a common, integrated IS solution was created by the changing competitive landscape in the financial services sector (although change had been a feature of this sector since the founding of the movement, with some of the most significant change only came about in the 1990s). The revised Credit Union Act, 1997 and the impending Single Regulatory Authority meant that credit unions would be required to produce strictly audited accounts. Falling interest rates had an impact on margins. In addition, growing competition in the retail-banking sector meant that the banks were targeting traditional credit union market segments such as holiday and car loans. New players, such as the supermarkets, were targeting the credit union membership base and threatening to saturate the market. Finally, new financial services had emerged, facilitated by developments in IT, including ATMs, direct debits, credit and laser cards, telephone and Internet banking.

Credit unions had been discussing the possibility of standard software and networking the diverse systems since an Armagh Credit Union proposal at the 1989 AGM. Although the ILCU began compiling a ‘standard specification’ document, it took years to produce and was never given to suppliers. It is against this backdrop that a group of five of the larger credit unions became frustrated at the lack of progress within the league and decided to act independently. As one of their directors explained, they had identified ‘a need to move their financial services into the electronic arena’, but there was a feeling that ‘the ILCU was not doing anything’. The five credit unions created the CUTECH company in 1994, in order to ‘pioneer the provision of automated services to their members’ (ILCUTECH, 2000). Their first, immediately successful, project was to offer ATM services to their members. It took 6 months to develop and cost h0.95 million.

This initial success did not go unnoticed by the ILCU. As one of the CUTECH founder members described it, the ILCU got ‘embarrassed into going one better’. While CUTECH was pressing ahead with innovative technological solutions, the ILCU was still discussing the idea of creating a credit union network. In 1997, however, following discussions about the need to collectively develop technology-enabled financial services, the ILCU and CUTECH formed ILCUTECH Limited, as the official technology arm of the ILCU. ILCUTECH’s mission was to ‘be the credit union movement’s professionally independent, cost efficient, self-financing, provider of technology-based services and advice which will support credit unions in their aim to be the primary financial institution of their members’ (ILCUTECH, 2000, p. 6). The initial board of ILCUTECH had 10 members: five from the ILCU and five from CUTECH.

## The ISIS project: a failed attempt at planned organisational transformation

Soon after the establishment of ILCUTECH. the ISIS project was initiated in order to create a centralised banking system for all credit unions affiliated to the ILCU. The system was conceived as a tool to enable all credit unions to offer a full range of financial products and services to their members, thus bringing the movement in line with marketplace trends and complying with the increased regulatory requirements. With ISIS, credit unions could offer services such as electronic fund transfers, ATM and debit cards. Furthermore, ISIS would remove dependence on suppliers because it was owned and managed by the credit union movement. A key feature of the system was that it would be flexible enough to cater to the needs of all credit unions, irrespective of their size and the systems already in use, thus allowing each credit union to preserve its decentralised, legally independent status while concurrently organising in a more networked fashion. The perceived importance of ISIS is illustrated by the ILCU president’s speech at the 2000 AGM

I have no doubt that the long-term viability of this movement depends, to a large degree, on the success of ISIS y . The real question remains, as it always has been, ‘can we afford not to have it?’. (ILCU, 2000, p. 2)

ILCUTECH hired a British financial services consultancy to develop the ISIS proposal in July 1998 and the consultants initially estimated that the project would cost h50 million. Difficulties emerged almost immediately, however, as a ‘core group’ of the CUTECH credit unions ‘balked at the price’ of ISIS and refused to participate in the project. Tullamore Credit Union, one of the leading players in CUTECH, commissioned an independent consultant’s report into the costing of ISIS. This report was highly critical of the ISIS proposal and so they asked their representative to resign from the ILCUTECH Board at the beginning of the project in order to register their opposition to it. The feeling in one of the credit unions was that ILCUTECH did not have ‘the loyalty, confidence and commitment of the large credit unions to go down that (ISIS) path’. As one director explained, there was ‘no benefit’ in his credit union signing up for ISIS because their existing systems offered just as much functionality and ‘is a lot cheaper’.

It was decided, however, that ILCUTECH would proceed with ISIS if 75% of the membership of the ILCU were prepared to back it. It was envisaged that ISIS would cost each credit union h25.40 per member, with an initial non-refundable payment of h12.70 per member, followed by a further h12.70 upon delivery of the system. By March 1999, ILCUTECH had raised slightly over h21 million from credit unions representing 1,301,367 members. In total, 74.36% of the movement was committed to the project (ILCUTECH, 2000; Sisk, 2001). This fell short of the 75% commitment needed for ISIS to be viable and many credit unions that signed up for ISIS failed to pay the non-refundable h12.70 per member. Despite this, the ILCUTECH Board agreed to proceed with ISIS. For the remainder of 1999, ILCUTECH and the consultants concentrated on selecting a software supplier, sourcing a managed service provider, establishing criteria for the pilot sites and selecting which credit unions should be used to test ISIS.

In October 1999, the consultants informed the Board of ILCUTECH that the project was on schedule and within the budget, yet a serious cost overrun was revealed when an initial project review was conducted just 3 months later in January 2000. It was then revealed that the initial project cost had been based on estimated figures; the figures for the managed service provider, VAT, ILCUTECH set-up, solution delivery, rollout and training were all under-estimated (ILCUTECH, 2000), yet this had not been communicated to the delegates voting on the project. At the ILCU’s AGM in April 2000, the credit unions were informed that the initial proposal underestimated the cost of ISIS and the project was then expected to cost h87 million instead of h50 million. To make matters worse, progress on the implementation phase of the project was slower than originally planned.

These figures should have cast serious doubts on the project’s viability, but rather than questioning ILCU-TECH, the ILCU Board sanctioned a loan of h7 million from the Savings Protection Scheme (SPS) Fund to the ISIS project, without informing the general ILCU membership. The SPS fund is a movement-wide scheme designed to protect members’ savings in the event of insolvency or other financial default at a credit union (ILCU, 1998, p. 29). The Registrar of Friendly Societies, who at that point had responsibility for the regulation of credit unions, wrote to the ILCU stating his ‘strong objection in principle’ to the use of SPS funds for ISIS ‘in the light of the precarious state of the project’ (Sisk, 2001, p. 11). His principal objection was that credit unions had already paid h22 million as initial funding for ISIS and, when the h7 million loan from the SPS fund was added to this, the credit unions were liable to lose h29 million if the project did not succeed (Sisk, 2001, 2002). However, he also claimed that the ILCU Board had misrepresented his position on the project to AGM delegates, creating an impression that he approved of the project.

The project’s operational and financial difficulties provoked tension in the movement as individual credit unions expressed anger, disbelief and disappointment about the findings. Opinion was divided over who was responsible for the project’s difficulty. As a result of the controversy, the ILCU was forced to convene a Special General Meeting (SGM) on 8–9 July 2000 in order to discuss the future of the project.

After the initial refusal to participate, some of the original CUTECH credit unions had turned their attention away from the project. However, the misuse of communal SPS funds proved to be the turning point for this group. In an interview conducted shortly before the July 2000 emergency SGM, one director explained that they ‘stayed quiet’ in the hope that other credit unions would see sense, but when no one was saying anything they felt a sense of responsibility to the movement and they decided to make a decisive statement, in case their silence was ‘misinterpreted as acquiescence’.

What cannot be overstated is their sense of frustration at the mismanagement and misinformation surrounding the project. Thus, in advance of the SGM, 19 of the larger credit unions in Ireland wrote a letter (which became known as the ‘Tullamore Letter’) to the Registrar of Friendly Societies and every credit union in the country outlining their concern about the developments in the project and what they understood to be the misuse of ILCU funds. The letter, dated 30 June 2000, stated that the project was the most divisive issue ever faced by the credit union movement:

We are concerned that the total investment in ISIS and the operations costs for Credit Unions have not been clearly identified and quantified y In our view, the League Board and the Board of ILCUTECH have not demonstrated that they have the competence or the expertise to manage a project of this size and complexity. y The project now appears to have gone out of control and is drifting into ongoing uncontainable costs.

In an unprecedented step, the letter, together with confidential reports, was leaked to the media (and to the researchers). Getting the media involved in the story was as significant as sending the letter to the Registrar of Friendly Societies; this was the first time in the movement’s history that credit unions had received negative press coverage. It was also a calculated risk, because the leak could also permanently damage the credit unions’ reputation, however, because of the size and scope of the movement, with over 500 credit unions registered with the ILCU, many credit union members would neither have been aware of the project, nor of its difficulties. Media involvement was thus the only way that the serious problems with the project could be communicated to the membership. The ISIS project became front-page news in the run-up to the SGM and the sense of crisis deepened. The media reports focused on the threat of a split in the movement, the cash crisis, the delays to the project and the manner in which various ILCU and ILCUTECH officials were trying to ‘play down’ the crisis. Nevertheless, delegates at the SGM voted to commit an extra h2.5 million in order to carry out a ‘root and branch’ review of the project.

Ultimately, however, the ISIS project never recovered from these events. Following the circulation of the letter, interpretations of ISIS came to be almost exclusively associated with negative sentiments. In discussing the ‘ISIS Crisis’, reporters in the national media comparing the non-profit credit unions with for-profit businesses, invariably found the credit unions wanting and used terms such as ‘debacle’ and ‘fiasco’ to describe the project. In February 2001, the ISIS project was officially terminated, with a loss of h35 million of members’ savings and having never gone beyond pilot testing.

Interestingly, the words ‘debacle’ and ‘fiasco’ have since become the commonly accepted terms used by credit union personnel to describe ISIS. For example, John O’Regan, President of the ILCU, referred to the ‘ISIS debacle’ in his inaugural interview with the Credit Union Review (ILCU, 2002, p. 5). Similarly, a founder member of one of the industrial credit unions, when asked about ISIS in 2003, said, ‘That was actually a fiasco. That whole thing was a fiasco. From one, day one’.

To use the Registrar’s phrase, ISIS was ‘dead in the water’ (Sisk, 2002, p. 11) and its spectacular failure has had several consequences for the ILCU, many of them unintended. Confidence in the movement's ability to implement a large-scale project has been eroded and, after years of favourable coverage, the media has become consistently critical of the movement. Furthermore, there was a very real danger of a split in the movement, with several of the larger credit unions (including many signatories of the Tullamore Letter) threatening to form a breakaway umbrella organisation (CUDA). In order to prevent a split developing, the ILCU commis sioned an independent report into its organisational structure and funding. The report, entitled ‘A Time for Change’, was circulated to credit unions in advance of the April 2002 AGM. One of the key findings of the report highlighted the extent of political manoeuvrings within the ‘dysfunctional’ ILCU Board:

The board functions not as a team but in a very fractured way; blame is spread around; suspicion is the order of the day; trust is absent. One consequence is that long periods of time can be spent approving the minutes of a previous meeting. (Keena, 2004, p. 16)

A key recommendation was that a CEO should be appointed to manage the ILCU professionally. The credit union movement endorsed the report at another SGM in June 2002 and they began the slow process of reforming the structures of the ILCU. The first CEO was appointed in 2003 and his successor recently took up office. Interestingly, in the years since the project’s collapse, the credit unions that set up CUDA have maintained their affiliation to the ILCU.

ICT and institutional change in the Irish credit union movement: making sense of the ISIS ‘fiasco In making sense of the ISIS ‘fiasco’ and its implications for the Irish credit union movement, we draw on some of the theoretical concepts outlined earlier to examine the complex institutional context that prevailed within the organisation, and how this shaped (and was shaped by) the actions of key agents. In particular, we draw attention to the important role of contradictions between different institutional logics that co-existed within the movement in creating the conditions that facilitated the emergence of important forms of institutional entrepreneurship. Furthermore, we argue that the institutional arrangements associated with the governance of the ILCU did not promote appropriate forms of accountability for managing the risks associated with a project of the scale and significance of ISIS. We conclude by arguing that the ISIS project inadvertently ended up playing a key role in processes of institutional change; the scope and intensity of the engagement around the change process led to the crystallisation of key tensions that had remained unarticulated and un-reconciled for many years, and triggered some very consequential interventions that finally forced the movement to openly confront these issues.

Since their inception in the 1950s, credit unions have straddled two distinctive institutional fields; those of cooperative movements and financial services.

Traditionally, the institutional logic of the cooperative field, emphasising the kind of values mentioned earlier, such as mutual self-help, social inclusion and justice, community service, democratic control and a not-forprofit ethos, has always enjoyed a pre-eminent position within the movement. Indeed, for most of the movement’s history this institutional logic peacefully co-existed with that of the financial services field. More recently, however, the latter field has undergone a fundamental transformation as a result of a range of regulatory, competitive and technological jolts.<sup>1</sup> Irish banking has always closely followed British banking models (McGowan, 1990; O<sup>´</sup> Gra´da, 1997), both in terms of lending practices and attitudes to customers. Although the Irish banks are not yet at par with the U.K. model (Kerfoot & Knights, 1993; Knights & McCabe, 2001), an entrepreneurial, competitive approach to banking has become widespread. Irish retail banks have increasingly accepted the dictum that financial institutions must harness the potential of IT in order to survive (Gandy & Chapman, 1997; Watkins, 1998). The rationalisation of the branch network, preference for urban rather than rural branches, the centralisation of key services, diversification of the product range and the move, facilitated by technological advances, to reduce inbranch transactions are all features of this new, aggressively competitive approach.

In response to these developments, many of the credit unions have begun to organise themselves on a more professional basis. Although the Boards of Directors are still comprised of voluntary officers, more emphasis is being placed on the professional management of each credit union and the new institutional logic of the financial services field is exerting a marked influence. This logic implies that credit unions adopt the norms and practices of for-profit business in order to compete more effectively with the commercially run financial institutions and to ensure their survival, and it is particularly influential among larger credit unions that have significant savings to invest on their members’ behalf. Within these credit unions, the organisational structure is more formal, full-time management staff are employed and a greater range of financial products and services are offered to the members. These credit unions actively target new members in order to sustain growth rates. They compete against the for-profit financial institutions and manage daily operations according to detailed strategic, financial and marketing plans.

It is important to note, however, that the effects of the transformation of the financial services field have been experienced differentially by credit unions. Each branch is a distinct legal entity and, with over 500 credit unions affiliated to the ILCU, they represent a vast range of memberships, assets and practices. Over the years, the movement grew rapidly throughout Ireland, but different branches grew at different rates and in different trajectories reflecting the needs of an increasingly diverse membership base. While credit unions initially catered for those who did not have access to credit from banks, with increasing prosperity and modernisation in Ireland, many branches gradually began to attract more middle class and professional members who saw them as an attractive alternative to the for-profit banks. As a result, credit unions with a heterogeneous membership base, typically urban or larger rural offices, often had little in common with smaller credit unions that had limited opening hours and a smaller, more homogeneous membership.

As a result of these changes, the Irish credit union movement has become marked by interesting forms of organisational hybridity, as the two institutional logics in question have been mixed in varying ways. Different branches have exposure to different ideas and influences, and have differential access to resources, depending on their location within the conjoined institutional field of credit unions. Importantly, although it is possible to identify camps that are more influenced by one logic than the other, there has been no radical polarisation of the movement and the practices associated with both logics are generally seen as legitimate. For instance, those credit unions that have embraced the institutional logic of financial services most fervently do not consider their practices to be at odds with the traditional cooperative operating principles of the movement, while the more conservative unions are well disposed to modernisation efforts. Having said that, the institutional logic of the financial services field has become increasingly difficult to reconcile with the traditional logic of the cooperative field. giving rise to the emergence of a number of latent contradictions that were key to provoking praxis on the part of the group of well-resourced institutional entrepreneurs that formed CUTECH in 1994. New ideas and practices facilitated the emergence of new forms of actorhood (Hasselbladh & Kallinikos, 2000), which provided the means of framing issues and interventions in new ways.

With large memberships and significant savings to invest on their members’ behalf, the CUTECH unions had both the resources and the interest (associated with their location within the conjoined institutional field) to act as institutional entrepreneurs. As one of their directors explained, they had identified ‘a need to move their financial services into the electronic arena’, but there was a feeling that ‘the ILCU was not doing anything’. The formation of CUTECH, then, was borne out of frustration with the ILCU’s lack of urgency in relation to IS innovation, and implicitly signalled a move towards the creation of a twin track credit union movement, where the larger, more progressive unions united to pursue change initiatives in a faster way. By so doing, they could bypass what they considered to be ineffective governance structures that emphasised politicking at the expense of decisive action, and which were based on very diffuse forms of authority and accountability.

These frustrations with the traditional modus operandi of the ILCU were evident in some of the more critical interpretations of the motivation that underpinned their efforts to bring CUTECH back into the fold with the formation of ILCUTECH. As one of the CUTECH founder members described it, the ILCU got ‘embarrassed into going one better’ having witnessed the success of the ATM initiative. Another described the ILCU as a ‘monopolistic, political organisation’, and argued that their motivation for engaging with CUTECH in the manner that they did was so that they could ‘exercise control over it’. Despite such reservations, however, CUTECH’s willingness to work with the ILCU, and to agree to the formation of ILCUTECH, is indicative of their sense of loyalty to the broader Irish credit union movement; they saw this as an opportunity to bring the rest of the movement with them in their drive towards modernisation and to institutionalise their entrepreneurial activities. Moreover, the case for a shared, nationwide, IT infrastructure in the shape of a system like ISIS was very persuasive and appealing in the context of the pre-millennium dot com hype and the previous success enjoyed by CUTECH in relation to ATM facilities. At this point of time, the institution of IS innovation (Avgerou, 2000) was particularly influential and ISIS appeared to offer a technological elixir for the challenges faced by the movement. In particular, it promised the delivery of new services and greater organisational flexibility without more thoroughgoing institutional reform.

Despite encouraging signs in the early days – the initial board of ILCUTECH had 10 members: five from the ILCU and five from CUTECH, in itself a move that seems to indicate some kind of political compromise – serious divisions emerged early in the ISIS Project as some of the CUTECH-founder credit unions were dismayed at the way the ILCUTECH board was managing it. As we have seen, many of these credit unions refused to participate in the project and the Tullamore branch even went so far as to commission a report into the projected costs and resign their position on the ILCUTECH board in protest. It took the extraordinary events that gave rise to the calling of the SGM (i.e. the radical revision of the cost and time estimates for ISIS, the sanctioning of the loan from the SPS fund and the letter of admonishment from the Registrar of Friendly Societies) to provoke a concerted and decisive response from the CUTECH cohort, that had watched in frustration from the sidelines until that point.

These events acted as a trigger that catalysed a collective outpouring of frustration and appeared to crystallise, for many of the more progressive unions, the extent of the difficulties with the way the ILCU was run. A member of the Credit Union Advisory Committee expressed exasperation at the lack of accountability and suggested that the ILCU tried to pin responsibility on the consultants and that it was ‘typical of democratic, voluntary organisations that they would try to create scapegoats rather than accepting blame themselves’. Some of the credit union managers interviewed cited poor communication (‘the ILCU offers a dressed-up, shop window version of what they are doing’), ‘ludicrous’ financial estimates and ‘impossible logistics’. A senior member of the ISIS project team pointed to the ILCUTECH board’s lack of expertise with managing large-scale IT projects and their resultant over-dependence on the advice of consultants who had little experience of the Irish context and a limited understanding of ‘the special needs of the movement’.

Perhaps the key issue here pertained to governance structures and accountability, where clear contradictions existed between the institutional logic of the cooperative field and that of modern financial services. It could be argued that ILCUTECH became vulnerable because it incorporated the governance structures of cooperative institutions, with their emphasis on committees, diffused responsibility and governance by assent, which contrasted sharply with the emphasis on hierarchical responsibility, careful planning and budgetary control that is so central to the governance logic of the financial services field. In Flyvbjerg et al.’s (2003) terms, it might be argued that the institutional arrangements that formed the context of the ISIS project decision making, and which pertained more broadly within the ILCU, were inappropriate for managing this kind of large-scale infrastructural project. It was the power of this latent contradiction between different governance logics that led these disaffected and frustrated credit unions to take the unprecedented step of involving the national media and threatening to leave the ILCU for good.

Even in their rebellion, however, it was still possible to detect a sense of ambivalence on the part of these latter credit unions as they still maintained an overarching sense of loyalty towards the movement and its traditional values. Their attempt at disassociation with the ILCU through the formation of the CUDA appears to have been a half-hearted symbolic display of frustration, rather than a genuine effort to do it alone. The fact that they have all continued to maintain their affiliations to the ILCU indicates a desire to maintain the integrity of the broader Irish credit union movement. The ILCU, for their part, have made significant changes as well. Chastened by the ISIS ‘fiasco’, and fearing a broader collapse of the movement, they commissioned and accepted the findings of the independent report that, among other things, recommended the reformation of the governance structures of the ILCU and the appointment of a full-time CEO.

## Conclusions and implications

In this paper we have attempted to synthesise a distinctive theoretical approach that offers a rich perspective on processes of IS innovation and institutional change. Specifically, the approach draws on institutional theory as a means of unravelling the complex institutional heritage that can be very influential in shaping the outcomes of IS implementation attempts in a given context. This perspective was supplemented by the work of Flyvbjerg et al. (2003) and Avgerou (2000) so as to emphasise the importance of other institutional arrangements pertaining to governance structures/practices on large projects, and of assumptions about the transformative potential of ICT. In developing the approach, we have sought to avoid the dangers of structural or technological determinism by developing the notion of institutional entrepreneurship. Multiple institutional influences may give rise to latent contradictions, which can create the conditions for the emergence of specific forms of institutional entrepreneurship that facilitate praxis. The contingent and political nature of praxis, where the actions of specific institutional entrepreneurs often escape intentions, is stressed as a means of rejecting voluntaristic tendencies.

The development and refinement of this theoretical approach went hand in hand with our attempts to make sense of the complex and distinctive organisational setting that is the Irish credit union movement. This kind of empirical context is unusual in the IS literature (Tan & Pan, 2003), not only because of the semiprofessional, not-for-profit status of credit unions, but also because of the movement’s position at the intersection of two powerful institutional fields – those of cooperatives and financial services – which resulted in the emergence of interesting forms of organisational hybridity and attendant latent contradictions. Thus, the case serves as an excellent example of the role of alternative (and, at times, competing) institutional logics in shaping the distinctive texture of organisational life. In particular, the case illustrates the ambivalence at the heart of much of organisational life, where agencies are torn between competing logics and associated values in informing their actions. Furthermore, we emphasised the role of agents’ location within institutional fields in shaping how different mixes of such influences get articulated in practice.

Although the ISIS infrastructural project did not proceed very far, it played a central role in facilitating institutional change through the manner in which it inadvertently acted as a trigger for decisive action on the part of the group of institutional entrepreneurs in question. The scope and significance of the project was crucial in this respect, as it helped underscore the shortcomings of existing governance structures/practices.
