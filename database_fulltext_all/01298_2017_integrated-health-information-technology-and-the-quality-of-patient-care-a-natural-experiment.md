ABSTRACT: Fragmentation of care delivery remains one of the main challenges of healthcare systems. In this study, we examine health information technology (HIT) that is electronically integrated across care physicians, specialists, hospitals, and pharmacists and its impact on quality of care. Drawing on the structure–process–outcome framework, we hypothesize that integrated HIT will have both a direct and an indirect effect on quality of care. By providing more complete information to physicians and allowing them to detect and manage health problems early in the care cycle, integrated HIT is expected to directly improve quality of care. Integrated HIT is also hypothesized to improve quality of care indirectly by increasing continuity of care and reducing the odds of therapeutic errors. We test these hypotheses through a contemporaneous natural experiment of a matched set of 15,626 outpatients receiving ambulatory care through an integrated HIT system and 15,626 outpatients in a control group. The results show that the patients treated with the integrated system had better quality of care in the follow-up period than patients of the control group. Integrated HIT also had an indirect effect on quality of care through an increase in continuity of care. Our study contributes to research by examining how an integrated HIT system—rather than specific HIT components—influences patient-related outcomes, and how this effect is mediated by two key processes: continuity of care and therapeutic duplication errors. Our work also helps decision makers to better understand the impact of HIT in a complex and highly decentralized and fragmented care delivery system.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: continuity of care, health information technology, I-HIT, information technology impact, integrated HIT, IT and healthcare, quality of care.

Delivering high quality of care (QoC) to patients is an important, yet challenging task [47]. Such challenges are especially pronounced for ambulatory care, which is defined as the provision of health services that do not require overnight hospitalization [8]. Indeed, ambulatory care is composed of numerous diverse activities including providing preventive care, treating emergencies, managing chronic diseases, monitoring patients, verifying patients’ histories, and prescribing medications. Performing such activities effectively requires complex information processing with multiple players (e.g., general practitioners, specialists, pharmacists, patients) who are only partially connected in highly decentralized networks. Consequently, medical decisions are often made under uncertainty and without complete information. Fragmented care (i.e., the fact that patients often consult numerous primary care physicians and/or specialists) compounds these issues and often leads to prescription problems, incomplete medication information, and delays in the detection and management of changes in chronic illness. In turn, this can create health complications and fragment care even more.

In recent years, the use of health information technology (HIT)—which is defined as “an information system including all computer-based components which are used by healthcare professionals or the patients themselves in the context of inpatient or outpatient patient care to process patient-related data, information, or knowledge” [2, p. 46]—has been seen as a way to overcome these challenges and improve the overall performance of healthcare delivery [4, 14, 42]. HIT includes a range of technologies used by providers, such as electronic medical records (EMR), electronic health records (EHR), computerized physician order entry (CPOE), and clinical decision support systems (CDSS), among others.<sup>1</sup> By integrating distinct processes across the healthcare value chain, HIT can increase the efficiency, effectiveness, coordination, and responsiveness of the key stakeholders including physicians, retail pharmacies, hospitals, and insurance companies and improve the QoC [5, 46]. Above all, the benefits to patients can be significant, especially for those with multiple chronic conditions. For instance, HIT can decrease adverse drug effects, unnecessary hospitalizations, and unnecessary tests [33]. HIT can also allow physicians to allocate time more effectively by reducing the time spent on secondary activities (e.g., independently gathering redundant information about patients’ histories, recent visits, and current medications) and spending more time on activities that can directly improve the QoC [69].

The substantial interest and investments in HIT have spawned a wealth of research in healthcare and IS on the impact of HIT on patient QoC. Extant studies have focused on the factors that influence QoC processes and/or outcomes, such as the presence of HIT components (e.g., [1, 4, 10, 46]), the extent of HIT use (e.g., [3, 33, 69, 70]), the level of HIT automation [6], and overall HIT expenditure [47].

While extant studies have independently provided excellent insights, our overall understanding of the impact of HIT on QoC is still limited and equivocal [14, 33], with the empirical results ranging between positive (e.g., [5, 69]), nonsignificant [1, 46], and negative [34]. Studies have also examined isolated parts within the nomological network that links HIT to intermediate process variables and to QoC outcomes (e.g., [5, 35, 46, 63]). Consequently, we have a partial view of the benefits of HIT. This limits our ability to provide precise actionable guidelines to healthcare managers and public administrators and also contributes to the mixed state of empirical results. Prior studies have also identified two process variables as important (adherence to recommended care and reduction of medication errors), which provides an opportunity to uncover additional variables that may help explain QoC outcomes. Furthermore, extant studies have focused on specific HIT components rather than comprehensive, integrated systems (e.g., [1, 18, 63]). Finally, it is difficult to make causal claims about the impact of HIT because most studies have been either cross-sectional in nature or have used longitudinal designs that lacked a control group.

The present study extends prior research in several ways. First, we study the impact of an integrated HIT application that supports both internal integration (within physician offices) and external integration (linking physicians, pharmacists, and hospitals) on patient-related outcomes. Second, drawing on the structure–process–outcome (SPO) framework for evaluating the QoC [15, 19], we examine the nomological network that links HIT to two key intermediate QoC process measures (continuity of care and therapeutic duplication errors) and to QoC outcomes. We thus provide a comprehensive approach to understanding the impact of HIT. Third, this research provides a new conceptualization of continuity of care (CoC) and argues that informational CoC achieved by the use of an integrated HIT is related to management and relational CoC and to QoC. Finally, we provide solid and unique empirical evidence based on a natural experiment of 31,252 patients examined over two years using a prospective controlled cohort study. Similarly, the availability of data for twelve months before and twelve months after the HIT implementation provides strong evidence on the organizational value of HIT.

## Health Information Technology and Quality of Care

We reviewed the empirical research on HIT and QoC in the IS and medical informatics literatures. We focused on quantitative studies that employed inferential statistical procedures and had large sample sizes to enable us to synthesize the relationships between HIT and QoC processes and outcomes.

Our review is organized according to the SPO framework [15, 19], which is premised on the idea that the resources used by providers (structure) improve the processes of delivering care to patients (process), which in turn enhances the ultimate outcomes experienced by patients (outcome). Structure variables can include physical, technological, and people resources and how they are used within the care delivery system [19]. Process variables focus on the actions and interactions that occur while providers are delivering care to patients and the results of those actions and interactions. These variables are under the control of the healthcare providers and are measured by the effectiveness of the clinical care and interpersonal care delivered by providers [4, 15]. Rather than looking at the final results achieved on the patients, the process variables examine whether “good medical care has been applied” [19, p. 169]. The outcome variables represent the consequences of care on patients as assessed by their health status and/or their health evaluations [15].<sup>2</sup>

Based on the SPO framework, we group studies into three categories: studies on the direct effects of HIT on QoC outcomes (see Online Appendix 1), studies that examined the effects of HIT on QoC processes such as adherence to recommended care and reduction of medical errors (see Online Appendix 2), and studies that included both process measures and outcome measures of care (see Online Appendix 3). In Appendixes 1–3, the structure variables listed in the third column focus on technological resources and specifically HIT systems or components (e.g., EHR, EMR, CPOE, CDSS, or a combination thereof). For the process and outcome variables (fourth and fifth columns, respectively), we focus on patient-related variables. We exclude studies where the processes or outcomes were not patient-centric, such as staff workflow [29] and hospital productivity [48].

The first category in Online Appendix 1 shows some studies reporting positive direct effects of HIT on QoC outcomes [5, 10, 47, 49, 54, 69] and other studies reporting no effects [1, 46] or even negative effects [34]. These mixed results were corroborated by a number of literature reviews and meta-analyses covering a range of contexts and time periods [13, 14, 30]. Also, the mixed results involved a range of HIT systems including EMR [1, 30], EHR [34, 46], CPOE [46], and CDSS [1, 13].

The results imply that the mixed results may be partly explained by examining the extent to which the HIT implemented were integrated. For instance, it was found that the benefits of reduced mortality rates were experienced primarily by patients with diagnoses requiring extensive coordination across specialties [46]. Similarly, a study of patients receiving obstetric care reported lower infant mortality rates for providers using HIT with integrative functionalities beyond bare-bone EMR systems [49]. In a study of hospital cardiology patients, higher QoC resulted from a greater number of integrated HIT and more interoperable integration sequences [5].

The second category shows that research has focused on two process measures of care (see Online Appendix 2). The first, adherence to recommended care, represents the extent to which providers deliver relevant and effective care services including advice, testing, and treatments for a range of clinical domains. The evidence suggests only weak effects of HIT on adherence to recommended care, with the results varying substantially across different practices (e.g., [66, 70]) and the specific measures used (e.g., [35, 45]).

The second process measure, medication errors, denotes the different types of prescribing errors committed by providers during care delivery and includes procedural errors (incomplete, unclear, or illegible orders) and clinical errors (prescribing wrong or excessive dosages, failing to detect different types of drug interactions, therapeutic duplication). Most of the studies in this category examined whether medication errors were influenced by the presence of CPOE systems with limited or with more advanced CDSS capabilities (alerts and recommendations). The studies in Online Appendix 2 show that CPOE systems generally reduce medication error rates (e.g., [20]) and odds (e.g., [43, 63]), especially when combined with advanced CDSS capabilities [32]. One study found that the effect was strongest on procedural errors [67].

The third category represents studies that include both process and outcome measures of care that might be influenced by HIT (see Online Appendix 3). Studies that examined the direct path between HIT and outcome measures were mixed. Some reported positive effects such as decreased rates of office visits [22] and decreased risks of thrombosis and embolism [38]. Others found nonsignificant effects of HIT on blood pressure control odds [36] and rate ratios [3]. Further, the effect of HIT on patients’ HbA1c levels was negative in some studies [33] and nonsignificant in others [3]. Some findings were also mixed across different systems (e.g., cardiology IT but not administrative IT having a positive impact [4]) and across different sites (e.g., [17, 60]).

Furthermore, the results on the path linking HIT to process measures again indicate limited evidence of a positive relationship between HIT and adherence to recommended care (e.g., [17, 22, 36, 60]). Finally, despite including intermediate and final outcomes, the mediating effects were either not hypothesized (e.g., [3, 17, 60]) or hypothesized but not tested [4, 33, 65] in the extant studies.

In summary, we highlight four important gaps in the literature. First, most studies have shed light on parts of the nomological network of HIT impact. However, our understanding of the overall path linking HIT to intermediate variables and to QoC outcomes remains limited. The direct effects of HIT on QoC outcomes have been mixed or inconclusive. One explanation can be that the dependent variables studied (e.g., mortality) are conceptually and empirically too distant from the independent variable of interest, in our case HIT, making it difficult to capture significant relationships between the two. Studies that have focused on the impact of HIT on intermediate variables (adherence to recommended care, reduction in medication errors) have not linked these to QoC outcomes. And those that have analyzed the complete path reported inconclusive results and/or did not hypothesize or test the mediating effects. It is also possible that given the complex nature of the delivery care processes, studies need to focus on more key intermediate variables to capture the mediating effects of HIT on QoC [4, 52].

Second, most research examined HIT components rather than integrated systems. For instance, studies looking at medication errors examined CPOE with varying degrees of CDSS capabilities [18, 32, 63, 67]. Others studied specific systems or components such as CDSS alerts [38], EMR [1, 49, 65], EHR (e.g., [21, 34]), cardiology IT [4, 10], and telemedicine [69]. Some studies looked at combinations of systems such as EHR and CPOE [45, 46] and commercially available EHR with CDSS capabilities [35].

While focusing on specific HIT components has provided insights into the finegrained benefits of specific HIT tools, it does not address the potential impact of overall, integrated systems. Some studies have argued that a system with isolated components that are combined together may be less effective than an integrated system with a comprehensive set of functionalities that are designed and built to work together [45]. A review of research on HIT implementations between 2007 and 2010 found negative effects of HIT on patient outcomes and suggested that these were due to the use of systems that were not well integrated with suppliers, inhibited internal interactions among providers, triggered work flow problems, or did not provide comprehensive information on patients [14]. Other scholars have also attributed mixed results to variations in the integrative capabilities of the EHR implemented at the different practices [66].

Hence, given the complex and highly decentralized and fragmented nature of the care delivery system, the HIT studied might not have been broad enough and sufficiently integrated to impact QoC. The ambulatory care practice is a complex setting where physicians’ tasks are often unstructured and interconnected with other specialists within the clinic, but also with external partners such as other physicians, pharmacists, and laboratories [3, 8, 18]. In this complex information-processing environment, using HIT systems that integrate processes both within practices (internal integration) and across practices (external integration) seems essential for improving care outcomes.

Third, prior studies have focused mainly on two intermediate variables (adherence to recommended care and reduction of medication errors), leaving room to extend the literature by uncovering other potential process variables that may help explain QoC outcomes. For instance, continuity of care, while absent from the quantitative studies we analyzed,<sup>3</sup> has been identified in qualitative interviews with providers as an important variable that can be enabled by HIT and that may improve QoC outcomes [52, 55]. While adherence to care and reduction of medication errors tap into the clinical care dimension of the process category of the SPO framework, continuity of care (CoC)—conceptualized as a patient’s perception that providers know what has happened before (informational continuity), that different providers agree on a management plan (management continuity), and that a provider who knows them will care for them in the future (relational continuity) [27]—covers both dimensions (clinical care and interpersonal care).

Finally, most studies have either employed cross-sectional designs (with a single posttest measure or with posttest and retrospective pretest measures) or longitudinal designs where a given group is compared with itself over time (pretest and posttest). While these studies have shed important light on HIT and QoC processes and outcomes, they lend themselves less well to causal inference given the absence of a control group that is compared to the treatment group over time. Only five studies employed a pretest–posttest panel design with a control group [1, 32, 63, 66, 67]. Of those, two studies did not follow the same patients before and after the HIT intervention [32, 67]. In another two studies, the patients in the treatment and control groups were seen by separate providers, which raises questions about heterogeneity among the two groups [1, 66].

Our study addresses all these issues and extends previous studies in several ways. We examine HIT that is integrated both internally and externally across different units of the care delivery system (primary care physicians, specialists, and pharmacists) and we thereby assess the impact of HIT in a comprehensive way. In addition, this study develops a nomological network of HIT impact grounded in the SPO framework. We analyze both the direct effects of HIT on QoC outcomes and its indirect effects through CoC and therapeutic duplication (a type of medication error). This integrated model allows us to determine whether the link between HIT and QoC outcomes is partially or fully mediated by process measures. This provides insights into how technology affects healthcare processes and outcomes. Finally, we conduct a natural experiment of 31,252 patients studied over two years using a prospective controlled cohort study (pretest–posttest design with control). This design provides strong causal evidence of the value of the HIT.

## Conceptual Model and Hypotheses

The ambulatory care practice is a complex environment in which information on patient treatments, diagnoses, prescriptions, and therapy is distributed across many providers within a care clinic (e.g., specialists, nurses, technicians) and outside the clinic (e.g., independent providers, providers from other clinics or hospitals, retail pharmacies) [8]. In fact, the ambulatory care system can be viewed as a decentralized network of specialized nodes, each of which is holding complementary knowledge about patients, treatments, test results, and current medications. Care delivery is episodic and fragmented across providers, times, and settings.

QoC in such an environment depends heavily on the ability to maintain CoC and to enhance patient safety by reducing medication errors. First, CoC is important because it allows different providers to deliver individualized and consistent care to patients over time. Second, reducing the odds of medication errors is challenging yet critical in this distributed environment because this can avoid serious consequences such as adverse drug events, hospital admissions, or even mortality [18, 63].

The essence of achieving CoC and reducing the odds of medication errors such as therapeutic duplication (i.e., prescribing two or more drugs with similar therapeutic effects) in a decentralized ambulatory care system is to enable all providers and units to behave in a well-coordinated way, as a whole from the patient’s perspective [14]. Each part is interdependent and needs to be responsive to the requirements of the other parts of the care system. This can be achieved only if the “system” is integrated (both internally among units of a given clinic and externally, between a clinic and its partners) and information flows seamlessly across the whole care system. Lack of integration can impede care continuity and patient safety.

Consequently, we define an integrated HIT as a comprehensive healthcare system that enables distinct and interdependent components to behave as a unified whole. The term components refers to systems, processes, and people (i.e., key stakeholders such as primary care physicians, specialists, and retail pharmacists). Integrated HIT can achieve both internal and external integration [11]. In ambulatory care, internal integration couples the components within a given practice through standardized electronic interfaces. External integration couples the components of a given practice with outside partners such as other clinics, providers, and retail pharmacies. An integrated HIT can support the clinical workflow and help coordinate care, both within and across primary care practices. It achieves this by standardizing procedures, tasks, information, language, and interfaces [51]. Therefore, regardless of whether a patient sees the same or different physicians or visits the same or different pharmacies, integrated HIT allows all stakeholders in the care system to continuously share consistent and timely information, coordinate treatments and other activities, and mutually adjust to each other. This can lead to greater CoC, lower odds of medication errors, and higher QoC. As shown in Figure 1, we hypothesize that integrated HIT will influence QoC both directly and indirectly through CoC and therapeutic duplication.

## Integrated HIT and Quality of Care

An integrated HIT allows an ambulatory care practice to fuse the functional processes of its different units and to transfer and share data, information, and knowledge internally and with external stakeholders, thereby improving QoC. An internally integrated HIT standardizes communications, processes, and data schema across a practice’s units. Consequently, care providers can better streamline and coordinate their activities, quickly adapt their routines and processes in response to changing demands (e.g., changes in patient health conditions), more easily reconfigure their processes, reschedule jobs, and reassign resources to adjust to changes in patient care [5, 51]. For example, internally integrated HIT in an ambulatory clinic allow a given physician to exploit knowledge from other units such as the laboratory and nurse departments, to be responsive to changes, and to act as a unified whole. Such integration can improve the QoC because of increased adherence to guidelines and protocol-based care [3, 4, 30, 60].

An externally integrated HIT extends these capabilities to external stakeholders and also facilitates knowledge exploration (i.e., sharing new knowledge) among them [51]. This is because it provides a common lexicon/language and enables knowledge to be transferred across providers of different practices. Consequently, providers can learn best practices and new routines, explore and gain new knowledge on patient conditions, medication levels, specialists’ diagnoses, and laboratory results, and generally make better care decisions, which can improve QoC [65]. Since the knowledge of specialized experts is distributed across the healthcare system, externally integrated HIT allows providers to draw upon such expertise and to access a range of knowledge domains. This also means that providers can integrate different perspectives and reconcile equivocal alternatives in decision making, which can lead to better care decisions. Further, externally integrated HIT enable computerized surveillance and monitoring of changes in patients’ conditions across the healthcare system and identification of high-risk patients. This can facilitate preventive care treatment and further improve QoC [30].

The positive impact on QoC can be compounded with an HIT that is both internally and externally integrated. In ambulatory care, the physician’s office is specialized and interdependent on other subunits and providers. For example, the chronic disease management and prescribing process involves obtaining prior history and drug information from pharmacies. When attending to a patient, physicians face uncertainty in their diagnoses and management decisions. Easy access to information about patients, dispensed prescriptions (from pharmacists), diseases, and prior medical visits (from internal and external providers) can reduce this uncertainty. Integrated HIT provides data, information, and knowledge along with process coupling between a provider and other units and enables the ambulatory care practice to sense and respond to changes in disease conditions. Such increased coupling between the different units enables everyone to be more responsive to changes in patient conditions, medication orders, and physicians’ decisions. It can also trigger alerts for drug interactions and adverse drug events. Thus, we expect that an internally and externally integrated HIT will improve QoC.

Hypothesis 1: Integrated HIT will be positively associated with quality of care.

We propose that in addition to its direct effect, integrated HIT will indirectly affect 4 QoC by increasing CoC and reducing the odds of therapy duplication errors.

## Indirect Effect of Integrated HIT on Quality of Care through Continuity of Care

Continuity of care (CoC) is a multidimensional construct that reflects a patient’s perception that providers know what has happened before (informational continuity), that different providers agree on a management plan (management continuity), and that a provider who knows them will care for them in the future (relational continuity) [27]. CoC requires that care of an individual patient and care over time are maintained. Traditionally, this has been realized through patients frequenting the same doctor to ensure an ongoing relationship and consistent care provision [25]. However, CoC is difficult to maintain in ambulatory settings with numerous physicians and specialists.

We argue that in decentralized contexts such as ambulatory care, integrated HIT will improve CoC through its first-order effects on informational continuity and secondorder effects on management continuity and relational continuity. First, integrated HIT affords information continuity, which is defined as the use of information about a patient’s previous health events and personal circumstances to make the current diagnosis and treatment most appropriate [26]. Integrated HIT allows providers to capture and share a wide range of patient information, such as their health status, treatment contexts, care histories including reasons for referrals or transfers, allergies, medications, vital signs, diagnoses, problem lists, recent procedures, preferences, and values. This wealth of information allows continuity between separate care events and across care transitions [52]. Without an electronically integrated system, this knowledge would exist primarily in the memories of providers who interact with their patients [27].

Through informational continuity, integrated HIT can achieve management continuity (i.e., use of a consistent and coherent approach to managing a health condition in a way that is responsive to a patient’s changing needs and condition [26]). Because integrated HIT organizes information about patients’ health, medications, test results, and notes, care providers across the healthcare delivery system can access and synthesize such information. Armed with comprehensive and timely information, providers can develop and coordinate a shared care management plan that provides a consistent treatment of a patient’s health condition [27]. Such a plan can also be flexible and responsive to the patient’s needs.

Finally, through informational continuity, integrated HIT can also attain relational continuity, which refers to an ongoing therapeutic relationship between a patient and one or more providers [26]. This is because the integrated system establishes a bridge between the past, current, and future aspects of care [27]. By keeping a complete track record of treatments and other health data, integrated HIT enable providers—especially primary care physicians—to establish a growing comprehensive knowledge of the patient. In a sense, by being electronically integrated into the care delivery system, the primary care provider becomes the central node of a patient’s care network, which helps foster trust and a continuous relationship over time. We thus hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2: Integrated HIT will be positively associated with continuity of care.

By allowing providers to obtain useful information, coordinate care plans, and develop relationships with patients, CoC helps the providers to accumulate relevant knowledge and to become familiar with their patients’ conditions and health statuses. This enables them to identify drug and health-related problems early in the care delivery cycle, which improves patient therapy and treatment [62]. It also enables them to make better decisions on drug and health-related problems and on patient selection for surgery and other procedures [63]. Also, CoC improves interpersonal communications and care provision and increases the odds that patients will adhere to their physicians’ advice [24].

CoC has been empirically shown to improve several aspects of QoC (e.g., [52, 64]) and especially two measures that are most relevant for ambulatory care: hospital admission and emergency room visits [64]. CoC was found to reduce the odds of hospitalization for various types of outpatients, such as patients with hypertension [50] and Medicaid patients [44]. Further, CoC was shown to reduce both the odds and the number of emergency room visits [23]. There is also evidence showing that patients not experiencing CoC had a higher number of visits to the emergency room [61].

The positive relationship between CoC and QoC is not automatic. In fact, the literature suggests that CoC—when occurring in the traditional sense involving a long-term relationship between a patient and a single provider—may debilitate QoC because patients may get stuck with providers who lose objectivity or miss important diagnoses [24]. However, with an integrated HIT, this is less of a problem because the system allows patients to see multiple providers who are all connected to a central database. Thus, the integrated system can achieve continuity without constraining patients to having a relationship with a single provider. In sum, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: Continuity of care will be positively associated with quality of care.

## Indirect Effect of Integrated Health Information Technology on Quality of Care Through Therapeutic Duplication

Medication errors are an important impediment to QoC. A major type of medication error occurring in ambulatory care settings is therapeutic duplication, which is defined as the concurrent prescription of two or more drugs with similar therapeutic effects [39]. Therapy duplication may involve orders for two different drugs from the same therapy class or orders for the same drug administered through different routes. Ambulatory settings are especially susceptible to therapeutic duplication because they depict decentralized networks in which care is fragmented and information is distributed across multiple providers.

An integrated HIT’s decision support functionalities can offer automatic surveillance and tracking of the patient’s drug profile (e.g., dosage changes, refill dates, number of refills given). The system also provides digital alerts during order entry about potential therapeutic duplications. It can issue the alert based on all patient characteristics drawn from the different modules of the integrated HIT [28]. The system then provides the opportunity for providers to cancel the duplicate order or to override the alert in the event that the order was intended (e.g., transitioning from one medication to another [58]). To reduce the number of false positive alerts, the system can be customized to provide only the most relevant therapeutic duplication alerts (e.g., for high-risk drugs), which will be accepted by the physicians [28, 58].

Importantly, integrated HIT provides visibility—at the time of prescribing—on whether there are other active drugs from the same therapeutic class that have been prescribed by other providers in the care delivery system. This allows providers and other stakeholders (e.g., pharmacists) across the care network to communicate and discuss the reasons for the duplication and to reconcile their different perspectives.

By interfacing with pharmacy information systems, integrated HIT also provides physicians with a visual display of the dispensed prescriptions from pharmacies [43]. Hence, the odds of therapy duplication errors will likely decrease at both the physicians and the pharmacists’ ends [20]. Furthermore, therapy duplication odds are reduced through the integration of the prescription functionality with the pharmacy module so that errors in transcription and transmission are avoided. In sum, we hypothesize that by providing visibility, timely information and reminders, and communication facilities, integrated HIT will lead to lower odds of therapeutic duplication errors.

Hypothesis 4: Integrated HIT will be negatively associated with therapeutic duplication errors.

Therapy duplication errors are especially likely to occur in ambulatory settings and during care transitions [59]. They can worsen patients’ health conditions and are an important cause of adverse drug events [62, 68], in one study accounting for over half such events [41]. These adverse consequences from therapy duplication can be serious and potentially life-threatening [40], and have led to poor health conditions, hospitalizations, emergency room visits, and even mortality [52, 59, 62, 68]. For example, case studies on patients with diabetes mellitus found that therapeutic duplication resulted in recurrent hypoglycemia and increased emergency department visits [59]. Hence, we hypothesize that the higher the odds of therapy duplication errors, the lower the QoC (i.e., higher odds of hospitalization and emergency room visits).

Hypothesis 5: Therapy duplication errors will be negatively associated with quality of care.

## Method

## Context

The context for our study is the implementation of an electronically integrated prescription, drug, and chronic disease management information system (we refer to this integrated HIT as I-HIT hereafter).<sup>5</sup> Online Appendix 4 presents the key features of I-HIT, classified by whether they are mainly facilitating internal, external, or both types of electronic integration. The third column presents the potential impact of each feature on the key health outcome variables of interest.

As indicated in Online Appendix 4, the 14 key features of I-HIT together form a comprehensive, integrated system that is likely to have a significant impact on the three outcomes of interest. For physicians, I-HIT provides a central information repository and computer-triggered and on-demand alerts about drug and disease interactions. This information is delivered at the point-of-care, when physicians are reviewing or prescribing medication. These prescription alerts are based on data from multiple sources located in different organizations. For example, data on patient demographics from patient records, drug interaction data from an integrated drug knowledge module, and medication profile and medication adherence information and other historical data from the regional healthcare service provider are all used to create these alerts. I-HIT makes this information available in real time so that physicians become aware of patients’ details including visits to emergency rooms, hospitals, and specialists, health problems identified by other physicians, reasons for emergency visits, as well as information on drugs prescribed by other physicians. For pharmacists, I-HIT makes changes in patient data, such as new prescriptions, available to regional healthcare service providers. I-HIT also includes an automated health problem list, automated alerts for prescribing problems, and a graphical representation of patients’ medication profiles (i.e., the medications prescribed or dispensed in the past six months are shown along with medications prescribed by other physicians, lapses in treatment, and therapy overlap days).

## Constructs and Measures

Quality of care (QoC): QoC depicts the patient-centric consequences of the care delivery process. Various measures of QoC outcomes have been used in the literature, the most common of which are hospital readmission [10, 69, 60], hospital length of stay [4, 46, 60], and patient mortality (e.g., [1, 4, 46]). As these measures were designed for patients receiving care in the hospital (inpatients) and our study is concerned with patients in ambulatory care settings (outpatients), we used two measures of health outcomes that reflect the level of care received by patients’ primary care and specialty physicians: emergency room visits and hospital admission [64].

QoC has been previously measured as a rate (i.e., a frequency with which a favorable or unfavorable outcome occurs in a defined population in a defined period of time [e.g., (1, 46, 69)]), a duration (e.g., average hospital length of stay [5]), or the occurrence of a favorable or unfavorable outcome (e.g., [10, 23, 44, 50, 60]). In this study, we measured the occurrence of an emergency room (ER) visit or hospitalization as the QoC outcomes and used odds ratios to estimate their association with I-HIT and the hypothesized mediating processes (therapeutic duplication and continuity of care). To obtain the values for the odds ratios, we used medical services claims to health insurers to identify, for each patient, whether they had one or more ER visits and one or more hospitalizations in the baseline and in the follow-up periods [63].

Therapy duplication errors: Therapy duplication errors are a subset of clinical prescribing errors. In this study, we assessed therapy duplication errors in the three most frequently prescribed drug groups: cardiovascular, psychotropic, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications. We measured this construct as the occurrence of concurrent prescriptions for two or more drugs from the same therapeutic class that were prescribed by different physicians within the baseline and follow-up periods (cf. [18, 43, 63]).

Population data from the regional insurer was used to measure therapy duplication for the patient groups with and without I-HIT in the one-year baseline and the one-year follow-up periods after patient enrollment in I-HIT. We included only patients who were prescribed at least one medication from the three drug groups in the baseline period.

Continuity of care (CoC): We conceptualize CoC as having three continuity dimensions: informational, management, and relational [27]. Because informational continuity is already provided for by the presence of the integrated HIT, we measured the impact of I-HIT on the remaining aspects, management continuity and relational continuity (i.e., the second-order effects). We measured management continuity using the Bice and Boxerman formula [46], an index that measures the number of visits to the primary care provider and to other physicians that were coordinated by the primary care provider among all ambulatory visits. We used the usual provider care index to measure relational continuity, defined as the proportion of ambulatory visits that are made to the usual care provider (primary care physician) among all ambulatory visits [57].

Integrated HIT: This construct was measured through two conditions in our natural experiment design that indicate whether or not patients treated by the same physician were being served through the I-HIT system. That is, each of the physicians in our study had similar numbers of patients treated and not treated with I-HIT as part of this study. Our use of a binary variable to capture these conditions follows standard procedures used in the literature (e.g., [10, 40, 46]).

Control Variables: To control for differences in the patient populations with and without access to I-HIT, we first measured and adjusted in the analysis for patient characteristics that are associated with our QoC outcomes including age, gender, number of different medications used in the baseline year, and comorbidity. Comorbidity was assessed using the Charlson comorbidity index [46], a widely used measure that is associated with ER visits and hospitalization. Second, to control for baseline differences in QoC in the treatment and control groups, we calculated the difference between baseline and followup CoC and estimated—using change in CoC as the outcome variable—whether there are greater improvements in CoC in the treatment group relative to the control group. For prescribing errors, ER visits, and hospitalizations, we used the baseline rate in the year prior to I-HIT to adjust, for each patient, in the likelihood of prescribing errors, ER visits, and hospitalizations in the follow-up period. Online Appendix 6 illustrates the baseline characteristics that were controlled for in the different analyses.

## Study Design and Sample

We conducted a natural experiment with two conditions: I-HIT patients and control patients. The I-HIT patients and control patients were each being treated by one of 95 primary care physicians who participated in this study. The I-HIT patients were enrolled in I-HIT by their treating physicians. Enrollment in I-HIT meant that the patients had consented to allow their primary care physician to electronically access information on all their medical and hospital visits and all their medications. Enrollment of the patients was done prospectively on a dynamic basis within each physician’s practice. As each patient was enrolled in I-HIT, a control patient who had a visit within ± 2 days with the same physician and had similar characteristics but was not enrolled in I-HIT would be selected. Each matched pair of I-HIT patients and control patients was thus being treated by the same physician. The treatment given to the control group patients did not change after I-HIT, that is, they received the same standard treatment by their treating physicians as they did before I-HIT was implemented even if they did not enroll in I-HIT. Patients who enrolled in I-HIT also received standard treatments, but their primary care physicians had access to more complete information on these patients (e.g., their visits to or prescriptions by other physicians, visits to the emergency rooms, etc.).

Each matched pair of I-HIT and control patients was followed up for a year after I-HIT enrollment for assessment of outcomes. These patients were also required to have had at least one year of treatment by their primary care physician in the year prior to I-HIT enrollment for calculation of baseline covariates. A total of 15,626 pairs of I-HIT patients and matched control patients met these criteria, which resulted in 31,252 patients who were followed up during the study period. Figure 2 illustrates the timeline of the study and the experimental design.

The availability of a contemporaneous control group allowed us to compare changes in the CoC, prescribing errors, and QoC among patients of the same physician, with and without the benefits of I-HIT and over time. We also compared data for patients before and after their enrollment in I-HIT.

## Analytical Approach

To determine whether I-HIT improved relational and management CoC (H2), we used multivariate least squares regression within a generalized estimating equation (GEE) framework. GEE methods are suitable for dealing with correlated data—in this case the outcomes of patients seen by the same physician [9]. GEE methods estimate efficient and unbiased regression parameters by specifying a correlation matrix that accounts for within-subject correlations of responses on dependent variables [9]. In this study, we assumed an exchangeable correlation structure, which assumes an equivalent correlation in outcomes for all patients seen by the same physician. We estimated two models, one to assess changes in relational continuity and a second for management continuity. In both models, the patient was the unit of analysis, the physician was the clustering factor, change in continuity between the baseline and follow-up periods was the dependent variable, and I-HIT was the binary independent variable. The estimated regression coefficient tests the hypothesis that the magnitude of change in continuity in the I-HIT group was greater than in the control group. Each model was adjusted for other patient-level covariates: number of medications, Charlson comorbidity index, age, gender, as well as baseline continuity.<sup>6</sup> To avoid systematic confounding effects resulting from differences between the two patient groups, we conducted a difference in differences analysis where we statistically controlled for baseline continuity. This allowed us to compare the impact of I-HIT among patients with equivalent baseline levels of CoC.

To test the remaining hypotheses (H1, H3, H4, and H5), we used conditional logistic regression within a GEE framework. This approach accounted for the paired baseline and follow-up measures of patients in the treatment and control groups and the three dichotomous dependent variables: therapy duplication error, ER visits, and hospitalization. Similar to the CoC models, we modeled each outcome separately. ## Discussion

Our findings provide support for most of our hypotheses. First, the results show that integrated HIT has a direct effect on QoC by reducing the patients’ likelihood of having an ER visit, but not their likelihood of hospitalization, which was less frequent and thus reduced the power of the test. This finding might also suggest that HIT exerts its direct effect by enabling providers to deliver effective ambulatory care to patients with conditions that may have required them to go to the emergency room but that it is less effective for patients with more serious health conditions that will eventually require hospitalization. Alternatively, the nonsignificant effect on hospitalization may be a result of the time frame of the study, which tracked HIT enrollment over a one-year period. It has been suggested that HIT implementations typically take two years to bear full fruit [49] and that shorter time frames may explain the lack of significant effect of HIT on hospitalization odds [10]. This issue can be addressed in future studies by adopting longer time lags and/or more fine-grained measures, such as meaningful use of HIT [10].

Second, our results indicate that integrated HIT increases both relational (frequent interactions among healthcare partners in the care cycle) and management (the coordination of care delivery through shared care plans and treatment protocols) CoC (H2). We also found that the relational and management CoC provided through integrated HIT improve the patient QoC (H3). We argue that the improvement in CoC occurs because of better coordination and sharing of management plans and protocols through the I-HIT system. By providing IT-enabled learning and knowledge coordination, integrated HIT enables a physician to identify drug and healthrelated problems and provide patients with predictable care. Primary care physicians are thus able to provide integrated treatment to patients. In other words, HIT provides an electronic means to CoC, which seems to enforce common treatment protocols and guidelines that are lacking in many healthcare systems because of paper-based communication and coordination. Through this improved continuity and coordination, integrated HIT reduces emergency room visits and hospitalization, thereby improving the QoC delivered to patients.

Third, the effect of I-HIT on therapy duplication errors (H4) was in the hypothesized direction but not significant $( p \ = \ 0 . 1 5 )$ , whereas the impact of therapy duplication on ER visits and hospitalization in the follow-up period was significant (H5). One possibility that could explain the nonsignificant effect is that the functionalities of the system that alert physicians about therapeutic duplication are being ignored or overridden. This can happen when therapeutic duplication is actually intended by the physicians, such as when they are transitioning from one medication to another [58], or when they create two prescriptions of the same medicine via two routes and add a note saying that one route is preferred if the other one is not already in use. The near-significant finding could also be due to the power of the test being considerably less for detecting the effect of I-HIT on therapeutic duplication than for detecting the other effects in the model. This is because our analysis of prescribing errors was restricted to the 5,516 patients where both the I-HIT patients and their matched controls had at least one of the target medications in the baseline (2,758 matched pairs).

Although we did not find evidence supporting the path from I-HIT to QoC through therapy duplication, our analysis showed an additional path that connects CoC to QoC through therapy duplication. More specifically, we found that CoC was associated with a decrease in the odds of having a prescribing error: for a 10 percent increase in baseline CoC, the odds of therapy duplication decreased by 12 percent (OR: 0.88, 95 percent CI: 0.84–0.93, p < 0.001). Because therapy duplication was also associated with reduced QoC, this suggests that it might play a mediating role between CoC and QoC.

Our results provide several insights about the role of integrated HIT in improving health processes and outcomes. The IS literature on healthcare has examined different aspects of the phenomenon including HIT investments [56], adoption [8, 37, 65], knowledge transfer [42], and the impact of HIT on healthcare organizations [7, 47] and on patient outcomes [4, 47, 69]. Our study contributes to this last stream and extends it by examining a comprehensive HIT solution and uncovering the nomological network that links integrated HIT to intermediate processes and the ultimate patient-related outcomes. Given our longitudinal design and the use of a pretest– posttest panel with a control group, our findings provide strong evidence of the causal relationships between integrated HIT and healthcare-related processes and outcomes.

## Contributions to Research and Practice

The present study makes several contributions to research. First, it extends prior research that has focused on the impact of specific components of HIT. Our study looks at a comprehensive HIT solution and sheds light on how an integrated HIT system with components that are designed and built to work together can influence patient-related processes and outcomes. We conceptualized integrated HIT as systems that can afford both internal integration (within practice) and external integration (linking physician practices, pharmacists, and hospitals). We suggested that an integrated HIT is beneficial because it can afford knowledge exploitation and exploration, uncertainty reduction, flexibility, quick response, and the ability to act as a unified whole. Hence, our work provides a foundation to further explore the concept of integrated HIT and its potential consequences (e.g., teasing out the relative impact of internal and external integration; explicitly examining other outcomes such as knowledge creation through HIT).

Second, our work draws attention to the importance of studying both processbased and outcome-based consequences of HIT. Drawing on the SPO framework [15, 19], our study helps clarify the nomological network that links HIT to two key intermediate QoC process measures (continuity of care and therapeutic duplication errors) and to QoC outcomes. This, in combination with our first contribution of studying an integrated system, helps explain the mixed empirical results found in the literature.

Additionally, to our knowledge, this study is the first to empirically show the relationship between HIT and CoC and the path linking HIT to QoC through CoC. Prior literature has hinted that CoC might play an important role [52, 55]. The present research validates this claim by formally conceptualizing and testing the role of CoC. CoC has been conceptualized in traditional care environments as being achieved through direct and repeated interactions between a provider and a patient. In this study, we have argued that in a complex and decentralized care network, continuity can also be achieved through the use of an integrated HIT that affords informational continuity and subsequently management and relational continuity. In essence, integrated HIT can achieve continuity without constraining patients to having a relationship with a single provider. Our results provide evidence that HIT improves CoC (management and relational), which in turn improves QoC. This has direct implications for the literature on IS and healthcare and potentially for other literatures as well, where continuity of relationships is important (e.g., company– customer relationships, e-loyalty). Furthermore, while prior studies have investigated physician- and patient-related perceptions of CoC (e.g., [25, 26]), our work contributes to the literature by developing objective measures of continuity.

Finally, our research design extends the tradition of experimental research in the healthcare domain by using and applying a contemporaneous natural experiment of 31,252 patients studied over two years using a prospective controlled cohort study (pretest–posttest design with control). Our design included data for 12 months before and 12 months after enrollment in I-HIT. Additionally, we selected patients in the treatment and control groups who were seen by the same providers and we followed the same patients before and after the HIT intervention. Overall, our design provides strong causal evidence on the impact of HIT on patient care processes and outcomes and, more generally, on the organizational value of IS.

Our study contributes to practice by helping decision makers to better understand the impact of HIT in a complex and highly decentralized and fragmented care delivery system. For organizations and hospitals developing and implementing HIT, our results suggest that the impact of these systems can be realized more fully when they provide a comprehensive and integrated solution. Implementing isolated components of HIT has been found to achieve mixed results, whereas fully integrated systems can increase QoC both directly and indirectly.

Another important implication for practitioners is to better understand and develop the central role of the primary care providers in leveraging HIT. Our findings suggest that integrated HIT can improve CoC and subsequently QoC. As we have argued, these benefits are achieved because integrated HIT can empower primary care providers to help enable and coordinate continuity of care with their patients. Consequently, it is important that adequate policies, incentives, and mechanisms are put in place to ensure that providers are trained and motivated to use HIT effectively (e.g., the EHR Incentive Program for meaningful use of HIT).

## Study Limitations and Future Research

One limitation of this study is that it relates constructs across levels of analysis, which can make it difficult to establish causal relationships. Specifically, integrated HIT is an organization-level construct whereas the healthcare outcomes are at the level of the individual. While our approach to analyzing the data is valid and suited for within-subject correlations and group differences, future research could study similar contexts using multilevel approaches. In addition to using the GEE framework to account for within-physician differences and correlations, we have adjusted for the patient-level physical differences between the subjects. However, because of data limitations, we were unable to adjust for any psychological or sociological differences between the I-HIT and control patients. Furthermore, while our outcome measures compared I-HIT and control patients within physicians, and this limited potential confounding as a result of physician differences, we did not explicitly control for physician effects.

Another limitation concerns our use of dichotomous measures of QoC. While these measures may be less precise than rate measures, few patients in our sample had more than one ER visit and hospitalization over the duration of a year. Therefore, adopting different measures is unlikely to have substantively altered the results. It is also possible that more significant effects can be observed over longer periods because there can be a temporal gap between better treatment and health outcomes (e.g., hospitalization rates). Therefore, future research is needed to better understand the impact of HIT on more finegrained measures of QoC (e.g., rates of adverse events) and over longer time periods. Furthermore, in measuring QoC, our measure of ER visits included all ER visits. A portion of these visits may not have been caused by our exposures of interest. As a result, the estimated risks may be slightly overestimated. However, it has been shown that some of the top reasons for ER visits are vertigo-dizziness, general weakness, and injury, which are all often caused by therapy duplication, or cumulative toxicity resulting from combined side effects of medications [31]. Consequently, it is unlikely that our adjusted risk is highly overestimated. Also, the potential for overestimation is countered by the fact that this effect is also present for patients in the control group.

Another avenue for future research is to explore other ways to conceptualize and measure HIT, such as by adopting finer-grained measures of use (e.g., the number of features used or measures of meaningful use [54]) or by more fully exploring the concept of electronic integration [11] and the role it plays in realizing health-related benefits (e.g., relationships between extent of HIT integration and outcomes). For example, future research can investigate how different types of interdependence within the healthcare delivery system can be supported by integrated HIT, and identify the resulting outcomes of this investigation. Some units may be sequentially interdependent whereas others could be linked though pooled or reciprocal interdependence. It is plausible that the impact of HIT might be different based on the type of interdependence [11]. It would be interesting to determine what types of activities or processes in a physician’s office create interdependence and what types of integrated HIT are most efficient. However, similar to Angst et al. [4], we also caution that the use of finergrained HIT measures may make it more difficult to attain large sample sizes, which is a strength of the present study.

Finally, more work is needed to determine whether the results obtained in our study apply to patient groups with different conditions (e.g., different or multiple chronic illnesses). It would also be important to study whether or not the different types of continuities achieved through integrated HIT can be applied to other organizational contexts where long-term relationships are important.

## Conclusion

By studying the direct and indirect effects of an integrated HIT, our study provides a rather comprehensive examination of the relationships between HIT, intermediate processes, and patient-related outcomes. Our study also extends the extant literature by examining the important phenomenon of the impact of HIT on quality of care based on a large data set and a robust design using a natural experiment. As such, the present study provides strong evidence of the benefits of IS in the healthcare context and about IS value in general. The study also raises many other questions that we hope will stimulate research on this important topic.
