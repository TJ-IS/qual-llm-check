## Abstract

This paper investigates the impact of mandatory adoption of the eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL) on performance of listed state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and non-SOEs. Building on the institutional theory, we hypothesize and find that non-SOEs benefit from the adoption of XBRL by operating more effectively which results in better performance. Due to the institutional factors, for SOEs, financial report users still need much more information beyond financial reports before they can make appropriate decisions. Implications on the use of XBRL-formatted financial reporting information for international business with the existence of SOEs in China are discussed.

Keywords: eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL), State-Owned Enterprise (SOE), Institutional Theory, Business Information Supply Chain

## 1. Introduction

The use of financial reporting information is often limited even with the advancement of information technology. To improve the re-usability and integration of financial reporting information, the eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL), a standard XML reporting language, was developed to electronically communicate business information [38, 82]. XBRL is expected to make financial reports more understandable and closer to the underlying economics of the business (including financial reporting choices and assumptions) [38, 69, 70]. It also has the potential to be a global standard for financial and non-financial data that facilitates the information exchange across countries [16]. Given the expected benefits, U.S., European, and Asian countries have started to mandatorily adopt XBRL for financial reporting for all listed firms. This is the first time that most of the countries in the world mandatorily adopt the same financial reporting technology.

XBRL is expected to facilitate the access and process of firms’ financial reporting information [38]. The improved transparency helps external users of a firm’s financial reporting information better understand the firm’s decisions that may potentially serve as an external monitoring function to make the firm to make decision more appropriately, which in turn improve the firm’s performance. However, prior literature suggests that the ownership structure, state-owned enterprises (SOEs) in particular, can be an important factor that may affect a firm’s performance [42]. Most of prior studies, such as [11], show that state-owned firms are inefficient. For example, Dewenter and Malatesta [27] demonstrate that public offerings of state-owned firms are significantly underpriced as these firms are directed not by social welfare improvement but by political interests and objectives including creating jobs, promoting industrialization, and

subsidizing underdeveloped areas of the country [30, 42]. In a multi-country setting, La Porta et al. [49] show that state ownership is related to lower economic growth while Barth et al. [8] find that state ownership of banks are associated with less developed banking system and security market. Compared with non-SOEs, the boards of SOEs are less independence and SOEs are often characterized with complicated shareholding structure as well as different governance systems [41]. In this vein, the benefits of improved transparency and firm performance through XBRL adoption may be different for SOEs and non-SOEs.

In this paper, we would like to investigate whether the mandatory adoption of XBRL in China would lead to more effective business operations due to the transparency of financial reports and the ease of access of such information.    We focus on China as China is the first country that officially adopts XBRL and is still leading the development of XBRL with other countries.    In addition, SOEs are playing a critical role in China’s economy.    We also examine whether such impact is different between SOEs and non‐SOEs.    The institutional theory provides us a framework to understand the mandatory adoption of XBRL.    In particular, the coercive force and the regulatory pressure seem to ensure the conformity of adoption.    Such conformity may bring the potential benefits of using XBRL‐formatted information which in turn results in more effective business operations as reflected by better performance.    However, though prior studies, such as [78] and [61], have highlighted how the characteristics of SOEs and non‐SOEs affect business decisions, it is not clear from the institutional theory how different ownership structure, especially the political affiliation or the tie to the government, would affect the mandatory adoption of a new technology.    Given the characteristics of SOEs, we provide arguments that may supplement the theory in understanding the effectiveness of the adoption of a new technology, XBRL in particular.    To capture whether a firm is more effective

#

after the adoption of XBRL, we use two performance measures in our analysis: one is accounting‐based (return‐on‐assets, ROA) and the other one is market‐based (market‐to‐book ratio, MB).    We collect our data from the China Security Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database in the period from 2003 to 2010.    Our findings show that overall, the firms’ performance (both ROA and MB) is better in the post‐mandatory‐adoption period comparing to the pre‐mandatory‐adoption period.    However, when we split the firms into SOEs and non‐SOEs, comparing to SOEs, non‐SOEs show a better improvement of ROA and MB in the post‐mandatory‐adoption period.

Our paper contributes to the literature and sheds lights on the use of financial reporting Our paper fills in the gap of the literature when applying institutional theory to the mandatory adoption of information technology.    Though prior studies have emphasized the importance of SOEs in business decisions (e.g., [61, 78]), prior literature does not emphasize on how the ownership structure, SOE in particular, would affect the effectiveness of the mandatory adoption of an information technology.    In our paper, we show how SOEs can result in different post‐adoption performance.    This finding also supplements the literature regarding how political affiliations may affect the mandatory adoption of a new technology.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we discuss the institutional background and develop our hypotheses. We present our research methodology in Section 3. Our main results and additional tests are given in Section 4. We conclude with discussions of limitations and possible future researches in Section 5.

## 2. Institutional Background and Hypothesis Development

## 2.1. Institutional Background in China

SOEs are business entities that are de facto controlled by the government [73] and have a long history in China. They dominated the market and created about 75% of the national industrial output value in the past [18]. China underwent a reform starting in the late 70s which was originally set to make SOEs more efficient [10, 52, 65] but has transformed the economy into a market economy with private ownership. Private firms started to show in the market. The privatization of firms made the non-state-owned firms obtain more control apart from the government/central planning (e.g., [72]). As the liberalization continued, some SOEs were sold or dissolved while other SOEs were encouraged to merge into business groups [45] or became joint stock firms [76]. It seems that the private firms have gained their roles in the market and China becomes an economy with less central planning and more market competition. However, the government at the same time imposed certain restrictions and set protection policies in certain so-called strategic industries, such as telecommunication, petrochemical, civil aviation, shipping, etc. In its latest five-year plan, these restrictions and policies were also applied to the emerging industries such as health care, energy, and technology [73]. Furthermore, though banking is not explicitly included in the list of strategic or emerging industries, it is tightly controlled by SOEs (75% of China’s bank assets and 11 out of 13 major joint stock commercial banks) [73]. SOEs still play an important role in the market and such role has been strengthened by the subsidies and preferential financing, taxes and regulations for SOEs [15].

Not surprisingly, today, 70% of the ultimate owners of the listed firms in China are SOEs [50] and SOEs directly or indirectly control about 50% of non-agriculture GDP [73].

Nevertheless, on the road of liberalization and privatization, the China government notices that China needs to have some strategies not only to modernize its financial market but also to improve its SOE operations.    In addition to the marketization and the involvement of professional managers, the transparency of financial reporting is one of the elements that the government would like to promote [41].    In particular, a more reliable financial reporting system can improve the transparency of firms which can help the market better oversight the firms, attract foreign investors, and link the China market to the global economy.    To achieve it goal, the China authority decides to adopt a new technique, the eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL), to potentially streamline and standardize the business information supply chain and to make financial reporting information more accessible and transparent.

## 2.2. eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL)

The eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL) is an XML‐based technique that was proposed in the late 90s to early 2000s in the U.S. with the expectation to increase the re‐usability and integration of financial reporting information [24, 25, 63, 64].    XBRL is later developed by XBRL International (XII) with more than 600 members from different countries and constituencies of the business information value chain.    The consortium consists of regulatory bodies, public audit firms, financial institutions, and software vendors as well as international and national standard setters and public entities.    It is considered a global revolutionary technology for the electronic communication of business and financial data that transform the business reporting intra‐ and inter‐organizationally around the world [14, 35].

#

XBRL provides an identifying tag for financial facts, such as earnings per share, to help financial report users more precisely obtain the information they need [39].    As all the financial reporting elements are standardized through a widely accepted taxonomy (i.e., a dictionary of terms used in financial reports) and are separated from its format, XBRL‐formatted business data is computer‐readable and searchable so that users can download it directly into analytical software [4, 5, 33, 75, 83].    Accordingly, it is expected to improve accessibility and transparency for the adopting organizations [19, 57, 68, 75, 83].    In addition, XBRL is expected to facilitate the integration of different items in financial reports even in the case when such link(s) does not exist explicitly and cannot be found easily before [38].    Due to this integration of information, it becomes easier for users to observe management’s disclosure decisions as well as the motivation for such decision [37, 38].    XBRL can also potentially reduce the professional users’ (e.g., analysts) cognitive costs and non‐professional users’ information processing costs [38].    Stated by Liu Shiping, the vice chairman of the executive committee of XBRL China, “…Credit Suisse HOLT has used XBRL to expand its coverage of the Chinese A‐share market from 300 firms…to well over 1,000…automatically” [23].    Similarly, Liu et al. [53] show that analyst followings and forecast accuracy are positively associated with the adoption of XBRL. Kim et al. [47] and Yoon et al. [82] find that disclosure quality improves (e.g., decreased information asymmetry and decreased information risk) with XBRL adoption as well.    Last, XBRL has made information more readily accessible across countries based on its multi‐language and multi‐GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) converting capabilities [63]. International organizations view XBRL as a key enabling financial‐reporting technology to help them differentiate themselves from their competition in the global capital markets [3, 64].

#

Given the potential benefits of XBRL, China was the first country that formally and mandatorily adopted XBRL for its capital market in 2004 [46]. China is now leading the development of XBRL with the U.S. and Europe [23]. China’s central planning strategy in adopting and developing XBRL application as well as techniques has cast away many of the main concerns, such as the cost of implementation, faced by the U.S. [79]. China has also rapidly leveraged the knowledge from U.S. and Europe to develop its own taxonomies and applications. In particular, supported by the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC), in early 2003, the Shanghai Security Exchange (SSE) began the electronic reporting program for its listed firms’ financial reports based on XBRL standards. Based on the information on http://www.xbrl-cn.org, initially, 50 listed pilot firms in SSE were chosen to use XBRL at the beginning of 2004. In addition, though the firms are called pilot firms, in the same quarter of 2004, about 90% of the other firms have filed their quarterly reports based on XBRL. The rest of the firms have also filed their half-year financial reports in XBRL format before August. There is no obvious time lag between the pilot firms and the other firms in terms of the adoption. However, none of these filings (both the pilot and the others) are publicly available for unknown reasons. After three years, in 2006, all SSE listed XBRL-formatted financial reports were available through the standard reporting system. This regulation change provides us a natural experiment context to study the firms’ pre- and post-adoption performance as detailed in Section 3. That is, after the XBRL-formatted information becomes publicly available (i.e., 2006), investors may start to benefit from easy access and analysis of financial reporting information which in turn may potentially affect a firm’s decisions and subsequent performance.

However, as stated earlier, China is quite different from other countries in terms of the uniqueness of its institutional context.    This unique characteristic is state‐owned enterprises (SOEs).    SOEs are enterprises that are de facto controlled by the central or local government(s) in China.    Though firms in many countries, such as France, Australia, and Singapore, have the ownership structure similar to SOEs, such ownership structure is more prominent in China. For example, a recent Wall Street Journal article states that “it is the omnipresence and girth of its SOEs that distinguish China.    Supported by large state subsidies and preferential financing, taxes and regulations, the SOEs (state‐owned enterprises) are at the center of China's drive for ‘indigenous innovation’” [15].    Specifically, different from non‐SOEs, SOEs operate based on the institutions’ interests they are controlled by.    In addition, even today, SOEs are still lack of professional management and appropriate (in effect) corporate governance mechanism [41]. As reflected by the quote, with China's engagement in the international economy [54, 60], SOEs have imposed significant influence on investment decisions (e.g., listed SOEs in the U.S.) and affected firms that operate or trade in China.    For example, Shanghai Volkswagen is a joint venture between Volkswagen and SAIC Motor (an SOE in China) which may affect Volkswagen’s global strategic decisions.    It is this unique ownership structure (i.e., SOEs and non‐SOEs) that makes the expected benefits of mandatorily implementing XBRL being not ex ante clear.

## 2.3. Hypothesis Development

The institutional theory provides us a framework to understand the mandatory adoption of XBRL.    Institutions are social structures based on taken‐for‐granted, formal or informal rules that affect social behavior [9].    Scott [66] defines institutions are “comprised of regulative, normative and cultural‐cognitive elements that, together with associated activities and resources, provide stability and meaning to social life” (p.48).    Each element is correspondent with three different mechanisms of isomorphism that are detailed below: coercive, normative

#

and mimetic [28, 29, 56, 66].    First, regulatory rules and laws are the basis of coercive isomorphism to control organizational behaviors through enforcement activities and compliance, such as environmental regulations [28].    The impact of coercive force on a firm’s decision has been investigated in different context in prior literature, such as environmental practices [43], Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPPA) [6], and Sarbanes Oxley Act [7]. Second, normative isomorphism is associated with the compliance of a set of norms and values or social obligations in a social system [28, 66].    Last, cultural‐cognitive belief also affects organizational actions.    That is, the mimetic mechanism can help organizations to achieve cultural‐cognitive legitimacy by imitating the structure or actions of other organizations.    The organization field [29] which is defined as “those organizations that…constitute a recognized area of institutional life: key suppliers, resource and product consumers, regulatory agencies, and other organizations that produce similar services or products” (p.148).

As stated earlier, the time lag between the early filers and the majority of other filers is about two months and all the filings at that time are not publicly available.    Such conditions rule out the possibility of learning from the peers (or mimetic effect).    In this vein, the mandatory adoption of XBRL is mainly based on the coercive force of regulation changes that apply to all listed firms (both SOEs and non‐SOEs) in SSE.    How strong is the force?    The China government promotes the use of XBRL in a planned manner.    The official document shows that SSE organized a task force in October, 2003 and determined the first 50 pilot firms on Dec.30, 2003 (http://www.xbrl‐cn/org).    On April 30, 2004, about 90% of the listed firms on SSE had their quarterly reports in XBRL format though such information was not publicly available until

2006.    From task force to adoption, SSE has reached a conformity ratio of 90% in about 7 months.    To further expedite the adoption process, SSE designed the system that the firms need to use.    Specifically, the firms only needed to fill out the forms and the XBRL‐formatted financial reports were generated accordingly.    Consistent with prior literature [29, 34, 66], a strong regulatory pressure can induce conformity.    This conformity is not just the structure. The regulator in this case makes sure that all the requirements are met by setting up the tools “as planned”.    Accordingly, not only the firms but also the users can reap the benefits, i.e., more efficiently and effectively use information which in turn ensures firms to make strategic decisions more appropriately.    Such decisions may result in more effective business operations as reflected by better performance.    Formally, we state our first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. The performance is better in the post-mandatory-adoption period than in the pre-mandatory-adoption period of XBRL.

However, Hypothesis 1 does not take into account the ownership forms, namely, SOEs and non-SOEs. As pointed out by Cui and Jiang [22], SOEs are not motivated by self-interests. Instead, SOEs’ interests are tied to the institutions that they are directly or indirectly controlled by. They can be asked to follow policies without considering their own interests (e.g., [66]). Their behavior is different from other “active agents” (i.e., non-SOEs) as discussed in prior literature [22]. Compared with non-SOEs, SOEs are characterized with lack of independence of the board, complicated shareholding structure (through tunneling and propping), professional decision makings, different evaluation and governance systems, etc. [41]. SOEs also have subsidies and different tax protections [15]. In light of all these differences between SOEs and non-SOEs, though the institutional theory does not provide a clear guidance on the firms that have political affiliates [22] regarding the mandatory adoption of a new technology, the theory does suggest that

#

different organization fields (e.g., SOEs and non-SOEs are different in terms of the shareholder structure, resources and regulatory regimes) might result in different practices. In addition, Tan and Litschert [74] show that the state regulatory regime is the most influential and complex factor that would affect firm performance. In our context, it is expected that the real value of XBRL obtained by SOEs and non-SOEs might vary. Specifically, though the adoption of XBRL seems to be smooth because everything has been arranged by the government, such strong coercive pressure will affect the form or structure but might not the value or the meaning of the practice that will be adopted (e.g., [43]). This can be especially true for SOEs. When the firm is dependent on the institution that exerts the pressure, it will conform to the pressure [58]. Following the government’s policy is part of their operational goals. However, SOEs do not really take into account how the use of XBRL would affect the information supply chain or how the firm can leverage XBRL but simply considering it as another mandated reporting tool. Furthermore, SOEs’ characteristics make it hard for the market to understand what happens in the firm, not to mention its future even after digitizing all the information in financial reports. Therefore, though as XBRL helps the market get easy access to the financial reporting information and analyze such information efficiently and effectively, such monitoring effect from the market through financial reporting can be less likely to really force a firm to be more effective SOEs. Accordingly,

Hypothesis 2. The performance is better in the post-mandatory-adoption period than in the pre-mandatory-adoption period of XBRL for non-SOEs, comparing to SOEs.

SOEs are under different jurisdiction levels: SOEs controlled by the central government or SOEs controlled by the local government. Through the reform, the central government decentralized to local government which changed the control right of a local government’s fiscal

#

activities [65]. Instead of negotiating the budget with the central government, a local government can now increase its revenues (taxes) by attracting businesses. It seems that this provides great incentives for the local government to boost up the performance of the firms in its jurisdiction. In addition, local officials can pose great influence power over local SOEs [65]. The local officials’ main incentives are not about profit maximization of the firm but about his/her political career and the local government’s performance [81]. As long as the local government has good performance in his/her tenure period (generally in the short-run), all the concerns or problems of the firms “should” be delayed until the official gets promoted [81]. From this point of view, it seems that the performance will be better for SOEs under the jurisdiction of local government after the adoption of XBRL in the short-run because of the interests of the local officials and the fiscal performance of the local government. More formally,

Hypothesis 3a. The performance is worse in the post-mandatory-adoption period than in the pre-mandatory-adoption period of XBRL for SOEs under the jurisdiction of central government, comparing to SOEs under the jurisdiction of local government.

Differently, Leung and Hu [51] suggest that the centrally-controlled SOEs have better market supervision than locally-controlled SOEs which may affect the effectiveness of the adoption. The involvement of unprofessional management styles from the local officials would worsen the operational effectiveness of a firm not to mention the complex transaction arrangements. In this vein, it is expected that the performance is better for SOEs under the jurisdiction of central government after the adoption of XBRL than that for local SOEs.

Hypothesis 3b. The performance is better in the post-mandatory-adoption period than in the pre-mandatory-adoption period of XBRL for SOEs under the

jurisdiction of central government, comparing to SOEs under the jurisdiction of local government.

In summary, on one hand, local government officials have career and revenue increasing incentives which may positively affect the firm’s performance under the jurisdiction of local government after the adoption of XBRL. On the other hand, firms controlled by local government may have unprofessional managers which may negatively affect the performance comparing to those controlled by central government which usually has better governance mechanisms. It seems that the net impact from the above two arguments is not clear. Accordingly, we consider it as an empirical question regarding whether local SOEs outperform central SOEs with the adoption of XBRL.

## 3. Research Methodology

## 3.1. Data and Variables

In this research, we gather the data from the China Security Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database.    The CSMAR database is the world’s largest and leading provider of China financial market data as well as China industries and economic data to international financial and educational institutions.    CSMAR provides 11 modules of historical data and 77 databases regarding stock market, corporate profiles, fund market, bond market, derivatives market, money market, economy and industry, and etc.    For the purpose of this study, we collect data from the following databases: China stock market financial database, China listed firm’s shareholders research database, and China listed firm’s corporate governance research database.

Our initial sample includes all the A share listed firms in Shanghai Stock Exchange (SSE) till the end of year 2010 except financial institutions and early pilot filers as in prior studies (e.g.,

#

[53, 71]). We exclude financial institutions because given the unique characteristics of financial institutions, the accounting performance is not comparable with that of firms in other industries. For example, financing and investing activities are included in the calculation of return-on-assets for financial institutions while financing activities are generally considered liabilities for other industries. In addition, we exclude early pilot filers in our sample as these filers are “chosen” by the government as illustrative examples because of their outstanding performance which may potentially affect the results. Given that the information regarding the ultimate owner of a firm is not available until 2003, we collect the corresponding variables from 2003 to 2010 as detailed below. To approach our research question, we consider two performance measures that are commonly used in prior literature (e.g., [31, 36]): return on assets (ROA, an accounting-based measure) (e.g., [26, 32]) and market-to-book ratio (MB, a market-based measure) [80]. Accounting based measures captures a firm’s historical performance but has been criticized of being not able to reflect the future performance (e.g., [13]) while stock market based measures can incorporate the market’s perception regarding a firm’s future prospects [67]. We use the accounting and the market based measures to capture both the historical performance and the market’s perception. ROA is calculated by dividing the earnings before interests and tax (EBIT) by the average total assets for firm i at time t (i.e., EBIT/average total assets). MB is a ratio to compare a stock's market value for firm i, to its book value. It is calculated by dividing the closing price of the stock times the outstanding shares by the book value of stockholders’ equity for firm i at time t (i.e., closing price\*outstanding shares/book value). We adjust the above two measures by subtracting industry j’s median ROA or MB at time t from firm i’s ROA or MB where firm i is a member of industry j (i.e., $R O A _ { i t } - R O A _ { j t }$ or $M B _ { i t } - M B _ { j t } )$ as per Hochberg and

Lindsey [36], Holthausen and Larcker [40], and Siegel and Hambrick [67] (see Table 1 for variable definitions).

We use a dummy variable, DXBRL, to capture the adoption year of XBRL which help us distinguish the performance before and after the adoption.    In particular, our argument earlier states that the adoption of XBRL can help users more efficiently and effectively use information which in turn ensures firms to make strategic decisions more appropriately as reflected by better performance.    Though China adopts XBRL in 2004, such information is not publicly information till 2006 which does not result in any external monitoring impact on the firm’s decisions.    Accordingly, we define DXBRL to be 0 if the year period is between 2003 and 2005, and 1 if the period is after 2006.    For Hypotheses 2, we use another dummy variable to indicate whether a firm is a China state‐owned enterprise (SOE) or not.    This variable, DSOE, equals 1 when the company is always controlled by the government (i.e., the ultimate owner is the government) before 2010 and 0 otherwise.<sup>1</sup>    To further consider the authority level of the SOEs as in Hypothesis 3, we split the sample into SOEs controlled by the central or the local government.    Note that we do not use number of shares controlled by SOEs (or by central/local government) because such data are not disclosed or publicly available as discussed

earlier regarding the transparency of SOEs.

We also control for the following variables that may affect a firm’s performance as in prior literature.    First, we control for the firm performance at time t‐1 (namely, LAGROA and LAGMB) which has been shown to be highly related to performance at time t [1, 36]. Furthermore, we control for firm characteristics that are associated with performance [1, 26, 32, 48, 81]: firm size (SIZE, the logarithm of total assets for firm i at time t‐1), financial leverage (LEVERAGE, total liabilities divided by total assets for firm i at time t‐1), number of years a firm is listed (AGE, year t‐1 minus the year the company was listed and plus one), sales growth rate (GROWTH, the sales revenue at year t‐1 minus the sales revenue at year t‐2 divided by the sales revenue at year t‐2).    Third, we control the size of the board (BOARDSIZE), percentage of independent directors (IBOARDSIZE), and CEO duality (DUALITY) as proxies of a firm’s corporate governance environment [12, 44, 77]. BOARDSIZE is calculated as the logarithm of the number of board members for firm i at year t‐1.    IBOARDSIZE is the percentage of independent directors on the board for firm i at year t‐1.    DUALITY is a dummy variable.    It equals 1, if the CEO is also the chairman for firm i at year t‐1, 0 otherwise.    Finally, we control for a firm’s complexity and the possible additional capital investments as the economy grows [81].    Capital intensity (CAPITALINT) is calculated by dividing the total fixed assets by the total assets for firm i at time t‐1.

Though the market perceives that local SOEs would be more profoundly affected by the characteristics of SOEs as reflected by MB, the incentives of local officials indeed improve the ROA of the local SOEs in the short‐run.

## 4.2. Additional Analyses

We perform the following analyses to further validate our main results.    For brevity, we do not tabulate our results of additional analyses.    First, we control for GDP change as a proxy of the overall macroeconomic conditions and the change between outstanding and non‐outstanding shares as a proxy of the liberalization of the market. Our results remain largely similar.    Second, as shown in the descriptive statistics, about 56% of our sample firms are in the manufacturing industry.    In order to verify whether our results are mainly affected by manufacturing firms, we split our sample into manufacturing and non‐manufacturing firms. Our main results hold for both groups.    Third, as mentioned earlier, though about 90% of the firms have started to file with XBRL, the filings are not available till 2006.    In order to take into account this grey period, we exclude the observations in 2004 and 2005 and re‐perform our analyses.    Our results remain qualitatively similar.    Furthermore, the market‐to‐book ratio (MB) could be significantly affected by the global economic downturn in 2008 and 2009.    That is, the stock market performance during the economic crisis would potentially alter our results. We exclude these periods and our results remain similar.    Fifth, many firms in SSE are in the Fortune and Forbes list or are cross‐listed in Hong Kong, Singapore, New York, and London. These firms have better reputation and need to follow the rules in the developed stock market. Accordingly, they might operate more effectively than other firms inherently.    We exclude these firms from our analyses and our results remain similar.    Last, we re‐perform our analyses by focusing only on one period before and the period(s) after the adoption.    That is, there are two observations for each firm: one from the period before the adoption and the other one from the period(s) after the adoption by taking averages of the values of our variables. ## 5. Conclusions and Discussions

XBRL is expected to improve the re‐usability of financial reporting information by improving accessibility and transparency of the adopting organizations.    In light of the potential benefits brought by XBRL, China was the first country in the world that mandates the publicly traded firms to file in compliance with XBRL [20, 21].    However, a majority of the firms in China are characterized by intensive government control, i.e., state‐owned enterprises (SOEs), which may affect the potential benefits brought by XBRL.    Based on a sample of all the listed firms in Shanghai Stock Exchange between 2003 and 2010 (excluding financial institutions and early pilot filers), our results show that the mandatory adoption of XBRL increases both ROA and MB for mandatory filers.    However, this positive impact is larger for non‐SOEs comparing to SOEs.    When we investigate the effects given different levels of authority, our findings

suggest that, comparing to locally‐controlled SOEs, centrally‐controlled SOEs can benefit more by the adoption of XBRL in terms of MB but not ROA.

For managers, after the adoption of XBRL, China indeed attracts more foreign investors and draws more attentions from foreign analysts due to a lowered barrier of accessing financial reporting information.    However, SOEs do not change as fast as other non‐SOEs under the pressure of transparency.    The shareholder structure and interests alignments among different political institutions are far more complicated than what XBRL can solve at the current stage. Therefore, even we have observed benefits of adopting XBRL in the U.S. or Europe, market participants still need to be aware of the limitation of adopting XBRL in China especially for SOEs when competing or collaborating with them.    Second, our findings suggest that the market still expects to observe growth or lower uncertainties as reflected by a higher MB in the post‐adoption period.    However, as SOEs do not act based on their own interests and can obtain subsidies and low‐interest loans from the government, their performance and uncertainties do not mainly result from their operations but instead from political purposes.    A high MB in the post‐adoption period needs to be interpreted with caution especially for international investors since XBRL does not really result in expected benefits as reflected by the post‐adoption period ROA.    Last, our study shows how XBRL has been adopted in an emerging capital market.    The steps it takes focus on how to use the “tool” in the shortest time through a strong force from the authority.    We have seen similar patterns in other emerging market such as Taiwan.    Our results can shed lights on the potential benefits/drawbacks on adoption in this route.    When everything is set by the government, the conformity rate will be high but the real essence of connecting the players in the business information supply chain (e.g., auditors,

financial institutions, government, software vendors, etc.) might have been forgotten.    It may take another round of implementation to link all the pieces in the business information supply chain together with potentially higher costs.

Our study has several limitations. First, as mentioned in Section 3, though we can observe the ultimate owner of a firm, we are not able to collect the data for the shares held by different government agents or government controlled organizations. The complexity of the shareholder structure could also affect a firm’s operational effectiveness which we do not take into consideration in the analyses. Second, for SOEs in China, there are non-outstanding shares and outstanding shares. The former is held by the government and is not publicly traded in the market. The latter can be traded in the market. We can observe the shares change of these two categories which might be a proxy for the openness of an SOE. Nevertheless, as discussed in the first point, we do not have the information regarding whether other government affiliated institutions hold the shares through the market which would lower the appropriateness of the proxy. Third, another outsider monitoring function that would affect a firm’s performance is the shares held by foreign institutions. However, such data is not considered in the analyses. Fourth, though we find significant performance improvement after the adoption of XBRL, we are not able to clearly argue and show how long it takes for the potential benefits to realize. Last, given the adoption was mandatory and the adoption process till full adoption was in a short period of time, we were not able to form a control group without XBRL adoption.

There are several possible future research avenues. As stated by the government in China, the potential benefits they would expect to observe by implementing XBRL are attracting foreign investors and increasing the visibility of firms in China, for instance. Accordingly, possible future researches can consider the association between the adoption of XBRL and

foreign investors’ interests or analysts’ forecast behaviors. It may also be beneficial to investigate how the adoption of XBRL would affect the quality of financial reports and the cost of capital in China’s capital market.

Highlights of “The Association between Mandatory Adoption of XBRL and Performance of Listed State-Owned Enterprises and Non-State-Owned Enterprises in China” (Manuscript # INFMAN-D-12-00432 R2)

• Our study documents how XBRL has been adopted in an emerging capital market.

Overall, firms’ performance is better in the post-XBRL-mandatory-adoption period.

• Non-SOEs show better improvement of performance in the post-adoption period.

Transparency is not improved for SOEs given the complicated shareholder structure.
