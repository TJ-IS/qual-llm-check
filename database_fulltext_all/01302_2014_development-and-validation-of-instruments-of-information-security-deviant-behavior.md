# Development and validation of instruments of information security deviant behavior

Amanda M.Y. Chu <sup>a</sup>, Patrick Y.K. Chau <sup>b,</sup>⁎

<sup>a</sup> Department of Management Sciences, The City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong

<sup>b</sup> Faculty of Business and Economics, The University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 11 July 2013 Received in revised form 6 June 2014 Accepted 10 June 2014 Available online 19 June 2014

Keywords: Information security Deviant behavior Instrument development Measurement Validity Reliability

## a b s t r a c t

Information security deviant behavior (ISDB) of employees is a serious threat to organizations. However, not much empirical research on ISDB has been carried out. This paper attempts to develop and validate instruments of ISDB using an empirical method. Two instruments of ISDB are proposed and tested, including a four-item instrument of resource misuse (ISDB that is related to the misuse of information systems resources) and a three-item instrument of security carelessness (ISDB that is related to the employees' omissive activities when using computers or handling data). A rigorous instrument development process which includes three surveys and addresses six crucial measurement properties (content analysis, factorial validity, reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and nomological validity) is adopted. The implications of these two instruments for future empirical studies on ISDB are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Information security deviant behavior (ISDB) of employees, such as leaving removable storage devices unattended and using untrusted applications at work, is a serious threat to organizations. A recent survey reported that 63% of interviewed information security professionals deemed employees to be a high concern for organizations; the percentage was higher than that of hackers (55%) or organized crime (38%) [24]. ISDB also results in serious <sup>fi</sup>nancial losses for organizations, with a 2009 security survey reporting the average annual such losses arising from security incidents to be US\$234,244 per company [59]. A quarter of respondents to this survey believed that at least 60% of these <sup>fi</sup>nancial losses stem from insiders' actions.

Despite the increasing prevalence and high associated costs of ISDB in the workplace, our understanding of this topic remains limited and fragmented [30,64,78]. The lack of instruments to measure ISDB presents a barrier to our understanding of the relationship between ISDB and correlated constructs and the development of theories and frameworks to tackle security problems [48]. In order to understand ISDB, it is important to develop reliable and valid instruments to measure it. This study aims to <sup>fi</sup>ll this research gap by developing instruments for the measurement of ISDB under a rigorous instrument development process. The instruments developed are useful for researchers to investigate the different properties of such behavior.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We review related studies and discuss the background theory in Section 2, and then describe how we used a four-stage process to develop the instruments for ISDB in Sections 3 and 4. Section 3 focuses on the domain speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, instrument development and instrument re<sup>fi</sup>nement while Section 4 focuses on the instrument validity. Finally, we discuss the implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and draw our conclusion in Section 5.

## 2. Background theory

## 2.1. Information security deviant behavior

Workplace deviant behavior is not a new concept. A number of studies in sociology, psychology, and organizational behavior have attempted to study acts related to workplace deviant behavior and used different terminologies to denote the behavior. Examples include antisocial behavior [26], counterproductive workplace behavior [44], organizational misbehavior [74], organizational retaliation behavior [65], workplace aggression [47], and workplace deviance [60]. Regardless of the different terminologies, prior literature tended to vary workplace deviant behavior based on its target — interpersonal deviance and organizational deviance. Table 1 summarizes the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of different terminologies used to describe the behavior and examples on interpersonal deviance and organizational deviance in each terminology. Interpersonal deviance was further categorized into political deviance and personal aggression as well as organizational deviance into property deviance and production deviance [60].

Workplace deviant behavior has been investigated from various theoretical perspectives. For example, O'Leary-Kelly et al. [50] developed a framework and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed sets of antecedents in terms of individual and organizational environment characteristics using social learning theory [2]. Martinko et al. [44] further expanded the framework by considering attribution theory [76] and causal reasoning process. Some theories may be more appropriate to explain speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of workplace deviant behavior. For instance, self-interest and role con<sup>fl</sup>ict may be more applicable to explaining lying in organizations [28] while identity theory may be more useful to explain employee alcohol use and illicit drug use [23].

Traditionally, workplace deviant behavior seldom took information systems or technology into consideration. As a result, the measures and models developed for the constructs therein may be unsuitable for a consideration of ISDB because the nature of traditional workplace deviance discussed in the prior literature (in which the actor reveals his or her identity, and a computer is not necessarily involved) and ISDB (in which the actor is dependent on a computer and can hide his or her identity) differs considerably. For example, in our previous discussion, researchers tended to classify the workplace deviant behavior into interpersonal deviance and organizational deviance but interpersonal deviance may not be applicable when we examine ISDB. Another example is that many of previous studies in workplace deviant behavior assumed intentionality (e.g., Refs. [44,51,74]). However, ISDB is a voluntary behavior rather than an intentional behavior.

With organizations' widespread adoption of computers and networks, workplace deviant behavior is no longer restricted to physical actions and direct harm to the organization or its members, but may include threats to information security on organizational computer systems. Little empirical research to date focuses on understanding deviant employee behavior that threatens information security within organizations because of the sensitive nature of the topic [36] and the absence of valid instruments [69]. Therefore, ISDB concept remains underdeveloped. To enhance academic discipline growth, it is important to de<sup>fi</sup>ne differentiating constructs for ISDB and to identify their instruments clearly and systematically. ISDB varies along a continuum of dimensions and can be explained by a typological theory [13]. One of the dimensions to organize ISDB is frequency and found that two common types of ISDB are misuse of information systems resource (resource misuse) and information security careless (security carelessness). Resource misuse is related to the misuse of any information systems resources including applications, the Internet, and networks in the workplace, while security carelessness involves employees' omissive activities when using computers or handling data in daily operations. The nature of resource misuse and security careless is different. Our target is to validate and test instruments of ISDB that can be commonly found in the workplace and in a variety of industries and occupations using an empirical method. Therefore, in this study, we attempt to develop instruments to measure resource misuse and security carelessness. In order to create instruments for ISDB for application in a variety of industries and occupations, we integrate knowledge from the literature with industry wisdom. As Chiasson and Davidson [12] stated, “Industry provides an important contextual “space” to build new IS theory and to evaluate the boundaries of existing IS theory” (p. 591).

## 2.2. Decision support for information security management

Organizations are increasingly dependent on computers, rendering information security management a crucial organizational concern [21,41]. Researchers suggest that information security is not only a technical or economic issue but also a human one and that is of concern not only to management but to everyone in the organization [34,64]. Mahmood et al. [42] emphasized that “without a better and truer understanding of the antisocial behavior that prompts individuals to attack computer systems, we cannot readily design the most effective countermeasures” (p. 432). Some previous works have attempted to examine speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of deviant workplace behavior in information security such as software piracy [53] and nonworkrelated computing [6]. However, the understanding of ISDB, which is a major source of computer systems security risk and necessary component on the foundation for designing DSS for security decision making and planning in organizations, is still not clear [79]. One possible explanation of the shortage of the research is the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in measuring the behavior. The availability of validated instruments of ISDB may be useful for facilitating the much-needed empirical research for the behavior so as to provide ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of decision support for proper security strategy and controls implemented within organizations. The aim of this study is to produce such instruments.

## 3. Instrument development

Churchill [14] provided a methodological guide used in instrument development and recommended a paradigm for instrument development comprising three stages: 1) de<sup>fi</sup>nition and speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the construct domain, 2) generation of items for the speci<sup>fi</sup>ed domain, and 3) instrument re<sup>fi</sup>nement. Many MIS research that addressed instrument development and process highlighted the importance of instrument validation (e.g., Refs. [35,69]) and therefore, suggested that instrument development usually include three steps, including item creation, instrument development, and instrument testing [45]. To further emphasize on the instrument validation, Lewis et al. [40] addressed that a quality measurement instrument should achieve an adequate level of construct validity which includes content validity, factorial validity, reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and nomological validity. Based on the previous recommendations on instrument development, we integrate the knowledge and develop a rigorous instrument development process (Table 2). The process involves four key stages. Stage I is domain speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. We did a content analysis on the topic in order to set the boundaries for the instrument development. Stage II is instrument development. We conducted a survey to compile a list of items for the instruments. Stage III is instrument re<sup>fi</sup>nement. We carried out a second survey and obtained industry and academic opinions to determine the most appropriate items for the instruments via item variances, item means, and exploratory assessment. Stage IV is instrument validation. We conducted a third survey to validate the instrument using con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory assessment and related correlation analysis. To make sure the instruments developed are representative and can be broadly applied in most of organizational environments, our samples in all three surveys were from various backgrounds and different organizations and occupations. ## 3.1. Stage I: domain specification

A clear domain speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is useful to develop appropriate items on the instruments and thus ensuring the content validity. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned ISDB as “the voluntary behavior of employees within organizations that differs markedly from the information security norms of the organizations and that is normally considered by other employees to be wrong.” Regarding the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of information security, we adopted Whitman and Mattord's de<sup>fi</sup>nition [77] which refers to “the protection of information and its critical elements, including the systems and hardware that use, store, and transmit that information” (p. 4). Our target is then to identify what is and what is not included in the domain of interest. Three key elements of ISDB are: i) intent (which) — it is a voluntary behavior; ii) nature (what) — it is a negative form of behavior; iii) perpetrator (who) — it is conducted by employees within organizations. Therefore, any behavior that occurs accidentally (e.g., accidental entry of incorrect data) or involves external parties (e.g., hackers), or any incidents unrelated to human factors (e.g., electrical power failures) are excluded because they cannot ful<sup>fi</sup>ll at least one of the three elements of ISDB.

## 3.2. Stage II: instrument development

We conducted a literature review on the examples (observable activities) of ISDB. However, previous literature seldom described whether or not the examples are typical ones. To enhance the content validity, we developed a pool of typical examples of ISDB by drawing on practitioners' knowledge. We conducted a survey (Survey 1) for which we recruited 204 employees from a variety of industry sectors who used computers at work via a number of activities and seminars organized by <sup>fi</sup>ve nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t making organizations in Hong Kong.

The respondents were given de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of information security and ISDB, and asked to describe three incidents of “employee engaging in something considered to be information security deviant behavior in organization.” After simplifying and rephrasing the descriptions, we created an initial pool of 43 items in ISDB.

These initial items were then reviewed by eight judges, including four academics and four information security professionals, who were asked to assess whether the items described different forms of ISDB on seven-point Likert scales (1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree), based on three criteria. First, the behavior in question must be consistent with the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ISDB — it must be voluntary, committed by employees within organizations, and violate the information security norms of most organizations. ## 3.3. Stage III: instrument refinement

We conducted a survey (Survey 2) and asked the respondents to provide self-reports of their ISDB in order to purify the instruments. An anonymous web-based survey was employed in order to collect more honest and faster responses. The survey consisted of the aforementioned 32-item list of forms of the behavior. Respondents were asked to indicate on seven-point Likert scales the extent to which they had been engaged in each form of behavior in the last year. The scale anchors were (1) never, (2) almost never, (3) a very few times, (4) occasionally, (5) often, (6) quite often, and (7) very many times. To ensure survey quality, the survey was pretested on eight MIS professionals and eight non-MIS employees. Following the pretest, another 20 participants who used computers at work and were employees in a nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t making organization were pilot-tested on the revised survey. The responses suggested that the survey was clearly communicated and understood. Only very minor changes to the wording were made in the revised survey.

The email invitation with the web link to the <sup>fi</sup>nal version of the survey, along with an information sheet and a password for survey access, was e-mailed to 1000 employees working in a variety of industries and from various backgrounds who used computers at work. They were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed via a membership database collected from a Hong Kong-based events portal that provides information on public seminars and activities to its members. A total of 242 questionnaires were successfully completed, representing a response rate of 24%. Of these respondents, 133 were male (56%) and 232 were degree holders (96%). The average age of the respondents was 31. Three item re<sup>fi</sup>nement steps were followed to identify the most suitable items to represent the instruments.

## 3.3.1. Step 1: item variances

We generated the mean and variance of each item, eliminating those items with a relatively low variance, as recommended by DeVellis [20] who advised that low variance is not desirable for arriving at a set of reliable items because it cannot discriminate among different individuals with various interests on the construct. We followed previous researchers studying deviant behaviors in organizations and adopted 1.5 as a low variance in seven-point Likert scales to investigate employee deviant behavior in the workplace [3]. Twenty items were subsequently dropped, retaining 12 items for Step 2.

## 3.3.2. Step 2: item means

The mean scores varied from item to item, indicating that some forms of ISDB are common in the workplace, whereas others are unusual. As this survey examined more commonly found ISDB, we retained forms of behavior in which respondents were engaged, on average, a very few times a year, which means item means equal or greater than 3 in seven-point Likert scales. Three items were removed in this selection process. Table 4 presents the means, standard deviations, and participation rates of the nine remaining items for further investigation. The participation rates of the acts described by these items were quite high (ranging from 75.6% to 93.4%), indicating that most of the respondents were engaged in them at least once in the last year.

## 3.3.3. Step 3: exploratory factor analysis

To determine the number of factors underlying the items retained in Step 2, we next proceeded with exploratory factor analysis (EFA). The FACTOR procedure in the SAS system [15] was employed along with the maximum likelihood method to explore the structure of the items. We adopted two widely used criteria, Kaiser's eigenvalue rule [32] and

Cattell's scree test [8], to determine the number of factors we should extract. Kaiser's eigenvalue rule suggests that the eigenvalues of the factors extracted should be greater than 1. Eigenvalue inspection identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two factors, as only the <sup>fi</sup>rst two eigenvalues were greater than 1 (<sup>fi</sup>rst: 38.8; second: 2.46). The results of Cattell's scree test also suggested that two factors be retained, as there is a distinct elbow in the screen plot at the point labeled “3.” The results of these two tests were in line with our expectation that instruments could be developed for two types of ISDB.

Having con<sup>fi</sup>rmed to have two factors, we reran the program specifying a two-factor solution and VARIMAX rotation. We employed VARIMAX rotation because it is the most common orthogonal rotation method and maximizes the variance of squared loadings. To ensure the representativeness of the items that can explain the underlying factor, items with factor loadings equal or below 0.4 were dropped for further analysis [22]. Based on this requirement, two items were removed. Table 5 shows that the two factors appear to represent two distinct forms of ISDB, one is related to the misuse of information systems resources, another is related to the omissive activities when using computers or handling data. From the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we developed two instruments — one is labeled resource misuse and another, labeled security carelessness. Resource misuse comprises four items, including “Using instant messaging services at work without permission,” “Using untrusted network (e.g., the Internet) for data transmission at work,” “Installing untrusted applications for personal purposes at work,” and “Running untrusted applications for personal purposes at work” while security carelessness consists of three items, including “Not locking work computer when away for convenience,” “Not shutting down work computer after <sup>fi</sup>nished using it for convenience,” and “Leaving removable storage devices with company information unattended in of<sup>fi</sup>ce.”

We used the CORR procedure in the SAS system [62] with ALPHA options to generate coef<sup>fi</sup>cient α's for the reliabilities of the two-factor model (with the above four items for resource misuse and three for security carelessness). It was suggested that a reliability level equal to or higher than 0.7 is acceptable for exploratory studies [49]. The two instruments displayed an acceptable degree of interrelatedness among their item sets, with a coef<sup>fi</sup>cient α of 0.89 for resource misuse and of 0.72 for security carelessness. The EFA solution can re<sup>fl</sup>ect the theoretical organizations of ISDB [13] and thus satisfying factorial validity that shows the factor solution is in agreement with a priori theoretical expectations.

## 4. Stage IV: instrument validity

Straub [69] suggested that instrument validity can be further demonstrated in con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory empirical research. Accordingly, we conducted another survey (Survey 3) to assess the reliability and validity of the two instruments developed in Survey 2, again adopting a web-based survey platform. The quality control methods were similar to those used for Survey 2 and described in Section 3.3. Respondents were asked to indicate on seven-point Likert scales the frequency with which they were engaged in the seven forms of ISDB identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as resource misuse or security carelessness in the last year. The scales were the same as those used in the previous survey and ranged from 1 (never) to 7 (very many times). Respondents were also asked a number of questions about constructs related to their company's information security policy and security awareness program, in addition to questions concerning their engagement in knowledge sharing, helping behavior, software piracy behavior, and nonwork-related computing behavior. They indicated their answers on seven-point Likert scales ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. Respondents were asked about software piracy and non work-related computing behavior because these types of organizational behavior are similar to ISDB. Their engagement in knowledge sharing and helping behavior was asked because these types of organizational behavior are dissimilar to ISDB. They were also questioned about their company's information security policy and security awareness program because these constructs are theoretically related to ISDB. All of these constructs were used for validating the two instruments and the details will be discussed in Section 4.2. To ensure anonymity, no names or identifying details were recorded in the survey. For this survey, we drew upon another database of 1000 people in a wide variety of careers compiled by a market research company. We collected 213 completed responses, for a response rate of 21%. Nearly half of these respondents (51%) were male and 82% were degree holders. The average age was 37.

Table 6 presents the descriptive statistics of the survey items. To assess the reliability and validity of the two instruments, we conducted the following tests. We <sup>fi</sup>rst conducted con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the achievement of unidimensional construct measurement, that is, each set of items measures one construct and that each item measures only one construct [1]. Reliability, which is based on internal consistency, was also assessed by examining the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient α's of the scale items [55]. Although the unidimensional groupings indicate convergent and discriminant validity, as the two constructs are unique and independent of each other, we further examined the convergent and discriminant validity of the two instruments by linking them with related and unrelated constructs. Convergent validity was assessed to investigate the degree to which related constructs are similar to the newly developed instruments, with high correlations expected. Discriminant validity was assessed to ensure that the two instruments are different from unrelated constructs, with low correlations anticipated. To show the ability to which resource misuse and security carelessness behave as they should within a system of theoretically related constructs, nomological validity was also assessed. Details are provided in the following sections.

## 4.1. Confirmatory factor analysis

The two newly developed instruments were veri<sup>fi</sup>ed through CFA, and the data were analyzed through the application of the CALIS procedure in the SAS system [63]. We followed Hu and Bentler's [31] recom mendations in interpreting the adequacy of the model <sup>fi</sup>t. We examined both absolute <sup>fi</sup>t indexes, including the goodness-of-<sup>fi</sup>t index (GFI; [70])

and the root mean square of approximation (RMSEA; [68]), and incremental <sup>fi</sup>t indexes, including the normed <sup>fi</sup>t index (NFI; [5]) and the comparative <sup>fi</sup>t index (CFI; [4]). An absolute <sup>fi</sup>t index assesses model <sup>fi</sup>t without reference to other models, whereas an incremental <sup>fi</sup>t index judges the <sup>fi</sup>t by comparing different models on a common basis. We expected all the index values (GIF, NFI, and CFI) equal to or greater than the cut-off value of 0.90 and the RMSEA is less than 0.06, representing a good model <sup>fi</sup>t. CFA indicated a moderate <sup>fi</sup>t to the data (RMSEA = 0.10; GFI = 0.93; NFI = 0.92; CFI = 0.93). With the exception of RMSEA, which has a moderately large value, all of the indexes are acceptable, with values greater than the cut-off value of 0.90.

The two instruments demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with coef<sup>fi</sup>cient α's of 0.89 and 0.71 for resource misuse and security carelessness, respectively. These results are consistent with those for the previous survey. The correlation between the two instruments was not high, but it was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (r = 0.11, p b 0.05), thus suggesting that the two types of ISDB are different but related. The results are, again, consistent with the previous theoretical <sup>fi</sup>ndings that commonly found types of ISDB can be captured with two factors — resource misuse and security carelessness [13], further con<sup>fi</sup>rming with the factorial validity of the two instruments developed. The <sup>fi</sup>nal instrument items are shown in the Appendix A.

## 4.2.1. Comparing similar organizational behaviors

4.2.1.1. Software piracy behavior. Software piracy is a common workplace problem [10,11,53] that is considered illegal (e.g., [11]), immoral (e.g., [37]), or unethical (e.g., [9]). Considering our de<sup>fi</sup>nition on ISDB, software piracy can be treated as one of the variants of information security deviance because it is a voluntary and negative form of employee behavior in the workplace. It was predicted to be closer in nature to resource misuse than to security carelessness, as employees who are engaged in it usually download illegal software for personal use, and less related to their habits. Software piracy was assessed with reference to the software piracy measure developed by Kwan et al. [37]. Two items were used: “I often copy software that I want without authorization at work” and “I have a lot of software that was copied without authorization at work.”

4.2.1.2. Nonwork-related computing behavior. Nonwork-related computing refers to the personal use of the Internet while at work [54]. It involves the misuse of e-mail/web facilities and may increase an organization's security risk [6]. Nonwork-related computing is a relatively new form of unethical behavior [39]. As nonwork-related computing can be treated as a manifestation of resource misuse, we expected such computing to be more related to resource misuse than to security carelessness, with only a weak or even insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship anticipated for the latter. Nonwork-related computing behavior was measured using the personal web usage measure developed by Lee et al. [39]. Two items were included: “I often use the Internet for nonwork-related purposes during working hours” and “I spend many hours on the Internet for nonwork-related purposes during working hours.”

## 4.2.2. Comparing dissimilar organizational behaviors

4.2.2.1. Knowledge sharing. Knowledge sharing refers to the willingness of organizational members to share with others the knowledge they have acquired or generated [27,38,57,75,81]. It involves both the distri bution of information to others and communication [46]. Previous research has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a number of organizational factors that affect employees' knowledge sharing behavior, including organizational structure, culture, and climate and anticipated reciprocal relationships [7,19,71]. Gibbert and Krause [27] noted that organizations can encourage knowledge sharing but cannot make it compulsory. We thus posited that the organization setting is an important determinant of knowledge sharing behavior among employees. However, there is no evidence to suggest that such behavior is directly associated with ISDB. Accordingly, we expected to <sup>fi</sup>nd no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between knowledge sharing and either type of deviance. Knowledge sharing was measured with three items adapted from the implicit knowledge sharing scale proposed by Bock et al. [7]: “I share my experience or know-how from work with other organizational members,” “I always provide my know-where or know-whom at the request of other organizational members,” and “I share my expertise from my education or training with other organizational members.”

4.2.2.2. Helping behavior. Helping behavior is cooperative and prosocial behavior that emphasizes acts of consideration and interpersonal harmony [73]. In the organizational context, helping behavior is an important dimension of extra-role behavior that helps to improve performance quality [56]. To our best knowledge, no empirical research has examined the correlation between helping or extra-role behavior and deviant behavior in the information security arena, and there is thus no evidence to indicate a positive or negative relationship between such behavior and ISDB. However, some researchers suggested that employees who offer help to colleagues are generally satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their organizations [72], whereas some researchers found internal computer crimes to be committed by disgruntled employees [66]. In this way, we expected helping behavior to exhibit no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant direct relationship with resource misuse or security carelessness, but an indirect negative relationship might be possible. Helping behavior was measured with two items from Colquitt [16]: “I put more effort into helping my teammates than is generally expected of me” and “I frequently help my teammates when they have a heavy workload.”

4.2.2.3. The results. We compared the knowledge sharing and helping behavior scores with those for resource misuse and security carelessness obtained in Survey 3. No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant correlation was found between knowledge sharing and either type of deviance. However, the survey <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicated helping behavior to have a negative correlation with resource misuse $( r = - 0 . 1 7 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ but no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship with security carelessness. The results are quite consistent with our prediction.

## 4.2.3. Linking to theoretically-related constructs

4.2.3.1. Security policy. Numerous researchers have adopted the criminological theory of general deterrence (GDT) to investigate the impact of security countermeasures on information security deviance (e.g., Refs. [17,18]). Some studies have proved that preventive controls such as security policy and security awareness program can help deter employees from engaging in information security deviance. For example, some researchers found that security policy can signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly reduce employees' information systems misuse intention (e.g. Refs.[17,18]) and nonmalicious security violation intention (e.g., Refs. [29]). It was thus expected that both resource misuse and security carelessness would have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly negative correlation with security policy. Security policy was assessed with <sup>fi</sup>ve items based on D'Arcy et al. [18]: “My organization has speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines that describe acceptable use of e-mail,” “My organization has established rules of behavior for the use of computer resources,” “My organization has a formal policy that forbids employees from accessing computer systems that they are not authorized to use,” “My organization has speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines that describe acceptable use of computer passwords,” and “My organization has speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines that govern what employees are allowed to do with their computers.”

4.2.3.2. Security awareness program. It is widely believed that user awareness is crucial to reducing security deviance (e.g., Refs. [25, 67]). Some researchers reported that security awareness program can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce employees' information systems misuse intention (e.g., Refs. [17,18]). Other studies provided evidence to suggest that prevention and training program can discourage certain types of deviant behavior such as shoplifting [61] and workplace drug use [58]. Accordingly, the establishment of a security awareness program is expected to have some effect on preventing the occurrence of ISDB. The existence of a security awareness program was assessed on the basis of D'Arcy and Hovav's [17] security awareness program dimension, with two items used, namely “My organization provides training to help employees improve their awareness of information security issues,” and “My organization educates employees on their information security responsibilities.”

4.2.3.3. The results. We compared the security policy and security awareness program scores with those for resource misuse and security carelessness obtained in Survey 3. As predicted, security policy and security awareness program provide some help in the prevention of security deviance. Consistent with our expectations, the existence of an organizational security policy was found to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly negative association with both resource misuse $( r = - 0 . 3 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and security carelessness $( r = - 0 . 3 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . The establishment of a security awareness program was found to have similar effects (resource misuse: $r = - 0 . 2 1 , p < 0 . 0 5$ ; security carelessness: r = −0.33, p b 0.01).

## 5. Conclusion and implications

There is a lack of empirical studies on the ISDB and therefore, previous discussion on the topic was described as “fragmented” and “incomplete” ([78], pp. 412). The paper adopted a rigorous instrument development process to distinguish between resource misuse and security carelessness, two commonly found types of ISDB, and to develop valid and reliable instruments for them. Past research has paid very little attention to the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and measurement of ISDB, instead employing hypothetical scenarios (e.g., Refs. [17,18]), and, in general, lacks a de<sup>fi</sup>nition for the behavior under study (e.g., Ref. [80]). The choice of resource misuse or security carelessness scenarios should be careful, as incorrect selection could incorporate inappropriate forms of behavior, thus leading to misinterpretation of the results. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the convergent and discriminant validity tests also tell us that resource misuse operates differently from security carelessness when the two types of deviance are compared with software piracy and nonwork-related computing. As predicted, resource misuse was found to have a relationship with software piracy and nonwork-related computing, both of which can be treated as speci<sup>fi</sup>c forms of such misuse. In contrast, no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed between security carelessness and software piracy or nonwork-related computing.

Further contributions of this paper are as follows. We developed useful and valid instruments for resource misuse (a four-item scale) and security carelessness (a three-item scale) on the basis of multiple empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. These instruments can facilitate further empirical studies aimed at understanding why employees are engaged in ISDB in the workplace. They can also serve as helpful tools for researchers who wish to investigate deviant employee behavior in the information security arena on the basis of sound de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and valid measurements. In addition, we emphasize the four important instrument development stages (domain speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, instrument development, instrument re<sup>fi</sup>nement, and instrument validity) and six crucial measurement properties (content analysis, factorial validity, reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and nomological validity) and demonstrate how to apply them in the development of the instruments of ISDB. The methodology presented in this study is useful to provide a practical guide for developing new instruments in research in MIS and other disciplines.

Regarding the practical implications, the generally high participation rates in different forms of ISDB found in the surveys (shown in Tables 4 and 6) could be a dangerous signal to organizations, as it suggests that some employees may not consider the behavior inappropriate. Information security deviance in the workplace involves different behavioral manifestations and two distinct instruments of ISDB (resource misuse and security carelessness) have been developed in this paper. It is found that if an employee engages in behavior that belongs to a particular behavioral family, then he or she has a greater tendency to engage in other forms of behavior within that family relative to other families. However, those in different families differ considerably. In other words, if an employee always leaves removable storage devices with company information unattended in of<sup>fi</sup>ce, the employee has a high tendency not locking work computer when away for convenience and not shutting down work computer after <sup>fi</sup>nished using it for convenience. However, this employee may not engage in the resource misuse. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide managers a clearer picture on the circumstances some forms of ISDB might be interrelated and some might not as well as a broader view on ISDB. The two instruments may be useful as the foundation for designing DSS and provide knowledge in developing DSS for security decision making and planning in organizations.

The limitations of this study are mainly those frequently connected to scale development, including limited item consideration and selfreport bias. It is impossible to cover all types of ISDB and include all ISDB activities. We, therefore, decided to follow a rigorous instrument development process to develop instruments for two commonly found types of ISDB. In order to develop typical examples for ISDB, we integrated knowledge not only from literature but also industry wisdom by asking employees to suggest examples of ISDB for survey. To minimize the effect from the self-report bias, we have made every effort to improve the internal and external validity, and generalizability, of the newly developed instruments for resource misuse and security carelessness, including the use of different samples as well as various reliability and validity tests. It is worth noting that the participation rates for deviant acts were lower in Survey 3 than in the Survey 2 (comparing Tables 4 and 6), possibly due to demographic differences. The respondents in Survey 3 were, on average, older than those in Survey 2, which may explain why they were less likely to engage in security deviance. Future research can be conducted to examine how individual factors, such as age, affect employees' likelihood of committing resource misuse or security carelessness. Some researchers may have concerns on social desirability biases due to the sensitivity of ISDB concept. Their concerns may be unwarranted, as Ones et al.'s meta-analytic results [52] suggest that self-reported criteria tended to result in higher estimates of validities than external measures of counterproductive behaviors (some forms of deviant behavior). It was also found that such biases are not substantial in IS area [43]. The results of the current study provide evidence (generally high participation rates in different forms of ISDB found in the surveys) that those respondents who answered our questionnaire were willing to admit to engaging in ISDB. It is our belief that self-reported questionnaire may be useful for assessing ISDB, at least if the respondents are assured anonymity.

Technology is advancing every day and new forms of ISDB emerge all the time. Future research can adopt and update the instruments developed in this study according to the technology development. For instance, some years ago, we used the standalone instant messaging services provided by communication platforms (e.g., MSN, ICQ) but now instant messaging services have been integrated in social networking sites (e.g., Facebook, Twitter). The two instruments developed in this paper allow two speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of ISDB to be measured and thus should prompt further empirical studies. Instrument building is an iterative process and the development of a validated and reliable instrument to measure a construct is never complete. Additional research is useful to further support the validity and reliability of the two instruments for resource misuse and security carelessness.
