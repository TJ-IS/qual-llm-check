## Abstract

It is widely accepted that the successful adoption of an information system depends to a great extent on users’ perceptions of the information system. It follows then that an understanding of users’ cognitive frames should be a key factor in managing the adoption of information systems. To reach such an understanding is not an easy task as cognitive frames are dynamic phenomena. For example, what an individual perceives as ‘ease of use’ and ‘usefulness’ may depend not only on intrinsic qualities of the information system but also on the changing contexts in which the information system is evaluated. This work argues that the management of information systems’ adoption is a social and political process in which stakeholders frame and reframe their perceptions of an information system. A case study carried out in a European bank illustrates how the Bank’s technical team influenced users’ technological frames, including those of senior management in order to ensure a smooth implementation process. In conclusion, the paper introduces a set of theoretical propositions relating to the social and political processes that occur during information systems adoption.

Keywords: Information systems adoption; implementation; socio-cognitive approach; technological frames analysis; power; politics; social interactions; interpretive and qualitative research

## Introduction

The adoption of information systems (IS) has been a long-standing topic in IS research. It has been argued that during acceptance users draw on specific cognitive devices to make sense of a new system and that these cognitive devices are crucial in shaping users’ perceptions of the ease of use and usefulness of the technology (Compeau et al., 1999; Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). For IS that cross functional boundaries in an organization, the perceptions of managers and IS professionals are also relevant (Beath & Orlikowski, 1994).

This paper seeks to investigate the extent to which stakeholders’ beliefs and perceptions during the adoption of information systems are influenced by social and political processes. This is achieved by drawing on technological frames analysis (Orlikowski & Gash, 1991, 1994). The work demonstrates that the management of information systems adoption is a social and political process in which stakeholders frame and reframe their perceptions of an information system (Orlikowski, 1992; Walsham, 1995). A case study was carried out in an International Bank based in Switzerland that successfully managed and implemented a new information and communication system.

The results of our investigation have implications for research and practice. From a research perspective, the results add to our understanding of systems adoption by offering a theoretical explanation of how cognitive frames can be transformed. Based on the results, we formulate a set of propositions about the social process that transforms frames, and that are open to empirical testing. This paper is also relevant to managers and IS practitioners since it highlights the relevance of the meanings associated with the system and how these meanings are crucial for the adoption and rejection of the system.

The paper is structured as follows. First, the concepts of frames and technological frames analysis are introduced. Second, the research approach is outlined and the background to the case study is described. An analysis of the case study is then provided and this is followed by the implications and limitations of the investigation.

## Research framework

## Frames in information systems

A frame can be understood as a cognitive device that enables individuals to comprehend, understand, and explain the world around them. Individuals rely on frames to make sense of their world. The concept of a ‘frame’ in IS research can be traced back to Boland (1978), where he argued that a lack of mutual understanding between systems users and analysts originated from the lack of a shared frame between both parties. Here, a frame was deemed to be a set of assumptions, understandings, and expectations that individuals have about a new information system and which was used by individuals to facilitate and articulate their requirements for the new system (Boland, 1978).

Concepts that are similar to the notion of a frame have also been put forward in various IS studies. For example, Lyytinen & Hirschheim (1987) argue that ‘failure is the embodiment of a perceived situation’ rather than actual ‘failure’ itself. Similarly, Carroll & Perin (1988, p. 348) point out that ‘the kinds of expectations with which an organization approaches new information technology does much to define the consequences that will be observed’. In these studies, the term ‘frame’ or ‘cognitive device’ was not used explicitly although the importance of the concept – expectations, in both cases – was acknowledged.

A frame can be defined as a set of ‘definitions of organisational reality that serve as a vehicle for understanding and action’ (Gioia & Sims, 1986, p. 5). Similarly, when individuals try to make sense of technology, it can be argued that their understanding of and actions towards technology are framed by a set of assumptions, perceptions, understandings, and interpretations of the technology (Bijker, 1995). The sense-making device that an individual uses in relation to technology is termed a technological frame. A technological frame can be defined as

ythat subset of members’ organizational frames that concern the assumptions, expectations, and knowledge they use to understand technology in organizations. This includes not only the nature and role of the technology itself, but the specific conditions, applications, and consequences of that technology in particular contexts (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994, p. 178).

A technological frame possesses specific characteristics. First, it is a device for sense making. Individuals use it to organize and make sense of, and to ascribe meaning to, a technology. As a cognitive device, a technological frame serves as a short cut and filter with which to understand some things and to ignore others. If an individual had used a similar technology before, it is likely that the individual will use the new technology on the basis of his/her previous experience and knowledge and expect to see a similar result. If an individual does not have any experience with the new technology, the individual is likely to search for knowledge of other technology in the same category in order to facilitate his/her understanding and use of the technology. In short, technological frames structure experience, allow interpretation of ambiguous situations, reduce uncertainty in situations of complexity and change, and provide a basis for taking action.

Second, a frame is interpretive, flexible, and context specific. Individuals who rely on the same frame to make sense of the same object, in different contexts, may arrive at different interpretations of and conclusions about the same object. Therefore, it is not possible to identify a set of components and contents of one’s technological frame outside the context or in advance (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Lin & Cornford, 2000). A technological frame emerges from and is transformed by interactions among individuals, technology and context. The emerging nature of a technological frame in context is a critical concept in technological frames analysis as it stresses the importance of social interactions in situ.

## Technological frames analysis

Technological frames analysis (Orlikowski & Gash, 1991, 1994) studies the underlying assumptions, expectations, and knowledge that people have about information technology. According to Orlikowski & Gash (1991), it is important to understand such underlying cognitive threads as they have an influential effect on an individual’s acceptance of the technology:

We argue that an understanding of people’s interpretations of a technology is critical to understanding their interaction with it. To interact with technology, people have to make sense of it; and in this sense-making process, they develop particular assumptions expectations, and knowledge of the technology, which then serve to shape subsequent actions toward it. While these interpretations become taken-for-granted and are rarely brought to the surface and reflected on, they nevertheless remain significant in influencing how actors in organizations think about and act toward technology (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994, p. 175).

Orlikowski & Gash (1994) argue that members of a community are likely to share some core assumptions, knowledge, and expectations. A frame shared by the members of a group serves as a device to facilitate the group’s understanding of events and in turn to take actions in response to the events. The shared technological frame of one group is not necessarily accepted and shared by another group. In other words, different groups are likely to have their own shared frame and, on the basis of their own frame, they interpret and interact with the technology. Any differences in expectations, assumptions, or knowledge with regard to the key aspects of the technology are known as an ‘incongruence of technological frames’ (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). By contrast, a ‘congruence of technological frames’ takes place when different groups share ‘similar expectations around the role of the technology in business processes, the nature of technological use, or the type and frequency of support and maintenance’ (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994).

For the purposes of this work, technological frames analysis was used as a theoretical lens to tease meanings out of our data. It was used both to identify the underlying assumptions and perceptions of the technology that the different stakeholders had of the system under investigation and to trace the changes in their assumptions and perceptions of the technology. We established how the technological frames of each group of stakeholders were transformed throughout the implementation process; from the initial conception of the idea to its final acceptance. Hence, technological frames analysis was used not only to organize and classify the data but also to interpret and to make sense of the data. Our interpretation and analysis of the data then enabled us to formulate a set of propositions regarding the social and political processes that led to the framing and reframing of stakeholders’ beliefs and values.

Previous studies have failed to recognize the fact that individuals’ expectations, perceptions, and attitudes toward technology change overtime, and that social and political processes influence such changes. The remainder of the paper aims to explore how the stakeholders’ beliefs and perceptions of the system influence their attitudes towards the system and how their beliefs and perceptions can be framed and reframed through social interactions. Subsequently, the study investigates how management’s ability to harness the framing and reframing process, during an IS adoption, is one of the keys to its success.

## Research approach

## The research site

The case study presented in this paper is concerned with a project in an International Bank based in Switzerland. The project, named GroupWork, was carried out with the aim of implementing a new e-mail system in the Bank. This particular project was selected because it was identified by the Bank as being the most successful IS project in the Bank’s entire IS history; and because it presented a number of interesting aspects of technological frames in IS implementation. The project was regarded as ‘successful’ for a number of reasons. First, the new system, GroupWise, was available to and used by all employees in the Bank. Second, the project was rolled out in the Bank within the timeframe and budget. Third, the project had the senior manager’s public endorsement. Fourth, unlike previous projects there were not many technical hiccups during the implementation of the GroupWork project. Fifth, despite the conflicting views of the project team and the user group at the user requirements stage, there was little resistance to the new system after the system was rolled out in the Bank. The post-project user group survey also indicated that 60% of the members agreed that the project had been a success.

## Data collection

Data were collected from various sources: organizational and project documentation; structured, semi-structured and open interviews. Documentation relevant to the project played an important role in this research as it provided us with important background information (Klein & Myers, 1999). A total of 162 documents were ultimately analysed. The documentation that was used and reviewed for this research includes internal memoranda, e-mail messages, meetings minutes, and other project documents. Semi-structured and formal interviews were carried out to collect the project members’ view. Each interview lasted between 1 and 2 h. in length. Seven formal scheduled interviews were conducted. The interviewees were those who were involved in the project, including the manager of the IT department, the project coordinator, project manager, training officer, a member of the User Group, and a member of the IT department. All interviews were tape recorded and transcribed for subsequent analysis. Lunchtime in the canteen at the Bank provided another research opportunity, to meet and talk to further members of the user group. It was noted that at lunchtime people were more relaxed and willing to share their personal views of the project, and discuss what actually happened during the project. Informal chat with the interviewees provided further information about the project. Observations of conversations between staff were also made, both in the office and in the canteen. Follow-up e-mails were sent to the interviewees to clarify questions about the project during the data analysis phase.

## Data analysis

We adopted an interpretive approach to the data analysis. The analysis and interpretation of the data informed by the framework of technological frames analysis focused on aspects of technological frames. Data collected from the field were first read to gain background knowledge of the organization and of the project that was generated; and then interpretations were formed through continual reading and re-examination of the materials. Continuous engagement was pursued through a dialogue with the materials in order to re-examine and revise emergent arguments and assumptions (Introna, 1997). During the data analysis, we returned to the interviewees for further information or clarification of the statements made in the initial interviews either by arranging another interview or by follow-up e-mails. Sometimes, follow-up e-mails asked questions to clarify our initial interpretation of the data. The correspondents either confirmed or rejected our interpretations, thus providing us with scope for further elaboration of these issues.

## Case study

The GroupWork project was initiated with the aim of replacing the Bank’s old e-mail system, Higgins, which the Bank had used since 1988. Over the years, the Bank switched its computer operating system to Microsoft Windows. Other office applications were also replaced with Windows-based applications and as a result MS-DOS-based Higgins seemed to be out of date compared with the rest of the applications in the Bank. The Office Information Systems (OIS), a division within the IT department, initiated an investigation to evaluate the need for replacing Higgins.

## Project initiation

The initial motivation for the project was based upon complaints about Higgins, from the user departments. Statements such as ‘many people did not like to use Higgins because it was inconvenient and it kept falling over for a lot of reasons’; ‘we cannot depend on current system’; ‘not user friendly’ were often made to the OIS. Besides this, OIS also received feedback from the new employees who made comments like ‘my previous company used this and that systemy’; ‘DOS based Higgins is out of date’. OIS carried out an internal user evaluation of Higgins and the result of the survey indicated that in general users felt that there was plenty of room for improvement in Higgins. The users felt strongly about the inconvenience of using Higgins in the Windows working environment, because in order to receive and send e-mails in Higgins they had to switch from the Windows working environment to the DOS environment. This meant that when mail was received they might need to interrupt what they were doing. That is why many employees, especially those who worked in the dealing room, often switched off Higgins during trading hours in order to avoid interruption. Besides, since Higgins was not initially designed for a Windows working environment the switch between the two environments was not always smooth and often caused computer crashes.

The demand for replacing Higgins also came from the IT department. Not only did the IT department have to deal with a growing number of complaints from user departments, but it also had to spend at least one working day per week fixing the problems caused by Higgins. As one interviewee noted:

The system, Higgins, used to fall over everyday. It could just about to manage the internal e-mail requirements. It was just a funny DOS box within the Windows and it was not stable enough. I had to spend half day to a day per week to keep the system active. We realised that Higgins was not robust enough to meet the requirement that we have today (project team member).

After the initial investigation, the OIS decided to initiate the GroupWork project that aimed to replace DOS-based Higgins with a Windows-based system. Three groups of individuals were directly involved in the GroupWork project: the management group, the project team, and the user group. The management group consisted of senior managers who made decisions about resource allocations for the project. The user group’s members came from the main user departments in the Bank. On the one hand, the user group represented a wider user community’s interests; and on the other, the user group acted on behalf of the project team to inform the user community about the development of the project. Table 1 describes the composition of each group, as well as summarizing their objectives. The rest of this section describes the three groups’ involvement.

The GroupWork project was understood and perceived by the project team as a demand-pull IS project: users demanded to have an updated e-mail system to keep up with the existing infrastructure (e.g. Windows). Since Higgins was mainly used for sending messages and electronic files, the users expected that the new system would have the same features but only better. The users also expected that the new system would be easy to use and compatible with the existing IS infrastructure in order to avoid system down time, which would cause interruptions to their work. The project team’s expectation for the new system was its compatibility and stability, which means that the system could operate within the existing infrastructure with minimal technical hiccups.

## Product selection

The project team sent out bidding invitations to various software houses. European Software Publishing and Novell, who were on the short list, were invited to give demonstration sessions for their products: BeyondMail and GroupWise. The aim of the demonstration sessions was to give the user group an opportunity to try out the software in order to make an informed decision about the product selection. After the demonstration, BeyondMail was chosen by the user group while GroupWise was preferred by the project team. BeyondMail was chosen on the basis of users’ requirements for an easy to use system, with good e-mail facilities. Although in principle the project team agreed with the user group’s choice, it argued that GroupWise fitted better into the current IT infrastructure as the system was by Novell and part of the IT infrastructure in the Bank was also by Novell. There would thus be no issue about the compatibility of the system with the existing infrastructure.

Instead of giving the above technical reason to the user group, the project manger chose to give a softer reason, by redefining e-mail with his own interpretation: ‘e-mail is a means to support groupwork which is more than just to support communication’. He told the user group that BeyondMail might be a good communication system but GroupWise was a superior system because it supported both communication and groupwork. After this point, the project team then gave a ‘hard’ version of the reason and explained that from a technical perspective Group-Wise would be a better fit with the existing infrastructure:

GroupWise is a mainstream product (one of the top three market players), it is cheaper to purchase, install, and maintain, and provides the Bank with a number of additional advantages (availability of manufacturer support in Switzerland, support for UNIXy) (memorandum 30 September, 1995).

Subsequently, the user group agreed with the project team’s choice of system; in part because they were becoming unsure about their own choice and felt that the project team seemed to know better than they did about the technical issues of the system:

y We had a vote in the group and the majority voted for BeyondMail. After a certain amount of lobbying it was then decided to actually look at the systems from the system point of view because it had to be very compatible with our system. PT people felt that GroupWise fits into our environment better (interview with a member of the user group).

## Project publicity

After GroupWise was chosen, the project team had to prepare a proposal to apply for resource allocations from the management group. Knowing that return on investment was the management group’s primary concern about the project, the project team carefully crafted the project proposal by addressing the potential of the project from a business point of view. The project manager felt that a good project name could speak for the project and entice the management group into allocating the necessary resources and most importantly endorsing it. Subsequently, the name GroupWork was chosen with certain ideas in mind:

The word ‘work’ means enhancing, simplifying and automating the flow of information and its availability between a group of people inside the institution and with other groups inside or outside the institution. ‘Group’ is about improving efficiency of a group of people working together by automating organisation and co-ordination tasks within this group (interview with the project manager).

Statements such as ‘yimproving the efficiency of a group of people working together by automating organisation and coordination tasks within this group’; ‘y enhancing, simplifying and automating the flow and availability of the information within this group and with other groups inside or outside the institution’ were made in the project manager’s presentation to the management group. According to the project manager, terms such as ‘workgroup’ and ‘groupware’ were buzzwords in both the IT and commercial sectors at the time. The name ‘GroupWork’ could give people the impression that the project was about group/teamwork technology. In so doing, the management group and the people at the management level in the Bank could relate to this business concept easily. Nevertheless, it was clear to the project manager and the team that implementing a groupware system or introducing a group working concept was never the intention or part of the project plan.

In a memorandum to the project team, the management group demanded to know how necessary the project was to the Bank, the benefits of having the project in relation to the costs, the effects of the project on the organization, and the impact of the system on security (e.g. risks and sensitivities of data). In the same memorandum, the management group also questioned whether the project intended to take full advantage of the new functionalities that the new system could offer with a clear appraisal of operational benefits. In a different memorandum, the management group asked the project team to clarify the difference between the workgroup functions available in Higgins (the old system) and those available in GroupWise. Only when the project team provided satisfactory answers to these questions would the management group then allocate the necessary resources to the project.

Apart from having the necessary financial backup, the project team would also like to have active support and public endorsement of the project from the management group. Based on their experiences of implementing other information systems in the Bank, the project team knew that without the management group’s active support for the project, user departments were unlikely to take the project seriously. As an interviewee pointed out ‘it is the culture in the Bank that if you are not a banker, you are nothing’. Employees saw IT department only generating costs but bringing no revenues into the Bank.’

After being successfully sold to the management group, the GroupWork project had both financial support and public endorsement from the management. The General Secretary of the Bank issued a formal memorandum to address the role of the new system in the Bank in the future:

Electronic mail (e-mail) is an essential means of efficient communication, both within an organisation and increasingly, on a global scaley I expect e-mail to become the normal form of communication in the Bank for short notes and memos. y E-mail should no longer be used to send private announcements to large lists of recipients. Instead, a new bulletin board facility (discussion groups) will be introduced for this purpose (Memorandum from the General Secretary to the employees in the Bank)

## System roll-out

Although the project team’s views about the system and system implementation seemed to dominate the agenda, when it came to the system roll-out the project team had to take into account various organizational issues in order to minimize resistance from user departments and management.

The unique organizational setting imposed a specific restriction on when the system roll-out could take place in the Bank. Publishing an annual report is a big event in the Bank, hence it was critical for the project team to ensure that the project timetable was not going to take place during the annual report preparation period. Indeed, this issue was raised by the management group in the project meeting.

The user departments were only concerned about whether or not the system roll-out, including training on the new system, would interrupt their daily operations. They expected that it would be fairly easy for them to pick up the basics and the training section should be no longer than necessary. Such views were clearly stated in a memorandum:

Users felt strongly that only minimum training is required as they have a job to do. They feel that e-mail is only one more tool to help them do their job effectively. They are expecting to receive a replacement soon and they also expect and want to pick up the basics quickly and run with that.

The project team had its own views of the system rollout too. Based on their experience of rolling out information systems in the Bank, the project team was aware that issues relating to training and system installation were critical to smooth system roll-out. Issues such as who will provide the training, who will be trained, how they will be trained, and what the venue will be like were all taken into account when the project team was planning the training session. The timing of system installation was also critical. An interviewee pointed out that the timing of the system installation had to be handin-hand with the training. In the past, training on the new system took place either too early or too late and users were not actually benefiting from the training. Hence, the project team decided to install the system on employees’ desktops while they were away for training. In so doing, employees could use the system immediately upon their return. A project team member said:

We knew that this strategy was very risky because the employees might feel that we were doing things ‘behind their back’. But we felt that it is crucial that the system was ready in time for them to use when their memory is still fresh.

Indeed, this strategy seemed to be acceptable and to be welcomed by the user department:

The procedure proved to be successful, I’m not aware of any person in the department who was not satisfied.

## Analysis

The necessity of the GroupWork project was agreed and accepted at all levels of the Bank. At first sight, the acceptance of the project could be interpreted as the result of a congruence in the technological frames of different groups. Nevertheless, on closer examination an incongruence in the technological frames of the three groups indeed existed. Table 2 summarizes the shared technological frames of each group.

The contents of the technological frames held by each group differed, and reflected each group’s position in the project and within the Bank, and their experience with information systems projects in the past.

## Incongruence of technological frames

To begin with their understandings of the problem, the three groups understood their problems differently because their experiences with office information systems and their jobs in the Bank were different (see Table 3). The members of the project team understood the problems with a technological mindset so phrases like: ‘not robust enough’, ‘incompatible’, ‘fall apart’ were frequently used during the interviews and in the project documents to describe the old system. Users, on the other hand, addressed the problems with concerns about their own job performance. Terms such as ‘interruptions’ and ‘unfriendly’ were used by users to express their needs for a new system. The management group’s concerns about the project were neither technological nor operational but business related (i.e. the project should reflect the Bank’s business needs). Therefore in meetings with the project team, terms such as ‘costs’, ‘benefits’, ‘not interrupting the daily operation’ were used.

Nevertheless, when looking through the lens of technological frames analysis a different story emerges. It seems that the congruence of technological frames was achieved through framing and reframing of technological frames via social interaction.

Clearly, there was a disagreement over which system should be chosen as the Bank’s new e-mail system. In order to persuade the user group to change its choice from BeyondMail to GroupWise, the project team created ‘public fora’ to reframe the project team’s perception and expectation of the new system. The public fora took many forms including memoranda and meetings. For example, in a memorandum to the senior management, the project team pointed out some facts with supporting evidence (e.g. statistics) and explained why GroupWise was superior to BeyondMail. In the meeting with the project team, the project manager redefined the e-mail system in the context of the project. And the project team also targeted specific members of the user group, with the aim that they would lobby GroupWise in the user group.

In different public fora the project team carefully chose a different language in order to engage user group in the discussion of the new system. The project team tried to change the user group’s perception and understanding of the project, hence it introduced the concept of groupware to the user group and argued that BeyondMail did not meet the project requirements to support groupwork. The project team then stressed that technologically the BeyondMail was not compatible with the existing Novell infrastructure and that its groupware functionalities were not as advanced as those in GroupWise. Here, the project team tried to reframe the user group’s expectations of the new system. At the end of this reframing process, it was evident that the user group’s initial view of the project had changed from ‘having a new e-mail system’ to ‘having a groupware system’. Their expectation of the new system had also changed from being user friendly to being a technological fit with the existing infrastructure. Indeed, it can be argued that fora act as carefully constructed common ground that people can refer to so as to believe that they engaged in rational decisionmaking.

However, the project team used different tactics when they interacted with the management group. Instead of trying to reframe the management group’s technological frames, the project team carefully chose the project name and constructed and rephrased the purpose of the project so that the image of the project could ‘match’ the management group’s frames. For example, the project team knew very well that the management group’s primary concerns about the project were costs, necessity of the project, and implications of the project. Thus, when it gave its presentation to the management group, the project team focused its attention on addressing these issues in order to assure the management group that the project made sound business sense and was justified in terms of costs and benefits.

Bearing the above in mind, it was evident that in order to overcome the incongruence in the technological frames of the three groups involved, the Bank’s project team did more than just communicate. Through their interactions with the three groups, the project team actively tried to frame and reframe stakeholders’ frames in order to ensure a smooth systems implementation.

It is interesting to note that there appeared to be a large amount of initial ‘congruence’ about the nature of the project, replacing Higgins with a new e-mail system, among all stakeholders. However, incongruence of technological frames arose among stakeholders when choosing a new e-mail system as stakeholders had different needs and expectations for the new system. But such incongruence gradually disappeared and congruence re-emerged through a purposeful reframing process.

## Implications and limitations

In this section, we discuss our contribution to both theory and practice. In so doing, we have formulated three theoretical propositions, along with their practical implications. We conclude this section by reflecting on the limitations of our study. As indicated earlier, these propositions may be the subject of empirical testing. The empirical testing of the propositions would require the operationalization of the concepts that constitute the antecedent and consequent of each proposition. The operationalization of each concept will require a rigorous theoretical exploration and justification to which our paper is an important first step. After the operationalization of the concepts, the researcher may design instruments to measure them. These instruments could be survey questionnaires that could then be applied in one or several organizational samples. The results of analysing the data could provide grounds to confirm or modify the following propositions in a variety of organizational domains:

Proposition 1. The perceived usefulness and ease of use of a system is not only a function of the system itself but also depends on the context in which it is evaluated.

Our data show that the users’ cognitive frames, associated with the e-mail system, were stable until they were aware of newly available technology (see Tables 2 and 3). Users’ perceptions regarding the appropriateness of their e-mail system were influenced when they learned about other systems that were compatible with Windows and that had a more adequate user system interface (Table 4 shows the initial evaluation criteria). The meanings and expectations linked to their then current system, Higgins, were transformed from useful to obsolete. In other words, their understanding of technology was reframed. Theoretically speaking, Proposition 1 is interesting given that it adds to the TAM model (Davis, 1989) in the sense of indicating that perceived usefulness and ease of use are a function of users’ experience. This proposition also brings a practical implication for inducing technological changes in organizations. For example, managers concerned to change current systems may show potentially friendlier and more useful versions of systems to users so that the latter may start demanding a technological shift. IS professionals will thus concentrate on aspects of the systems that may be attractive to the users. This implies that IS professionals should make efforts to identify the technological frames of their users. In our case, this was achieved by frequent meetings and by engaging the user group in discussions about the new system.

Proposition 2. Successful implementation of an information system will be facilitated by achieving congruent technological frames.

Our findings show that different organizational roles, identities, and interests will suggest different technological frames (this is illustrated in Tables 2 and 3). As was demonstrated in our case the different groups of stakeholders – users, managers and IT professionals – had different perceptions and understandings of the new system (see Table 4). The possibility of different groups having different interpretations of the same technology is consistent with previous research in this area (Orlikowski & Gash, 1991, 1994; Bijker, 1995). In this sense, our contribution not only confirms those early propositions but also to suggests that frame congruence can be achieved as the result of purposeful actions from those championing the system. The practical implication is that information systems professionals interested in designing and implementing information systems should add the identification of technological frames to the analysis stage. In this sense, user requirements reports should also include an assessment and description of the technological frames of their users. This can be done through detailed observation and careful note-taking. Our discussion and definition of technological frames can be used as a guide.

Our a posteriori analysis suggests that, unwittingly or not, the information systems’ professionals identified that their users’ technological frames were mainly focusing upon practical aspects of e-mail usage. By way of meetings, discussions, and presentations, in which users were exposed to other types of technologies, the project team was able to transform the user group’s initial technological frames. Our research, and this proposition in particular, suggests a set of actions that information systems’ professionals could follow for incorporating ideas about technological frames into their tasks. These sets of actions would first require the realization of different frames among groups of individuals involved in a project; and second require the execution of a plan for either reframing the understanding of one group or for adapting the discourses associated with the system.

Furthermore, we argue that without these interventions to reframe the understandings and expectations associated with the new e-mail system, its implementation may have failed. Proposition 2 is related to what Silva & Backhouse (2003) have found to be required in order to institutionalize systems. Silva & Backhouse (2003) have shown that in order for a system to become stable its associated meanings and interpretations should be aligned with working practices and beliefs. In addition, this proposition has a managerial implication as it suggests that managers and IT professionals should assess the technological frames of stakeholders before introducing a new system. Being aware of the different frames would allow managers to develop a more effective implementation strategy either by influencing the frames or by adapting the discourses associated with the system.

Proposition 3. Understandings, interpretations, and expectations of information systems are framed and reframed through the exercise of power.

In terms of how social phenomena affect or influence technological frames, we found an interesting relationship with power and language. Through engaging the user group in a discussion of the definition of the e-mail system and its technical feasibility, the project team successfully changed the user group’s initial choice to GroupWise. Our data show that the project team deliberately used abstract concepts and technical jargon to muddy the user group’s understanding of the project and their expectations of the new system. We argue that the project team possessed valuable resources such as technical knowledge of the new system and of the existing infrastructure to make this reframing process happen.

The power wielded by the technical team was symbolic and it was exercised through language (Bourdieu, 1989, 1991). The main tactic deployed by the the project team was the use of jargon and technical lingo to demonstrate their superior expertise and to sideline the arguments by the users. The project team achieved its objective – that of selecting GroupWise – through meetings and personal communications. It persuaded the user group to shift its attention from the e-mail features offered by the first selected system to other features such as its compatibility and usefulness for enabling group work. The power exercised by the project team over the user group is what French & Raven (1959) call expert power. Expert power occurs when one individual influences another based on the extent of the knowledge that the latter attributes to the former. Users considered that the technical team were knowledgeable experts on information systems. This was key to the altering of users’ technological frames. In a way, the concept of expert power is reflected in the way Actor Network Theoreticians (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1987) interpret the act of claiming the position of the spokesperson, as being an act of power. In our case, the project team acted as speaking on behalf of information technology. Thus, this proposition suggests that in a case in which the power asymmetry favours those proposing the system, they can affect the technological frames of the users by drawing on symbolic superiority and expert power (French & Raven, 1959; Markus, 1983; Markus & Bjorn-Andersen, 1987; Bloomfield & Coombs, 1992).

Reflection on this proposition suggests that whenever the balance of power disadvantages those proposing the system, then frame incongruence will prevail. Therefore, in a situation like this, for the adoption of systems, what is reframed is not the understanding and expectations of the technology but the discourses and meanings associated with the new system. This occurred in our case when the project team purposefully presented the system to the management group as a tool for enabling group work, for linking ideas and for bringing the organization together. The members of the project team purposefully aligned the connotations of implementing GroupWise with overall organizational goals that they knew would be welcomed by the senior management. This reflection on Proposition 3 is supported by previous work, such as the one conducted by Beath (1991), who showed how champions of information systems adapt the discourses associated with systems to those of senior management in order to ensure the latter’s support. It is in this manner that Proposition 3 highlights the relationship between reframing technology interpretations and symbolic power.

## Limitations

Our fieldwork was conducted in an organization that was no doubt permeated by the culture of its social environment (Hofstede, 1991). The strong democratic tradition in Switzerland may have shaped the practices of the different groups in the Bank. One may argue that in a more autocratic organization changes would have been made without consulting the users. This has to be observed particularly when gauging the participative tactics and strategies adopted by the project team. Another consideration of our study is that we dealt with a system that was neither strategic nor embedded in core business processes of the organization. It is possible, then, that the social processes for framing or reframing meanings would be different for strategic or core systems. The testing of our theoretical propositions in those scenarios may be areas to be explored in further research.

It can also be argued that the initial objectives of the system, as they were manifest to senior management, were not achieved. Senior managers were told that the system would be used as a tool to enhance group work. In the end not all of the group-oriented functions were utilized. In this sense, the implementation could have qualified as being unsuccessful. One of the strengths of the approach adopted here, however, is to bring issues previously submerged to light; and in this case, it enabled contradictions between the beliefs of organizational members on the one hand and the actual functionality of the system on the other to be brought to the surface.

## Conclusion

The main contribution of this paper is the linking of three different concepts, technological frames, social phenomena, and implementation of information systems by way of a robust empirical and theoretical study. We have done that by looking at the literature of ${ \mathrm { I S } } ,$ by conducting a case study and by formulating a set of theoretical propositions. We found that social phenomena such as language, symbolic power, and communication processes are fundamental for understanding how technological interpretations are framed. Accordingly, we learned that technological frames are not static but dynamic. This inherent quality of frames may be intriguing not only for researchers but also for practitioners interested in finding new techniques for systems implementation.
