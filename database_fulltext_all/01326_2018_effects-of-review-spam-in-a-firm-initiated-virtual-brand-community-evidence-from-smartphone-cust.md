## Abstract

Previous studies of spam focus on how to develop effective and efficient algorithms and technologies to monitor, identify, and filter spam to minimize its negative consequences. Few studies have examined the social and psychological impacts of spam on social media users and participants of virtual brand communities (VBCs). This paper develops and tests a research model to explain the mechanism of how review spam influences customer participation in VBCs. We collected both objective data and

#

subjective data from smartphone users in China. We also developed a measure of review spam using objective data. We found that hyper review had positive effects on membership and influence (two dimensions of sense of virtual communities (SOVC)), while the effects of defaming review on membership and influence were negative. Neither hyper view nor defaming review impacted immersion (the third dimension of SOVC). Further, non-review had negative effects on all three dimensions of SOVC that positively influenced future customer participation.

Keywords: virtual brand community, review spam, sense of virtual community, customer participation

## Introduction

Spam is useless and misleading information that hinders individual users in the process of information searches and interpersonal communication (Caruana and Li, 2012; Chakraborty et al., 2016; Zitar and Hamdan, 2013). Spam costs businesses and consumers billions of dollars every year (Rao and Reiley, 2012). According to a recent report by Symantec (2016), spam accounted for more than 50% of the inbound emails that companies received in 2015. In addition, the volume of spam in social media has increased dramatically in the recent years (Chakraborty et al., 2016; Song et al., 2017; Symantec, 2016). Social spam that explodes on social media and virtual brand communities (VBCs) tends to mislead VBC participants and consumers of certain brands by providing fake or unfair reviews and opinions that damage VBC focal products and brands while deliberately denigrating and advocating competing brands and products. Social spam exerts powerful influences on consumers’ decision making, online purchasing, and customer referrals (Caruana and Li, 2012; Chakraborty et al., 2016; Jindal and Liu, 2007, 2008; Lau et al., 2010).

Most of the previous academic research of spam is focused on technical issues and aims to develop effective and efficient algorithms and technologies to monitor, identify, and filter spam so that the damages caused by these spams can be minimized (Caruana and Li, 2012; Chakraborty et al., 2016; Jindal and Liu, 2007, 2008; Lau et al., 2010; Savage et al., 2015; Zitar and Hamdan, 2013). Scarce research in the information systems literature has examined the social and psychological impacts of spam on participants of virtual communities, users of social media, and customers of VBCs, unlike some initial evidence in the marketing literature (e.g., Tsimonis and Dimitriadis, 2014, Hutter et al., 2013). There is limited knowledge of how spam affects consumer psychology, decision-making, and behaviors in the area of social media and VBCs. To our best knowledge, there is no research on the information systems literature that investigates the impacts of spam on customer participation in a VBC.

The purpose of this paper is to develop and test a research model to explain the mechanism of how one type of spam, review spam, influences customer participation in a VBC. Centered on the theory of sense of virtual community (SOVC) (Blanchard and Markus, 2004; Burroughs and Eby, 1998; Koh and Kim, 2003), we investigate how conducted an empirical study and collected both objective and subject data from a VBC to test the research model.

The structure of the paper is as follows. First, we review the relevant literature on spam, customer participation, and SOVC. Then, we develop a research model and hypotheses that were tested with empirical data. We conclude with discussing the findings and implications for research and practice.

## Literature Review

## Review spam and its categorization

Originating from email spam, spam has evolved into different forms because of the rapid technological advances on the internet (Caruana and Li, 2012; Chakraborty et al., 2016; Lau et al. 2010; Lim et al., 2010). Prior studies of spam have mostly examined web spam, which refers to all forms of malicious manipulation of user-generated data to influence the usage pattern of users and customers on the internet (Caruana and Li, 2012; Chakraborty et al., 2016; Lim et al., 2010). Examples of web spam include search engine spam, email spam, video spam, and review spam. A recent form of spam is social spam found in various social media platforms that is targeted at and directed to the participants of the social media sites (Chakraborty et al. 2016; Song et al., 2017).

Review spam is unsolicited and undesired reviews that are designed to give an unfair view of a product to influence consumers’ perceptions of the product by directly or indirectly inflating or damaging the product’s reputation (Jindal and Liu, 2007, 2008). In one of the first studies of review spam, Jindal and Liu (2007, 2008) investigated the spam of opinions and reviews provided by customers, businesses, or other parties toward certain brands and products. They provided one of the first classifications of review spam and categorized review spam into three types: false or untruthful opinions, reviews of brands only, and non-reviews. This classification has been commonly accepted by recent studies (Crawford et al., 2015; Lau et al. 2010; Lim et al. 2010; Savage et al., 2015).

According to Jindal and Liu (2007, 2008), false or untruthful opinions refer to extreme positive and negative spam reviews of a product or competing products. Reviews of brands only refer to the reviews that do not comment on a product but express opinions only about the company (or manufacturer and seller). Non-reviews are reviews that contain no opinions but rather advertisements as well as irrelevant questions and answers, and, thus, do not serve the purpose of a review.

Review spam also exists in VBCs. Following Jindal and Liu (2008), we focus on false or untruthful opinions (hyper review and defaming review) and non-review. Hyper review refers to the review in a VBC that expresses an undeserving positive opinion of an alternative competing brand against the preferred brand that is hosted in the VBC (Jindal and Liu 2008). In an iPhone community, a review or message that positively advocates Android phones, such as “I use Android, and it is extremely better than iPhone,” can be considered a hyper review. The purpose of this type of spam is to promote the competing product, i.e., Android in this scenario. Defaming review refers to the message in a VBC that includes a malicious and negative opinion on the brand that is promoted in the VBC (Jindal and Liu 2008). The purpose of this type of review is to damage the reputation of the brand in the VBC, which is clearly unwanted in the VBC by the person who posted the review. For example, a message that conveys an unfair criticism of iPhone in the same iPhone community, such as “iPhone seems to be not as good as advertised,” is an example of a defaming review. Non-review is information in a review posted in a VBC that is irrelevant to the VBC brand or its competing brands (Jindal and Liu, 2008). For example, a message may read “Come join an amazing food club” or “Claim %4asdf.” This type of message makes it difficult for customers to process and understand the meaning of the message (Jindal and Liu, 2008).

## Customer participation in VBCs

Extensive spam has significant impacts on the rapid growth of social media that has driven companies to build electronic platforms to develop and maintain customer relationships (Chakraborty et al. 2016; Song et al., 2017), such as VBCs. Muniz and O’Guinn (2001) define a VBC as “a specialized, non-geographically bound community based on a structured set of social relationships among admirers of a brand” (p.413). A VBC can be initiated by either the company or customers (Jang et al., 2008). In a firm-

#

initiated VBC, the company transforms its business activities such as customer support and services from off-line to on-line context (Koh and Kim, 2003; Kozinets et al. 2010).

A major value for a customer’s participation in a firm-initiated VBC is to learn the products and brands carried by the firm (Chiu et al., 2006; Zha et al., 2015). Customers access and obtain the firm’s product information, which assists customers in judging the value and quality of a product and even helps customers make purchasing decisions (Chiu et al., 2006; Kozinets et al. 2008; Nambisan and Baron 2009; Wasko and Faraj 2000). A VBC is also a repertoire where customers create, exchange, and accumulate information and knowledge that can be a source of user-centered innovations (Kozinet et al. 2008). A customer may build a reputation or identity by means of making contributions to a VBC, such as sharing knowledge, answering questions, reviewing products, and helping peer customers (Chiu et al., 2006; Kozinets et al. 2010; Wasko and Faraj 2000). Under some circumstances, customers participate in a VBC because they feel obligated to give back to the company by means of advocating the brand, providing positive reviews, and defending the brand supported by the VBC (Blanchard and Markus, 2004).

Customers who join a firm-initiated VBC are likely to be loyal customers and “determined fans” of a brand (McAlexander et al., 2002; Muniz and Hamer 2001; Muniz and O’Guinn 2001). A VBC is likely to be the hub for the firm’s customers to convene and bind together (McAlexander et al., 2002; Muniz and Hamer 2001; Muniz and O’Guinn 2001; Thompson and Sinha, 2008). These customers are more likely to participate in a firm-initiated VBC to develop a stronger sense of loyalty to the VBC brands and a sense of oppositional loyalty and an adversarial view of rival brands (Muniz and Hamer 2001; Muniz and O’Guinn 2001; Thompson and Sinha, 2008).

Therefore, continuous customer participation is critical for the sustainability of a VBC (Dholakia, Bagozzi, and Pearo 2004; Kozinets et al. 2010; Nambisan and Baron 2009; Wasko and Faraj 2000). However, the wide spread of spam in social media venues like VBCs makes it extremely difficult for customers and companies to explore the value of useful information resources in VBCs (Caruana and Li, 2012; Chakraborty et al., 2016; Lau et al., 2010). Review spam may have significant consequences on the customer’s beliefs and attitudes toward certain brands (Caruana and Li, 2012; Chakraborty et al., 2016; Lau et al., 2010; Zitar and Hamdan, 2013). ## Sense of virtual community (SOVC)

In search for building the linkage from review spam to customer participation, we have found that prior literature has studied customer participation in VBCs from different theoretical views, such as use and gratification (Nambisan and Baron 2009), social influence theory (Algesheimer et al. 2005; Dholakia, Bagozzi, and Pearo 2004), and self-construal theory (Wang et al., 2015). In addition, participation in a VBC also helps a customer develop a sense of virtual community (SOVC), which has been another plausible theoretical lens to investigate future customer participation (Blanchard and Markus 2004; Burroughs and Eby 1998; Koh and Kim, 2003). We have found that SOVC reveals an underlying psychological mechanism that explains a customer’s participation in a VBC and, therefore, is a valid theoretical perspective for us to examine the impacts of external factors (review spam in the current study) on the actual participation behavior by means of the underlying psychological forces.

SOVC originates from the concept of sense of community (McMillan and Chavis, 1986), which is defined as a community member’s feeling of belonging and mattering to one another and to the group, as well as a shared faith that the member’s social needs will be met through his/her commitment to being together in the community, such as in the traditional context of a societal community. Sense of community is also viewed as the perception of similarity to other members and an acknowledged interdependence with one another in the social community (Sarason, 1974).

Koh and Kim (2003), as well as Blanchard and Markus (2004), introduce the concept of SOVC to the information systems literature. These two studies define

SOVC as an individual’s feelings of membership, influence on other members, and immersion in the interaction process in a virtual or online community. SOVC reflects an online community member’s current psychological state toward a virtual community through participative activities and interactive experiences with the virtual community over time. Prior empirical research on SOVC has identified the following variables that may influence a member’s participation: sense of membership, boundaries with others, belongingness to the community, group symbols, influences of enforcing and challenging norms, and exchanges of support among members in a community (Pliskin and Romm, 1997).

Koh and Kim (2003) propose that there are three dimensions of SOVC, namely membership, influence, and immersion. This categorization is similar to Blanchard and Markus (2004). Membership indicates that members of a virtual community experience feelings of belonging to their virtual community. It is the emotional connection that ties an individual member to other virtual community members (Obst et al., 2002). Influence refers to the influence established by a member on other members in the virtual community. Influence implies that an individual member thinks that he/she can make a difference in the virtual community by perceiving that he/she can impose influences on other community members. Immersion indicates that a virtual community participant feels a state of flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975), which is a holistic sensation experienced through interactions with other members in the virtual world.

SOVC is closely related to a member’s participation in a community and the member’s perception of the benefits from such participation (Blanchard and Markus, 2004), similar to the traditional setting of a physical community (Burroughs & Eby, 1998). A VBC member can develop SOVC based on his/her prior experience and participation in the VBC; therefore, SOVC is expected to influence the member’s future participation (Blanchard and Markus, 2004; Koh and Kim, 2003). Prior empirical studies have found that sense of belonging to a community was a strong predictor of an individual member’s intention to continuously participate in the virtual community (Lu et al. 2011) and that sense of community was also significantly associated with actual participative behavior (Yoo et al. 2002).

Review spam and SOVC

Hyper review in firm-initiated VBCs aims to promote rival products and provide distracting praises of competing brands that are not supported by the customers in the VBC (Jindal and Lu 2007, 2008). However, this type of review may result in some consequences not expected by the advocators of competing brands (Muniz and Hamer 2001; Muniz and O’Guinn 2001; Thompson and Sinha, 2008). Overpraise of rival brands do not decrease but instead increase a member’s loyalty to the target or preferred brand and the adoption of the products of the preferred brand (Thompson and Sinha, 2008). Participants in a brand community show a sense of oppositional loyalty toward rival brands because these participants have already developed a stronger commitment to the focal brand (Muniz and Hamer 2001; Muniz and O’Guinn 2001). Nyhan and Reifler (2010) define this phenomenon as “backfire effect,” which means that individuals who receive unwelcome information from rival groups may look for ways to strengthen their original opinions about their own group, rather than simply resisting rival groups because people in a focal group are more likely to develop a shared sense of identity of who they are.

This “backfire effect’ may also apply to the context of VBC. Because VBC customers are more likely to have stronger identification with the brand (Algesheime, Dholakia, and Herrmann 2005; Hickman and Ward, 2007; Muniz and Hamer 2001; Muniz and O’Guinn 2001; Thompson and Sinha, 2008), extensive review spam that praises rival brands may provoke the VBC customers to engage in an extensive information search to invalidate the positive reviews of the alternative brands and develop countering arguments against these hyper reviews (Hickman and Ward, 2007; Muniz and Hamer 2001; Muniz and O’Guinn 2001). With the increase in time and effort that a VBC member invests in such an information search, a higher level of sense of community may develop to defend their own products and brands (Nambisan and Baron 2009).

Previous studies on social identification have confirmed that a customer’s increasing participation in a social group strengthened his/her relationship and identity with the group (Dholakia, Bagozzi, and Pearo 2004; Hickman and Ward, 2007; Nambisan and Baron 2009; Thompson and Sinha, 2008; Wang et al., 2015). Similarly, a hyper review may bring VBC members a greater sense of membership toward the VBC in the protection of their own brand and the rebuke of the hyper review of alternative brands (Hickman and Ward, 2007; Thompson and Sinha, 2008; Wang et al., 2015). These customers may become more obligated to defend their own brand. Thus, a VBC member’s identification and affiliation with the VBC will be enhanced in the process of brand protection (Algesheimer et al. 2005). The more a member observes hyper reviews, the more likely the member will defend the focal brand and demonstrate hostility toward competing brands (Dholakia, Bagozzi, and Pearo 2004; Hickman and Ward, 2007). In addition, the member may exert more influence on other members to identities (Huang et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2015). Finally, the frequency and amount of time a member invests in information seeking and posting in his/her reviews of rival brands may enhance the member’s immersion in information seeking and sharing in the VBC (Jang et al. 2008; Nambisan and Baron 2009). Thus, we hypothesize

H1: Hyper review of alternative brands is positively associated with a VBC member’s perception of membership (a), influence (b), and immersion (c).

Customers of alternative brands may also spam a VBC with reviews and messages that damage or defame the focal brand advocated in the VBC. By casting negative reviews toward the target brand, defaming review has an unjust or malicious attitude geared toward damaging the reputation of the focal brand in the VBC (Jindal and Lu 2007, 2008). Prior studies have examined the impacts of negative reviews (e.g., Hickmand and Ward 2007). Maheswaran and Meyers-Levy (1990) demonstrated that negative information was more diagnostic or informative than positive information. Therefore, negative reviews could have a stronger negative influence on a customer’s evaluation of, attitude toward, and intention to purchase a brand (Cheung and Thadan 2012; Chevalier et al. 2006). Chevalier et al. (2006) found that a negative review was more powerful in decreasing product sales than a positive review was to increase sales. The daily change in the volume of negative reviews was found to have a significant impact on the daily sale of a product (Zhang, Michel, and Pieters 2009). Lee et al. (2008) found that a higher volume of negative reviews increased the likelihood for consumers who were highly involved in a product or brand to agree with the perspectives conveyed in the negative reviews contingent on the quality of these reviews.

VBC customers tend to have a positive attitude toward and a sense of loyalty to the target brand and their peer customers of that brand (Dholakia, Bagozzi, and Pearo 2004; Muniz and Hamer 2001; Muniz and O’Guinn 2001; Nambisan and Baron 2009;

Thompson and Sinha, 2008). Defaming review of the focal brand is certainly inconsistent with VBC customers’ beliefs in and attitude toward the brand and their own group (Ahluwalia et al. 2000; Muniz and Hamer 2001; Muniz and O’Guinn 2001). Once a VBC member absorbs excessive negative information, the member will strive to argue with the negative information to keep his/her psychological perceptions and attitudes consistent (Ahluwalia et al. 2000). However, too many defaming reviews will make VBC members defenseless. Their dependence on the VBC may slowly decrease after their bad experience with excessive negativity in the VBC.

Defaming review will have a negative influence on the image of a company and its brand; therefore, it is reasonable to expect that defaming review will have a negative effect on membership perceived by VBC members (Hickmand and Ward 2007). Zha et al. (2015) found that negative information reduced a member’s participation in a community, which negatively impacted the member’s identification with the VBC and his/her role in maintaining the norm of the VBC. These members may start to disagree with the community’s norms, traditions, rules, and objectives because members develop negative attitudes toward the VBC (Bickart and Schindler, 2001). Therefore, we hypothesize

H2: Defaming review of a target brand is negatively associated with a VBC member’s perception of membership (a), influence (b), and immersion (c).

One of the triggers for a customer to participate in a firm-initiated VBC is a perceived lack of information about the firm’s products and services (Nambisan and Baron 2009; Wasko and Faraj 2000), as well as the customer’s perception of the bias inherent in the commercial advertisement sponsored by the firm (Tsai et al., 2011). These users will easily change their attitudes toward the VBC if the information or rewards they receive from the VBC cannot meet their needs (Wang et al., 2015).

Abundant spam that contains irrelevant information in a firm-initiated VBC will certainly influence the efficiency of information searches and cognitive processes conducted by a customer, leading to increased information costs. Hsu and Liao (2014) confirmed that if the accessibility of information was perceived to be difficult, it would have a negative impact on SOVC. This may be applicable to a firm-initiated VBC where too much non-review spam may lead to negative attitudes.

Non-review does not contain meaningful information related to the brand in a VBC and has almost nothing to do with the targeted brand or its rivals. Too much meaningless

#

information may impede self-image congruence in a VBC because a member may feel a lack of security and self-protection in the VBC (Kressmann et al., 2006). This may lower the member’s SOVC. In addition, a member’s participating behavior in a VBC also results from his/her motive to enhance self-worth (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004). The lack of credible knowledge caused by non-review in a VBC not only leads to low or ineffective returns but also interferes with the member’s information search and sharing behaviors. The value of identification that a member aims to establish in the VBC will be lost. Therefore, the member may be less engaged in the search process. Therefore, we hypothesize

H3: Non-review is negatively associated with a VBC member’s perception of membership (a), influence (b), and immersion (c).

## SOVC and participation

SOVC is an important component of successful VBCs (Keng et al., 2015; Tsai et al., 2011). Schouten et al. (2007) noted that, compared with consumers outside a VBC, consumers who joined the VBC usually had a stronger sense of community. Carlson et al. (2008) found that the psychological SOVC had a positive influence on product preference, willingness to attend community events, word-of-mouth promotion, and celebration of brand history. Moreover, sense of community was found to increase community loyalty (Burroughs and Eby, 1998). Lim (2014) proposed that SOVC had a significant effect on a consumer’s behavioral intention to engage in online group buying. Lin (2008) and Lu et al. (2011) indicated that sense of belonging was an appropriate measure of virtual community success and could exert a notable influence on community participation. Sense of community was also a significant predictor of actual participation in virtual communities (Yoo et al. 2002). Therefore, we hypothesize

H4: A VBC member’s perception of membership (a), influence (b), and immersion (c) is positively associated with participation.

## Research Method

## Data collection

The empirical context of this study was a VBC in China. We collected data from the Xiaomi community (bbs.xiaomi.cn), which was built and supported by Xiaomi Corporation, an emerging Chinese company that manufactures and distributes data communication products, including smartphones, routers, headsets, cameras, and so on.

#

We chose the Xiaomi community for two reasons. First, the Xiaomi community is one of the most popular VBCs in China. It is consistently ranked as one of the top websites in China in terms of the volume of traffic directed to the website. There were approximately 5 million registered users when we collected data for this study. Second, the Xiaomi community publishes a wide range of objective data about its members, such as a member’s profile and the products purchased by the member. Information about all posted messages, comments, and views by a member are also publicly available. This allows us to use these objective data to measure customer participation.

The unit of analysis in this study is an individual VBC member. We collected data about an individual member at three different times. At time t-1, we collected a member’s identification number (ID) and all the reviews posted or viewed by the member. We classified these reviews to develop measures for the three types of review spam faced by an individual member in the VBC. First, we randomly chose a discussion forum that focused on a particular Xiaomi product. In a discussion forum, we randomly selected a post that contained several reviews submitted by different authors identified by IDs. We randomly chose three or four IDs in one post. We then selected another post and repeated the process. In total, we collected 400 IDs.

Second, we fed these IDs into a spider program that was programmed specifically for this study. The spider program located a member’s homepage on the Xiaomi community by means of the given ID. It then started crawling from the member’s homepage that included the member’s participation data. This is explained in the measure section. On the member’s homepage, there was also a link to all the posts submitted, commented on, and browsed by the member. The spider program followed the link to retrieve all the posts and the corresponding reviews that included review spams that were of interest in this study. In total, the spider program collected 8,263 posts and 168,228 reviews associated with these 400 IDs. Each review was identified by a member ID. On average, each member had approximately 21 posts, and each post was followed by approximately 20 reviews.

After collecting the objective data about these 400 members, we subjectively split the data into either training group or experimental group. The training group included 47 members. All the reviews identified by these 47 member IDs were used as a training sample in the text classification process explained later. There were a total of 1,178 posts followed by 23,255 reviews in the training sample. On average, there were 25 posts for each member ID, 20 reviews for each post, and 494 reviews for each member

ID. We used the rest of the 353 members as potential survey respondents for the next stage of data collection.

At time t, which was six months after we collected the objective data through the spider program, we designed an online survey to collect data about a member’s SOVC. We emailed the 353 members in the experimental group, asking them to respond to an online questionnaire. To improve the response rate, we offered 30 RMB to every respondent who successfully finished the survey. We sent two reminder emails during the survey process. Finally, we received a total of 144 valid responses and kept these responses in the sample for further analyses. The response rate was 36% (144/400).

## Measures

To measure customer participation, we used three indicators provided by the Xiaomi community (VIP level, community point, and contribution value) for two reasons. First, these three indicators were publicly available on the homepage of each community member. Therefore, we were able to use objective data, rather than subjective data collected through online survey. Second, our approach was consistent with prior studies that used similar indicators to measure VBC participation in an objective manner (e.g., Nambisan and Baron 2009; Wasko and Faraj 2005; Wang et al. 2015). Instead of replying to subjective data reported by VBC members, using objective data collected from the VBC could increase our confidence to measure actual participative behavior and minimize common method bias.

The Xiaomi community provided some background information on how these three indicators were calculated<sup>1</sup>. As shown in the footnote, the first indicator was VIP level, which was a summarized indicator of a member’s level of participation. A member could apply for a VIP status, which started at 1 if the member was qualified for being in the VIP group. The qualification was based on a member’s prior participation data. The VIP level would increase if a member contributed more to the VBC, such as posting original posts or posts that gained popularity. The second indicator was community point, which was the number of participation points earned by a member. The Xiaomi community calculated this indicator based on a formula that included the number of sign-ins, the number of posts submitted, the number of post replied, and the amount of time of being online. The third indicator was contribution value, which represented a member’s participation in terms of the completion of assigned tasks and activities in the VBC, such as testing and purchasing Xiaomi products, completing identity authentication, and submitting original posts. A member could receive a certain amount of points after the member completed some tasks assigned by Xiaomi or bought some products. The typical ranges of these three indicators are shown in Table 2. Before data analyses, we transformed the original score of each indicator to a Likert scale from 1 to 7, similar to prior studies (e.g., Chiu et al., 2006). We measured customer participation as a reflective scale with these three indicators.

To measure SOVC, we adopted the scales developed by Koh and Kim (2003). There were four items for each of the three dimensions of SOVC. Membership was measured as “I feel as if I belong to Xiaomi community,” “I feel membership in my virtual community,” “I feel as if my virtual community members are my close friends,” and “I like my virtual community members.” The four items for influence included “I am well known as a member of my virtual community,” “I feel that I control the virtual community,” “My postings on BBS are often reviewed by other members,” and “Replies to my postings appear on BBS frequently.” Immersion was measured by “I spend much time on-line in Xiaomi community,” “I spend more time than I expected navigating Xiaomi community,” and “I feel as if I am addicted to Xiaomi community.” Each scale was reflective, ranging from 1 to 7.

We developed a novel approach to measure the three types of review spam. We measured each type of review spam faced by a VBC member using a single indicator. Each type of spam was first calculated as the percentage of the total number of reviews classified as the corresponding type of review spam out of the total number of reviews (including both normal review and the three types of review spam) browsed or viewed by a member.

We adopted Textgrocery to classify a review that was identified by a member ID into one of the three types of review spam or normal review in a text classification process. Textgrocery is a short-text classification tool based on LibLinear (Fan, et al. 2008), supporting both Chinese and English. The performance of Textgrocery i satisfactory, similar to other popular text classification software such as scikit-learn (https://www.ctolib.com/TextGrocery.html).

First, we used the training sample (a total of 23255 reviews) collected by the spider program as the training dataset for text classification. We hired four research assistants and provided trainings on the purpose of the research and how to classify a review. We divided the research assistants into two groups. Two assistants in each group analyzed and classified a review as either one of the three types of review spam or a normal review. A review was not classified into multiple categories even though the review included contents that were relevant to another type of review spam or a normal review. Disagreement on the classification of a review was discussed between the two assistants, and the review was then reclassified with their consensus.

Following the commonly accepted strategy on achieving a balanced classification of the training data (Al-Stouhi and Reddy 2016), we classified approximately the same number of normal reviews and each type of review spam in the training sample, approximately 25% for each category. Therefore, we did not go through all 23,255 reviews because normal reviews accounted for more than 60 percent of all the reviews. In the end, 13,936 reviews were labeled or classified by the research assistants as one of the three types of spam or a normal review. In total, there were 3,735 (26.8%) hyper reviews, 3,930 (28.2%) defaming reviews, 2,634 (18.9%) non-reviews, and 3,637 (26.1%) normal reviews.

Next, we applied these classified reviews to Textgrocery that developed a classification model based on the training data. We then fed the reviews of the respondent group (a total of 65,531 reviews for the 144 members) to the classification model in Textgrocery. Each review in the testing dataset was classified as either a normal review or one of the three types of review spam. In total, 7,717 (11.8%) were classified as hyper review, 8,479 (12.9%) as defaming review, 8,050 (12.3%) as nonreview, and 41,285 (63%) as normal review. In addition, we also classified the reviews by the non-respondent group (353 members). A comparison of all the classifications is shown in Table 3.

After the text classification process, each review was identified by the two identifiers of member ID and review ID (hyper review, defaming review, non-review, or normal review). These results worked as the basis for us to develop a scale for each type of spam. We used a single indicator, calculated as the percentage of the total number of each type of review spam over the total number of reviews (including both normal review and the three types of review spam) to measure each type of spam. Using member ID and review ID, we were able to calculate the percentages of the three types of review spam for each member ID. We then transformed the raw score of each review spam from a percentage to a Likert scale of 1 to 7, similar to prior studies (e.g., Chiu et al., 2006). In addition, we included the three control variables of gender, age, and education, which is similar to prior studies (e.g., Lu et al. 2011; Nambisan and Baron 2009; Zha et al. 2015).

We used PLS for data analyses because PLS was more appropriate for an exploratory study like this current research (Gefen et al. 2011), where we aimed to bridge the relationship between review spam and customer participation in a VBC for the first time in the literature. ## Discussions, Implications, and Future Directions

In this study, we have explored how review spam influences customer participation in a VBC. The empirical results indicated that hyper review had positive impacts on two of the three dimensions of SOVC, which were membership (H1a) and influence (H1b). These findings were consistent with social identity theory, which suggests that individuals identify with their own groups (Lam et al., 2010). VBC members develop preferences for their own groups but biases toward other groups. Hyper reviews usually convey excessive praise of homogeneous competing brands (e.g., Huawei and Apple) of a preferred brand (e.g., Xiaomi). For members in the Xiaomi community, hyper reviews are clearly hostile causing members of Xiaomi community to adopt various strategies to improve self-esteem once their social identity is threatened. Members become more sensitive to their own membership and exert influences on their own VBC group.

In addition, defaming review was found to have negative impacts on membership (H2a) and influence (H2b). Once extreme negative information is published in a VBC, it is difficult for VBC members to digest the information and identify with the VBC, which is consistent with social identity theory (Lam et al., 2010). This may gradually reduce a member’s confidence in the group. Once faced with an overwhelming threat to his/her self-esteem, an individual’s perception of membership will be affected, which will further impact an individual’s interactions with other members in the VBC, since the individual is less confident with the group and may be less interested in influencing other members.

Similarly, the empirical findings also provided support for the negative impacts of non-review on membership (H3a) and influence (H3b). An increasing amount of meaningless information will distract a VBC member’s attention away from information processing and upset the member; therefore, the member may give up his/her relationship in the VBC, causing a negative impact on membership. In addition, when VBC members find that a VBC has too much meaningless information and they realize the gaps in the level of knowledge of different units of members, they might cease to associate with other members. Gradually, members may prefer not to be active in the VBC, causing the perception of decreasing influence.

Another interesting finding is that neither hyper review (H1c) nor defaming review (H2c) had any impact on immersion, while non-review (H3c) was the only significant antecedent. $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ of immersion was relatively low compared with those of membership and influence. This suggests that the perception of immersion was not related to the sentiment but the relevance of the review. Hoffman and Novak (1998) find that seeking information is the easiest way to make people feel immersed in the process. Too many non-reviews or meaningless information in a VBC makes it more difficult for people to find the right information, leading to a lower level of immersion experience.

Finally, our results indicated that each of the three dimensions of SOVC (H4a, H4b, and H4c) was significantly associated with participation, consistent with the prior literature (e.g., Lim 2014; Lin 2008; Lu et al. 2011; Yoo et al. 2002).

## Theoretical contributions

From a theoretical perspective, this study makes three contributions to the

#

literature. First, although there have been many studies that investigate how to monitor, identify, and filter spam (Caruana and Li 2012; Chakraborty et al. 2016; Jindal and Liu 2007, 2008; Lau et al., 2010), there are few theoretical explanations of the role of spam in VBCs. Being one of the first studies in the information systems literature to link spam to SOVC, this paper extends the current research on spam, which has primarily focused on the technical issues, and initiates a research stream that examines the social and psychological impacts of spam. This is a major advance in the research on informational spam. By connecting spam to SOVC and customer participation, we can reveal the theoretical underpinnings that explain the impacts of spam. Therefore, we have laid a foundation for future empirical research of informational spam and provided an exemplary study of how to advance the studies of spam.

Second, by dividing review spam into three categories, we have analyzed and demonstrated differential effects of review spam in VBCs. We have found that different types of review spam had different impacts, which otherwise cannot be revealed if the nature of review spam is ignored and if we do not delve into the classification of spam. Hyper review has a positive rather than a negative impact on membership and influence, unlike defaming review and non-review. This is an important finding, consistent with the prior literature, which has revealed that both loyalty and oppositional loyalty exist in a VBC (Thompson and Sinha 2008).

Third, we have conducted a robust design of the empirical study by collecting both objective data and subjective data from VBC members. We used a spider program to collect objective data to measure three types of review spam and customer participation, as well as a survey to collect VBC members’ subjective perceptions of SOVC. By adopting this combinational data collection method, we could collect data from different sources that would improve our confidence in the quality of the data and address the concern about common method bias. We have provided an example for future studies of VBCs in terms of how to collect data from multiple sources. In addition, we have developed a sophisticated measure for spam. To our best knowledge, this study is one of the first to introduce such a measure, which may be useful for future studies of spam.

## Practical implications

The findings of the study will also help VBC managers and practitioners better understand how review spam affects VBC participation. First, VBC managers should attempt primarily to control defaming reviews and non-reviews, which were negatively related to SOVC. Managers should develop and implement stronger spam filtering programs. In addition, VBC managers should also build VBC norms and rules to avoid and minimize defaming reviews from becoming rampant. VBC members should be educated constantly so that they can comply with such norms and rules. VBC administrators should be proactive in implementing spam filters to eliminate meaningless information, such as preventing the amount of advertisements.

Second, our study suggests that hyper review stimulates rather than inhibits a VBC member’s SOVC. Therefore, VBC managers may intentionally keep a certain level of hyper reviews in the VBC. They may encourage VBC members to discuss hyper reviews actively so that members are more aware of the technological advances in their competing brands. To counter the pressure from rival brands, VBC members may be actively engaged in developing innovative ideas and knowledge that will help the manufacturer of the focal brand advance its own products.

Third, given the important finding that SOVC affects customer participation, VBC managers should continuously improve the design of their VBCs by implementing various strategies to promote VBC members’ SOVC. Advocating a consistent and healthy community culture may enhance the perception of membership among community members. Managers can also provide various incentives to encourage community members to participate actively in community activities, which can improve members’ perception of influence. To build psychological bonds with users, VBC managers should encourage members to express their views in the VBC by giving certain scores and rewards to these members. New design technologies, such as virtual reality and augmented reality, should be implemented in VBC design in order to improve a VBC member’s immersion experience.

## Limitations and future research

There are several limitations in the study; therefore, caution should be taken when generalizing our findings to other contexts. First, the research findings were based on one specific VBC in China. The contextual factors, such as country, company, and brand, and the type of VBC, need to be considered when interpreting the findings and trying to extend the external validity of the study. In particular, China has its own unique culture and philosophies, such as being group-oriented and having long-term orientation and collectivism. These features are different from the western culture that

#

emphasizes individualism and independence. Culture differences may have some major impact on the generalizability of the findings, as seen in previous VBC studies (e.g., Ahn et al. 2010). The Chinese government has also established its own policies and enhanced its supervision on the operations of online communities. These features are not always evident in the communities in other countries. Further research could test the research model in other contexts or test the model by comparing and studying different VBCs from different cultures and countries (Ahn et al. 2010).

Second, the VBC members in this study were younger than those utilized in other prior studies. This might be because the Xiaomi VBC consists of mainly smartphone users who tend to be young. The users are mainly Chinese because Xiaomi has a low penetration rate among users in other countries. Future studies can examine other age populations engaged in other types of VBCs or other communities that have a mixed population from different countries. Third, the current study focused on the impacts of spam on SOVC and did not consider how spam influences other social and psychological factors that were investigated in the prior VBC literature. Further research could explore these factors, such as cognition of information and perception. Various control and monitoring mechanisms imposed by providers of the communities or government regulations could be studied in future research.

Fourth, the scales to measure the three types of review spam could be improved by adding additional parameters to each type of spam. A single indicator has its weaknesses. Future studies are suggested to use other advanced text classification techniques, such as sentiment analysis, to develop more robust and accurate multipleitem scales to measure spam. Finally, this study had a relatively small sample size compared with prior studies. The small sample most likely included loyal customers but no other customer groups. Future studies could collect more data from other groups in the validation of the research model.
