# Consultancies and capabilities in innovating with IT

E. Burton Swanson

UCLA Anderson School of Management, 110 Westwood Plaza, Cornell Hall D507, Box 951481, Los Angeles, Ca 90095-1481, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Available online 25 January 2010

Keywords: IT Innovation Diffusion Consultancies Firm capabilities Organizing vision

## a b s t r a c t

When firms innovate with information technology (IT), they frequently retain consultants, who presumably bring certain capabilities to the process. But what capabilities are these and why do they seem to be so needed? In this essay, I consider several different consultancy specializations – business strategy, technology assessment, business process improvement, systems integration, business support services – and how they facilitate an IT innovation process both within and across firms. For each specialization, I examine the consultancy’s capabilities and contributions both to the client (within an engagement) and to the broader support of the innovation (across and beyond engagements). The analysis suggests a number of conjectures as to the influence of consultancies on an IT innovation’s adoption, diffusion and eventual institutionalization.

## 1. Introduction

When firms innovate with information technology (IT), they typically seek to apply IT to achieve new capabilities. They may aim for competitive advantage or simply parity within their industry, where with rapid change and turbulence, many inevitably play catch-up (see, e.g., McAfee and Brynjolfsson, 2008). The innovations themselves may be in the firms’ products or services, or in the associated business processes, or in both, as new IT may offer opportunities throughout (Swanson, 1994). In undertaking innovation, firms also frequently retain consultants, who bring their own capabilities to the process. But what capabilities are these and why do they seem to be so needed? Why, wherever innovation with IT takes place, do consultants always seem to be present?

A traditional view is that consultancies bring expertise in the innovation itself to their engagements (Sturdy et al., 2008). In certain cases, this view may be well founded. For instance, when helping a client innovate with knowledge management, consultancies may draw from their experience in implementing such systems for themselves (Alavi and Leidner, 1999). Indeed, consultancies are commonly seen as the archetype of knowledge-intensive firms (Starbuck, 1992; Werr and Stjernberg, 2003). And they do often portray themselves as if they were in possession of ‘‘business solutions” for prospective clients. According to Sarvary (1999), ‘‘Consultants live and breathe knowledge management because they sell business solutions and knowledge itself.” (p. 97)

But how does a consultancy possess say an ERP solution for one of its prospective manufacturing clients, when it not only does not own the needed software, but has never undertaken anything of the sort for itself? Here, scholars have advanced the notion of consultancies as technology brokers, working with clients across industries, finding common problems and bringing solutions from one industry to another (Hargadon, 1998; Sarvary, 1999). Still, even if they do act as brokers, what do consultancies actually know of such solutions? Perhaps, where the expertise is derived principally from prior consulting engagements, it may be as much anchored in the nature of these engagements, as it is in the innovation itself.

In the context of innovating with IT, we note that consultants are frequently engaged by their clients as change agents and see themselves as such (Winston, 1999; Markus and Benjamin, 1996). They arguably bring a certain dynamic capability to their engagement that augments that of their client (Teece, 1998). They facilitate organizational change itself. As ‘‘outsiders” they come through an engagement to occupy what has been called a ‘‘liminal space” associated with certain rituals of transition that mark the temporality of their work (Czarniawska and Mazza, 2003). Thus, for instance, upon submission of their final report, ‘‘The consultants will leave the organization, and their product will be put to work. The organization will, at least temporarily, liquidate its liminal space; it will, again, become a working organization.” (Czarniawska and Mazza, 2003, p. 283)

At the same time, it may be helpful to consider consultancies’ contributions not only to their immediate clients, but to the process of innovating with IT more broadly. Consultancies are among those many players who do institutional work on behalf of innovations, contributing to their development, and promoting them so as to facilitate their diffusion (Lawrence and Suddaby, 2006). They have an obvious self-interest in such work (see McKenna, 2001). In particular, consultancies can be expected to both exploit and act to sustain the diffusion of an innovation in which they have established a line of practice (Anand et al., 2007). In this view, underrepresented in the literature, consultancies are not merely brokers of new technologies but active promoters of them as ‘‘business solutions” which they are equipped to mid-wife for willing clients. Consultancies arguably stake their reputations on those innovations they choose to support. Their participation in the market serves to legitimate and institutionalize the innovation itself.

Of course, consultancies must also see to their own competitive survival and distinctive capabilities, which suggest that they will likely attend very carefully to their lines of practice, engagement contracts, and propagation of their expertise, in an industry where reputation is of first importance and the risks of failure must be managed. Consultancies can be expected to build certain of their capabilities privately, before they promote them more publicly.

While consultancies have been extensively studied by scholars more broadly, particularly in the management context (Sturdy, 2004), but also in professional settings such as law and science (Robertson et al., 2003), they have received relatively less attention from information systems researchers (Jones, 2003), except in the case of software service outsourcing (e.g. Gopal et al., 2002; Ethiraj et al., 2005). While they often appear as players, even major ones, in the IS field’s many case studies, consultancies are rarely the focus of the research questions addressed. They tend to appear as shadowy figures around the periphery of the stories told, where lessons are extracted for the retaining firm’s CIO, for instance, often the researchers’ own implicit client. Many of these stories focus on implementation, where a firm and its management struggle in adopting an ERP system, for instance, with vendors and consultants cast as ‘‘outsiders” not so sympathetically portrayed (see, e.g., Willcocks and Sykes, 2000; Wagner et al., 2006). But might this bias in perspective have given us a distorted picture of consultancies and their contributions?

In the present essay, I take a closer look at consultancies and capabilities in innovating with IT. Taking a broad view of the innovation process itself, I focus on several different capability ‘‘niches” that consultancies commonly occupy as they facilitate the process both within and across firms. From this analysis, a myriad of contributions that consultancies make to innovating with IT are identified, contributions not only to individual clients, but to the broader community, or organizational field (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). This newly illuminates the sometimes competing, sometimes complementary views of consultancies as experts, brokers, change agents, and promoters.<sup>2</sup> It opens up in turn some new avenues for research.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, I draw from the foundational literature to discuss innovating with IT among firms, setting the stage. Following that, I consider the firm’s own process for innovating with IT, in its several parts. This allows us to identify several different capability niches that consultancies occupy in supporting their clients. In a separate section, the particular contributions that consultancies make to clients and to the field more broadly are then explored in-depth. A last section provides additional discussion and conclusions.

## 2. Innovating with IT among firms

Importantly, innovating with IT is both a process undertaken by the individual firm and an institutional process more broadly. At the firm level, the process has been widely studied (Swanson, 1994).<sup>3</sup> But at the institutional level, it has received comparatively little attention, a notable exception being the pioneering work of Rob Kling and colleagues (see, e.g., Kling and Iacono, 1989). Here we seek to weave the process together across the two levels, to better illuminate it and enable us to more clearly discern the roles and contributions of consultancies. We begin by briefly revisiting some familiar foundational concepts, drawn from the broader innovation literature.

As traditionally defined, an innovation is an idea, practice, or object new to the organization adopting it (Rogers, 1995). The process by which it spreads over time across an organizational population is referred to as innovation diffusion. The process typically involves more imitation than it does invention, especially among later adopters. Consultancies have been said to be heavily involved, helping through repeated engagements to ‘‘carry” the innovation from one firm to another (see, e.g., McKenna, 2001). What this actually amounts to, however, is not well understood.

The characteristic pattern of innovation diffusion in terms of cumulative adoptions over time follows the S-shaped or logistic curve (Rogers, 1995). Adoptions begin slowly, accelerate, peak, and then fall off. The ‘‘true” innovators and early adopters are commonly said to be roughly the first one-sixth of the adopters, while the early and late majorities comprise fully two-thirds, with the late adopters or ‘‘laggards” being the last one-sixth (Rogers, 1995, Chapter 7). Those who do not adopt at all go unrepresented. The S-shaped curve may be more or less steep. For any particular innovation, then, a central diffusion question is the rapidity of the process and the ultimate penetration of the population. The related question of interest here is the extent to which consultancies affect this rapidity and penetration.

Notably, the classic pattern of innovation diffusion takes a form similar to the epidemiological spread of a disease (Dodds and Watts, 2004). That is, the basic model is essentially a contagion model.<sup>4</sup> Much as a disease is communicated across say a human population, the innovation is similarly conceived to be ‘‘communicated” across an organizational population. But of course the nature of the communication is very different in the two cases, suggesting that in order to understand innovation diffusion, it is necessary to probe its underlying communication process more deeply. While consultancies may be important carriers in this regard, they are by no means the only important players beyond the adopters themselves. Where innovating with IT is concerned, IT vendors also have an important role to play, for instance. So too do mass media (Wang and Swanson, 2008).

Importantly for our purposes, innovations as concepts do not explain themselves and hence are not so easily communicated as in the simple diffusion model. Where new IT is involved, in particular, the innovation often presents uncertainties as to what it's all about, Here, the new concept is frequently identified by a buzzword and facilitated by what has been termed an organizing vision, defined as a focal community idea for applying new IT in organizations (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997). Popular examples are ERP, CRM, and Data warehouse. By focusing on the nature of organizing visions, the complexities of the communication processes supporting innovating with IT can be more readily grasped, as we shall see.

As theorized by Swanson and Ramiller (1997), an organizing vision is discursively produced within a broader institutional community, or organizational field (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). It defines the innovation in broad strokes and is the basis for its comprehension. Produced by and sustained through the community’s talk about it (e.g., at trade shows and in trade press), it provides for the innovation’s interpretation (what is it?), legitimation (why do it?), and the mobilization of entrepreneurial and market forces (in providing requisite products and services). It drives and is driven by the innovation’s adoption and diffusion. It has a characteristic ‘‘career” (in terms of its visibility, prominence, and influence over time). By studying such careers, we gain insight into the institutional aspects of innovating with IT, in particular (see, e.g., Wang and Swanson, 2007). The roles played by consultancies can also be more clearly and richly revealed.

An illustrative career is that of Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) in the 1990s, a vision first articulated by Gartner, a consultancy specializing in IT technology and market assessment (Wylie, 1990). In the mid to late 1990s, ERP achieved a high profile as reflected in the published practitioner literature (Wang, 2007). The German firm SAP became the market leader with its highly successful R/3 product offering, while Oracle and Peoplesoft were also major providers. Companies rushed to adopt ERP, in part in response to fears of the millennium bug. Major consultancies such as Anderson Consulting (now Accenture) and Deloitte grew their systems integration business as specialists in ERP, exploiting the opportunities created. Firms adopting ERP commonly paid far more for consulting services, than they did for the software license (Pollock and Williams, 2009). Excitement with ERP was however destined to wane. As the 1990s came to a close, with implementation problems widespread, ERP came to be seen by its adopters as less than a cure-all. Its high-fashion period ended. Companies began to turn their attention to newer Web-based solutions and the ERP vision receded from view in the literature, not so much rejected as relegated to the background, an idea no longer in urgent need of discussion. ERP diffusion and assimilation continued, but more slowly and without the hoopla. ERP vendors adapted to the Web and sought in particular to extend the adopter population to SMEs. Today, ERP is largely institutionalized among businesses and across industries (see, e.g., McAfee and Bryniolfsson, 2008). (For additional accounts of the ERP phenomenon, see Davenport. 1998: Pollock and Williams, 2009 and Shanks et al., 2003.)

The illustrative ERP career story serves to remind us that innovating with IT is first of all a historically situated social process that involves many players and transcends any one firm’s experience with it. It cautions us as researchers and practitioners that drawing lessons from one firm’s adoptive experience is problematic, unless the case is carefully positioned along the innovation’s broader career path. Most obviously, the ‘‘success factors” for an early adopter should be very different from those of a late adopter, for instance. More subtly, the innovation itself may be ‘‘translated” over the course of its diffusion such that later adoption is of something rather different. Relative to our interest in consultancies, it highlights for us that they too become participants in these same broader career processes. Indeed, as we shall argue, their basic engagement opportunities and the demands for their services arise from these processes. We obtain a glimpse of this from the ERP career story, where with ERP’s wide popularity, systems integrators were able to build their practices and achieve significant economies of scale, for instance.

It is against this broader background, then, that firms and their executives make their individual decisions to innovate with IT. Consider that at any one time, they commonly face a variety of new technologies being promoted through their organizing visions as solutions to their problems. They are challenged to make sense of these, as they consider how to advance their own particular interests (Ramiller and Swanson, 2003). How do they go about it? And do they innovate mindfully with IT, taking their own circumstances into careful account, or rather mindlessly, simply following the actions of others (Swanson and Ramiller, 2004)? How are consultancies engaged in the process, in particular, with what consequences? To address these questions, we delve into the firm’s own innovation process in its several parts next.

## 3. Engaging consultancies in the innovation process

The individual firm typically innovates with IT, then, largely in the context of other firms that are doing or have already done the same. It innovates too in the context of its own articulated strategy, or alternatively, its strategy may be emergent through the innovations it chooses (or declines) to undertake. The ‘‘alignment” of IT initiatives with the firm’s strategy is a subject of keen interest to practitioners. That these initiatives may (or may not) themselves be strategic is also now widely understood.

Whatever the strategic impetus, the firm typically innovates with IT through a characteristic multi-stage change process, as innovation can hardly be achieved overnight.<sup>5</sup> As described by Swanson and Ramiller (2004), the firm first engages in comprehension of a particular innovation through an organizing vision that comes to its attention. It notes which other firms have already adopted this innovation. If mindful, the firm seeks to grasp the innovation’s meaning in specific local context (‘‘Might this make sense for us?”). Should the vision be judged compelling, the firm next considers the innovation’s adoption, tailoring its business case to these same circumstances. The firm also considers its own readiness for the undertaking. Should the case be successfully made and the time judged to be right, the firm then undertakes implementation, carrying out a project to acquire and deploy the new technology, do the necessary training, and initiate operation and use. Finally, the firm must then allow for assimilation of the new IT into its everyday work, a learning process that involves appropriation of the technology by its users, and combines informal experimentation with routinization (Swanson, 2004). Only with this learning and assimilation is the firm likely over time to achieve new capabilities and benefits that justify the innovation’s adoption. In fact, as users discover new uses for the technology, reinventing it locally, these new capabilities may differ from those originally envisioned.

Again, it is important to place a single firm’s progress in innovating with a particular IT in the larger picture. Each firm’s experience with an innovation will be public to some degree, and conveyed back by boundary-spanners such as consultants into the community’s discourse and construction of the organizing vision. Further diffusion of the innovation is thereby facilitated. As adoption and implementation proceed more or less swiftly and successfully among firms, lessons learned are distilled for those that follow (or choose not to). Early on in the diffusion process, community members are on the lookout for success stories, in particular, where firms appear to achieve important benefits in support of the vision (Wang and Swanson, 2007). Too, failures or disappointments of various kinds must be addressed. Thus, where implementation gaps develop (Fichman and Kemerer, 1999), and adoptions outrun the abilities of firms to implement within the same time frame, questions may arise about the innovation’s practicality or cost. The vision may be adapted to incorporate implementation wisdom and tips.

Whatever capabilities the firm itself has to innovate with IT, as just described, it is also likely to engage one or more consultancies to support it in the task. Broadly, the kinds of consultancy capabilities needed vary according to the stages of innovation just described. In articulating a strategy that motivates innovation, the firm may engage a strategy consultancy, for instance. In comprehending new organizing visions, it may rely on technology assessments provided by IT research and analysis firms (Firth and Swanson, 2005). In choosing to adopt and in implementation, it may call upon consultancies specializing in business process improvement and systems integration. In assimilation, with implementation achieved, the firm may find itself on its own. Or, it may partner with a consultancy providing ongoing business support services. Here the consultancy becomes a part of the innovative solution itself

Five consultancy niches spanning the multi-stage innovation process are thus readily identified: (i) business strategy; (ii) technology assessment; (iii) business process improvement; (iv) systems integration; and (v) business support services. To explore further the contributions of consultancies in innovating with IT, it will be helpful to examine each niche in turn, which we undertake in the next section. Of course, in doing so, we recognize that the five niches identified are not exhaustive or even mutually exclusive as categories of consultancy work supportive of innovating with IT. Too, each has its own specializations within it. As but one example, change management is a specialized service which may be provided by systems integrators, or alternatively by a separate provider. The services offered by actual consultancies are much richer than described here, as may be grasped from a perusal of their Web pages.6 However, as will be seen, the five niches suffice to enable us to make some broad observations about the contributions of consultancies, both to their clients and to the organizational field more widely, in the particular context of innovating with IT.

## 4. The contributions of consultancies

On the face of it, consultancies through their capabilities make their contributions primarily to their clients. But where innovating with IT is involved, consultancies play a dual role. They serve both their clients and, it may be argued, the wider community engaged in furthering IT innovation diffusion and institutionalization more broadly. They become important stakeholders in this community. Their contributions in this respect are less than fully recognized in the literature. I will attempt to give them proper weight here. We consider each of the five consultancy niches in turn.

## 4.1. Business strategy

As already mentioned. firms may innovate with IT with or without an explicit business strategy that guides them in this Too, where they do engage a consultancy to help them with their strategy, this may or may not touch upon IT innovation. Still, many strategy exercises may set the stage for innovating with IT. So we should not ignore it here.

With regard to their capabilities, strategy consultancies offer a kind of diagnostic expertise that can help firms in their strategic assessments. According to Bessant and Rush (1995), ‘‘Consultants can provide a valuable input to this first stage of innovation, by creating a strategic framework for change; they can also move from identifying needs in this fashion to suggesting means whereby the identified problems can be solved. In this fashion they are analogous to a general medical practitioner whose main task is diagnosis and who then prescribes from a variety of treatments.” (p. 102)<sup>7</sup>

On the whole, where the focus is strategy, a consultancy’s capabilities probably derives more from its approaches, tools, and experience in undertaking similar assessments, than from the strategy solutions themselves, which in principal should be customized for the client, rather than drawn from some boilerplate set. Because strategy problems are typically presumed to be difficult and one of a kind, and call for ‘‘personalized” solutions, strategy consultancies are able to charge high fees for their services. They aim to ‘‘provide creative, analytically rigorous advice on high-level strategic problems by channeling individual expertise” through their own person-to-person networks (Hansen et al., 1999, p. 108).

From the viewpoint of innovating with IT, the important contributions of strategy exercises from the client’s perspective are threefold. First and foremost, they open the firm to new pursuits, which might not otherwise get a hearing. Second, they often provide the basic rationale for innovation, couching it in strategic terms. Third, they typically prepare the firm for change, as everyone understands that a reworked strategy is to be acted on.

But in their multiple engagements, strategy consultancies also make institutional contributions, beyond those to individual clients. First, they translate strategy theory into their own distinctive frameworks and normalized practices. Suddaby and Greenwood (2001) suggest that this allows for a kind of commodification: ‘‘Converting management knowledge into a routinized checklist or an easily recognizable template, such as the strategic grid of the Boston Consulting Group, the McKinsey 7-S framework or the ‘Five Forces’ template of Monitor Consulting Group, makes subsequent re-application of such knowledge by junior consultants more acceptable to clients. It provides a veneer of objectivity and universality to management knowledge, making it generalizable to a variety of local contexts and cultures.” (p. 939) Moreover, the frameworks of leading strategy consultancies diffuse beyond the engagements, finding their way into the curricula of business schools, for instance

And so strategy consultancies build their own reified tools and case experience which can not only be internally shared across engagements, but leveraged to advance their reputations as expert facilitators of strategic change. They diffuse their frameworks, normalized practice and case experience in the marketplace, contributing to the honing, sharing, and institutionalization of all of these. Most importantly, from our perspective, in doing so, they also repeatedly legitimate innovation as a strategic response.

Note that while for analytic purposes we have differentiated the contributions of strategy consultants to individual clients and to the institutionalization of the IT innovation through multiple engagements, these contributions are mutually reenforcing. Thus, for instance, the individual client may also leverage the institutional reputation of the strategy consultancy in legitimating its own move to innovate with IT.

## 4.2. Technology assessment

The firm’s strategy may thus pave the way toward its innovating with IT. Expecting this, technology champions within the firm may already have new directions and solutions in mind, ready to be advanced. As discussed above, a number of organizing visions for innovation are likely to be in discursive play in the broader community, each appealing to would-be adopters. Thus, for example, should the firm’s strategy suggest that it needs to focus more on the different value of its various customers, the organizing vision for Customer Relationship Management (CRM) might, with a little push from its proponents, commend itself to management’s attention. But what is CRM all about and where is it going and who has been successfu with it and who are the leading providers? To help answer these questions, firms often turn to IT research and analysis firms, such as Gartner or Forrester Research, who are in the business of providing such technology assessments

IT research and analysis firms provide a variety of services to their clients. They offer subscriptions to a wide range of written research reports. They hold conferences that allow for informative exchanges on topics of wide interest. They make their analysts available for a limited amount of direct consultation. They undertake special studies in the particular interests of their clients.<sup>8</sup>

The technology assessments provided by Gartner are perhaps the most widely recognized in the industry. Gartner’s most known tool is its two-by-two ‘‘Magic Quadrant” which assesses competing technology providers on the two dimensions of vision and execution. The assessments are developed from Gartner’s many interactions with both vendors and their customers (Pollock and Williams, 2009). Another Gartner tool assesses the progress of a particular technology, such as CRM, over the course of what is called its ‘‘hype cycle” (Nelson, 2001), using a graphic that portrays the rise, fall, and recovery of expectations for the technology during its evolution and diffusion. From the client’s viewpoint, the important contributions of these and comparable reports are two. First, the technology assessments facilitate the comprehension of new IT and its reception in markets. This includes the recognition of the hype phenomenon (see too Konrad and Ruef, 2007). Second, the reports facilitate the identification and evaluation of alternative providers, which is supportive of the client’s innovation adoption decisions (Firth and Swanson, 2005).

But the contributions of leading IT research and analysis firms go beyond the immediate services provided to their clients. Their technology and market assessments, in particular, become widely known in the organizational field. More than that, they fill what amount to an institutional void, synthesizing the community's diverse views, Pollock and Williams (2009) from their in-depth study of Gartner, find that, ‘‘Gartner, by enabling systematic, commodified access to community knowledge, help would-be adopters who, given the incompleteness of information and the importance of intangible issues, would otherwise be hard-placed to assess vendors and their offerings.” (p. 240)

Additionally, Gartner’s and others’ analysts can achieve substantial reputations in their specialties and may be actively engaged in promoting particular organizing visions, in both their reports and at conferences. They may thus become important plavers in legitimating a new technology (see e g. Wang and Swanson. 2007). An essential aspect of such legitimation is the reduction of uncertainty surrounding the technology’s practicality and acceptance. Through their widely-available reports, analysts address this uncertainty and interpret the market and developmental path for the new technology. Finally, leading analysts may also contribute to the hype associated with new technology, even as they point to it.

## 4.3. Business process improvement

The firm’s strategy may also suggest that to achieve new capabilities, business processes need to be revamped in certain areas. The firm’s cycle time in fulfilling customer orders may no longer be competitive, for instance, because it fails to exploit IT across the supply chain. While the firm may accordingly engage the organizing vision for supply chain management (SCM) and ask questions similar to those asked above in the case of CRM, it may also move to retain a consultancy that specializes in business process improvement, to help it move forward on this, and to determine what new IT may be needed.

As business process improvement is almost always in play in innovating with IT to achieve new capabilities, and because such improvement implies organizational change, it offers rich opportunities for consulting. Specialized consultancies contribute expertise and tools for modeling and evaluating both existing and proposed business processes (Kettinger et al., 1997; Aguilar-Savén, 2004). They enable specification of proposed new business processes and IT. They provide a business model for change, justifying adoption of new IT, whether a customized or standard off-the-shelf solution is sought. With models of both existing and proposed processes, these consultancies are also well positioned to suggest the implementation approach to be taken.<sup>9</sup> Depending on the magnitude and nature of the change proposed, for instance, implementation may need to be conducted in several steps over a substantial time period.

Much like strategy consultants, business process consultants also contribute to innovating with IT beyond their individua client engagements. More broadly, they translate knowledge of business processes into their own normalized consultative practices. They contribute in particular new modeling tools to these practices, advancing the art and their own reputations. They diffuse their practices, tools, and case experience through multiple engagements. Lastly and importantly, they legitimate new IT for business process improvement.

Prior to the ERP movement in the 1990s, in which standard software packages were embraced as means to force business process improvement, the business process reengineering (BPR) movement served the same purpose, with rather grander promises of orders of magnitude improvements, usually through custom solutions (Davenport and Short, 1990; Davenport, 1993). Today, while the heyday of BPR is long past, consultancies that focus their work on business processes, more than packaged software solutions, remain important players in the community committed to innovating with IT.

## 4.4. Systems integration

Once the firm decides to make a particular move in innovating with IT, it may call upon a consultancy to help manage the project, source pieces of the needed new technology, integrate these with existing systems, train users in advance of the system’s introduction, and complete implementation according to schedule and budget. Because the new technology must typically be fitted to existing systems other than those replaced, systems integration is a central task and aims to make the software work seamlessly together, so that users in effect interact with one system, rather than several (Rariden, 2002). While the heart of systems integration work can be highly technical, involving ‘‘middleware” to link across different software components, for instance, its concerns are also typically broader, addressing usability issues and closely related business processes, in particular. We take the broader view of systems integration work here.

Quite in contrast to strategy consultancies and IT research and analysis firms, but much like business process improvement consultancies, systems integrators provide hands-on design and implementation expertise. Given the technical requirements, they are in fact typically prepared to write code. In addition to providing for systems integration, they may develop application modules especially for the customer. Indeed, they may bid to provide complete custom-built solutions wherever packaged software is judged inadequate to the task. Among the important organizational capabilities they develop in providing software services is project management (Ethiraj et al., 2005).

For their clients, then, systems integrators provide know-how for specification and implementation of systems solutions They assist in choice of IT providers. They provide for integration with other systems. They may also support change management.

Like other consultancies, systems integrators further make institutional contributions to innovating with IT. First, they translate knowledge of IT solutions into normalized consultative practice, which in contrast to that of strategy consultancies, is highly codified (Hansen et al., 1999). Each is typically supported by an electronic repository allowing for the internal dissemination and reuse of products from previous engagements, the aim being to ‘‘provide high-quality, reliable, and fast information-systems implementation by reusing codified knowledge.” (Hansen et al., 1999, p. 108; see too Hsiao, 2008) In sum, systems integrators support the replication of solutions in whole or in part across clients, speeding and extending diffusion of the innovation in the form of ‘‘best practice.” The process is nicely illustrated in the case of ERP, where systems integrators developed configuration templates from initial engagements that they then carried over to later ones, allowing for faster and less costly but highly imitative implementations as adoptions ramped up. The institutional contribution of systems integrators in the diffusion of an IT innovation is thus quite clear. Along the way and to the extent they are successful, systems integrators also legitimate packaged software solutions such as ERP.

## 4.5. Business support services

Once the firm, in collaboration with a systems integrator, completes its implementation of whatever new systems constitute the innovation, it remains for it to assimilate it over time. This is essentially a process of organizational learning, necessary to achieving new firm capabilities. Users must gradually become familiar with the new technology, experiment with its features, develop new routines, and appropriate it into their everyday work. On the face of it, the firm is essentially on its own in accomplishing this, as to the extent consultants remain in activities mix, the firm remains dependent on them as part of the business solution.

Of course, consultancies may be very willing to be a part of the business solution, And the firm that innovates with IT may not be prepared to provide all aspects of the solution on its own. In fact, it may look to others to provide complementary ongoing IT services, in particular. In short, it may look to outsourcing. And here, consultancies may not only advise in the choice of providers, they may volunteer themselves.

Systems integrators may offer to provide ongoing technical support of a new enterprise system. They may be prepared to host the system themselves. Some may, in fact, be willing to take over the new business processes themselves (see, e.g., Glückler and Armbrüster, 2003). They may be willing to assume responsibility for the routine work of a firm’s human resources function, for instance.

In sum, as a provider of ongoing business support services, the consultancy makes several contributions to its clients. It provides for an ongoing business partnership, e.g., through a Web service or outsourced operation. It enables its client to contract in part for its new capabilities. It allows its client to leverage a longer-term relationship with a preferred consultancy.

More broadly, as a provider of certain business support services, the consultancy makes institutional contributions as well. It provides a normalized business support service to multiple clients as part of an innovation solution. It thereby enriches the market offerings in support of the innovation. It further legitimates outsourced business solutions.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

To revisit the questions asked at the outset, what capabilities do consultancies actually possess and contribute in support of innovating with IT, and why are they found seemingly everywhere in the process? What implications follow? In this concluding section, we summarize from the above analysis and offer what we hope are some fresh insights.

Not at all surprisingly, we find first of all that in their client engagements, consultancies bring various complementary capabilities that help their clients innovate with IT and thereby adapt to the ongoing challenges of doing business. Different capabilities are seen to be called for at different stages of the innovation process. Essentially, consultancies act in different roles as supportive change agents across the process (see too Birkinshaw et al., 2008). Individually, they are typically specialized in their capabilities. But collectively the work they are prepared to do may be pretty much everything that the client is not prepared or inclined to do. Otherwise put, where a client has a need, there will likely be a consultancy ready to fill it. In a similar vein, Bloomfield and Danieli (1995) suggest, ‘‘ the role of consultants can be understood in relation to the use of stratagems for making themselves indispensable to clients.” (p. 24)

But beyond their client engagements, we find too that in their many interactions in public forums and elsewhere, consultancies make important institutional contributions toward innovating with IT. They strive to achieve prominence in their associations with certain high-profile innovations in particular and to become influential players in the broader institutional field. Because they develop large stakes in the success of innovations that come to wide management attention, they join in articulating and promulgating the organization visions that attract and hold this attention (Wang and Swanson, 2008). They seek to advance and position their ‘‘thought leadership” accordingly. As a consequence, through their own commitment and lines of practice, they help to legitimate selected innovations. And so, as observed by McKenna (2001, p. 679), ‘‘In a world apparently beset by managerial fads and fashions, management consultants have become the sales force for the newly styled ‘knowledge industry’.”

In sum, in both their engagements and in their participation in the community’s discourse, consultancies bring capabilities that contribute not just to their clients, but to the development and diffusion of selected IT innovations, and thus their institutionalization, Consultancies are major plavers in the broader innovation phenomenon and should be seen as such in IS research. At present, however, beyond these generalizations, we still know relatively little about how consultancies fit into the broader scheme of things. To promote further inquiry along these lines, I venture some conjectures deserving of further study.

To first address a question raised earlier, it is conjectured that consultancies serve to increase both the rate and extent of diffusion of certain IT innovations. They likely speed the rate and extent of adoption of those innovations in which they establish a supportive line of practice, in particular. Here they may aggressively market their services and scale up their business to achieve economies, as was evidenced with ERP in the 1990s, for example. Consultancies also provide important know-how for replicable implementation. As already mentioned, in the case of ERP, in collaboration with vendors, they developed templates to facilitate the configuration process, for instance, speeding it up and reducing the effort involved for adopters willing to accept ‘‘plain vanilla” solutions. The more autonomy given to the consultancies in implementation, the less likely the solution would be customized to the client’s special situation, according to one study (Hislop, 2002).

Consultancies as intermediaries thus serve to ‘‘lubricate” the innovation process among firms, an idea that is not entirely new to the literature (Howells, 2006), although to my knowledge, it has yet to be explicated and explored as to its importance in the IT consultancy context.

But in close association with these effects, it may also be conjectured that consultancies exploit and amplify the hype and fashion associated with an IT innovation (see also, Kieser, 1997; Abrahamson and Fairchild, 1999; Ramiller, 2006; David and Strang, 2006). Importantly, consultancies can make money on bandwagons, where the demand for their services rises sharply. What is called Willie’s Principle enters in here. Asked why he robbed banks, the notorious Willie Sutton (1901–1980) is reported to have said, ‘‘That’s where the money is.” Consultancies accordingly focus their work in support of the current widely adopted and fashionable innovations. They have less to gain from the early adoption of yet-to-be-embraced innovations, where there are fewer clients to work with. Of course, consultancies will work with an important early-adopter client, with the idea of gaining a foothold in a likely new market. But, broadly, from the perspective of the consultancies, the more adopters, the merrier will be their situation.

In the light of these arguments, it may be further conjectured that consultancies contribute more to mindless, than to mindful innovation with IT (Swanson and Ramiller, 2004). They likely contribute more to the processes by which firms imitate each other, than to those by which firms differentiate themselves (see Lieberman and Asaba, 2006). As stakeholders in an innovation’s success, consultancies have a direct interest in promulgating and extending its adoption and diffusion. In their engagements, they further have a direct interest in implementations that take an approach based in their own normalized practices. They are thus more likely to bring borrowed or similar approaches and solutions to their clients, more than novel ones, consistent with the view that they act as knowledge brokers. While these approaches and solutions may sometimes be tailored for the client, they tend to be cut in common ways from common cloth.<sup>10</sup>

This said, the homogenizing influence of consultancies may largely conclude with implementation. In their traditional engagements, consultancies contribute relatively little to the assimilation of an IT innovation within and across firms. Once implementation is achieved, their traditional role as agents of change ends. Their contracts may in fact terminate. Assimilation in use is often left to the client. And while this is as it should be in most situations, it is no small matter, as it requires considerable organizational learning.

From this, we see that while consultancies may profess to bring business solutions to their clients, it commonly remains for the client to actually achieve a solution from whatever has been delivered and put into operation. The consultant’s ‘‘solution” is typically incomplete, even after implementation (Newell et al., 2001), pending assimilation, a highly sophisticated process (Armstrong and Sambamurthy, 1999; Wang, 2008). Here, the firm, left to its own devices, is likely to adapt the solution in novel ways suited to its unique situation. Users are likely to appropriate only a fraction of the functionality a new system presents to them. They may also devise new functionality on their own. The consequence is that firms may achieve very different outcomes and new capabilities from assimilating the same IT innovation (Swanson, 2004). Even when put on the same path, they are likely to differentiate themselves once again, although in new ways. To a degree, this differentiation offsets the earlier homogenization.

Nevertheless, the argument thus far suggests a further conjecture that conflicts with the popular understanding of the firm’s innovating with IT to achieve competitive advantage, in particular. The widespread engagement of consultancies in this arena, even those that advise on strategy, suggests otherwise. Broadly, consultancies that advance innovating with IT may serve the achievement of competitive parity among firms, more than they do the achievement of competitive advantage. With commodified services, consultancies may be better positioned to serve the firms that play catch-up, more than those that aim to lead

Most firms will likely tailor their strategies to the innovations with IT that promise to achieve success in their industries, often aided and abetted by consultancies. Organizing visions speak to this promise and thus carry the broader innovative process along. The threat of ‘‘creative destruction” posed by a particular innovation with IT in an industry can be highly compelling.

However, as we have seen, consultancies can also be retained to become part of the ongoing business solution. In innovating with IT, new systems may require ongoing services and infrastructure. The major consultancies have positioned themselves to be important providers of these, reflecting their tendencies toward colonization of adiacent domains (Suddaby and Greenwood, 2001). Clients for their part may prefer to retain consultancies as ongoing business partners through outsourcing contracts, rather than building up new capabilities themselves.

In the end, then, when firms innovate with IT and engage consultancies to help them in the process, they need to be particularly aware of whether the consultancies and their complementary capabilities are there for the transition, or for the duration. They will want to fashion their contracts accordingly. Whether a consultancy merely brings a business solution to the client or is to become part of the solution itself would seem to be an important question for everyone to keep uppermost in mind.

In conclusion, I have sought here to direct some new light on consultancies and their capabilities and roles in advancing IT innovation among firms. I have tried to stretch our thinking in this regard, advancing some broad conjectures with this in mind. It remains to design and execute the more pointed research studies that can inform us better along the lines suggested. The kinds of studies needed are likely to be cases of a rather different kind than those we typically see. Pollock’s and Willliam’s (2009) recent probe of the institutional role of Gartner, illuminated through a case study in which an enterprise system client plays off its relationships to both Gartner and the system vendor in seeking to hold the latter to its commitments, provides inspiration in this regard. In general the studies needed are those which are highly sensitized to an institutional view.

Some 25 years ago, DiMaggio and Powell (1983), in their classic treatise on institutional isomorphism, pointed clearly toward the importance of consultancies in the diffusion of management innovations, suggesting that ‘‘Large organizations choose from a relatively small set of consulting firms, which like Johnny Appleseeds, spread a few organizational models throughout the land.” (p. 152) Today, IT innovations are commonly among the most visible of organizational models, yet DiMaggio and Powell’s suggestion remains little explored in the IT context. Much as consultancies have since the 1990s become a recognized focus of management research, they should in the future become more of a focus of IS research.
