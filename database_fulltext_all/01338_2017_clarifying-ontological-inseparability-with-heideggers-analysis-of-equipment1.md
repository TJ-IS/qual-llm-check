# CLARIFYING ONTOLOGICAL INSEPARABILITY WITH HEIDEGGER’S ANALYSIS OF EQUIPMENT<sup>1</sup>

Kai Riemer The University of Sydney Business School, Abercrombie Building, Darlington, NSW 2006 AUSTRALIA {kai.riemer@sydney.edu.au}

Robert B. Johnston

The University of Sydney Business School, Abercrombe Building, Darlington, NSW 2006 AUSTRALIA {robert.johnston@sydney.edu.au} and Monash University, Horizons Building, Clayton, VIC 3800 AUSTRALIA

The founders of sociomateriality argue that the difficulty of maintaining distinctions between material and social entities, increasingly encountered empirically, also necessitates a commitment to inseparability at the ontological level. Yet reservations have been voiced that this proposal is contrary to experience, incoherent, and of little practical use. While not denying the phenomenon, the critics insist that the ontological commitment to separated entities must be maintained. We show that Heidegger’s work in Being and Time (1927, 1962) provides a careful exploration of ontological inseparability that reveals it to be plausible, coherent, and useful. Although his terminology is alien, Heidegger’s analysis proceeds from everyday experiences to provide a plausible account of ontological inseparability. His work provides a coherent ontology that treats inseparability as fundamental, but also shows that our familiar dualist experience of the world, far from justifying ontological separation, is actually derivative. We demonstrate the usefulness of Heidegger’s concepts with a case. Instead of proceeding from a priori separated categories meaningful to the researcher, Heidegger’s concepts allow investigation of what IT “is” when involved in particular user worlds. We conclude that IS must engage with the “being of IT” if it wants to adequately deal with the increasing ubiquity of IT in practice.

Keywords: Ontological inseparability, Heidegger, sociomateriality, IT use, ubiquitous IT, holism

## Introduction

Sociomateriality was put forward in IS and organization studies<sup>2</sup> by Orlikowski (2007) and Orlikowski and Scott (2008) in response to the observation that it is becoming increasingly difficult to frame the pervasiveness of information technology in everyday life, work and organizing using the traditional concepts of IS, such as the IT artifact, IT user, and IT task. These concepts, they argue, involve distinctions between what is material and what is social that appear, in practice, increasingly blurred and arbitrary. These authors further argue that this phenomenon of inseparability should guide empirical enquiry and theorizing, which in turn would require a radical revision of the philosophical underpinning for research in IS. To do justice to inseparability, they argue, inseparability of these entities must be taken as fundamental and ontological. This leads them to advocate an “ontology [that] is explicitly opposed to viewing the social and material as separate” (Scott and Orlikowski 2013, p. 78) in that material and social entities always co-constitute each other ontologically.

While panels at conferences (e.g., Mueller et al. 2012) and special issues in our leading journals (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. 2014) point to a growing interest in sociomateriality, reservations about this core proposition of ontological inseparability have been voiced in the literature (Faulkner and Runde 2012; Leonardi 2013; Mutch 2013) and in the IS discipline more broadly. Two broad skeptical themes recur: (1) that without the separation of IT (material) and people (social), important distinctions for carrying out fieldwork in IS are missing; and (2) that inseparability is at odds with people’s everyday dualistic experience of a world of individuated objects that are separate from themselves (see e.g., Leonardi 2013; Mutch 2013). In essence, these objections characterize the proposition of ontological inseparability as implausible, incoherent, and of little practical use in IS research. Consequently, while acknowledging the phenomenon of inseparability, some authors have argued that this phenomenon can, and indeed must, be explained from within the orthodox tradition<sup>3</sup> (Leonardi 2013; Mutch 2013). Alternative conceptualizations of sociomateriality, consistent with an ontology of separation, have been put forward as a result (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. 2014), for example, those that revive more traditional sociotechnical positions (Mutch 2013). The emergence of variants of sociomateriality with divergent ontological commitments (Jones 2014) leaves the wider IS field confused as to the significance of ontological inseparability for the broader project of IS as it struggles to come to grips with a world increasingly infused with IT.

In response, our aim in this paper is to argue, both theoretically and by empirical example, that the early work of Martin Heidegger in Being and Time (1927, 1962) provides an alternative, independent exploration of the phenomenon of inseparability (1) that reveals the notion of ontological inseparability to be plausible, coherent, and actionable, contrary to the objections; (2) that shows that these objections are grounded in the very assumptions that a commitment to ontological inseparability repudiates; and (3) that taking seriously this commitment opens new ways for IS to deal with a world of ubiquitous IT.

We begin by reviewing the arguments for ontological inseparability put forward by the proponents of sociomateriality and the objections subsequently raised. We then introduce Heidegger’s analysis of equipment. We draw on Heidegger because his project in Being and Time was explicitly to explore the ontological implications of the phenomenon of inseparability. We then employ an empirical case of a software usability field study that produced anomalous results until approached from a Heideggerian perspective, to demonstrate both the plausibility of ontological inseparability and the usefulness of Heidegger’s concepts and distinctions for carrying out analyses of field data. Finally, we reflect on the significance of ontological inseparability for IS in general.

Our paper contributes to the growing literature on sociomateriality a Heideggerian exploration of the nature and ontological implications of inseparability. To the IS literature more broadly we contribute important terminology derived from Heidegger’s early work, which we show can provide a compelling account of how IT is holistically involved in organizational practices. We further contribute a detailed case to show how Heidegger’s concepts and distinctions can be applied in practice. While sociomateriality has been criticized as “extremely theoretical” (Leonardi 2013, p. 60) we demonstrate, through Heidegger’s ideas, that the phenomenon of inseparability is already fully part of everyday practical engagement with the world, and that ontological inseparability is a matter of immediate practical importance.

## The Issue of Ontological Inseparability

We begin by distinguishing between the phenomenon of inseparability in practice and the position that this phenomenon necessitates a commitment to inseparability at the ontological level as the basis for conducting research in IS. We then discuss objections to this claim frequently voiced in the discipline. Finally, we outline our reasons for drawing on Heidegger’s analysis of equipment to clarify this position of ontological inseparability.

## From Empirical Inseparability to Ontological Inseparability

We now live in a world characterized by a “pervasive presence of technology in organizational work” (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 433). Against this backdrop, Orlikowski and her collaborators have reported a series of case studies (Orlikowski 2007; Schultze and Orlikowski 2010; Scott and Orlikowski 2014) illustrating how everyday organizing with IT challenges familiar descriptive approaches that use traditional IS categories such as the IT artifact, the IT user, and the IT task, because distinctions between material and social entities in practice appear increasingly blurred and arbitrary. For example, Orlikowski (2007), in her study of BlackBerrybased communication among managers, concludes that

viewing the professionals as ‘using’ their Black-Berrys to communicate with each other significantly overlooks how their communication practices have been substantially reconfigured through their engagement with BlackBerrys (p. 1441).

Rather than presenting as clearly delineated entities, technologies (such as the BlackBerry), social activities (such as communication), and identities (such as that of a modern manager) appear inseparable when encountered in particular organizational practices. In this article, we will apply the term empirical inseparability to the observation that IS phenomena frequently resist framing in terms of clearly delineated and fixed descriptive categories.

Significantly, the study of such encounters led Orlikowski and Scott additionally to conclude that this inseparability is not just an empirical issue but an ontological one also, in that “the social and the material are inseparable in practice and ontologically indeterminate” (Orlikowski and Scott 2016, p. 92). They argue that “the challenge for organization scholars is to figure out how to take seriously the recursive intertwining of humans and technology in practice” (Orlikowski 2007, p. 1437), requiring “perspectives that are grounded in ontological and epistemological sensibilities that take seriously the sociomateriality of organizing” (ibid, p. 1445). Consequently, sociomateriality for these authors denotes an alternative research orientation based on the proposition that empirical inseparability also necessitates a revision of commitments at the ontological level that reflects this inseparability. We will refer to such a position as ontological inseparability.

In claiming that inseparability must be taken as ontological, Orlikowski and Scott advocate a departure from established positions in IS that treat (material) technologies and the social aspects of organizing as entities separated a priori and which are founded upon what Orlikowski (2010) calls an “ontology of separateness” (p. 125). Instead they argue that

if we take [inseparability] seriously the primary unit for research is not independent objects with inherent boundaries and properties but phenomena materially enacted in practice (Orlikowski and Scott 2016, p. 92).

This necessitates adoption of “a relational ontology that presumes the social and the material are inherently inseparable” (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 456) and thus co-constitute each other. Importantly, such relationships of mutual constitution denote that material and social aspects of organizing depend on each other for their very existence, an ontological dependence which differs markedly from the causal dependencies between independent entities that are frequently at the core of theories in IS.

As to the issue of how ontological inseparability should be treated theoretically, Orlikowski and Scott (2008) have discussed a range of approaches including actor network theory (Latour 2005), mangle of practice (Pickering 1995), human–machine (re)configurations (Suchman 1987), intraactions (Introna 2007), various practice approaches (Nicolini 2012; Reckwitz 2002; Schatzki 2002), and agential realism (Barad 2003, 2007).

## Objections to Ontological Inseparability

Recently, reservations have been expressed about the coherence and usefulness of sociomateriality for IS (Faulkner and Runde 2012; Kautz and Jensen 2013; Leonardi 2013; Mutch 2013; Robey et al. 2012), reflecting reservations that are heard more broadly in the discipline (Mueller et al. 2012). While some criticism is directed specifically at Barad’s agential realism used by Orlikowski and Scott to underpin sociomateriality (Mutch 2013), the most fundamental criticism challenges the argument that empirical inseparability necessitates ontological inseparability, and thus that a radical revision of the philosophical grounding is required for IS research. That is, while empirical inseparability is acknowledged, it is argued that it can be explained from within the orthodox dualist<sup>4</sup> tradition and that this is indeed necessary as only ontological separation provides the basis for carrying out concrete social analysis (Mutch 2013). Among the various objections to ontological inseparability that are frequently voiced, two stand out as typical.

First, it has been argued that concepts cannot be operationalized properly without separation of material and social entities a priori: “interpenetration of the material and the social makes the operationalization of empirical constructs difficult” (Leonardi 2013, p. 66). Put differently, without ontological separation the causal interaction between the (material) technology and the (social) use context cannot be investigated.

Second, ontological inseparability is criticized for being at odds with peoples’ familiar experiences of the world as containing clearly separated entities. For example, Leonardi (2013) points out that

this philosophical stance presents empirical problems because actors in the world do not perceive the material and the social or the technological and the organizational as interpenetrated entities. Instead, they can relatively easily point to a hammer or a piece of software and say “this is material” but they would likely have a hard time fathoming that a hammer was in any way social (Leonardi 2013, p. 66).

Moreover, people are said to perceive the world in dualistic terms: they see themselves as subjects separate from and interacting with a world of clearly delineated objects. IS research thus must assume the same dualistic position if it wants to follow “the admonition that field researchers should strive to take the point-of-view of the actors they observe” (Leonardi 2013, p. 66).

Overall, ontological inseparability has been portrayed as an overly ontologically focused position that might make some sense philosophically but offers little of practical value to the IS researcher (Leonardi 2013; Sutton 2010). Various alternative proposals for explaining empirical inseparability have been put forward (Johri 2011; Leonardi 2013; Mueller et al. 2012) that hold on to an ontology of separateness that sees “materiality as distinct from and existing prior to the social context into which it will be placed” (Leonardi 2013, p. 69), and offer “a non-conflationary approach, in which the social and the material are held apart for the purpose of exploring their interplay” (Mutch 2013, p. 29).

To summarize, while there appears to be broad agreement about empirical inseparability, disagreement exists both in the literature and in wider discourse about whether or not empirical inseparability necessitates ontological inseparability and thus challenges the status of the IT object and user subject as foundational concepts of the discipline.

## Exploring Inseparability Using Heidegger

In this article, we will draw on the early work of Martin Heidegger, in particular his analysis of equipment in Being and Time (1927, 1962), to provide an alternative set of concepts and distinctions with which to clarify ontological inseparability and its implications for the study of IS phenomena, such as the everyday use of IT. Heidegger’s work is well suited for this project for the following reasons.

First, Heidegger’s early work proceeded explicitly from the phenomenon of inseparability to recast ontology on the basis of a careful phenomenological analysis<sup>5</sup> of the relationship between humans, material entities, and others. Importantly, he rejected the separateness of these entities as a starting point for ontology, which he argued had been the traditional error of philosophy. Rather, Heidegger took as his starting point our practical involvement in a world of social and material entities and thus inverted the traditional priority of reflection over action in philosophy. This led him to develop a systematic and comprehensive terminology that, while unfamiliar, articulates everyday phenomena that are familiar and accessible to everyone. Using his distinctions and terminology, it becomes possible to talk about our engagement in a social and material world in ways that do not succumb to the Cartesian bias of everyday language (Spinosa et al. 1997). Thus, Heidegger’s concepts allow us to make inseparability intelligible as a topic with new clarity, and they also provide a basis for analyzing empirical research data from the field in new ways, as we will show through our case example.

Second, Heidegger not only worked out a coherent ontology that treats the phenomenon of inseparability as fundamental, but also showed that our everyday experience of the world in dualist terms, far from justifying ontological separation, is actually a derivative phenomenon. This demonstrates that the apparent self-evident status of separation does not provide a forceful argument against ontological inseparability. On the contrary, Heidegger holds that all ontology is necessarily hermeneutic. This means that all ontological schemes are essentially interpretations from some standpoint and reveals the often taken-for-granted ontology of separateness in a new light, not as what the world is really like, but as a particular interpretation that privileges reflection and abstraction and seeks a view from nowhere (Nagel 1986).

Heidegger’s equipment analysis has been employed in IS and cognate disciplines previously (Budde and Züllighoven 1992; Dourish 2001; Ehn 1990; Introna 1997; Mingers 2001; Riemer and Johnston 2013; Spinosa et al. 1997; Sutton 2001; Turner 2005; Waller and Johnston 2009; Winograd 1995; Winograd and Flores 1987), and others have appropriated related concepts from Being and Time to investigate topics such as situated action (Dourish 2001), humanness (Porra 1999), management in practice (Introna 1997), practical rationality (Sandberg and Tsoukas 2011), and mood (Ciborra and Willcocks 2006). Others have drawn on his methodological contributions, such as phenomenology (Holt and Sandberg 2011; Introna and Ilharco 2003), or hermeneutics (Butler and Murphy 2007; Cole and Avison 2007; Whitley 1999). Here we apply Heidegger’s equipment analysis to explore the notion of ontological inseparability articulated in the sociomateriality research position in IS.

## Heidegger’s Analysis of Equipment

In outlining Heidegger’s analysis of equipment our primary source is Heidegger’s original work in Being and Time but we also draw on secondary sources (e.g., Blattner 2006; Dreyfus 1991; Dreyfus and Wrathall 2008; Harman 2010; Mulhall 2005; Taylor 2006). We draw on only a small part of Heidegger’s philosophical corpus. We focus mainly on Division I of Being and Time in which Heidegger provides a detailed phenomenological analysis of human engagement with a world of things and others. Because of the specific interest of this paper, we do not make use of the more existential parts of Being and Time (Division II) or his later work, such as “The Question Concerning Technology” (Heidegger 1993). We do not deny, however, that these might be useful for other IS topics.

## From Dualistic Experience to Being-in-the-World: Heidegger’s Philosophical Grounding

When we make the nature of the world and our place in it an explicit theme of our own reflection, we naturally bring to mind ourself as an individuated subject within and observing a world of independent things. Thus, in thought we tend to experience the world in dualistic and external ways, as a disembodied ego “in here” viewing an independent public universe “out there.” Because our Western culture tends to give primacy to thought and deliberation, this dualistic subject/object separation can appear entirely self-evident (Spinosa et al. 1997).

Heidegger argues that this reflective dualistic experience has been taken over by the philosophical tradition as its implicit and unquestioned starting point. This tradition originates in later Greek philosophy, but reaches its most explicit exposition with Descartes (1644). It has become known as the Cartesian worldview (for a review, see Scada 2004), even though many other authors, such as Locke (1706), Hume (1738), and Kant (1787) contributed to its development and proliferation. The wide acceptance of Cartesian dualism as an unquestioned background assumption in Western culture (Spinosa et al. 1997) underpins the commonly held belief that we can know what is really out there.

Heidegger proposes to overthrow this traditional view by beginning anew and developing ontology from a radically different grounding. While not denying that we can take a deliberative orientation to the world, he points out that for the most part we deal with the world in an absorbed, nondeliberative way, based on practiced mastery of skilled interaction with things and people. Heidegger proposes to take this phenomenon as his point of departure, which he argues is more basic than, and in fact underlies, any relation of separation of ourselves from the world that we experience in deliberation.

He coins the compound expression “being-in-the-world” to name this most basic condition of humans, which is to already be in the world prior to any experience of it as separate from them. The term is hyphenated to indicate that the “in” does not refer to a relationship of spatial containment between separate substances (res cogitans and res extensio), as Cartesian dualism views it. Rather, being-in-the-world denotes an alternative position where humans and their world are so intimately involved that they cannot exist without each other.

Adopting being-in-the-world as a new ground for ontology brings with it new questions which need new methods. The traditional question of ontology has been what kinds of entities there are in the world, answered by distinguishing and categorizing the “furniture of the world” (Bunge 1977). This makes sense in the Cartesian worldview because the existence of independent self-sufficient entities is assumed from the outset. However, when being-in-the-world is taken as the starting point, a more basic question comes into view: What does it mean to be an entity in the first place?

To tackle this fundamental question of being, Heidegger uses a detailed phenomenological analysis of everyday human engagement with the world of things and others to uncover a kind of pre-thematic background understanding that makes being-in-the-world possible, and which has been overlooked by Cartesianism. This understanding is not intellectual or explicit but rather a kind of skillful familiarity with the world that we grow into as we are raised in a particular culture or way of life and become an adult. For example, children learn to deal competently with the world in a practical way long before they have explicit beliefs about it (Spinosa et al. 1997, p. 29; see also Mingers 2001). Furthermore, it is only on the basis of this background understanding that we can encounter any entity as the entity it is. This means that the being of any entity (what it means to be; what it is) is always an interpretation on the basis of this precognitive, background understanding of being-in-the-world.

This led Heidegger (1962, p. 26), for the first time, to make the important analytical distinction between entities (what we encounter) and the being of entities (our understanding of what they are) and thus to move ontology beyond the issue of kinds of entities to ways of being of entities. As such, Heidegger distinguishes three ways of being: readiness-tohand refers to the way of being of entities when encountered during skilful, absorbed dealing; presence-at-hand refers to the way of being of objects; whereas existence is reserved exclusively to denote the way of being of humans. The following sections will elaborate these ways of being.

## Equipment and Readiness-to-Hand

The Cartesian dualist worldview, Heidegger argues, fails to account for some of the most basic and ubiquitous phenomena of being-in-the-world. One such phenomenon is that, when we engage with entities in a purely practical and nondeliberative way, they do not show up as objects of attention at all. Heidegger identifies this as the particular way of being of entities encountered in fluent use, which he calls readinessto-hand. Heidegger’s name for such entities is equipment.<sup>6</sup>

The peculiar nature of readiness-to-hand is that, when we are fully absorbed in what we are doing, we do not notice equipment at all. In fact, equipment when being most genuinely ready-to-hand during fluent activity cannot be an object of attention but must “withdraw” (Heidegger 1962, p. 99; see also Tsoukas 2011; Winograd and Flores 1987). Hence, almost paradoxically, equipment is truly encountered as what it is only when it is not experienced at all. A user engaged in writing text with a word processor will, if everything goes in the normal way, not notice the computer nor the keyboard nor the word processing program; the equipment withdraws from the user’s experience, allowing the user to be fully engaged with the ideas and production of the text. What is more, during such absorbed dealing with a task at hand, the dualistic distinction between self and external world (including others) fades; we are absorbed with the task at hand in such a way that “the self must forget itself, if lost in the world of equipment, it’s able to actually go to work and manipulate something” (Heidegger 1962, p. 405; see also Spinosa et al. 1997, p. 18).

In our everyday activity we do not encounter equipment as (a collection of) objects with properties, but rather as a practical means, or what Heidegger calls an in-order-to. When in fluent use a hammer is not encountered as a wooden shank with a metal blob but as a to-put-nails-in; a word processor is not seen as a software artifact with a set of features but encountered practically as a to-write-letters, to-capture-ideas, or to-edit-a-memo, depending on the context.

Heidegger uses this phenomenology of equipment to demonstrate that readiness-to-hand as a way of being cannot be brought under the traditional Cartesian account which assumes the existence of self-sufficient entities a priori. He shows that because equipment is what it is for, its being necessarily depends on a series of references or assignments to other parts of a whole activity context:

1. Equipment always bears for what it is on other equipment with which it is used. “Taken strictly, there ‘is’ no such thing as an equipment. To the being of any equipment there always belongs a totality of equipment, in which it can be this equipment that it is” (Heidegger 1962, p. 97). A hammer can be a hammer only in a world where there are nails, wood, workshops, and structures built from wood (see Winograd and Flores’ (1987, p. 37) notion of networks of equipment).

2. Equipment is always involved in an activity and draws its particular in-order-to from a chain of assignments to the for-which (tasks) and toward-which (goals) of the activity (Heidegger 1962, p. 116). For example, the particular being of a hammer arises from its place in such a chain of practical assignments: as an in-order-to put in nails, for joining wood, toward building a house.

3. Equipment always has a bearing on enacting a particular identity. The ultimate for-which of the chain of referential assignments is termed the for-the-sake-of-which, the bearing that equipment has on a particular human enterprise (e.g., for-the-sake-of being a carpenter) (ibid., p. 117). This for-the-sake-of-which is not simply a goal or future state but an existential identity that is ultimately possible only against established, shared, and public ways of living and understanding.

These three points show that equipment cannot be viewed as a collection of self-sufficient entities because any equipment draws its being (as an in-order-to) from a chain of practical involvements that leads ultimately to a human identity, that is, to what it is to be human.

## Dasein and Existence

What then is it to be human? Heidegger uses the term Dasein for the human kind of entity and reserves the term existence for its unique way of being. Dasein is the only entity for which its own being is an issue for it: “Only Dasein exists in the sense that the continued living of its life, as well as the form that its life will take, is something with which it must concern itself” (Mulhall 2005, p. 15). Existence is the way Dasein takes a stand on its own being in every instance of its being-in-the-world, captured in the term for-the-sake-ofwhich. To be human is to be such-and-such by doing suchand-such. For instance, a carpenter not only builds houses but is a carpenter because s/he builds houses.

This for-the-sake-of-which is central to the pre-thematic understanding that characterizes our being-in-the-world because it gives meaning, not only to ourselves, but to all entities and situations we encounter. So not only is the forthe-sake-of-which what equipment is ultimately for, it is also what makes this equipment intelligible as equipment: hammers are for driving nails, for joining wood, for making houses, for being a carpenter; but being a carpenter reveals building houses as a carpentry purpose, building houses reveals joining wood as a carpentry activity, which in turn reveals driving nails as the carpentry task at hand, which distinguishes this tool as a hammer. This tool is a hammer because it allows for a particular way of being human (as a carpenter). On the other hand, to be a carpenter requires that one do what a carpenter does, which means that human existence depends on equipment; the two co-constitute each other ontologically (see also Chia and Holt 2006).

How then can Heidegger relate the shared for-the-sake-ofwhich of Dasein to the private “I” of self-reflection? For Descartes to be an “I” was to think, to be a thinking substance, a mind. Heidegger’s insight might be summarized as “I am what I do” (Dreyfus 2007). However, what I do always also depends on others; therefore “I” cannot be a selfsufficient individual to begin with. First, we always grow up into an already shared world and become who we are by assuming certain shared for-the-sake-of-which, both in a general sense (e.g., as modern Western citizens) and in very particular ways (e.g., as members of certain professions). Second, normally we use and understand equipment in a way that anyone would use and understand equipment: it is essential to the functioning of activities such as carpentry and identities such as being a carpenter (but also to more general life practices) that they are shared and commonly understood. Hence, Dasein in its everyday being-in-the-world takes on a kind of “averageness” by doing what is normal, because “the self of everyday Dasein is the one-self” (Heidegger 1962, p. 167).

Furthermore, it is only on the background of this shared, everyday averageness that “I” am able to take a stand on my own individual being. I might interpret my being in ways that conform to or deviate from what “one” normally does and is, but in either case the average understanding of being is the ground of my self-interpretation (see Sandberg and Dall’Alba

2010). Thus, Heidegger demonstrates that, ontologically, any particular Dasein can only be an I on the basis of what one is and does. Our experience of ourselves as subjects when deliberating on our own existence in a reflective way is thus founded upon the intelligibility provided by our practical engagement with the shared social and material world, that is, our being-in-the-world.

Importantly, world in this sense is multiple. Where Cartesianism knows only one world, the universe of physical entities, Heidegger’s notion of world can refer to local worlds (Spinosa et al. 1997, p. 17) such as the business world, the world of academia, but also one’s family or society generally. Correspondingly, our identity can be multiple as “I” can be a manager, professor, mother, or modern citizen, depending on my particular involvement in these worlds.

## Objects and Presence-at-Hand

Heidegger does not deny our dualistic experience as subjects interacting with a world of separate objects. In fact, his ontology would not have currency if it were unable to make sense of this familiar experience. Yet just as any experience of “myself” as a particular inner subject is derivative of a more basic understanding of self as a shared life lived, experiencing the world reflectively as a collection of exterior objects is equally derivative of the more basic practical familiarity with ready-to-hand equipment (see Tsoukas 2010). This can be illustrated by beginning with the ready-to-hand way of being of equipment in fluent use and exploring what happens when it comes into view as an object of reflection (Dreyfus 2002).

Equipment is most genuinely ready to hand in fluent use, in which case it is fully withdrawn from experience. But if fluent use is impaired in some way, some awareness of equipment may occur. In such “breakdown situations” (Dreyfus 1991), some level of attention to equipment is required to resolve the problem. If the problem can be repaired without breaking off use, what shows up in such peripheral awareness are not properties of an object but certain aspects of the situation that relate to both the task and equipment, such as “the hammer is too heavy.” This can be seen because too heavy refers to the feel of a hammer relative to the task and user, and is therefore not a property of an object as such. Here the hammer is clearly being encountered on the basis of practical involvement in hammering. But if the situation cannot be resolved in a routine way, we must step back from performing our task to inspect the hammer. Our comportment is then “changed over” from practical involvement to an attitude of analyzing and theorizing (Heidegger 1962, p. 412) and we fully bring into view the hammer as an object and inspect its properties. But even then, such a present-at-hand hammer will not be encountered as a meaningless wooden shank with a metal blob but as an object with properties of weight and balance that still reflect its involvement in hammering.

This analysis of the breakdown of equipment shows that entities we encounter present-at-hand are not self-sufficient bundles of meaningless properties. The present-at-hand hammer of reflective inspection still draws its being as an object from our understanding of its place in the chain of practical involvements. This is best illustrated by how we have to give an explicit account of the chain of involvements when a child asks the simple question “What is this?” about a hammer. Then, in order to satisfy the child’s curiosity, we typically have to illustrate how the hammer is for hammering nails, for building walls that make a house so that the family has a home or a shelter against the rain. What is more, the particular properties of the present-at-hand object that come into view will capture what is relevant and meaningful to us against our practical understanding of the object (e.g., whether the hammer is of a suitable weight and balance). Consequently, we do not draw on its properties to recognize what the object is. Rather, the opposite happens; we understand properties in terms of the being of the object as something for something.

However, a prior breakdown of equipment is not necessary for an encounter with a present-at-hand object. We encounter present-at-hand objects all the time: when analyzing, examining, creating, repairing, strategizing about, or otherwise paying attention to the world around us. Even with equipment, we can readily change over to an analytical stance without a breakdown. Nor does the present-at-hand object in anyway stand behind the ready-to-hand equipment waiting to be revealed in breakdown. Neither way-of-being underlies the other (see Harman 2010): they both interpret in different ways our being-in-a-material-world. While we can certainly interpret a hammer as an object having properties of hardness, mass, and balance, it is important to see that the motivation and feasibility of this present-at-hand interpretation derives from our prior practical familiarity with hammers, just as much as our practical familiarity with hammers as equipment depends on these particular properties.

Very rarely do we encounter apparently meaningless objects. This is what we might experience when we encounter archaeological artifacts in a museum that are clearly designed but for which we have lost the practical context to discern what they are for, and hence what they are (e.g., Preston 1998). Then, all we are left with is inspecting this entity from a distance and taking it as an unfamiliar object with particular piecemeal properties that we pick out on the basis of our own general background, because we are unable to successfully place it within any specific practical context that we know and thus to understand what it is. This, however, is certainly not what happens in any everyday encounter with familiar objects.

Finally, Heidegger (1962, pp. 401-421) outlines as a special case the possibility of Dasein to encounter worldly things as objects of science. He demonstrates that scientific practice rests on the assumption that we encounter the world as a collection of present-at-hand objects and nothing else (ibid, p. 414). But unlike our analyzing or theorizing of present-athand objects when involved in the world practically, science assumes a distanced attitude and engages in a kind of deworlding, a special skill which attempts to lay bare objects removed from all human enterprise, with particular properties that are assumed to inhere in the present-at-hand entity itself, such as a hammer’s mass and center of gravity. However, Heidegger makes it clear that science is still grounded in being-in-the-world (ibid, p. 417) and is not a final “view from nowhere” (Nagel 1986). Rather, it employs its own practices, understandings, and equipment (Pickering 1995). Thus, science also does not discover self-sufficient substances with properties as they really are, although it may aim to do this and is commonly understood this way, not least by its practitioners.

## Summary of Heidegger’s Ontology

Heidegger presents no less than a radical reanalysis of being. In the philosophical tradition, “to be” has been taken “to be a substance” which is a self-sufficient bundle of properties, and humans have been included in this view as thinking substances (minds) separated from physical substances and interacting with them only indirectly via mental representations. However, Heidegger uses the familiar everyday phenomenon of our involvement with equipment to destabilize this view. He shows that no set of properties attached to an entity can account for our encounter with an entity as equipment. In doing so, he makes an analytical distinction between an entity (what we refer to in discourse or interact with in practice) and the being of that entity (how we take it to be). In the Cartesian tradition, these two notions had been conflated: self-sufficient properties defined both the entity (substance) and its being (substantiality).

This analytical distinction allows Heidegger to reveal multiple ways of being. When we encounter equipment in the course of practical involvement, its being is tied up with other equipment with which it is used, the activities for which it is used, and the social identity for the sake of which we use it. Each part of this involvement—equipment, activity, and identity— depends for what it is on the others, making such an involvement a holism.<sup>7</sup> The whole of our involvement with equipment and others is Heidegger’s notion of world. We are always already in and part of such a world and so we cannot step outside such a world to obtain the view from nowhere that the dualist Cartesian worldview seeks.

It is, however, possible to experience the world as though we have stepped outside it. This happens in thoughtful deliberation when we experience ourselves as individuated subjects in here contemplating an independent exterior universe of objects out there. But any such self and any such objects remain interpretations grounded in, and performed on the basis of, a more basic holistic material, social, and embodied understanding, largely tacit and unnoticed, into which we have grown or later enrolled. We can inhabit multiple such worlds on the basis of the various for-the-sakes-of-which of our nested identities, and we can encounter entities in those worlds practically as equipment or reflectively as objects, but what we encounter them as (their being) is always an interpretation on the background of our lived familiarity with—our being-in—such worlds.

Heidegger’s particular notion of interpretation should not to be confused with mental manipulation of representations within the mind of a detached observer. Heidegger takes our ability to get around in the world prior to reflection as implying that we have a precognitive, performative kind of understanding of entities and their being, which provides the basis of interpretation. So such an interpretation is not subjective; it does not reside in the head of a subject but in the shared social and material practices that make possible this getting around in the world. Equally it is not objective; what is interpreted—entities—are not things external and independent of us as observers that are somehow behind objects or equipment. For Heidegger, an entity is simply the referent of interpretation and its being is how it is interpreted. Any idea of an entity existing beyond and separate from the intelligibility provided by being-in-the-world would be simply metaphysical speculation for Heidegger. In fact, these categories of subjective and objective only arise when the Cartesian split between an observer and an external world is assumed at the outset (Cerbone 2003).

## Applying Heidegger’s Analysis of Equipment

In the next two sections we will employ an illustrative case to demonstrate how Heidegger’s analysis of equipment can be applied to the phenomenon of inseparability of the social and material aspects of IT use in everyday practices. We draw on our own problematic encounter with inseparability during a field project, and our later reinterpretation of its anomalous observations using Heidegger’s equipment analysis. As well as illustrating the concepts of the previous section, the primary aim of these case sections will be to demonstrate the clarity and coherence that Heidegger’s analysis of equipment can bring to understanding the phenomenon of inseparability and its ontological implications.

First we introduce the field project. This project aimed to elicit and compare use patterns and user perceptions of a software product across different case contexts.<sup>8</sup> In carrying out the research, the team encountered certain difficulties in conversing with interviewees about IT use and in comparing IT use patterns across cases, which put the success of the project in question and left the team despondent. We will retrospectively interpret these difficulties as evidence of empirical inseparability and note that the project team followed an orthodox research design that was founded upon the same tacit assumptions of ontological separation that are widely shared within the discipline. Thus, the unexpected difficulties, and surprising failure of the empirical phenomena to submit to the taken-for-granted worldview of the researchers, corresponds with the observations that motivated the position of ontological inseparability in the first place.

We will then reanalyze the difficulties encountered by the project team using Heidegger’s analysis of equipment. Using the resources that Heidegger provides we are able to expose the holistic structure of IT as equipment in the users’ unique world of each case and reveal the difficulties, not as methodological problems to be corrected, but as consistent with ontological inseparability. We will thus show that the phenomenon of inseparability can be both subjected to precise analysis and made plausible and coherent, provided that we are prepared to give up the assumption that software is a selfsufficient entity (a substance) defined universally and a priori and encountered in the same way by each user.

## A Failed Field Project

In this section we report on a consulting project commissioned by a German software company, Swyx (www.swyx. com). The company develops and sells a software-based telephony exchange for small businesses and a Microsoft Windows end-user software that allows making phone calls from a computer. Reacting to disappointing results from a prior laboratory test, Swyx developers wanted to learn about the use and usability of its end-user software product by eliciting perceptions from actual users in different cases through interviews carried out in their everyday work environments.

## The Swyx Software Products

SwyxWare is an integrated solution for an organization’s internal and external telephone traffic. Based on a clientserver architecture, the product replaces a traditional public branch exchange (PBX) to connect a company’s phone network to the public telephone service. The focus of our project was on the user front-end software SwyxIt!. This MS Windows client turns a PC into an advanced telephone. Users interact with this component when placing, receiving, or diverting calls. It allows users to receive multiple calls at the same time, and to merge and forward calls to colleagues. It also offers advanced features for setting up complex call routes that divert incoming calls based on caller ID or time of day. SwyxIt! allows users to assign frequently used contacts to quick-dial buttons that provide presence information through interface colors (e.g., green means a user is logged in). The software connects and interacts with different types of hardware such as headsets (headphones and microphone), handsets (receiver without dial pad), or full-featured IP telephones. With the latter, users can access a range of advanced telephony functions.

## Interviews with Users in the Field

The research team was tasked by the company with carrying out interviews with users in their everyday work environments, in order to get a view of the usage patterns and perceptions of usability of actual users. In total, we interviewed 28 users from 7 companies in different industries. Interviews were carried out in the workplace, in order to also observe users interacting with the product and to allow users to demonstrate how they used certain features. Two main issues organized the interview questions: (1) Which features of the software are used in each case and in what way? (2) What is the perceived usability of (or problems with) the different features? All interviews were tape-recorded and transcribed. Written notes were taken of observations and interactions with users, such as when users demonstrated the use of certain software features. The data were then analyzed by hand-coding transcripts according to the various software features that organized the interviews and by tabulating the results.

We intended to derive from this data common themes about software use and perceived usability. However, we encountered a number of difficulties, which meant that much of the collected interview data turned out to be of little value for the purpose of the commissioned project and was omitted from the analysis and the report at the time. The small amount of collected material that directly addressed the issue of software use and usability, the original motivation for the project, created a sense among the research team that the project had been unsuccessful.

## Difficulty 1: Comparing the Use of the Software Product Across Cases

The first difficulty we faced was that the approach of comparing use and user perceptions of the software product across multiple cases was challenged by the diversity of use patterns we encountered in the field. Quite surprisingly, in each case the company’s software product was used very differently, for very different purposes, and in conjunction with different telephony hardware, which made comparisons of a core software product across cases difficult.

This is best illustrated by the different roles the software takes on when different workplaces take different attitudes toward telephony and communication. On one end of the spectrum, City Council is a government agency in a small German country town with rather traditional communication practices. Accordingly, all of its workplaces are equipped with desktop phones that are connected to the Swyx! software. As a result, the users’ focus was mainly on the desktop phone to the extent that the software only served as a panel of quick dial buttons and for changing phone settings. Users in this organization did not accept headsets as this made them “feel like working in a call center” while desktop phones were said to serve as a “status symbol” whose size represented a user’s position in the organization. On the other end of the spectrum, Multimedia Agency had implemented SwyxWare without any traditional telephony hardware. Instead, all users wore wireless headsets for making phone calls. Here, the context was described as innovative, technology-enabled, where people were highly computer savvy. Users personalized the appearance of the software client and used advanced features. It was obvious that this company had broken with the world of traditional telephony artifacts. Other workplaces in the sample adopted yet more use patterns that fell between these extremes.

As a result of these very different uses of the software product across the cases, we found ourselves comparing apples with oranges when trying to focus on feature use, which made compiling a consolidated list of patterns of feature use for the client company difficult.

## Difficulty 2: Structuring Conversations around Features of the Software Product

The second difficulty we faced was in conversing with users about the software, its features, and feature use. Unexpectedly, this turned out to be problematic because interviewees frequently did not distinguish between separate software and hardware entities in describing their work, and often had difficulty separating out or even recognizing particular features of the software product. For example, users with physical phones had problems focusing on the software as the object of discussion and had to be reminded continually that this was the focus of the interview. The user in the following exchange, when queried about certain software features, would often instinctively refer to the phone on her table:

Q: And what if there is a call coming in when you are not at your desk? Will you look at this [missed call list]?

A: Well, you see this immediately and then you can press the button.

Q: Ah, you mean you do this on the phone?

A: Yes, exactly, on the phone, here, you can see this directly… (points to the phone).

Also, users with physical phones would often carry out dayto-day routines such as switching the call diversion or voicebox features on or off on the hardware phone instead of the software. In essence, because these users did not distinguish software and hardware, let alone particular software features, it was impossible to elicit clear perceptions of the software as a topic of conversation.

Second, those users who interacted mainly with the software frequently had difficulty recalling how they used certain features or if they used a feature at all, even though they were interacting with the software many times every day:

Q: Do you use the button or how do you transfer a call?

A: How do I transfer really? Hm. Well I go to line ... no I don’t, no, well, I do this here, say I have someone on the phone ….

Q: On line 1?

A: Then I go to line 2 and then I hang up, once I have the other one on the line. Yes, I only do this here and not here.

Q: You transfer the call via the phone?

A: Exactly.

Q: Aha. You key in the short dial of someone you want to transfer the call to? (points to the phone)

A: Exactly. I want to … for example I do have a customer externally in the line who wants to speak to Frank, then I go to line 2 (indicates the software interface), dial Frank (points to phone), talk to him and then hang up. And then he takes the call.

Q: Aha. So you key in the number via the key pad, but you don’t use the short dial button (in the software)?

A: Hm, well, that is … no, actually I do it here … Yes. This yes. But I do not use the transfer button. Yes.

Q: Yes?

A: So a combination of both.

We encountered these difficulties in most interviews and some users acknowledged explicitly that they found it hard to explain and find words for how they use the software: “Well, that’s difficult, I’m doing this for the first time, this explaining here, and all ....”

## The Difficulties as Evidence of an Encounter with Inseparability

The research team had taken into the field the tacit assumptions that (1) the software product could and should be understood as a common entity present across different use contexts, and that (2) users would encounter entities such as software, hardware, and IT tasks in the same way as the researchers did, which would allow straightforward conversations with the users about feature use.

Both assumptions were challenged by what we experienced. First, although as researchers we knew each company used the same software, the use patterns and purposes to which the software was put in each case varied so greatly that it was impossible in practice to make out any constant focal entity that could form a common basis for comparing user experiences. Second, we found that users had difficulty seeing and focusing on the entities and features that we had chosen to organize the research interviews. As a result, the research team was left despondent since these difficulties challenged the viability of the research design.

We acknowledge that the above difficulties have been recognized in IS research previously. For example, the observation that IT artifacts take on different roles in different contexts has been discussed under the term interpretive flexibility (Doherty et al. 2006; Pinch and Bijker 1987). Similarly, the observation that users have difficulty talking about their use of IT artifacts is frequently taken to underpin the manifold problems in eliciting requirements from users (Coughlan et al. 2003; Sarkkinen and Karsten 2005).

While these difficulties threatened the success of the project at the time, in view of our discussion above we are now able to reinterpret our experience as an encounter with the inseparability of entities such as IT artifact, IT task, and IT user in everyday use. In the next section we will take these difficulties, not as problematic observations to be explained, but as phenomena that can be interpreted using Heidegger’s analysis of equipment to reveal the unique nature of IT in the users’ world in each case. In doing so, we will illustrate how Heidegger’s resources allow us to trace inseparability to the ontological level. This will show that the observations present as difficulties only if interpreted from an ontology of separation, but are quite to be expected when interpreted on the basis of ontological inseparability.

## Reinterpreting the Difficulties Employing Heidegger’s Analysis of Equipment

With this new view of the outcome of the project, we revisited data that were discarded as unhelpful in the original project. We now found that those observations and interview transcripts that had been passed over in the original study actually provided the most valuable input for the current purpose. Consequently, we have recoded this data using two complementary techniques. First, using Atlas.ti qualitative coding software, those passages in the interviews that initially presented as difficulties were coded according to concepts and distinctions derived from Heidegger’s ontology to give a thematic overview of the data. Then an interpretive analysis of the case data was used to create a number of text vignettes that capture typical software use practices in the various case companies. In line with Heidegger’s project, this analysis is hermeneutic in nature (Cole and Avison 2007; Klein and Myers 1999): in order to be able to analyze each interview and synthesize this data into vignettes, we had to iteratively gain an appreciation for the users’ world as a whole for each use case while simultaneously interpreting detailed interview data and observations against that emerging context.

We now attend to the two difficulties in light of this reinterpretation of the data. For each we will briefly provide an interpretation from the orthodox standpoint that is based on an ontology of separation, before we show that, when reinterpreted on Heidegger’s ontology, each emerges as more significant than a mere difficulty.

## Reinterpreting Difficulty 1: The Uniqueness of IT as Equipment in Each User’s World

In the consulting project we went into the field expecting to elicit user perceptions of the SwyxIt! software product and compare these perceptions across contexts. However, it turned out to be surprisingly difficult in practice to discern the software as a common stable entity within and behind the variation across those different use contexts.

On an orthodox reading, this observation could be interpreted in terms of combinatorial complexity: the same software artifact has been implemented in the various cases, but it interacts with a collection of other entities (hardware, tasks, social norms, user) that influence software use such that certain features become activated or deactivated in order to adjust to the particular environment. Therefore, even while it may not be readily apparent, the same software entity is being used in each case.

However, Heidegger’s analysis of equipment offers an alternative interpretation. What we encountered was not subjects interacting with the same entity in different contexts, but different equipment in each case, as the software was implicated differently in each local world. This makes the observed phenomenon of inseparability not evidence of a methodological difficulty in isolating the common entity to compare across cases, but an indicator of a genuine ontological difference in the being of software across the cases. In the following we demonstrate the plausibility of this interpretation by presenting three vignettes that illustrate how the software has been uniquely appropriated into the work practices of different users.

Vignette 1: This frontline worker in the social security and employment division of City Council helps social security recipients find work. He also needs to make sure that they take available positions and do not receive excess benefit payments. He has to liaise frequently with other government agencies and health funds. The organization itself is conservative, traditional, and quite hierarchical. Dealing with the clients is delicate and the interviewee needs to understand each client’s situation but also needs to keep his distance and protect their privacy. He stresses several times that the Swyx solution is a means to carry out his work more effectively. In particular, he mentions the quick-dial buttons he has set up to call other agencies and to transfer calls to other workers. Most of the time he draws on the phone hardware only. Even though he acknowledges that a headset would make his work easier by allowing him to move around the office while on a call, he deems it inappropriate to appear in front of his clients in this way as it would create “social distance.” Moreover, while physical phones are regarded as a status symbol in the organization, headsets carry a stigma (“I would feel stupid”). Finally, he stresses that the hardware-software configuration is important: his office is set up so that his clients can see his computer screen. But because of privacy concerns he does not want them to see which number he is dialing or who is calling. Hence, he draws on the physical phone with the software serving only as a panel of short-dial buttons. At the same time, one feature that he deems very important, but was rarely mentioned by other users, is suppressing his own telephone number on a call-by-call basis, as he frequently needs to call recalcitrant clients who would not answer his calls otherwise.

Vignette 2: This user works as part of a three-person team, operating the customer service switchboard at Communication Technology Inc., a company that services communication infrastructures in residential high-rise buildings. She takes service calls from customers who are typically real estate management businesses reporting a service interruption. The team needs to handle about 200 calls a day. She has the software’s “group” feature activated, which allows calls to be taken in turn by the operators as they become free. When a call comes in, the Swyx software pops to the foreground of her screen. Also, based on the caller ID, preloaded customer data appears immediately in a third-party customer management system (CMS). The user then keys the customer problem description into the CMS and transfers the call to whoever needs to deal with the issue. While doing this she always wears a headset as she needs her hands free for typing. The main software feature she uses is call transfer. For this, the short dial buttons together with the availability status are an important means to select the right expert for a pending call. The most important aspect of the software is to be able “to react quickly when it’s crunch time and the calls keep coming in.” At the same time, the interviewee does not use other features that were frequently mentioned by other users, such as conference calls, which she says are more for her superiors, but “not for us.” Interestingly, the user rarely ever places phone calls herself, nor does she have a personal voicebox. In fact, the software has been configured for her in order to maintain a constant environment for interchangeability. She is thus very anxious not to customize anything or try out unknown features, in order not to “mess up” anything.

Vignette 3: This user is a consultant and executive manager with Multimedia Agency. He spends most of his office time in front of the computer and uses a headset and no physical phone. The latter is regarded as old fashioned within this organization. Rather, he operates his computer and the Swyx software with keyboard short cuts and considers himself a “computer person.” In his day-to-day business he interacts with many external clients and thus draws heavily on the integration with his Microsoft Outlook address book to initiate calls. When on a call with clients he often needs to draw on colleagues internally and thus regards the availability status feature as important to be able to get to the right people when he needs information urgently. Moreover, he uses the call recording feature on conversations that might contain legal matters. During his frequent conference calls he often makes use of the microphone mute feature to be able to do other things while still listening into the call. He also travels regularly to client sites, works from home, or spends time in meeting rooms at his office site. He praises the simplicity of setting up complex call-routing cascades that allow him to fine-tune the forwarding of incoming calls in line with his changing locations during a typical workday and to be “in charge” of his communications.

The three vignettes present very different accounts of how the software has become implicated in the practices of these three users. In the following, we employ Heidegger’s analysis of the holistic structure of being-in-the-world in order to show that in each case software is not an external object of the dualist account, but is indeed different equipment implicated uniquely in each local holistic world. We present an analysis of the vignettes, following the model set forth in Being and Time (Heidegger 1962, pp. 114-122), to carefully locate the parts of the local holism—equipment, activity, and identity— and how they co-constitute each other in practice.

In this way we demonstrate that what IT is (its being) in each case can only be grasped when one appreciates (1) how IT as equipment always depends on other equipment as part of a local setting; (2) how IT as equipment derives its in-order-to from its place in the chain of practical involvements; and (3) how IT is (partly) constitutive of the user’s professional identity. Our aim here is to elucidate the chain of involvements that forms the holistic structure of the user world described in each vignette. For descriptive convenience we will label certain parts with names that are meaningful to the researcher. Later we will consider how such parts may present to users when they encounter them present-at-hand.

First, in each case, the software depends on other equipment as part of a local setting:

1. In vignette 1, the being of the software depends on its relationship with the attached phone hardware; the selection of the desktop phone in turn depends on the social inappropriateness of the headset locally, which bears on both the nature of the client relationship and the particular workplace culture, which in turn arises from the wider context of the German bureaucratic system. At the same, time equipment bears on the office layout for its particular role in consultation situations.

2. In vignette 2, the being of the software depends on its relationship with the customer management system, which together draw their significance as a nonpersonalized work environment from the way the workplace has been pre-configured for the user by the organization and the particular role the user performs as an efficient phone operator in taking and distributing calls to other people.

3. In vignette 3, the being of the software depends on Microsoft Outlook and the user’s computer skills as well as the nature of interactions with various stakeholders, which in turn are interwoven with often changing locational contexts and the knowledge-intensive nature of this managerial work.

Second, in each case, the software draws its particular inorder-to from its place in the local chain of practical involvements:

1. In vignette 1, the software, as equipment, is an in-orderto effectively and quickly make phone calls to other council agencies, for checking a client’s payment status, for answering a client’s question, for closing a client case as quickly as possible, toward enacting effective public service practice.

2. In vignette 2, the software, as equipment, is an in-orderto quickly record a customer problem and hand over a call, for solving the customer problem, for helping the organization cope with a system outage situation, for achieving customer satisfaction, toward enacting effective systems recovery practice.

3. In vignette 3, the software, as equipment, is an in-orderto effectively coordinate call routing, for being able to receive calls when traveling, for coordinating work requirements in changing contexts, toward enacting a successful consulting practice.

Finally, in each case it becomes clear that rather than standing outside this complex of relations as a user, each interviewee can only be the professional that they are (a particular Dasein) at least partly by depending on the complex of equipment which includes software:

1. In vignette 1, the equipment is constitutive of the identity of the interviewee, who needs to care about and solve problems for his clients quickly, while at the same time maintaining a distanced relationship, for-the-sake-of being the effective, caring, yet detached public servant professional that he is.

2. In vignette 2, the equipment is an integral part of a machine-like workplace that enables interchangeability of the interviewee within a team that shares the same practice, for-the-sake-of being the disciplined, “welloiled cog” that she is as a professional switchboard operator.

3. In vignette 3, the equipment is constitutive of the interviewee’s identity, as it enables him to be “in charge” of his complex communications and travel arrangements, for-the-sake-of being the computer-savvy, alwaysconnected, road-warrior professional executive manager that he is.

Ultimately, it is these different professional identities (for-thesake-of-which) that endow meaning to both purpose (towardwhich) and task (for-which) and thus distinguish software as a certain, particular in-order-to in each local world, completing each holism.

By employing Heidegger’s analysis of the holistic structure of being-in-the-world we have located what we know as software as equipment in each of the local worlds and shown how it is implicated differently in each case. We have also evidenced the relations of co-constitution between the parts of each local holism and, in doing so, demonstrated how software as equipment is co-constitutive of the particular professional identities of its users. We note that while we have here explicated one chain of involvement for one user, in each case the software as equipment might be involved in further chains of involvement as different in-order-to, co-constituting different user identities in each organization.

Whereas in the original project the variation in software configuration, use, and purpose across the different cases presented as a difficulty in identifying a common entity around which to organize and compare results, we can now interpret this variation as what should be expected if software has become part of each unique professional world. Rather than being a constant common element standing outside different use contexts, on Heidegger’s analysis, software when fully appropriated into practice, is unique equipment in each local world. But this uniqueness of IT in practice stands in sharp contradistinction to what the IS discipline normally studies, which explains why the difficulties came as a surprise to the research team in its quest to elicit user perceptions of common features across contexts.

## Reinterpreting Difficulty 2: The Way of Being of IT in the Users’ World

Our field study found that interviewees when asked to explain software use had difficulties distinguishing between separate software and hardware entities, and that they were often unable to single out and talk about those software features.

On an orthodox reading, the difficulty would likely be located in the user entity as an epistemological problem: although both the user and the researcher are talking about the same software thing, the user lacked the facility to explicate his or her knowledge of how s/he uses the software, which makes communication difficult. Or put more strongly: users are unable to talk about the software artifact because they lack the explicit, and perhaps technical, knowledge necessary for seeing the software as it really is (an artifact with certain features). Such a view reaffirms the dualistic split between user subject and IT object.

When reinterpreted using Heidegger’s analysis of equipment, however, the difficulty can now be seen instead as an ontological problem. In our study we set out to converse with users about software thematically, as though it were a presentat-hand object available for analysis and conversation for both researcher and user in the same way. However, we have already shown that software in the users’ world is different equipment in each case. Hence, for an experienced user, software will normally be ready-to-hand, withdrawn from experience, and un-thematized during normal activity. As such, this ready-to-hand way of being of software in fluent use is not directly observable to an outsider, but we would expect it to manifest in certain characteristic difficulties during interviews.

First, interviewees would be expected to have difficulty in reflecting and talking about software and their use of it, because reflection is not (and for reasons of fluency cannot be) their normal way of engaging with equipment (Dreyfus and Dreyfus 1996; Tsoukas 2011). Second, interviewees would be expected to divide their local world in a way quite different from the decomposition into entities that researchers will bring to the interview. Since the users’ understanding of software is situated in everyday activity, what will normally show up for them are certain aspects of typical use situations for which the software is used. Finally, we would expect that, when singling out software as an object, what will show up for the user will be quite different from the object known to the researcher. Rather, they will see certain properties (features) of the software that make sense in their world—a list of features that is different from the features that make up the software entity as understood by the researchers. This is because even a present-at-hand object will be understood in terms of its place in the chain of practical involvements of the user’s local world. In revisiting the interview data we found evidence of each of these three difficulties.

First, we found interviewees had certain difficulties in reflecting on and talking about software and their use of it. We found evidence on a number of occasions that users had difficulty recalling how they used the software, to the extent that they needed to actually perform certain actions to be able to give an account of how they used certain features. We provided one such example earlier. Similarly, the following user had to actually make a phone call to find out if his software was configured to come automatically to the foreground upon an incoming call:

Yes … after hanging up it will minimize automatically, because once the call is over … or actually I don’t have the settings configured that way … once you work with it you don’t really remember it that much, it is just like that … (the phone rings again). Yes, my software opens automatically, it opens up, now I will hang up, mine opens up when I take the handle, then the client comes up. So, whenever I need the phone, (pause) so and now the client goes away … ok, only when I actually use the phone do I have the software.

Second, we found indications that interviewees divided their local world quite differently from the division used by the researchers to organize the interviews. Indeed the main difficulty we experienced and a source of constant frustration was that interviewees would provide elaborate accounts of everyday work situations in which the software played a role, but which were not focused on what we understood as “software use” or “manipulation of features.” For example, on many occasions users would provide an account of “that for which” the software was used but not the feature itself. In the following example, the availability signaling feature was not perceived as a feature at all but only in terms of some person’s availability, indicating the opacity of the software as an object and its transparency as in-order-to:

A: I often check who is online and when I want Frank for example, I check and I can see he’s talking … I don’t have to walk over.

Q: Is this important for you? The presence signaling? (points to the screen)

A: Eh, you mean the dots here? (points to the presence signaling feature on the screen) Yes, sure. Because I can see which people are here or if they are online.

Finally, when asked to single out software as an object we found evidence that the interviewees did not do so as the researchers would. On multiple occasions we found certain features we asked about had never before been noticed by the interviewees, because they did not have a place in their world. This led to surprising moments when a button or interface feature would all of a sudden show up for the user, even though on the researchers’ understanding “it must have always been there” in plain sight all along:

Q: You know that you can access the phone book via a button? That you don’t have to go via the menu bar?

A: No …

Q: Next to the Redial button, left, that’s the button.

A: Oh, I’ve never seen this …

Q: That’s the phone book. There you have it direct and don’t have to click twice, only once.

The next quote highlights a similar incident. For the interviewer it is clearly visible that a visual stimulus is being displayed in plain sight of the interviewee. Yet, she has never noticed it before:

Q: By the way, it is blinking in your caller list right now. (laughing)

A: Yes, I see it now. But for the first time really! Normally I’ve only ever used it down here.

Whereas in the original study, communication problems encountered in the interviews presented as an unexpected difficulty in eliciting user perceptions of the software product and its properties, we can now interpret these observations as traces to be expected of the different ways of being of software in the world of the researcher and the user. The researcher who makes the IT product an object of analysis picks out the software as a present-at-hand object with certain features, but in the users’ world, IT is normally encountered as ready-to-hand equipment that goes un-thematized. Moreover, when forced to reflect on it by the interview situation, users perform a categorical separation of their world based on the practical role of IT in it, which can be expected to be different from the categories used by the researcher.

## Case Conclusion and Reflection

In the two sections above we have narrated an incident from our prior research experience in which certain field observations proved anomalous and surprising. With hindsight and equipped with Heidegger’s insights we can now reflect (Alvesson and Kärreman 2007) on the source of this surprise and what its subsequent resolution revealed.

At the time, the research team took for granted that software was a separate and self-sufficient entity (a substance) whose properties were its features and its usability and that this entity was the entity encountered by researchers and users alike. This was in keeping with the orthodox research approach in IS more broadly and software usability specifically (Dumas and Redish 1999; Goodwin 1987), and was also the basis of the client company’s prior laboratory studies. However, when research was conducted on this basis in the field, where IT was already part of local work practices, it failed because both researchers and users experienced problems in separating IT as a clearly delineated entity: the research team in locating a stable software entity across cases, the users in separating out a software entity that corresponded to the entity known to the research team. Thus, our surprise derived from an encounter with the empirical inseparability of the local user worlds.

On the basis of Heidegger’s insight that ontology is always an interpretation, we were then able to see that our initial unarticulated assumption about IT—that it is a separate, universally defined entity—is a particular interpretation and not simply what the world is really like: it derives from the particular starting assumption of the dominant ontology of separation. This insight opened a space for us to make sense of the initially surprising experience with new clarity, by employing Heidegger’s holistic ontology which proceeds explicitly from the inseparability of being-in-the-world. We found that Heidegger’s notions of world, the holism structure, being-in, and ways of being were particularly useful in achieving this clarity. First, the holism structure allowed us to trace what IT is as equipment when part of each holistic world in each of our seven cases. This demonstrated that when already fully adopted and in fluent use, IT takes on different in-orders-to in different worlds and is thus different equipment in different contexts. This explained the researchers’ inability to locate the same entity across all cases. Second, we were able to show that, even when IT was encountered as a present-athand object, its properties were different for researchers and users. This demonstrated that a present-at-hand object is still not an independent entity out there, but always dependent on its involvement within a world. This in turn explained how the interview partners came to talk at cross purposes.

Our analysis has thus demonstrated that what IT is cannot be determined a priori and universally. And while it would not have occurred to us at the time to question what IT is, with hindsight we can now see that it was this question that held the key to understanding and effectively addressing the problems created by our empirical encounter with inseparability. It was only when we approached inseparability as an ontological issue that we were able to make sense of the anomalous case observations with new clarity. Drawing on Heidegger’s holistic ontology, we could then bring what IT is into the open as an issue and avoid inadvertently fixing it a priori.

## Discussion

In this section we will make good on our claim that Heidegger’s analysis of equipment renders ontological inseparability intelligible as a position with new clarity. We then return to the objections frequently voiced in the discipline and show that the apparent self-evident status of separation does not provide a forceful argument against ontological inseparability. Finally, we discuss implications of ontological inseparability for IS research: building on our case experience we argue that the question of the being of IT must be put on the IS agenda if the discipline is to adequately deal with the phenomenon of increasing ubiquity of IT in practice.

## Heidegger’s Analysis of Equipment Clarifies Ontological Inseparability

The aim of the paper was to use Heidegger’s analysis of equipment to clarify the proposition (initially articulated by the proponents of sociomateriality) that the empirical observation that the social and material are becoming more difficult to associate with separate entities, also necessitates a revision of commitments at the ontological level that reflects this inseparability. We have termed this position ontological inseparability. Specifically, we set out to show both theoretically and by case illustration that Heidegger’s concepts and distinctions reveal ontological inseparability to be (1) plausible, (2) coherent, and (3) actionable, contrary to frequently heard objections to the position. We now summarize how we have demonstrated each of these claims.

First, an ontological proposition of this kind is plausible if it does not contradict common experience. Although his terminology might be alien, Heidegger’s phenomenological approach uses a hermeneutic analysis of quite everyday experiences of practical involvement in a world of equipment and others as the basis for his holistic ontology. This shows that ontological inseparability need not be fundamentally at odds with experience, and we have illustrated this by using his ontology to analyze the everyday experiences of our interviewees with IT in both fluent use and reflection. This reveals ontological inseparability as plausible.

Second, ontological inseparability is coherent if it covers all aspect of the phenomenon without inconsistency. Heidegger, by taking the inseparability of being-in-the-world as his starting point arrived at a comprehensive and detailed ontological scheme that does justice to the holistic nature of our everyday involvement in a sociomaterial world, but importantly, also incorporates our dualist experience as subjects separated from a world of objects out there. Accordingly, in our case analysis we were able to use this scheme to reconcile the way IT was encountered as equipment as part of local holistic worlds in fluent use, with the way IT was encountered as an object when software was thematized in our interviews. This demonstrates that apparently conflicting presentations of IT in practice can be reconciled, and thus that ontological inseparability is rendered coherent by Heidegger’s ontology.

Finally, we have demonstrated the usefulness of Heidegger’s concepts, in particular his notion of world, holism structure, ways of being, and being-in, for analyzing and making sense of empirical data on phenomena of inseparability from the field. With our case, we have demonstrated how existing field data could be reanalyzed using these concepts to trace the particular being of IT as equipment in each local user world and to reveal how IT is encountered as an object differently by researchers and by users. This demonstrates that ontological inseparability can be rendered actionable on the basis of Heidegger’s ontology.

## Objections to Ontological Inseparability Reconsidered

Since the criticisms of ontological inseparability discussed earlier in the background section amount to asserting that the position is implausible, incoherent, and not actionable, we should now consider how the critics arrive at these opposing conclusions. We will argue that these objections subtly assume at the outset what they intend to prove and thus do not carry weight against the position (see Scott and Orlikowski 2013). We will then discuss how the hidden assumption of separateness at the ontological level, which informs these objections, has become pervasive in its influence.

First, when critics say that concrete analysis requires a commitment to separated social and material entities, they already presume the particular kind of reductive, causal analysis that assumes ontological separation at the outset.

However, with our case, we have demonstrated that empirical analysis on the basis of ontological inseparability is possible. This analysis took the form of tracing co-constitutive relationships between parts of a holism. It showed that there is no contradiction in analyzing holisms: by definition, they always have multiple parts that are in certain ontological relations and therefore have structure (Esfeld 2001). Heidegger has shown that such structure can be uncovered and explicated (1962, p. 413) and that, for equipment, it is a definite chain of involvements of equipment with activities and identities. Thus, we see that this objection derives from a particular view of what analysis might entail, and can be traced back to the very assumptions that ontological inseparability aims to repudiate.

Second, when critics argue that ontological inseparability is at odds with peoples’ familiar experiences of the world as containing clearly separated entities, they have in mind how people experience entities at a distance in reflection, and neglect that most of the time entities we manipulate fluently are not experienced at all. In contrast, it turned out that the users in our case found it quite difficult to assume this distanced relationship to their software, precisely because, for users to perform effectively, IT cannot normally be present as an object of reflection, but must withdraw from experience. As a result they did not readily distinguish neatly divided entities of IT hardware, software, features, and tasks in the way expected by the researchers. Heidegger’s ontology has a place for both ways we can encounter entities: as object or as equipment. It is therefore not valid to suggest that people’s everyday dualistic experience of objects is in any way an argument for a dualistic underpinning for research. It is rather that when a researcher already adopts such a dualistic position (as the project team did originally), they focus on that phenomenon, and fail to see the significance of the phenomenon of inseparability.

We conclude that both these commonly expressed objections derive their plausibility from a deeply embedded tendency to over-privilege a reflective and distancing stance toward the world. Heidegger acknowledges this stance as a possible way of being-in-the-world, but a Cartesian dualist position goes further than this by asserting that this is what the world is really like. This amounts to asserting that entities such as hardware, software, users, and tasks are substances having self-sufficient properties. Importantly, this interpretation must not be confused with the presence-at-hand way of being. According to Heidegger, presence-at-hand is one way that humans actually encounter entities on the basis of their beingin a particular world, whereas substances are a theoretical (metaphysical) abstraction.

We have to concede that such a radically distanced view of the world is the view that science typically seeks to obtain, and has developed sophisticated practices to achieve. On this view, a hammer is a collection of components of certain intrinsic mass having a certain center of mass and moment of inertia. The feel of a hammer when it is tried out is already quite remote from this view, perhaps to be recaptured as an emergent property of the joint system of such a hammer and its human user. But the lack of any feel at all of the hammer in skilled performance has now receded beyond the horizon of this distanced view of a hammer, and yet this is how the hammer is most of the time for a carpenter craftsperson. Similarly, on such a view the Swyx software is just a bitstring of zeros and ones transmitted on a compact disk to all of the case study companies. But this view of software as a virtual substance discounts a multitude of social, material, and discursive translation practices (encoding conventions, program compilation methods, hierarchical task decomposition standards, etc.) that must be maintained to support such a de-worlded view of what software is, practices that if ever lost to humanity would render the compact disk simply a curiously marked piece of material. Moreover, this radically de-worlded interpretation of software is so remote from any user’s encounter with software in the context of their holistic practices that it has no relevance to understanding what it is for them. This casts doubt on the feasibility of giving any authentic account of the phenomenon of inseparability starting from such de-worlded primitives, as some critics have suggested can be done with “a non-conflationary approach” (Mutch 2013, p. 29).

## Implications for IS: Putting the Being of IT on the Agenda

The traditional understanding of IT in substantive terms has rendered the being of IT a non-question for IS. On this interpretation, the issue of what IT is appears completely settled: IT is a collection of discrete, self-sufficient entities defined by intrinsic properties that can be clearly separated into objective categories such as hardware and software independent of, and equally separated from, human use-practices and the user. We note that this view underpins the widely accepted view that the IT artifact is the core entity for research in IS (see Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Pentland and Feldman 2008), as well as how the discipline approaches many of its research topics, such as IT implementation (Markus 1983; Swanson 1974); the acceptance of IT artifacts by users (Davis 1989; Davis and Bagozzi 1989); software selection in term of fit between characteristics of IT user, IT task, and IT artifact (Goodhue and Thompson 1995); and the impact of IT on business success (DeLone and McLean 1992).

By contrast, our analysis shows how leaving the being of IT as an open question to be answered in the course of IS research can make possible new insights into emerging phenomena. As IT becomes ubiquitous in significant parts of our daily personal and work lives (Yoo 2010), IT will increasingly be ready-to-hand equipment much of the time for most people. While encountering IT as an object of reflection will still occur—when equipment malfunctions, when learning to use a new system, or when making decisions about IT—these present-at-hand encounters with IT will no longer serve as a model for what IT intrinsically is. Both possible ways that IT can “be” in practice—object of reflection or equipment in fluent use—will need to be acknowledged by IS researchers who wish to investigate authentically how people encounter IT in their local worlds.

Our case experience shows how unreflectively parsing such worlds into discrete IT user, IT artifact, or IT task entities will increasingly fail to produce an adequate account of what it is like to work, manage, or live with IT. On the one hand, it will fail to capture the difference between using IT and involvement with IT. Many people today have grown up as “digital natives” (Wang et al. 2013) always and already involved with IT existentially. For an entire generation the separation of self from IT, as captured in user and use, no longer makes any sense at all. On the other hand, work today can no longer be readily parsed into discrete IT tasks and IT artifacts (as opposed to non-IT tasks and artifacts) as much of today’s work is inexorably underpinned and constituted by IT: IT is simply the milieu amid which work takes place. Thus, increasingly there is a mismatch between the conventional categories we have carried into the field in the past as IS researchers, and the emerging phenomenon where the referents of these categories are holistically constituted as the worlds of the people we study.

Consequently, there is now an important open question about what are the appropriate conceptual underpinnings that IS researchers should draw upon to study IT as it is encountered in situ. But this is exactly the question that the founders of the sociomateriality orientation to IS research have voiced, namely, what are the foundational ontological implications of the inseparability phenomenon that increasingly characterizes our research domain? We should not dismiss this question simply on the grounds that it is unfamiliar or does not make sense on the dominant, orthodox view of separation. This would amount to shutting down debate in the field about how to respond to the being of IT in the practice domain under study, just when that domain is experiencing unprecedented new challenges from the increasing ubiquity of IT.

## Conclusion

Our aim was to show that Heidegger’s analysis of equipment from Being and Time can be used to clarify the core claim of sociomateriality: that the entanglement of social and material aspects of IT use phenomena necessitates a revision of commitments at the ontological level reflecting this inseparability. With concepts and distinctions furnished by Heidegger’s ontology, and our reflexive reanalysis of a failed field project, we have revealed this proposal of ontological inseparability to be plausible, coherent, and actionable, contrary to the objections frequently voiced in the discipline.

We thus contribute to the literature on sociomateriality a clarification of its core claim of ontological inseparability in a new way. Heidegger’s analysis of equipment makes it clear that understanding ontological inseparability requires questioning the taken-for-granted and largely unacknowledged assumption that IT is a substance with properties and features. Rather, what IT is—its being—is a function of our involvement with it, and derives in each case from its place in a holistic world of equipment, activities, and identities.

We further contribute a reevaluation of certain widely voiced objections to the ontological inseparability claim of sociomateriality. Heidegger’s insight that every ontology is an interpretation from some standpoint reveals that the a priori separateness of the social and material entities, which is frequently argued to be fundamental and in need of preservation, is actually a particular, albeit widely held, interpretation that privileges reflection over action. Once it is acknowledged that ontological separateness is tacitly assumed at the base of these objections, they can have no force as arguments against the plausibility and coherence of ontological inseparability.

To IS theory more broadly, we contribute an accessible presentation of important concepts from Heidegger’s analysis of equipment in Being and Time, most notably being-in-theworld, being-in, ways-of-being, and his holistic notion of world. While some parts of his work are not new to IS, in many cases they have been introduced piecemeal out of the context of his whole scheme, which encourages certain dualist and cognitivist readings at odds with the original work.

To IS research practice, we contribute a detailed case that demonstrates how Heidegger’s distinctions and concepts can be applied. As well as illustrating Heidegger’s concepts, it provides novel methodological insights. We demonstrate how the particular being of IT in a local world can be investigated by tracing the chain of co-constitutive relationships that make up its holism. As a result, a researcher must be attuned to the particular being of IT when it has become equipment in a particular local world. This suggests that IS research must be open to investigating the situatedness and uniqueness of IT in particular practices, rather than always seeking abstraction and generalization across cases as an aim.

Finally, we note that while Heidegger’s language is alien, his work proceeds from, and seeks to give an account of, quite familiar phenomena such as the withdrawal of equipment in use. Our experience is that many of his insights are tacitly familiar to practitioners and are easily communicated. We note that the ability to communicate with practitioners about everyday phenomena of inseparability is a prerequisite for deriving concrete practical implications of sociomateriality (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. 2014).

As IT becomes a ubiquitous part of our everyday life and indeed our collective identities, IS researchers will increasingly encounter inseparability in the field. It is thus urgent for IS to draw from the phenomenon of inseparability its deepest conceptual and methodological implications. This is the project of sociomateriality as formulated by its originators. We have shown that Heidegger’s work gains new relevance in this context as it offers to IS important terminology and distinctions to deal with these emerging phenomena in authentic ways. His work further reminds us that it is important for any discipline to stay attuned to changes in its main phenomena, and to revisit tacitly held assumptions that underpin established ways of doing research. In sum, we argue that taking inseparability seriously will disclose new ways forward for IS research, avenues that are not available in principle to the orthodox position of separation, or any reformulations of sociomateriality on that basis.

## About the Authors

Kai Riemer is professor of Information Technology and Organisation in the Discipline of Business Information Systems at the University of Sydney Business School. Kai has held positions with Münster University, Germany, University College Dublin, Ireland, and the University of Melbourne, Australia. Kai’s expertise and research interests cover the areas of social networking, technology appropriation, collaborative technology, virtual work, digital disruption, and the philosophy of technology. His research follows practice theoretical and non-orthodox approaches and has appeared in journals such as the European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of Information Technology, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, and Communications of the Association for Information Systems. Kai is a board member of the Journal of Information Technology, Electronic Markets, and the Business and Information Systems Engineering Journal.

Robert Johnston is professor of Business information Systems at the University of Sydney Business School, an adjunct professor at Monash University, and Professor Emeritus at University College Dublin. His main research areas are electronic commerce, supply chain management, interorganizational information systems, and theoretical foundations of information systems. He has over 150 refereed publications, many in leading international journals, including Information Systems Research, Management Science, European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of Stategic Information Systems, Communications of the ACM, Communications of the AIS, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Elecronic Markets, OMEGA, Journal of the Operational Research Society, International Journal of Production Economics, and Supply Chain Management. Prior to becoming an academic he spent 13 years as an IT practitioner in Australia.
