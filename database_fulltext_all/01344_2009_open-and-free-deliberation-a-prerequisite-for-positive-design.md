# Open and free deliberation: A prerequisite for positive design

Zaheeruddin Asif <sup>a,\*</sup>, Heinz K. Klein <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Fox School of Business and Management, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA, USA <sup>b</sup> School of Management, SUNY Binghamton and Salford Business School, UK

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 28 March 2006 Received in revised form 25 September 2008 Accepted 7 April 2009

Keywords: Organization design Positive design Appreciative Inquiry Deliberative Inquiry Habermas Theory of Communicative Action

## a b s t r a c t

Success in designing organizations and management with a positive lens will require multiple forms of positive inquiry. We introduce the model of Deliberative Inquiry based on Habermas’ Theory of Communicative Action as a way to create an additional form of positive inquiry. We argue that Deliberative Inquiry and Appreciative Inquiry naturally complement and support each other in providing a suitable approach to positive design. Whereas the flagship contribution of Appreciative Inquiry is a breakaway from deficitoriented thinking and a focus on life-generating forces, Deliberative Inquiry’s hallmark is the elimination of artificially imposed constraints through rational discourse. Appreciative Inquiry seeks strengths from positive emotions, while Deliberative Inquiry employs positive critique to unmask barriers to change and arrive at morally, ethically and pragmatically sound decisions by mutua and authentic agreement. We conclude by raising some guiding questions for the possibility of providing computer support for such inquiring systems.

## 1. Introduction

The above quote from Pericles’ Funeral Oration highlights some of the qualities that supposedly distinguished Athens from its neighbors and contributed to its rise to power during its Golden Age between the Persian and Peloponnesian Wars (ending with Athens’ surrender in 404 BC). One of the qualities highlighted is Athens’ ability to sustain deliberative inquiry leading to knowledge ‘‘which i gained by discussion preparatory to action.” Pericles significant insight is that discussion or deliberation is a key to knowledge that facilitates effective action. By its very nature, discussion is dialogical requiring at least two participants and from the nature of Athenian democracy at Pericles’ times, we know that much of it was public.

We argue that designing organizations and management with a positive lens requires the enabling of positive forms of inquiry that are open-ended, appreciative, relatively free from social controls and carried out in the spirit of open-mindedness, mutual respect and understanding. This conjectured linkage between design and inquiry is based on the fundamental insight of Churchman’s The Design of Inquiring Systems (1971). In order to improve design we must improve upon the way we inquire into the design problem and the potential approaches to its resolution.

Building on these historical insights, this paper aims to clarify two points. First, it defines and strengthens the concept of Deliberative Inquiry as part and parcel of a successful approach to positive design. For this purpose, the paper draws on recent literature in social action theory and its application to the theory of democracy. Secondly, it elaborates the essential linkages between Deliberative Inquiry, Appreciative Inquiry and positive design. We demonstrate that the model of Deliberative Inquiry complements the approach of Appreciative Inquiry, which applies a positive lens to organization design. With its close relationship to positive psychology, Appreciative Inquiry accentu ates the importance of positive feelings, emotions and attitudes for bringing about a positive change. Deliberative Inquiry on the other hand, emphasizes the importance of open, free and rationally guided communications as a basis of arriving at pragmatically sound decisions that can propel the process of positive change. We argue that Deliberative Inquiry, as practiced through open discourse, currently provides the most preferred baseline for positive inquiry. Deliberative Inquiry sees critique as a constructive and essential component of positive inquiry. Therefore, if our argument is valid, constructive critique should also have an important role in positive design, if it is employed at appropriate phases in the design process.

The rest of the paper is organized into three sections. Section 2 introduces positive design and summarizes the Appreciative Inquiry model based on Cooperrider and Srivastva (1987). Section 3 outlines the model of Deliberative Inquiry. After a brief review of some prominent models of democracy, it focuses primarily on selective aspects of Habermas’ theory of the public sphere as developed over time in The Transformation of the Public Sphere (1962), and Between Facts and Norms (1996). Section 4 clarifies the relationship between Deliberative Inquiry and Appreciative Inquiry, and explains how the two concepts can support and strengthen each other. After briefly recapitulating the main contri butions of this paper, our conclusions observe that earlier approaches to designing computer support for Deliberative Inquiry have not received the attention that they might have deserved. To address the issue, we formulated three priorities for future research

## 2. Positive design and appreciative inquiry

Design is one of the core activities in the disciplines of engineering, organization studies, information systems, and management. It produces artifacts, systems, and practices which are appropriated by users to fulfill their goals. The quality of design determines the extent to which these goals are fulfilled. Design sciences are differentiated from natural sciences in that the latter aim to describe, explain and predict existing natural phenomena, while the former aim to create entities and phenomena that do not yet exist (Simon, 1981). Creating new objects and entities requires not only an understanding of how the world works, but also a vision of potential new worlds. While design activity may benefit from structured methods and scientific principles. better designs invariably involve flair, creativity. intuitior and spontaneity (Eder, 1998). Designing therefore calls for a confluence of science and art. Artful design is not a straightforward linear process, rather it is non-linear, recursive, iterative and heuristic (Koen, 1985). Teamwork and collaboration among the participants is of paramount importance, as designing does not take place in isolation. Rather it is engulfed by organizational discourse and is affected by worldviews, attitudes and understandings of the designers and users.

When these worldviews, attitudes and understandings are optimistic, affirmative and constructive they produce positive designs. The positive lens makes designing more collaborative and participatory. Rather than specifying fixed and finite designs, it creates a space where the users are invited to participate in an ongoing dialog for designing and creating positive and hopeful organizations (Yoo, 2004). Positive design seeks value and synthesis, and is grounded in the lived experiences of the users, rather than being unattached, uninterested, impersonal and averse to ethical controversies (Avital et al., 2006). The positive lens enhances attention to social issues, and brings ethical considerations to the forefront, which makes design and designing more organic, malleable and robust. Being more socially and ethically conscious, positive design is better able to navigate the usual workplace tensions and conflicts. In short, positive design is concerned with creating innovative, human-centered and sustainable organizations in a collaborative fashion (Romme, 2005). However, designing can not be independent of the designers, whose interests. biases and dispositions influence design. Therefore these personal traits must be accounted for by any positive approach to design.

Appreciative Inquiry is an approach to organization change and development that has been employed for designing organizations and information systems in a positive way. It is based on the philosophy of social constructionism and represents a mindset different from the traditional problem-solving one. The traditional mindset focuses on finding faults and fixing them up. Appreciative Inquiry on the other hand focuses on what is positive and gives life and energy to a system. Appreciative Inquiry is a ‘‘way of thinking, seeing and acting for powerful, purposeful change in organizations” (Hall & Hammond, 2004).

## 2.1. Strengths of appreciative inquiry

Appreciative Inquiry breaks away from the deficit-oriented mode of thinking. This pervasive approach can easily create a negative culture of blame and put people on the defensive (Finegold, Holland, & Lingham, 2002, p. 236), which in turn is likely to put brakes on organizational learning (Barrett, 1995). In contrast, Appreciative Inquiry urges us to look at the strengths, core values, and highest achievements and aspirations present in all human organizations. These positive dimensions inspire us to strive for the most ambitious and noble visions of ourselves

Appreciative Inquiry draws its positive power to transform from its underlying theoretical perspective of social constructionism (Berger & Luckmann, 1966). Because logical positivism obliges the researcher to gather objective data about human systems and discover quasi natural law-like regularities in them (Sussman & Evered, 1978), it can yield spurious results, as social reality is not independent of social actors and is neither static nor a-historical. Rather, ‘‘social order is a subject matter capable of infinite variation through the linkage of ideas and actions” (Cooperrider & Srivastva, 1987, p. 139). The researcher’s actions inevitably change social reality, making all social theory morally relevant and placing an ethical responsibility on the researcher.

This key insight about the link between words and worlds explains Appreciative Inquiry’s rationale for using symbolic content to bring about concrete changes. The ‘‘creation of new and evocative theories of groups, organizations, and societies, are a powerful way to aid in their change and development” (Bushe, 1995, p. 15). This point is reinforced by noting that the purpose of inquiry ‘‘is the creation of ‘generative theory,’ not so much mappings or explanations of yesterday’s world but anticipatory articulations of tomorrow’s possibilities” (Cooperrider & Whitney, 2001, p. 20)

Having established that new worlds are created through words (see also Austin, 1962; Searle, 1969), Appreciative Inquiry adds another insight in the form of the ‘heliotropic hypothesis (Cooperrider, 1990), which proposes that all human organizations have a natural tendency to evolve toward ‘the light’ – symbolic images that are positive and life-giving. However, problems are not to be completely ignored. To be effective Appreciative Inquiry also needs to have a spirit of curiosity, openness and collaboration towards finding and solving the causes of problems. These and other characteristics are summarized in the Appreciative Inquiry literature (Finegold et al., 2002).

In practice, Appreciative Inquiry is usually operationalized as a four-phase cycle, commonly referred to as the 4D cycle (cf. Fig. 1). The cycle starts with the phase called Discovery. In this phase, the whole system engages in a search for existing strengths, retrieving the moments of high achieve ment and bringing them to the front of organizational consciousness. This phase is followed by the Dream phase in which the strengths and potentials identified in the first phase are used as a springboard for the envisioning of an ideal, results-oriented organization; an organization that would be the dream of everyone if no constraints existed. The next phase of Designing takes this vision and works out its practical and feasible implementation by connecting it to organizational strengths. Finally, Des tiny is the time for acting out these designs and staying connected to sustain forward momentum.

## 2.2. Critique of appreciative inquiry

Appreciative Inquiry has attracted its fair share of criticism from academics and organizational scholars. Some criticisms continually appear in literature, such as: (1) positive scholarship leaves out the discussion of real problems from organization discourse, which may lead to complacency, (2) it may create a false sense of euphoria, and (3) there is a lack of hard evidence that positiveness produces concrete bottom line results (Fineman, 2006; Roberts, 2006). One scholar notes, often the results are expressed in aspirational statements such as positiveness improves ‘‘appreciation, collab oration, virtuousness, vitality and meaningfulness”, and comments that the ‘‘positive movement appears to be loose assemblage of signatories whose current or past research resonates with positiveness’s overall theme” (Fineman, 2006, p. 271).

Most of these criticisms are based on a lack of true appreciation of Appreciative Inquiry’s aims, aspirations and methodology (Avital. 2005). We believe it is important to counter these criticisms by linking Appreciative Inquiry to the epistemic insights from the broader critical social theory literature, so that it can redeem its original claims. Going back to Cooperrider and Srivastva (1987). these claims being that Appreciative Inquiry should have a generative ‘‘capacity to challenge the guiding assumptions of the culture, to raise fundamental questions regarding contemporary social life, to foster reconsideration of that which is ‘taken for granted’ and thereby furnish new alternatives for social actions” (p. 131). A general theory of communicative action can certainly contribute to the achievement of Appreciative Inquiry’s aims by challenging the status quo in order to remove artificial constraints on positive action. Critical social thought acts as a source of legitimate critique; it explains what is wrong with current social reality. and identifies achievable practical means for social transformation (Bohman, 2005). In order to raise “fundamental questions" and challenge unexamined beliefs

Appreciative Inquiry must be able to provide positive critique. Deliberative Inquiry can support and strengthen Appreciative Inquiry by lending it a theoretical foundation for generating constructive assessments. This will not only bring Appreciative Inquiry closer to its original claims, but also shield it against the usual criticisms.

In order to elaborate on the above ideas and explore the concept of Deliberative Inquiry in more detail, we will pursue a body of literature that so far has received little attention in the literature on design, including that of design science. It means to follow a different path, less traveled, that has been pioneered by philosophers and political scientists interested in the theory of democracy.

## 3. The model of deliberative inquiry

Deliberative Inquiry is a key concept in the theory of deliberative democracy, which represents a major shift in political thought about the relationship between citizens and government. A brief exposition of the theoretical model of deliberative democracy assists in explicating the notion of Deliberative Inquiry. To put this model in perspective we will compare and contrast it with the other two most prominent models of democracy. We will then address the question of how Deliberative Inquiry might apply to strengthen positive design.

## 3.1. Models of democracy

The political philosophy of the West has been dominated by two traditions; liberalism and civic republicanism. The two differ in the way they treat the concept of rights or autonomy. While liberalism regards individual rights as of prime importance, republicanism awards pride of place to the freedom of a community to self-govern.

The republican philosophy can be traced as far back as Aristotle (Encarta, 2005). Aristotle envisioned an ideal state that is modeled over the basic characteristics of human soul-appetite, spirit and philosophy. Hobbes’ fundamental assumption was that since all human beings are basically the same in their ‘natural’ state, they need an absolutely powerful sovereign (Nuttall, 2002, p. 217). Rousseau’s The Social Contract ((1762) 1973), explains order in society by positing that human beings implicitly enter into a contract with the state and fellow citizens to abide by the law in order to safeguard their rights. The republican model views society as a collective actor, and assumes homogeneity of worldviews, preferences and conceptions of common good. Therefore, it holds a preference for collective rights or popular sovereignty over individual rights or private autonomy.

On the other hand, liberalism views society as composed of autonomous individuals and government as a mechanism of avoiding the danger of the ‘tyranny of the majority’ (see, for example, de Tocqueville, 1835/1945). Liberalism asserts that all individuals should be free to lead their lives as they wish; their personal freedom of thought, action and expression is only limited by the similar freedoms of others. This idea is consistent with the concept of a minimal state where government exists solely to mediate conflicts that arise due to clashes of individual interests (Finlayson, 2005, p. 110). Thus in liberalism, it is not the people who enjoy sovereignty; rather, it is the rule of law that is supreme.

Parallel to the above two, a relatively new model of democracy has been proposed and is attracting considerable attention from the scholars of political philosophy (see, for example, Benhabib, 1996; Bohman, 1996; Cohen, 1989; Dryzek, 2000; Fishkin, 1995; Gutmann & Thompson, 1996; Habermas, 1996). In this deliberative model of democracy, citizens’ preferences are not simply aggregated at the time of voting, without there being an obligation on the voters to provide a rationale for their choice. Rather, citizens are given a chance to make their preferences known publicly and to support them by reasons that can be considered by all before a decision is made. This phase of deliberation allows for the formation of public opinion in a rational manner.

Under the deliberative model, being aware and appreciative of others’ perspectives and willing to move toward a more consensual position is the goal. The decisions are considered legitimate if they are arrived at through deliberations in the public sphere in which all points of views are accepted or rejected solely on the basis of the force of better arguments and the soundness of their logic (Habermas, 1989).

We will now look at the core concept of deliberative democracy, i.e. Deliberative Inquiry, in more detail to clarify how it might apply to positive design.

## 3.2. Deliberative inquiry

Deliberative Inquiry is key to coordination, collaboration, and conflict resolution and plays an important role in organizing human action. Human beings essentially coordinate their actions by adopting the practice of ‘‘giving and asking for reasons” (Habermas & Cooke, 2000, p. 1). This exchange of reason forms the foundational concept of Deliberative Inquiry. We may contrast it with ‘strategic communication to make its meaning more evident. Strategic communication employs ‘any means possible’ including deceit, manipulation, or outright coercion in an attempt to influence behavior of the listeners (Warnke, 1995). This type of communication is aimed at getting others to do as one wishes by threatening them, imposing economic sanctions or by using political or other types of force. There is no sincere effort to convince others about the merits of a cause solely through the ‘unforced force of the better argument. Rather, strategic communication is oriented to success, achieved by applying best means to given ends. Deliberative Inquiry on the other hand, is about the ends themselves and is oriented toward unforced consensus.

## 3.3. Deliberative inquiry’s nature and purpose

In the philosophical tradition going back to Aristotle’s time, deliberation is considered as a process of will formation. It refers to those moments that precede decision making in which individuals or groups ponder over and weigh together various alternative solutions before deciding on one (London, 1995, p. 40). Deliberative Inquiry aims to discover truth and arrive at agreement or consensus in a rational manner. In other words, it proceeds in the spirit of enlightenment as defined by Kant:

Enlightenment is man’s emergence from his self-incurred immaturity. Immaturity is the inability to use one’s own understanding without the guidance of another. . . .Dogmas and formulas, those mechanical instruments for rational use (or rather misuse) of his natural endowments, are the ball and chain of his permanent immaturity. (Immanuel Kant, An Answer to the Question: ‘‘What is Enlightenment?” Konigsberg in Prussia, 30th September, 1784, p. 1)

Deliberative Inquiry is open-ended in the sense that it depends upon a person’s capacity to let rationality guide choices between collective and particular interests (Habermas. 1996, p. 54 quoted in Curtin, 1999). While Deliberative Inquiry participants reject all coercion, manipulation or deception, they are open to persuasion and willing to change their judgments, preferences and views on the basis of logical arguments (Dryzek, 2000, p. 1). The participants chip away falsehood, disagreement or dis cord by mutually exchanging logical and rational arguments. They bring out relevant facts and knowledge at appropriate points in time, and incorporate the logic of partner deliberators into their own positions, opinions and judgments. Hence, they arrive closer to truth, agreement and consensus. In this sense, Deliberative Inquiry seeks to foster mutual understanding that enables rational collective action (Price, Cappella, & Nir, 2002 quoted in; Witschge, 2002, p. 5).

Deliberative Inquiry usually takes place in an open, public environment. Kavanaugh et al (2005, p. 3) believe

. . .deliberation differs from participation in that it involves more public discussion, negotiation, pri oritizing, consensus building, and agenda setting. It is a multifaceted group process, one that typically occurs in public spaces, such as voluntary associations and public meetings that transpire in what Oldenburg (1989) characterizes as ‘Great Good Places’.

There are numerous avenues within commercial organizations where Deliberative Inquiry may be fruitfully employed. Participative system design, strategic management, teamwork, shareholder debates, and employee empowerment are just a few such opportunities. More flexible and robust organizations can be developed by allowing employees and small groups to participate in and deliberate about work system design and its operation (Mumford, 2003). In general, Deliberative Inquiry caters to the needs of postindustrial societies, whose members have a greater need for self-expression and greater say in decision-making (Inglehart, 1997, p. 29, 193).

Deliberation plays an important role in policy-making, which involves managing conflicting values and differing worldviews held by a large number of stakeholders (Horita & Iwahashi, 2002). The German philosopher Hans-Georg Gadamer observed that ‘‘the knowledge that gives direction to action is essentially called for by concrete situations in which we are to choose the thing to be done; and no learned or mastered technique can spare us the task of deliberation and decision” (quoted in London, 1995).

## 3.4. Role of critique

The role of critique is important under the rubric of Deliberative Inquiry for improving our understanding of the situation at hand. Deliberative Inquiry does not construe critique as a negative contest between opposing positions, rather it sees critique as a first step in a positive and cooperative search for consensus. Positive criticism informs and motivates the advancement of constructive discourses through the dialectic interactions of multiple viewpoints. In addition, critical and open debate is also necessary to vent internal conflicts and vested interests, which interfere with motivation for collaboration and have many negative side-effects on individuals. These are typically summarized under the label of alienation. However, critique also needs to proceed in a proper forum including self-reflection and ethical rules of discourse, so that it is productive and does not deteriorate into mere finger pointing or even ‘show trials’ or witch hunts. Deliberative Inquiry, also known as deliberative communication and public deliberations<sup>1</sup>, has recently received much attention in the literature of communication studies and political science. It is still under development (see, for example, Habermas, 2006) and we believe that key insights from this discussion could contribute some good pointers for strengthening the model of Appreciative Inquiry.

## 3.5. Deliberative inquiry as design

Simon’s (1981) proposal to establish a science of the artificial foreshadowed the beginning of design sciences. According to these sciences, design is both an artifact and a process (Walls, Widmeyer, & E Sawy, 1992), which depends intimately upon the collaborative construction of reality. In this sense design is not planned – it emerges through social dialog (Lyytinen, 2002). Lyytinen describes design as any activity that is inherently teleological<sup>2</sup>, cognitive and anticipatory in its content and orientation. It therefore demands a certain level of proficiency and capability of those engaged and often involves a rational or analytic moment of analysis, reflection, and reasoning. From this perspective, deliberation appears akin to design itself. Like design, deliberation is always oriented to the future. It seeks to undertake a cooperative search for a legitimate course of action, and a vision of a future world that is satisfying and acceptable to all. Unlike rhetoric or argumentation, deliberation depends solely upon reason and rationality for success and is thus cognitive in nature. These observations complement Churchman’s original insight that design and inquiry are interrelated and that the character and quality of design will depend upon the character of the inquiry process.

## 4. Inquiry and positive design

Appreciative Inquiry and Deliberative Inquiry have a natural affinity for each other. As defined by Cooperrider and Avital (2004), Appreciative Inquiry depends closely on the idea of ideal speech ‘‘in all its characteristics – of the minimization of power asymmetries, a reduction in the intrusiveness of ideology, an increase in the level of commitment to the process, and the adoption of a critical self-reflection by all the parties as they seek to uncover the truth of the text” (Elliott, 1999). These are precisely the pre-conditions of Deliberative Inquiry as noted above (see Section 3.2). Both Appreciative and Deliberative Inquiry are inclusive, participatory and conducive to free thinking idea exploration, which makes them well poised for carrying out positive changes in an organization.

Cooperrider and Avital’s (2004) writing on Appreciative Inquiry anticipates some important characteristics of what we associate with a social model of Deliberative Inquiry. They emphasize the social nature of deliberations and its ‘‘openness” challenging ‘‘treasured certainties”. Openness requires us to accept, even welcome the indeterminacy of searching conversations in the following sense:

Co-inquiry in the presence of other human beings almost always discloses views not quite like our own and is capable, therefore, of dislodging treasured certainties. When we enter inquiry’s theater we are often surprised with the ending. But then we are gifted, not with solid certainty but with something even better – the vertigo of new vision. And this is the special paradox of Appreciative Inquiry. Inquiry into the good or the life-generating is neither comfortable nor stable, even if positive. (Cooperrider & Avital, 2004, p. xiii).

In summary, deliberative forms of inquiry are open-ended, relatively free from social controls and carried out in the spirit of mutual respect and understanding. Free from social controls means minimizing the effects of formal authority and other forms of domination, constraining group pressures, preconceived positions, ideologies etc. that inhibit positive discourse. Carried out in the spirit of mutual understanding implies that we are prepared to listen to all arguments with sincerity, giving them the benefit of the doubt rather than rejecting them based on our accepted opinions. It recommends that we present our differing opinions in the spirit of genuine search for truth, sine ira et studio and without pursuing hidden agendas. It does not mean that we hesitate to express our opinions or even hide them or pretend to agree when we do not. It also means that we admit ignorance when we do not command enough information or arguments to provide evidence for our opinions. These guideline enhance trust and understanding and provide a mechanism for conflict resolution that is in agreement with the spirit of positive design. Finally, Deliberative Inquiry means accepting the key principle of agnostic ethics: If we are not logically entitled to a claim or proposition, then we are not morally entitled to it either. We believe these ideas relate closely to what Cooperrider and Avital (2004 p. xviii) referred to as ‘‘dialogic processes” in the ‘‘extension of relatedness” in Appreciative Inquiry. These ideas also resonate harmoniously with the positive lens’ sensitivity to ethical issues and other con cerns such as human-centeredness and flexibility. The attributes of being receptive, inclusive and participatory make Deliberative Inquiry a promising platform for designing organizations with a positive lens.

## 4.1. Consensus and agreement

Designing organizations is also a process of constructing social realities. Positive social realities cannot be constructed if conflict and conceit stand in the way – it requires consensus as much as it requires positive visions of an even better tomorrow. While Appreciative Inquiry asks us to abandon the ‘fill in the gaps’ approach and look at the road ahead, Deliberative Inquiry leads us to decide upon a course of action that is not imposed, but chosen by consensus. In certain cases, there may be a need to bring together groups of people with diverse or even conflicting worldviews and values. For example, in projects of software or information systems development, reaching agreement becomes important for the progress and completion of the project. Especially in these cases, Deliberative Inquiry may complement and strengthen Appreciative Inquiry by providing a mechanism for reaching agreements through rational communication. A synthesis of the two approaches helps adhering to the positive de sign goals more closely. Some of the salient differences and similarities between the two are listed in Table 1.

Deliberative Inquiry facilitates positive design, as it helps us in defining what is ‘positive’ in the first place. According to the deliberative spirit, there are no ‘given’ standards of what is right or good or valuable. A legitimate definition of what is ‘good’ can only be arrived at by engaging in free, fair and open discussions. The characteristics of being free, fair and open also mean that no sincere critique must be ignored or kept out of discussion. Sincere fundamental criticism is seen as positive, even necessary, for the effectiveness of Deliberative Inquiry. Without fundamental criticism, conformity and habit stand in the way of perceiving the need for improvements, and continued openness of positive design activities is in jeopardy. The use of the term critical may seem odd in the context of Appreciative Inquiry; however, this term is not meant to be understood as being negative or judgmental. Rather, it refers to intense self-awareness and as such is a positive term (Elliott, 1999, p. 27). Critique here does not refer to fault-finding, instead it is a tool to chip away artificial constraints to building positive organizations.

Positive language and emotions are important lynchpins of Appreciative Inquiry. Nevertheless, even though positive attitudes and feelings are important in bringing about a positive change, they can benefit from rational discourse in order to legitimize that change. We propose, therefore, to balance the emotional side of Appreciative Inquiry with the cognitive or rational side of Deliberative Inquiry, and suggest that Appreciative Inquiry’s 4D cycle is strengthened if supported by another D – Deliberative Inquiry. This fifth D highlights the importance of reaching rationally guided agreements in all phases of Appreciative Inquiry, although in some phases it may prove to be more useful than in others. We anticipate that deliberative Inquiry may be more effective in the last two phases of Appreciative Inquiry, where some collective decisions need to be taken. Because Deliberative Inquiry brings positive critique into the appreciative process, it helps reduce negative, judgmental, or dogmatic moments, and reinforces a positive constructive attitude of all participants at all times. The focus on creating inclusive and participatory environments ensures the legitimacy of all decisions taken in the various phases of Appreciative Inquiry.

## 4.2. Computer support for inquiry

The insight that better design requires better forms of inquiry was first achieved by Churchman (1971), who explored five different kinds of inquiry models based on the evolving forms of epistemology. These five ‘archetypes’ of inquiry were based on the epistemic views of Leibniz, Locke, Kant, Hegel, and Singer about the nature of human knowledge and its limits. However, as we have seen, the evolution of epistemology did not end there – continuing on with the Deliberative and Appreciative Inquiry models. It is high time, therefore, to look into the relation between design and the new inquiry models, and to validate the general principle that design incarnates and encapsulates modes of inquiry. Design reflects the patterns and qualitative characteristics of the communications that take place among the design participants. Therefore, distorted communication distorts the design itself and positive communication inevitably makes design more positive.

It is interesting to note that the theoretical basis of Deliberative Inquiry is quite well developed, even though it has not yet entered discourses in IS research or positive design literatures. It is equally underrepresented in design science and the methods and tools of requirements specification, although there is some affinity with the past debate on socio-technical systems development approaches and user participation. However, the empirical exploration of Deliberative Inquiry has barely begun. This is evident from the literature reported in the GRASS project (cf. Heng & Moor, 2003), a prototype system for supporting Deliberative Inquiry into the ecology issues of forest management. Some precursory support for the practical workability of Deliberative Inquiry can be seen in earlier special approaches in IS literature (Conklin & Begeman, 1988; Gordon & Karacapilidis, 1997; Gordon, Voss, Richter, & Marker, 2001; Kunz & Rittel, 1970; Mackenzie et al., 2004), which dealt with communications related to the improvement of deliberations preceding goal formation and decisions making. The experimental results with these kinds of systems so far have been as varied as the approaches taken.

Most recently research approaches to Deliberative Inquiry have received important ideas for new directions from the increased capabilities of Internet-based ICTs. Notably, the debate on and experiments in e-democracy have provided much food for thought and shed additional light on the issue of how to support public deliberations. Although the Internet has ample potential for improving delib eration and social communication in policy design and problem solving processes, for some reason it has not materialized yet (Klein & Huynh, 2004, pp. 223–224). It is important from a research perspective to understand why this has not happened thus far. If indeed there is such a potential then IS re search must focus on the design of appropriate systems to support Deliberative Inquiry at all levels of analysis – individual, group, organization and society.

## 5. Conclusions

This paper has argued that designing organizations and management with a positive lens, can benefit from the positive effects of Deliberative Inquiry because it is not only compatible with Appreciative Inquiry, but also complements it. Originally, this argument was inspired by Churchman’s fundamental insight that the quality of design is related to the mode of inquiry employed in designing. This is an important linkage, which the current literature has not examined thus far.

The paper has also argued that critique should not be sidestepped. Rather critique should be viewed as something positive and in line with the spirit of Appreciative Inquiry and should be carefully positioned and brought in at appropriate phases. Constructive critique is an integral element of high quality inquiry and part and parcel of the ways in which Deliberative Inquiry can complemen and strengthen Appreciative Inquiry

Although there have been several attempts in the past to provide computer support for Deliberative Inquiry, they have only met with a varying level of success and attention. Therefore, we conclude at this point that the potential effectiveness of Deliberative Inquiry in the practice of organizational design is mostly a stimulating hypothesis, although a complex and theoretically well elaborated one.

In this regard, further research is needed to answer at least three questions. First, What is the best way to support Deliberative Inquiry? How does such inquiry take place in real life settings, offline as well as online? and Are there any aspects of the process that could be supported by technological features? Second, How strong is the link between improved Deliberative Inquiry and better design? Be yond theoretical grounds, we have some evidence that a link does exist in the behavior of open source, wiki and other online communities. This calls for a thorough empirical study of the triangular interdependencies among deliberative communities, the technologies they use, and the quality of their accomplishments at different levels of society. Third, What is the relationship between the structure of design, (e.g. the degree of modularity in software) and the social structure of communities practicing Deliberative Inquiry? We contend that research in these areas would greatly benefit any future design sciences.
