# Effect of Knowledge-Sharing Trajectories on Innovative Outcomes in Temporary Online Crowds

Ann Majchrzak

Marshall School of Business, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, California 90089; and ESADE Business School, 08034 Barcelona, Spain, majchrza@usc.edu

Arvind Malhotra

Kenan–Flagler Business School, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Chapel Hill, North Carolina 27599, malhotra@unc.edu

here is substantial research on the effects of formal control structures (i.e., incentives, identities, organiza-Ttion, norms) on knowledge sharing leading to innovative outcomes in online communities. However, there is little research on how knowledge-sharing trajectories in temporary online crowds create innovative outcomes without these structures. Such research is particularly of interest in the context of temporary online crowds solicited with crowdsourcing in which there is only minimal structure for knowledge sharing. We identify eight types of crowdsourcing with different knowledge-sharing patterns. The focus of this study is on the one type of crowdsourcing—collaborative innovation challenges—in which there is the least restriction on knowledge sharing in the crowd. A content analysis was conducted of all time-stamped posts made in five different collaborative innovation challenges to identify different knowledge-sharing trajectories used. We found that a paradox-framed trajectory was more likely to be followed by innovative outcomes compared to three other knowledge-sharing trajectories. A paradox-framed trajectory is one in which a novel solution emerges when different participants post in the following sequence: (1) contributing a paradox associated with the problem objective, (2) sharing assumptions to validate the paradox, and (3) sharing initial ideas for resolving the paradox in a manner that meets the problem statement. Based on the findings, a theory of paradox-framed trajectories in temporary online crowds is presented along with implications for knowledge creation theories in general and online knowledge-creating communities in particular.

Keywords: innovation; sequences; crowdsourcing; knowledge; online communities

## Introduction

Knowledge sharing in online communities is increasingly becoming the source for innovative outcomes (Faraj et al. 2011, von Hippel and von Krogh 2003). Yet little is known about the effect on innovative outcomes of what knowledge is shared and in what sequence, what we refer to as knowledge-sharing trajectories. Collaborative innovative challenges initiate temporary online crowds in which participants come together for short finite collaboration windows with few formalized structures limiting knowledge sharing. Such temporary online crowds are an ideal context to isolate the impact of knowledge-sharing trajectories. Specifically, we ask the research questions: Do knowledge-sharing trajectories affect the innovative outcomes achieved by a crowd? If so, are there some trajectories that lead to more innovative outcomes than others?

Innovative outcomes refer to novel and implementable ideas (Amabile 1988). In the context of an online community, innovative outcomes may be in the form of solving a problem, as in open-source software or innovation challenges, or identifying ways to seize opportunities such as in user innovation communities (Boudreau and Lakhani 2009, Enkel et al. 2009, Jeppesen and Frederiksen 2006, Surowiecki 2005, Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Wallin and von Krogh 2010). The creation of innovative outcomes in organizations occurs through a knowledge-sharing process as it requires reframing, reconceptualizing, and/or recombining knowledge contributed by others (Galunic and Rodan 1998). Reframing in an online community requires individuals to examine the existing framing of a problem, consider other alternative frames and facts they have acquired from others, observe others’ reactions to others’ attempts at reframing, pose a possible reframing, observe others’ reactions to that reframing, and then participate in the ongoing refinement of the reframing (Avital and Te’eni 2009, Jarvenpaa and Lang 2011). In a pairwise interaction, this would be referred to as a conversation (Tsoukas 2009); in online communities, the temporal string of these posts becomes a knowledge-sharing trajectory of posts in a virtual workspace viewable by participants. The knowledge-sharing trajectory then provides a salient means by which members can influence each other in online communities, and consequentially the outcomes (Gong et al. 2013). A knowledge-sharing trajectory, then, is defined as the types of knowledge shared (i.e., “what”) and the sequence (i.e., in what order) in which different types of knowledge are shared.

Online communities differ in their “temporariness.” Some of these online communities, such as open source, Wikipedia, and user innovation communities are temporally persistent, with formal control structures governing the motivation, mechanism, and manner of knowledge sharing. These formal structures include norms (Bagozzi and Dholakia 2006, Ransbotham and Kane 2011, Ren et al. 2007), task modularization (Howison and Crowston 2014, MacCormack et al. 2006, Shah 2006), organizational hierarchy (Butler et al. 2008, O’Mahony and Ferraro 2007) and formal leaders (O’Mahony and Ferraro 2007). These formal control structures are present even while specific participants come and go (Forte et al. 2009, Gong et al. 2013). In contrast are temporary online crowds, characterized as a group of strangers coming together for a short period of time for a particular purpose, disbanding once the time period or purpose is achieved, and with minimal normative effects created by a shadow of the future (Malhotra and Majchrzak 2014). In this study, we focus on a specific instance of temporary online crowds: those initiated by collaborative innovation challenges.

Temporary online crowds are an important context to study for two reasons. First, the research elaborating how formal control structures of online communities affect knowledge sharing (Franke and Shah 2003, Füller et al. 2014, Howison and Crowston 2014, Kane et al. 2014, Kuk 2006, Shah 2006) does not explore which knowledge is shared over time—the knowledge-sharing trajectory. This past research does not address the fundamental issue as to whether a knowledge-sharing trajectory even exists without the benefit of formal structures. Consequently, there is a gap in the literature as to whether innovative outcomes are fostered by formal structures or are an inherent property of the way in which online participants share their knowledge.

A second reason why temporary online crowds are an important context to study is their increasing popularity among both practitioners and researchers. Temporary online crowds are often solicited as part of crowdsourcing initiatives (Afuah and Tucci 2012, Boudreau et al. 2011, Lakhani et al. 2012) and open innovation (Chesbrough 2006, Chesbrough and

Crowther 2006, Wallin and von Krogh 2010). In such crowdsourcing initiatives, participants are viewed as diverse sources of external knowledge with ideas or solutions to a well-structured task, problem, or question posed by a sponsoring organization. The growing body of research on crowdsourcing has not yet tested assumptions about knowledge-sharing trajectories among crowd participants that are most likely to lead to innovative outcomes, particularly with illstructured or wicked problems in which dependencies between elements are difficult to ascertain. For example, research on crowdsourcing has tended to assume that innovation-enabling trajectories generally do not exist for ill-structured problems since such problems cannot be solved by a crowd (Afuah and Tucci 2012), and when they do exist, they primarily consist of nothing more than a series of individual ideas with refinements of those ideas by others (Boudreau and Lakhani 2009, Terwiesch and Xu 2008). These assumptions about the nature of innovationenabling trajectories, particularly for ill-structured problems, should therefore be subjected to empirical testing.

Several theories of group innovation have suggested how knowledge sharing leads to innovation in groups solving ill-structured problems (Carlile 2004, Harvey 2014, Nonaka et al. 2000, Tsoukas 2009). These group innovation theories were originally developed in off-line contexts and therefore need to be adapted to understand online knowledge-sharing trajectories. In these theories, group innovation occurs through a knowledge-sharing process of first agreeing on semantic meanings of terms, then making explicit each person’s domain knowledge to make differences and conflicts clear, and finally, negotiating resolutions to the conflict for a final outcome. This process, however, is difficult to follow for temporary online crowds. Individuals post what they want and when they want, rarely following an issue from its beginning to its resolved end (Kane et al. 2014, Malhotra and Majchrzak 2014). This makes it difficult for the crowd to agree on anything, especially meanings of terms, sources of conflicts, and potential resolutions (Faraj et al. 2011). Moreover, as crowd members have little knowledge of the backgrounds of others in the crowd, and the collaboration window is finite and short, there is also little agreement on which differences are worth a negotiated resolution (Kittur and Kraut 2008). Therefore, existing theories of group innovation may require adaptation for deducing knowledge-sharing trajectories in temporary online crowds.

## Conceptual Development

To study how online knowledge-sharing trajectories may influence innovation in temporary online crowds, knowledge sharing itself must be encouraged within the crowd. Moreover, to isolate the impact of knowledge-sharing trajectories on innovative outcomes separate from other influences, formal control structures that govern how knowledge will be shared should be minimized. In this section, we briefly describe a context in which these conditions hold: collaborative innovation challenges. We then turn to conceptualizing various forms of knowledge-sharing trajectories that may occur in temporary online crowds that are initiated by collaborative innovation challenges.

## Context for Knowledge-Sharing Trajectories in Temporary Online Crowds

Crowdsourcing today has been configured in a variety of ways. Some of these configurations are shown in Table 1. As depicted in Table 1, not all crowdsourcing configurations involve significant knowledge sharing among the crowd. Moreover, of the five crowdsourcing configurations in which knowledge sharing is integral to the innovative process, four limit knowledge sharing in at least one of three ways: within predefined modularized tasks, to solutions-only without discussions, or by expecting the crowd to limit their contribution to refining others’ ideas rather than cocreate solutions with others. The single remaining configuration, referred to as collaborative innovation challenges, is one in which knowledge sharing about the complete problem is encouraged rather than modularized, and the sharing is unrestricted and encouraged. Consequently, we focus on collaborative innovation challenges.

## Knowledge-Sharing Trajectories in Temporary Online Crowds

Given our focus on temporary online crowds responding to collaborative innovation challenges, we turn to Cronin and Weingart’s (2007) theory of knowledge representational gaps to establish the type and sequence in which knowledge might be shared to foster the emergence of innovative ideas in such crowds. Their theory has been used to inform studies on innovation in diverse science teams (Salazar et al. 2012), decision-making groups (van Ginkel et al. 2009), cross-functional teams (Majchrzak et al. 2012), innovation-oriented teams (Gardner et al. 2012, Paletz and Schunn 2010), and group creativity (Paulus et al. 2010). The types of knowledge identified in this theory overlaps with types of knowledge suggested in group creativity research (e.g., Harvey 2014, Miron-Spektor et al. 2011).

Theory of Knowledge Representation Gaps. Cronin and Weingart (2007) examine the problem of why it is difficult for functionally diverse groups to share and build on each other’s knowledge. They suggest that the problem stems from what they refer to as “representational gaps.” Representational gaps are inconsistencies between individuals’ definitions of the problem the group is solving. These gaps surface because each group member has their own simplified model of the problem that guides their problem solving without overwhelming their limited cognitive capacity. Representational gaps are problematic in group problem solving because they are “places where information processing between teammates breaks down” (Cronin and Weingart 2007, p. 765). Group members with different representations may take a word, such as “quality,” and interpret it differently and place different values on it when solving the problem. These differences increase the possibilities that individuals in a group will take different actions to solve the problem, resulting in coordination difficulties and conflicts. The conflicts may be manifested as differences in how resources should be allocated, which assumptions can be violated, and which elements of the problem can be reframed.

Cronin and Weingart (2007) suggest that representational gaps are never completely resolved since individuals in functionally diverse groups are inherently different from each other. Moreover, the strength of the group comes from its differences, not just its similarities (Harvey 2014). Therefore, the issue is one of how a cross-functional group can problem solve, given the existence of representational gaps. The authors argue that, rather than assuming that group members will come to some semantic agreement on goals and other forms of representations, they suggest instead that cross-functional group members will be able to jointly problem solve because they “align” their representations with each other. Alignment means that the representations are not fundamentally incongruent or in conflict with each other. In this way, alignment is about surfacing differences rather than constructing shared mental models. Moreover, alignment among all forms of representations are not needed; rather, it is only alignment in goals and assumptions that is the most critical for the innovation process. By foregoing any requirement of a shared consensus or meaning among group members about their representations, and by limiting the less-constraining requirement of alignment to goals and assumptions only, Cronin and Weingart’s (2007) theory may help to explain how temporary online crowds are able to innovate without a shared consensus.

Examining the requirement of alignment of goals, the group creativity literature provides suggestions about how alignment without shared consensus might be possible. From this literature, some of the most challenging goals stimulating group creativity are paradoxical objectives (George 2007, Gong et al. 2013, Harvey 2014, Leonard and Swap 1999, Miron-Spektor et al. 2011). Paradoxical objectives refer to objectives for a group that appear to be in conflict, such that both sides of the paradox appear to not be achievable with the same solution (Miron-Spektor et al. 2011). For example, a design objective of “an expensive luxury sports car for middle-income car enthusiasts” is an example of a paradoxical objective.

Paradoxes have also been identified as critical components in organizational change, learning, and design (da Cunha et al. 2002, Eisenhardt 2000). Poole and Van de Ven (1989) present modes through which paradoxes can help create new knowledge for the organization by presenting “opportunities to discover different assumptions, shift perspectives, pose problems in fundamentally different ways 0 0 0 0” (p. 564). Paradoxes are interrelated yet contradictory sets of requirements that exist simultaneously (Jay 2013). In organizations, making such latent paradoxes explicit can bring about changes that result in innovation (Smith and Lewis 2011). Management of paradoxes in the form of strategic contradictions has been argued to be key for innovation-related cognitive processes in organizations (Smith and Tushman 2005).

Therefore, given that, according to the knowledge representation gap theory, group problem solving can proceed without explicit agreement on goals, paradoxical objectives may serve three purposes as a particularly important trigger for innovation in temporary online crowds. First, crowds are able to use paradoxical objectives identified in posts by crowd participants as a way to have implicit contradictions inherent in the crowd made explicit without agreeing that these objectives are the only ones to be served by a solution. Second, by making explicit the paradoxical objectives, the problem-solving activities of the crowd can be focused and evaluated based on meeting the paradoxical objectives. While not all crowd participants need to accept this single paradox as the personal objective they strive for, those that address the paradox are able to engage in a cocreative process of jointly focused actions, without explicit agreement that this aspect of the problem will be the focus of their efforts. Finally, because the achievement of paradoxical objectives is inherently a more creative exercise than solving nonparadoxical objectives, the crowd may be more inclined to pursue paradoxical objectives because of the inherent challenge of the task, an important factor influencing participation in crowdsourcing (Leimeister et al. 2009).

Not all paradoxical objectives are valid, however. This is where Cronin and Weingart’s (2007) second knowledge representation requiring alignment—assumptions—becomes important. Assumptions are “givens,” manifested as facts about the problem or personal experiences in analogically similar domains. According to their theory, assumptions must be aligned with the goal, and each group member’s assumptions must be aligned with each other’s assumptions. When paradoxical objectives are made explicit through a crowd member’s post, following the objectives with posts sharing assumptions aligning with the paradox can help to determine the validity of the paradox. In Cronin and Weingart’s (2007) theory, aligned goals and assumptions focus the team on specific goals and facts, allowing for effective problem solving. Similarly, paradoxical objectives as goals, together with facts and experiences aligned with the paradox, may help to focus the crowd on cocreating outcomes specifically addressing the paradox and related assumptions.

Finally, Cronin and Weingart (2007) explicitly suggest that two additional representations need not be aligned: elements and operators. Elements are individually held ideas about what components of the problem should be changed, and operators are descriptions of how these ideas can be implemented. Group members do not need to agree on these ideas, and in fact may want to surface divergent ideas in an effort to stimulate new ideas and an eventual innovative outcome. This suggests that, in temporary online crowds, ideas may flourish without helping the innovative process if goals and assumptions are misaligned.

In temporary online crowds, because members join and leave the crowd frequently over time (Dahlander and Frederiksen 2012), alignment is demonstrated not necessarily by such statements as “I agree” since any single person’s agreement is not an indication of the crowd’s collective attitude. Rather, alignment is more likely to be demonstrated through the knowledge shared by an individual in the crowd in the context of others’ contributions and easily observed by others (Howison and Crowston 2014). As the crowd observes others’ posts, the posts made immediately following others’ can be used to demonstrate alignment (Kane et al. 2014). As more crowd members post assumptions that align with the previous posts, it indicates a broader alignment, calling the attention of the rest of the crowd to further explore or develop the topic, in a form of preferential attachment (Faraj and Johnson 2011). By calling attention to an alignment, then, the crowd may be able to move its knowledge creation process forward despite an incomplete consensus and no single individual or core group managing the process.

In sum, building on Cronin and Weingart (2007), the knowledge-sharing sequence in which members of a temporary online crowd problem solves will consist of posted statements about several forms of representations: goals, assumptions, and solution ideas. We extend their theorizing to suggest that the order in which these representations occur may indicate alignment, thereby fostering more innovativeness from the crowd. Below, we describe a number of different potential sequential orders.

Paradox-Framed Trajectory. Given the importance allocated to goal alignment by Cronin and Weingart (2007) for cross-functional problem solving, and the role of paradoxical objectives as creativity catalyst goals, we suggest that temporary online crowds with a sequence that starts with statements about goals that eventually surface a paradoxical objective as a goal may be the beginning of a knowledge-sharing trajectory leading to innovation.

As indicated above, alignment with the paradoxical objective can occur in the form of validating assumptions, such as when a member of the crowd follows the posting of a paradoxical objective with an assumption (a concrete factual “given” and/or personal analogical experience) that is congruent with the paradox. This two-post sequence may then serve to signal to others that, not only is the paradox independently validated by different members of the crowd, but that there is alignment in the crowd between strangers on how they represent the problem. The motivational drive to solve a challenging problem presented as a validated paradoxical objective with concrete related facts then helps to elicit the attention of more crowd members to participate in generating solutions (Cabanac et al. 2010). Some paradoxes may create untenable conflicting demands or focus the group on pathways that are neither productive nor necessary. Therefore, signaling to the crowd which paradoxes are validated serves an important implicit coordination function. Social validation of the paradoxes may be needed to ensure that the group is focused on a paradox that is worth expending cognitive effort to develop an innovative outcome.

This attention to a validated paradox is likely to be immediately followed with initial ideas stimulated by the paradoxical objectives. If crowd members only contributed assumptions about the factual nature of the paradox, but then fail to offer initial ideas that are stimulated by the paradox, the paradoxical goal is of limited creative value. Miron-Spektor et al. (2011), for example, argued that paradoxical objectives should serve as evaluation criteria for initial ideas as well as catalyze those ideas that meet the criteria. The sharing of initial ideas should then immediately follow the validated paradox to demonstrate that the paradox is serving its creativity catalyst role. Since initial ideas do not need to be aligned among crowd members, according to Cronin and Weingart (2007), the ideas at this point need little explanation or understanding; it is simply the fact that the ideas exist as indicators of the stimulating or generative value of the validated paradox that continues to drive the knowledge-sharing trajectory forward toward innovation (Avital 2000).

In sum, we define a paradox-framed trajectory to be one that begins with a post made by a crowd member indicating that the initial problem statement requires solving an inherent paradox among a set of objectives, followed by a post from a different crowd member indicating an assumption that validates the paradox, and finally followed by a third member posting an initial idea. The precise structure of a paradox-framed trajectory is Paradox → Assumption → Initial Idea. This leads to our first hypothesis:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A</sup> <sup>(H1A).</sup> Paradox-framed trajectories are strongly associated with emergence of innovative outcomes in temporary online crowds.

In Hypothesis 1A, we have suggested that social validation of a posted paradox, i.e., posting an assumption aligned with a previously posted paradox, is critical for the emergence of innovative outcomes. In our next hypothesis, we explicitly examine the role of social validation of the paradox.

Research on online crowds has only recently focused on the importance of social validation and thus the assertion that validation is important for innovation is not universally supported. Metzger et al. (2010), for example, argue that validation is primarily important for credibility rather than for stimulating innovation. Harvey (2014) argues that the value of iterative synthesis in innovative groups is not about achieving alignment but simply as a suggestion for open discussion. Validation has also been suggested as harming group creativity by focusing the group’s attention on the paradox as it has been framed, when the most innovative outcome may only emerge by a reframing of the paradox (Tsoukas 2009). Therefore, innovative outcomes may emerge simply with the post of a paradox followed by the post of an initial idea to build on, without the alignment created by the post of an assumption. We refer to this trajectory in which the paradoxical objective is not followed by an assumption as the “unvalidated paradox” trajectory.

In contrast to the literature, we suggest that this alternative trajectory will not be sufficient to generate innovative outcomes. Validating a paradox with an aligned assumption is critical for temporary online crowds because it publicly demonstrates and draws attention to some degree of alignment within the crowd (Gong et al. 2013). Since different members may offer a variety of paradoxical objectives, the crowd needs a signal to indicate which paradox to focus on. Validating the paradox with an aligned assumption may provide that signal. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1B</sup> <sup>(H1B).</sup> Compared to unvalidated paradox trajectories, validated paradox-framed trajectories are more strongly associated with emergence of innovative outcomes in temporary online crowds.

Alternative Ideas-Only Trajectory. Alternatives to the paradox-framed trajectory can be deduced from the existing literature. One such alternative comes from the brainstorming literature. Online brainstorming (in contrast to face-to-face brainstorming) has been shown to foster creative idea generation, irrespective of paradoxical goals (Gallupe et al. 1992, Paulus and Brown 2003). Early idea generation by others helps to stimulate an individual’s recall of knowledge types as well as the creation of additional ideas (Finke et al. 1992). Online idea brainstorming in crowdsourcing has been depicted as leading to diverse ideas because of the divergent past experiences of different members (Terwiesch and Xu 2008). As more ideas are offered, participants are stimulated to offer ever more innovative outcomes. Therefore, an ideas-only trajectory might be one that begins with ideas and is followed by ideas and more ideas to stimulate more innovative outcomes. This type of brainstorming has been the focus of substantial prior research on crowd-based innovation (Boudreau et al. 2011, Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010, Lakhani et al. 2013).

Despite the substantial attention given to the ideasonly trajectory in the research literature on crowdsourcing, we suggest that ideas, without aligned goals and assumptions, are not as likely to foster innovative outcomes in a crowd as a paradox-framed trajectory. Posting only ideas does not resolve knowledge representation gaps as, according to Cronin and Weingart (2007), unresolved conflicts will emerge as divergent ideas are evaluated in the absence of aligned goals and assumptions. As such, different ideas may be misunderstood because of underlying differences in assumptions about the ideas. Moreover, there is substantial research on group creativity (see the review by Paulus and Nijstad 2003) arguing that idea generation harms the novelty of later ideas because each idea requires so much cognitive processing that there is less cognitive attention left over to generate new ideas. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2A</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> Compared to ideas-only trajectories, paradox-framed trajectories are more strongly associated with emergence of innovative outcomes in temporary online crowds.

Alternative Ideas-Framed Trajectory. It may be possible to overcome the limitations of an ideas-only trajectory. Initial ideas could be followed by assumptions aligned with the initial idea. Such assumptions could help to socially validate the initial idea, and thus signal to the crowd that there are multiple crowd members interested in the same idea (Stasser and Birchmeier 2003). This signaling can encourage crowd members to devote attention to developing the initial idea in more creative or novel ways. Thus a trajectory of an initial idea followed by assumptions may overcome the representational gaps of different ideas being misunderstood because of underlying differences in assumptions about the idea.

However, without the paradoxical goal starting the trajectory, the crowd may still suffer from representational gaps about the goals being sought when evaluating suggested ideas. The initial idea—even one validated with an assumption—may not lead to an innovative outcome because of the conflicts created by misaligned goals. To avoid such conflicts, a paradoxical objective could be inserted at this point in the trajectory, after the initial ideas have been validated with aligned assumptions. This would yield a trajectory, which we refer to as “ideas-framed” consisting of Idea → Assumptions → Paradox. This trajectory would be equivalent to idea brainstorming being done initially, followed by a discussion that filters the ideas based on whether they achieve the desired objectives, a process followed by OpenIDEO (Lakhani et al. 2012). Such a trajectory may overcome the representational gaps of H2A’s ideas-only trajectory in which idea generation occurs without aligned goals.

While an ideas-framed trajectory may be viable, it may not lead to more innovative outcomes than a paradox-framed trajectory. By inserting the paradoxical goal after idea generation—rather than before— representational conflict may become amplified rather than dampened. Since the paradox is not validated, there may be differences of opinion about whether the paradox is the best set of objectives for the crowd. These differences, left unsaid, may be confounded with disagreements over an idea or its validation. By contrast, when outcomes emerge from validated paradoxes—rather than paradoxes introduced after validated ideas—the validated paradox as the goal is made visibly stable. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2B</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> Compared to ideas-framed trajectories, paradox-framed trajectories are more strongly associated with emergence of innovative outcomes in temporary online crowds.

In summary, we hypothesize the impact of a paradox-framed trajectory on the emergence of innovative outcomes compared to three alternative trajectories.

## Research Methods

The analysis was conducted on an existing data source of all posts obtained from five collaborative innovation challenges. The posts were content analyzed to categorize knowledge types. For the knowledge that was categorized as outcomes, the innovativeness of each was rated. The data set was then reorganized into sequences, each ending with a rated outcome. Finally, a multistage regression was used to test which trajectories were positively associated with the emergence of innovative outcomes.

Data Source. The five collaborative innovation challenges had been conducted for five different organizations. The challenges were run similar to most innovation challenges: an organizational problem was broadcast to the public with incentives provided for solving the problem. To encourage crowd collaboration, incentives of equal value were provided for collaboration and having the best ideas as judged by the organization, and the broadcasted problem was an ill-structured one requiring integrating multiple perspectives to solve (Table 2 lists the challenge question posed by each organization). Instructions indicated that participants could start a discussion thread with assumptions about the question (e.g., facts or analogical evidence about the problem), or initial ideas and objectives for answering the question. There were no participants engaged in more than one challenge. The brief description of the five challenges shown in Table 2 indicates substantial similarity between the challenges. The invited crowd for each was selected based on similar criteria, with each soliciting people with demonstrated interest, opinions, and some expertise with respect to the question, and including both internal non-R&D (research and development) employees and potential or actual customers from outside the organization (Chesbrough 2006). The external participants were solicited from English-only online discussion forums identified by the organization. Incentive sizes, problem type, length of the challenge, and the means of participant solicitation were similar, as were all decisions made by the chief innovation officer (CIO) or the chief executive officer of the organization sponsoring the challenge. The websites for each challenge were configured in the same way, offered by the same platform provider (Brightidea), and included the same set of instructions: post your knowledge about the problem domain; tag your post by type of knowledge shared (they saw the definitions of the types in Table 3, but not the examples); and combine other’s knowledge posted in generating innovative outcomes. No one moderated the challenges, all participants were encouraged to use pseudonyms rather than their real names, and there was no involvement of executives in the discussion threads.

Construction of Trajectories for Analysis. Across the five events, there were 509 posts. All 509 posts were content analyzed and categorized into one of three knowledge types: paradox, assumption, or idea. Two independent raters categorized the posts using the definitions in Table 3. To categorize each post, the title of the post and the entire thread were read prior to coding to ensure understanding within the context of the discussion thread. A post could be coded in multiple categories. The interrater agreement was within the acceptable range (Cohen’s kappa coefficient $\kappa = 0 . 7 4 ; \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ (Landis and Koch 1977). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion. About 17% of the posts contained only “I agree” statements and were not included in further analysis.

Having coded the posts, we submitted those ideas posted as the first post in a discussion thread (what we call “top-level ideas”) 4n = 1075 to an evaluation of their innovativeness. Following Ford (1996), innovativeness was treated as an attribute of an idea that can be subjectively judged by members of a particular domain at a point in time for its novelty and value, “by specifying the target of creativity assessments, the nature of the judges providing those assessments and the domain served by those judges, this definition allow[s] creativity and innovation researchers to compare empirical findings within and across domains” (Ford 1996, p. 1116).

For each challenge, the CIO of the sponsoring organization who had championed the challenge and devised the question prompt and was aware of what other leaders in the organization would consider to be innovative ideas was asked to evaluate the set of ideas generated for that challenge. The CIOs were not aware of who posted the idea, what posts preceded the idea, and what posts followed the idea. The CIOs were asked to rate the ideas according to two criteria: novelty (defined as an idea that had not been discussed in the recent past within the organization to address the question prompt) and competitive advantage potential (defined as whether the idea, if implemented, was likely to help with the company’s advantage relative to competitors). The ratings used a 1–7 response scale (1 = not at all and 7 = to a great extent).

As the CIOs came from different organizations in various industries, we used a procedure by Lamastra (2009) to “normalize” the ratings across the five challenges. The CIOs were used as subject matter experts for defining criteria that independent research assistants could use to assess innovativeness. In this procedure, current product offerings and strategies for each organization and that of competitors were examined using the company’s website and The Gale Business Insights Complete Collection of Business and Company Resources. This examination was used to articulate repeatable heuristics that explained the logic underlying the ratings of each CIO. For example, for the tax advisory organization, the heuristic articulated for ideas rated as novel by the CIO was that the idea provided services to both employees and customers within the same mobile application. For ideas rated by the CIO as having competitive advantage potential, the heuristic was that the mobile application would increase the amount, relevance, and quality of information about tax preparation, status, and tax policy changes pushed to the customer. The heuristics were then used to train two independent research assistants. As with the CIOs, the raters were not aware of the trajectory within which the ideas were situated, who posted the idea, and the CIO’s rating of the idea. The independent raters rated each idea using the heuristics, achieving an interrater reliability for categorical data of 76.6% (Landis and Koch 1977). The ratings for the two criteria of novelty and competitive advantage were combined to create three levels of innovativeness. Therefore, the distribution of ratings across the 107 ideas were

3 = high (the idea met both criteria of competitive advantage and novelty; n = 24 ideas);

2 = medium (the idea met one or the other criteria; n = 31 ideas);

1 = low (the idea met neither criteria; n = 52 ideas).

Having rated all 107 top-level ideas, we were ready to construct the sequences leading up to a rated idea, within which hypothesized trajectories would be identified. Each sequence consisted of all posts prior to the top-level idea that had been contributed since a prior idea, ensuring that no single post appeared in more than one sequence. Across the 107 sequences, the number of posts in a sequence ranged from 2–21 posts, with a mean of 3. To ensure we included even short sequences in our analysis, we deconstructed the hypothesized trajectories into two subtrajectories. This deconstruction also afforded us the opportunity to examine the contribution of each subtrajectory separated. One trajectory—three ideas in a row—was not deconstructed since two ideas in a row did not appear to be an adequate test of the ideas-only brainstorming trajectory. The subtrajectories comprising each of our hypothesized trajectories are shown in Table 4 with the number of each subtrajectory found across all 107 sequences. The subscript notation refers to timing with reference to time t at which a top-level rated idea was contributed. Table 4 also includes the number of sequences, out of 107, containing each of our hypothesized subtrajectories. Because of the variation in sequence length, the frequencies were converted into a ratio of the total number of posts in the sequence before inclusion in the regression analysis.

Data Analysis. The unit of analysis consisted of 107 sequences, analyzed using a three-stage ordinary least squares (OLS) regression.<sup>1</sup> The dependent variable was the level of innovativeness of the idea (1 = low, 2 = medium, 3 = high), and the primary independent variables were the ratios for the subtrajectories in Table 4.

Stage 10 Control variables. The total number of participants in each of the five challenges ranged from 48 to 179 (mean = 96). Consequently, we included a control variable referred to as Participation intensity in a sequence as the number of people posting in a sequence divided by the total number of participants in the challenge. This control variable accounts for the effect that larger groups may generate more ideas and more high-quality ideas (Paulus 2000). Knowledge variety in a sequence was included as a surrogate control for diversity of participation. This control variable was operationalized as a ratio of the number of different knowledge types in a sequence to the number of participants in the challenge. We included a control for learning behavior of idea contributor. This variable was a binary indicator of whether the individual who posted the rated ideas at the end of the sequence had previously posted innovative ideas in other sequences, to assess the effect of prior activity (as suggested by Ebner et al. 2009 and Füller et al. 2014). If the individual had previously posted innovative ideas, we might conclude that a new innovative idea results from that individual’s learning effect rather than the effect of a trajectory of knowledge sharing.

The total number of posts in each of the five challenges ranged from 47 to 264 (mean = 101) with sequences obviously distributed throughout the challenge. Consequently, we included another control variable—crowd learning effects—that measured the number of previously posted innovative ideas prior to each specific sequence as a proportion of total posts in the challenge. This variable controlled for whether the crowd learning from previous innovative ideas influences the emergence of new innovative ideas. As a greater number of innovative ideas are posted over time, the probability of a new innovative idea being posted may be affected by prior ideas.

Finally, we included a control variable that measures a sequence being too highly constrained, which we call “overframing.” Overframing may impair idea generation and inherently result in less innovative solutions (Dunnette et al. 1963, Gallupe et al. 1992). We operationalized overframing as a trajectory of paradox followed by another paradox, which is then followed by a third paradox.<sup>2</sup> This variable was also normalized to the number of participants in the challenge.

## Results

Table 5 shows the descriptive statistics for the variables used in this study. The results of the regression are shown in Table 6. Apparent from Table 6, the results in stage 2 of the regression analysis indicate support for H1A. The two subtrajectories associated with the paradox-framed trajectory both have a significant positive relationship with innovativeness of the culminating idea: Paradox → Assumption $( \beta = 0 . 2 0 ;$ $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and Assumption → Idea $( \beta = 0 . 3 6 ; p < 0 . 0 1 )$ In stage 3, when entering the variables measuring the alternative trajectories, the paradox-framed subtrajectory variables remain significant. None of the variables associated with alternative trajectories have any significant relationship with innovativeness of the top-level rated ideas. This provides support for hypothesis H1B, H2A, and H2B as well. In sum, the paradox-framed trajectory is more likely to result in innovative outcomes compared to the unvalidated paradox-framed trajectory, ideas-only trajectory, and idea-framed trajectory.

Robustness Checks. We conducted several robustness checks. A test for serial correlation indicated no dependencies of residuals using the Durbin–Watson statistic and their homoscedasticity with the Breusch– Pagan test (Zeileis and Hothorn 2002). No presence of multicollinearity between predictors was found. We checked whether the innovativeness of the outcome could be attributed to the amount of each knowledge type and not to the sequence of the knowledge types. That is, we explored whether innovativeness of the rated ideas was affected simply by whether the sequence had a large number of certain knowledge types (such as initial ideas) regardless of the specific order of the knowledge types. Our results indicated that neither the number nor proportion of each knowledge type in the sequence preceding the rated idea explained a significant proportion of the variance in innovativeness.

## Discussion

Based on a sample of the discussion threads across five collaborative innovation challenges, we found that paradox-framed trajectories led to more innovative outcomes than three alternative knowledgesharing trajectories. In the paradox-framed trajectory, a paradox is shared first and then validated by an assumption, which then forms the basis for generation of initial ideas, which are then followed by an innovative outcome. The three alternative trajectories suggested in the literature on knowledge sharing in innovation contexts—unvalidated paradox framed, ideas only, and idea-framed trajectories—were outperformed by the paradox-framed trajectory. Given that these trajectories were discovered in collaborative innovation challenges in our sample in which collaboration was encouraged but none of these specific trajectories were imposed, it can be inferred that all four trajectories are emergent properties of the crowd’s knowledge sharing. Responding to a call for theorizing about how innovation occurs among the crowd (Bogers et al. 2010), we offer a theory of a knowledge-sharing trajectory in temporary online crowds that extends more general theories of knowledge creation to the online context. The theory is depicted in Figure 1 and explained below.

The context for crowd collaboration begins when a problem requires intensive interaction in the form of knowledge sharing. Subsequent to a broadcast of the problem to a large number of individuals, motivated individuals volunteer to become a member of a temporary online crowd attempting to solve the problem. One important context condition for the crowd is that individuals bring to the crowd a range of resources, such as cognitive diversity, and varying degrees of time, passion, and attention to devote to the problem (Faraj et al. 2011). The second complementary antecedent resource is a digital platform (in our case it was in the form of online discussion threads embedded in a challenge website) that enables online collaborative knowledge sharing.

With these resources, crowd members begin to contribute the knowledge they have about the problem they are solving. Goals, ideas, and assumptions about the problem are offered in no particular order by anyone who so chooses. At some point, there will be substantial representational gaps because of varying degrees of alignment between different individuals regarding goals and assumptions. The result will be an initial disorganized collage of knowledge that is difficult to evaluate, filter, understand, and build on (Majchrzak et al. 2012). This increases the potential for conflict over what knowledge the crowd should be considering and recombining to create new knowledge. In addition, conflicts may arise related to the manner in which the crowd accomplishes its task. These conflicts are often surfaced by the few crowd members most passionate about the topic or their ideas (Faraj et al. 2011).

Eventually, an individual in the crowd may integrate some of the contributed knowledge and post a paradox. This necessitates that both sides of the paradox will need to be solved, often simultaneously. By offering a paradox, diversity in the crowd about task objectives becomes more aligned (Harvey 2014). The alignment helps other individuals in the crowd realize that they can offer knowledge relevant to the paradox, which now becomes an objective for the crowd. When an individual follows the posted paradoxical objective by posting a factual or analogical assumption validating the paradox, the combination of the paradox and validating assumptions reduces knowledge representational gaps among crowd members through an increased clarity about the crowd’s newfound goal (the validated paradoxical objectives). This newly formed aligned representation about the goal then helps others in the crowd to understand how they can constructively contribute to the collaborative knowledge creation process by defining for them the constraint space and evaluation criteria for solution ideas. Because the goal is a paradoxical one, the constraints and evaluation criteria provide clarity that past solutions are unlikely to simultaneously meet conflicting objectives, creating a recognition that new ideas will be required and that these new ideas require collaborative and interdependent idea building with others in the crowd that must solve both sides of the paradox simultaneously. Ideas for solving the paradox are then collaboratively created and then reframed, reconceptualized, and recombined in novel ways.

## Theoretical Implications

Our theory of a paradox-framed trajectory in online crowds offers theoretical implications for research on online communities, knowledge creation process theories, collaborative interaction processes in online crowds, and group innovation in general.

Paradoxes represent the plurality of stakeholders and competing strategies for solving a problem (Smith and Lewis 2011). Therefore, expressing paradoxes publicly creates tensions. For research on online communities, this application of Cronin and Weingart’s (2007) group theory of knowledge representation gaps to an online context offers not simply opportunities for reconceptualizing how online communities cocreate, it also highlights unintended consequences to consider as well. Initially, it may be the case that members of a group may have contradictory knowledge (Stasser and Birchmeier 2003). Posting paradoxes that inherently are contradictory requires that one individual in the crowd publicly takes the risk to articulate what may have previously been held as implicit hidden knowledge. Therefore, making such contradictory knowledge public may expose the person and the group on the whole to destructive interpersonal conflict. For example, in solving a wicked problem associated with abortion, the paradox that preserving a fetus may lead to harming the mother’s life may be best left unsaid in public. The public traceability of the person who makes that paradox explicit may lead to personal attacks rather than innovative cocreation. Cronin and Weingart’s (2007) theory focuses only on groups, where the knowledge about who actually proposed the paradox may be difficult to remember after a long series of discussions (Majchrzak et al. 2012). However, when knowledge is persistent, as is the case when crowds use virtual workspaces to exchange knowledge, it is easy to identify the original person who made the paradox explicit. Therefore, when applying Cronin and Weingart (2007) to online communities, these additional implications should be considered.

For research on online communities, the theory adds to a growing set of studies identifying the important influence that emergent self-enacted, self-defined, inthe-moment roles play in online work.<sup>3</sup> Kane et al. (2014), for example, found several emergent roles as instrumental to the ability of a large crowd to deliberate thoughtfully in cowriting a contentious Wikipedia article. Faraj et al. (2011) argue for emergent roles that convert negative destructive tensions in the crowd into positive generative tensions. Vandal fighter, for example, has been identified as an emergent role in preserving the integrity of knowledge in Wikipedia (Gleave et al. 2009). Thom-Santelli et al. (2008) describe the emergent role of evangelist in knowledge-sharing communities as one of enhancing the reputation of the community through social tagging of knowledge created in the community. This accumulating research on emergent roles increasingly suggests that formal control structures may have their limits in knowledge-creating online communities. Instead of formal structures, enablers of emergent roles are needed. Prior experience with different selfenacted roles may be one enabler.

Our theory also extends general knowledge creation process theories, such as to the Nonaka et al. (2000) externalization (E) stage of their SECI process of knowledge creation. We suggest that, when their externalization stage consists of externalizing paradoxical objectives followed by validating and offering ideas for combination (C), the potential for cognitive conflict is greatly reduced leading to more innovative recombinations of existing knowledge. The paradox-framed trajectory proposed by our research can also be viewed as one way in which a crowd moves through Carlile’s (2004) syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic knowledge transformation stages. A validated paradoxical objective helps to focus the discussion on a semantically agreeable pragmatic aspect of the problem. This limits any conflict in the crowd to just that knowledge that contributes to solving the paradox. Our findings also help elaborate Tsoukas’s (2009) knowledgecreation process by explaining how knowledge can become “distanciated” from one’s personal self and become a part of a shared “intersubjective” meaning between relative strangers. By sharing validated paradoxes first, the subsequent idea contributions become interpreted as distanced from one’s personal preferences since they are now perceived as a part of the validated paradox.

Our theory of a paradox-framed trajectory also extends research on social processes in online crowds. Previous studies have demonstrated the importance of both pecuniary and nonpecuniary rewards in encouraging crowd participation, particularly with uncertain problems (Boudreau et al. 2011, Franke and von Hippel 2003). These nonpecuniary rewards have been found to include social processes such as perceived indispensability to a group (Hertel et al. 2003) and a sense of community (Bagozzi and Dholakia 2006, Hutter et al. 2011). It may be that the paradoxical framing trajectory provides the means for creating a sense of perceived indispensability since the validated paradox clarifies how an individual can contribute. Moreover, the paradoxical framing trajectory may serve as a much more subtle form of what von Krogh et al. (2003) refer to as joining scripts, i.e., cues to new crowd members about how to behave in the crowd.

The theory of a paradox-framed trajectory proposed in this research also questions certain existing assumptions in the literature on open innovation and crowdsourcing. First, our research provides evidence that an “idea-first” trajectory of crowd cocreation is less innovative. There is substantial research in face-to-face groups echoing our findings pertaining to crowds; however, in practice, the crowd platforms reviewed by Majchrzak and Malhotra (2013) and Leimeister et al. (2009) are limited to brainstorming. We hope that our findings will lead to a de-emphasis in the online crowd literature on idea generation and increase the focus on appropriate knowledge-sharing behaviors.

A second tenet in the literature questioned by our findings is whether crowds can be used for innovation tasks at all. Innovation tasks generally require solving an ill-structured problem in its entirety and through intense interactions between parties engaged in solving the problem. Wicked problems are characterized by interdependent and nondecomposable elements (Buchanan 1992, Pacanowsky 1996). Therefore, wicked problems are generally solved by integrating multiple perspectives (Harvey 2014). Afuah and Tucci (2012) suggest that, unless problems can be modularized, easily broadcast by an organization, and understood by potential crowd members, the probability of a crowd solving the problem would be greatly reduced. Wicked problems have a great deal of implicit knowledge associated with them (Kogut and Zander 1992, Levinthal and Warglien 1999). Therefore, Afuah and Tucci (2012) argue that such problems may be untenable for crowd solving. Therefore, based on Afuah and Tucci’s (2012) view, wicked problems requiring innovation can NOT be solved by crowds because of the organizations’ inability to transmit the problem by breaking it down into modules that can be solved independently by individuals.

Our results are in contrast to this second tenet. Based on our findings, we posit that this emphasis in the literature on modularizing tasks to accomplish distant search may not be needed if the proper knowledge-sharing trajectory is followed in the crowd. The knowledge trajectory needed requires modularizing knowledge, and not tasks. This knowledge modularization is done for the purpose of integration, not to separate out crowd members. Felin and Zenger (2014) and Boudreau and Lakhani (2009) have similarly suggested that, when an innovative problem is wicked, involving hidden skill beyond that of an individual, existing collaborative communities with norms of behavior and broad agreements should be used. Our findings modify this suggestion by no longer requiring that the crowd constitute a “community” with shared norms, long-term associations, and agreements. Instead, our findings suggest that wicked problems can be solved innovatively in temporary online crowds, if the crowd is allowed to share their knowledge in a manner that a paradox-framed trajectory emerges. The existence of these trajectories emerging as implicit coordination mechanisms provides, then, a new organizational design theory for explaining such new organizational forms as temporary online crowds, in contrast to Puranam et al. (2014), claims that such new theories are not needed.

Our model of knowledge sharing in temporary online crowds may have implications for organizations in general, particularly with respect to the literature on paradoxes in organizations. Smith and Lewis (2011) point out that paradoxical tensions in organizations are nested and cascade across multiple levels. Our model offers implications about the cross-level relationship between organizational-level paradoxes and individually perceived paradoxes, and therefore a perspective on the literature on the effect of organizational paradoxes on organizational effectiveness. In that organizational literature, paradoxes are seen as a core element of an organization, ensuring that the organization attains both stability and change. The dynamic equilibrium model suggested by Smith and Lewis (2011) incorporates paradoxes as the source of conflicting yet purposeful forces that require an organizational response that may be cyclical over time but create organizational sustainability. Organizational theory proposes that addressing paradoxes requires an ambidextrous organizational form (Andriopoulos and Lewis 2010), in which one side of the organization undertakes disruption and the other side maintains the stable production process. Our model suggests an alternative “resolution” to paradoxes—a resolution that takes the paradox down to the level of individuals solving problems. In our view, ambidextrous organizational design is replaced with crowdsourced problem solving, where individuals reflect the organizational-level paradox in the problem being solved, and then take the necessary creative steps to resolve the paradox. Individual actors in organizations who possess behavioral and cognitive complexity, and emotional equanimity have been argued to be more receptive than defensive of tensions in paradoxes (Smith and Lewis 2011). In a crowd, it is easier to find individuals who possess cognitive and behavioral complexity, and emotional equanimity required to integrate the tensions inherent in paradoxes into solutions. Therefore, instead of paradoxes being resolved by separated departments or task differentiation, they are resolved uniquely for each problem by combining the knowledge in a crowd (von Hippel and von Krogh 2003). This is a much different model of organizing than the task sub divisions proposed by Puranam et al. (2014).

## Implications for Future Research

The fact that different individuals in a crowd contribute different knowledge in sequences has numerous implications for research on a crowd as a new organizational form. First, it suggests that, in user innovation communities, users may need to play a variety of different roles, not simply that of an idea initiator.<sup>4</sup> Previous research has tended to overemphasize the idea initiator role (von Hippel and von Krogh 2003). User innovation community participants are needed to identify paradoxes, validate paradoxes, and repeatedly offer initial ideas to build toward more innovative ideas. In fact, it may be more innovative for the user innovation community if nonuser participants become involved because any single user may have only personally experienced one side of the paradox and may be too deeply a specialist to agree that an alternative side of the paradox exists.

In private-public collectives such as open-source software development (von Hippel and von Krogh 2003), research suggests that the community-based knowledge creation process proceeds as a process of challenges to the status quo. This is then followed by suggestions and critical evaluations of ideas (Lee and Cole 2003). However, it may be that in framing the process as one of status quo and challenges, researchers have ignored a more nuanced process. It may be that a challenge to the status quo that explicates the underlying paradox, rather than simply disagreeing with the status quo, fosters more innovation.

As a nascent study of knowledge-sharing behavior in a temporary online crowd, this study suffers from limitations that can become a focus of future research. The patterns that emerged across five different collaborative innovation challenges in very diverse companies in a variety of industries for very different organizations may hint at some generalizability. However, much more research in diverse contexts is needed. Moreover, we did not examine crowds varying in different levels of temporariness and thus our generalizations to online communities may not be borne out. The interplay between the context and emergence of trajectories offers a rich avenue for future research. In this vein, future research should explore if the results are robust for truly large-scale (1,000’s) crowds.

Researchers have hinted at the importance of who shares the knowledge in crowds (e.g., Dahlander and Frederiksen 2012, Malhotra and Majchrzak 2014). Therefore, future research is needed to integrate the paradoxical framing trajectory with existing research findings on who shares each of the knowledge types in the paradox-framed trajectory. The backgrounds of the various contributors in the crowd may offer a greater understanding of when different trajectories do or do not emerge. Who contributes validating facts and analogies? Do they differ from those who integrate ideas to address validated paradoxes? Integrating research on behavioral tendencies with knowledge-sharing behavior may yield greater insights into how innovation can be affected in crowds. Similarly, integrating research on incentives for collaboration in innovation contest communities (e.g., Hutter et al. 2011, Leimeister et al. 2009) with an appropriate trajectory may surface the need for dynamic incentives. Such dynamic incentivizing may be targeted to the specific knowledge type needed at that point in time to move the trajectory forward. Incentives of multiple forms may need to be offered upfront at the start of a challenge.

Substantial research is also needed for how to design platforms that promote the knowledge-sharing trajectory suggested by this research. Should functionality encourage explicit highlighting of contributed and validated paradoxes such as in the form of tag clouds and/or voting flags? Future research may benefit by conducting experiments to see how combinations of platform and incentive designs interact to affect the desired knowledge-sharing trajectory. Finally, future research should look at more dimensions of innovation. It is possible that some alternative sequences might be better for dimensions such as organizational acceptability of the innovation.

## Managerial Implications

Based on our findings, innovation managers intending to leverage crowd collaboration for their innovation benefit must encourage the crowd to share the right knowledge in the right order. This “right” knowledge is not simply in the form of ideas, since our research indicates that ideas too early in the process do not support innovation. While there may be many ways in which managers can encourage crowds to behave in this fashion, one suggestion is for managers to solicit crowd members to model and moderate the collaboration. The moderator could model the types of knowledge by offering paradoxes and facts early during a challenge. Blogs could be maintained on the challenge website that point out paradoxes emerging from the collaboration that the crowd can then adopt if desired. The moderator can contribute assumptions either in the conversation or on the same blog, encouraging the crowd to follow her lead. By modeling the knowledge contributions via the blog or even by seeding the discussion through hints and questions, the crowd is encouraged to hold off generating ideas until paradoxes are surfaced and highlighted. The platform can also be designed to conduct semantic analysis in real time of the knowledge being contributed. Such semantic analysis will help identify which types are missing given the point in the trajectory of the crowd. A human or bot moderator to explicitly encourage the crowd to contribute the missing or low volume knowledge elements can then use the results of such a semantic analysis. Employees involved in challenges can also be trained to become aware of the types of knowledge and the particular order in which they need to contribute to foster greater innovation. Overall, managers should help the crowd understand that everyone in the crowd— beyond those few with a few initial ideas—have something to contribute.

## Conclusion

This research is one of the earliest to focus on the trajectory of knowledge sharing in a temporary online crowd. In doing so, it extends prior research that has focused on participant motivation in crowds. Even the most motivated crowds may not create desired innovative outcomes if they fail to emerge with a paradoxframed trajectory for sharing their knowledge. This research identifies the importance of not just sharing certain types of knowledge, but also sharing the knowledge in a certain temporal order. It also indicates that temporary online crowds with little history can emerge with trajectories leading to innovative outcomes. The intent of this research is to refocus the “wisdom of the crowd” research not simply on having wise crowd members independently offer ideas, but on collective knowledge-sharing sequences that together create innovative outcomes.
