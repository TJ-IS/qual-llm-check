# PRIVACY CONCERNS AND DATA DONATIONS: DO SOCIETAL BENEFITS MATTER?<sup>1</sup>

Tawfiq Alashoor Department of Operations, Information and Technology, IESE Business School, University of Navarra, Barcelona, SPAIN {talashoor@iese.edu}

Mark Keil Department of Computer Information Systems, J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, GA, U.S.A. {mkeil@gsu.edu}

Zhenhui (Jack) Jiang Area of Innovation and Information Management, HKU Business School, The University of Hong Kong, HONG KONG {jiangz@hku.hk}

Kambiz Saffarizadeh Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, College of Business, University of Texas at Arlington, TX, U.S.A {kambiz.saffari@uta.edu}

Data donations, where individuals are encouraged to donate their personal information, have the potential to advance medical research and help limit the spread of pandemics, among other benefits. The decision to donate data is fundamentally a privacy decision. In this research, we build on the privacy calculus, a model describing privacy risks and benefits, and examine the impact of privacy concerns on data donation decisions, highlighting the role of societal benefits in privacy decisions. Based on two randomized experiments using the general context of data donation for medical research (Experiment 1) and the specific context of data donation for COVID-19 research (Experiment 2), we find that individuals who are highly concerned about privacy tend to donate less data (Experiments 1 and 2). This effect holds under a variety of conditions and is consistent with prevailing research. However, this effect is contingent on the privacy calculus. When implicit or explicit societal benefits are perceived, particularly in the absence of privacy controls, the association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions is less salient, highlighting the significant role that societal benefits play in privacy decisions. We discuss the theoretical, practical, social, and ethical implications of these findings.

Keywords: Privacy concerns, data donation, public health, privacy calculus, information disclosure, COVID-19, societal impact, behavioral experiment

## Introduction

“How much privacy would one be willing to give up to save one life … ten lives … one thousand lives … one million lives?” Angst (2009, p. 176). This question, posed more than 15 years ago, is of even greater importance today in the age of information privacy concerns and behavioral manipulations (Acquisti et al., 2015; Jarovsky, 2022; Sunstein, 2022; Rosenberg, 2023; Xu &

Dinev, 2022). Inspired by the idea of organ donation, the medical community is promoting data donation, whereby individuals are encouraged to donate their personal information, including medical, demographic, and behavioral data, for medical research (Shaw et al., 2015, 2016; Taylor & Mandl, 2015; Topol, 2015). Data donation has been mentioned as part of a precision medicine initiative announced in the U.S.<sup>2</sup> and has received attention at medical conferences, in webinars and media stories, and among scholars (Ågerfalk et al., 2020; Garber, 2015; Krutzinna & Floridi, 2019; Payne, 2017).<sup>3</sup>

Although little is known about the drivers and inhibitors of data donation decisions (Krutzinna & Floridi, 2019), the concept of data donation is gaining traction and there are a number of initiatives in this domain. For example, Open Humans, sponsored by multiple nonprofit healthcare organizations, is a data donation project that was launched in 2015.<sup>4</sup> A more recent example is Count Me In, a nonprofit cancer research initiative that encourages colorectal cancer patients to share their data with the research community (Noble, 2021). The COVID-19 pandemic gave rise to more data donation projects. For example, Germany initiated several data donation projects to complement other strategies aimed at containing the spread of SARS-CoV-2.<sup>5</sup>

Despite potential societal benefits, such as advancing medical research and limiting the spread of pandemics (Mandl et al., 2015), data donation also represents a significant risk to personal privacy, the cost of which is hard to estimate or predict (Acquist et al., 2020). Perhaps not surprisingly, many people are reluctant to donate their data (Krutzinna & Floridi, 2019), which may explain why some data donation initiatives have ceased operation (e.g., datadonors.org, donatehealthdata.com, and ourdatahelps.org). When presented with the choice to donate, individuals must decide whether to provide access to their personal data for medical research. Privacy concerns, especially in relation to medical information, can explain why some people refuse to donate their data (Ashford, 2016; Knapton, 2016; Sheehan & Hoy, 2000; Skatova & Goulding, 2019). Data donation advocates also agree that such initiatives are infeasible unless privacy concerns are addressed (Mandl et al., 2015; Shaw et al., 2016; Taylor & Mandl, 2015; Topol, 2015). Further, existing evidence suggests that people who have high privacy concerns donate less data for medical research purposes (Krutzinna & Floridi, 2019). This is consistent with the negative association between privacy concerns and disclosure decisions observed in the privacy literature (Smith et al., 2011).

Privacy concerns in the data donation context are likely to be consistent with those documented in other domains such as social media and the internet (Hong & Thong, 2013; Krasnova et al., 2010). However, the potential personal privacy risks associated with data donation could be substantial, particularly in the absence of legal frameworks and appropriate technical infrastructure (Hummel et al., 2019), which would increase the likelihood and potential severity of privacy risks to the donor (Karwatzki et al., 2022). Moreover, the success of data donation projects depends on those with privacy concerns being willing to help society despite risks to their personal privacy (Bietz et al., 2019; Skatova & Goulding, 2019; Taylor & Mandl, 2015; Topol, 2015). This trade-off highlights the unique nature of data donation, where people weigh the potential risk to their personal privacy against their desire to benefit society.

The trade-off in this context can result in the disclosure of a broad set of personal information (both health-related and non-healthrelated), some of which may be highly sensitive. From the donor’s perspective, the perceived benefits of data donation generally accrue to society (e.g., improvements in public health); any direct personal benefits tend to be less tangible. In contrast, the potential privacy risks of data donation directly impact the individual. This trade-off contrasts with typical privacy decisions, such as the decision to disclose personal information on social media (Krasnova et al., 2010), where both the risks and benefits are to the individual. Because it precipitates an individual’s consideration of societal along with personal benefits—and hence a broader range of net benefits—the context of data donation requires further testing of existing privacy theory.

The literature demonstrates that the association between privacy concerns and disclosure decisions is moderated by various factors (Alashoor et al., 2023b; Dinev et al., 2015). This association can be more complicated in the context of data donation given the societal benefits perceived from the donation act. Despite implications discussed in previous research (e.g., Angst, 2009), the societal aspect remains underexplored in the literature.<sup>6</sup> To contextualize this association and examine factors that can moderate it, we turn to the privacy calculus, a well-established model that explains privacy risks and benefits (Klopfer & Rubenstein, 1977; Laufer & Wolfe, 1977).

Previous research on the privacy calculus has primarily focused on the direct impacts of privacy risks and benefits on disclosure decisions. However, in the context of data donation, the dynamics among these and other factors are complex, as indicated by recent studies (Hillebrand et al., 2023; Pfiffner & Friemel, 2023). For example, while perceived risks of data leaks from different types of organizations (e.g., academic, government, and private industry) can diminish the willingness to donate personal data, this effect varies depending on both the data type and the organization (Hillebrand et al., 2023; Pfiffner & Friemel, 2023). In addition, while perceived societal benefits do not necessarily increase the willingness to donate (Hillebrand et al., 2023), perceived behavioral control over data donation processes can enhance it (Pfiffner & Friemel, 2023). We move beyond the direct effects and ask whether the presence of societal benefits and protective privacy controls (which provide a measure of perceived behavioral control) could attenuate the usual negative impact of privacy concerns on disclosure decisions. Thus, we explore whether a privacy calculus that incorporates societal benefits alongside personal risks and benefits moderates the association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions. This approach is especially relevant in data donation contexts, which involve considerations that extend beyond personal outcomes, highlighting the need to assess how broader societal benefits might influence individua privacy decisions beyond their direct effects.

RQ: How do societal benefits alongside personal risks and benefits moderate the impact of privacy concerns on data donation decisions?

To address our research question, we designed two data donation mobile apps, DataDonors (an app mockup) and Fight COVID-19 (an app simulation) and conducted two experiments. In Experiment 1, we used the general context of data donation for medical research, and in Experiment 2 we used the specific context of data donation for COVID-19 research. Our findings suggest that individuals who are highly concerned about privacy tend to donate less data for medical research (Experiments 1 and 2). However, this association depends on the privacy calculus. Specifically, when (1) societal benefits—either implicit or explicit<sup>7</sup>—are not perceived and (2) when privacy risk protections through privacy controls are absent, high privacy concerns are associated with low data donations (Experiments 1 and 2). This association suggests that under these conditions individuals’ privacy concerns align with their privacy decisions, consistent with findings from the privacy literature. However, this association does not hold when societal benefits are induced implicitly through empathic concern (Experiment 1) or explicitly by directly informing individuals about benefits to others (Experiment 2). The moderating effect of societal benefits is also conditional on the presence of privacy controls. Overall, our findings indicate that the association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions is significantly weakened by factors involved in the privacy calculus, suggesting that the impact of privacy concerns on data donation decisions becomes less salient when societal benefits or privacy controls are in play.

This study contributes to the literature in the following ways. First, we account for societal benefits in the trade-off that individuals consider when making a privacy decision. The rationale is that in the context of data donation, the privacy calculus is different because societal benefits need to be considered alongside personal benefits. This consideration informs the privacy calculus literature, where only personal benefits have typically been examined. Along with societal benefits, we theorize and test the moderating effect of privacy controls to deepen our understanding of the association between privacy concerns and disclosure decisions in the context of data donation. In doing so, we contribute to the literature emphasizing the moderating effect of various factors on the association between privacy concerns and privacy decisions (Dinev et al., 2015). Second, we address the call for more empirical research on data donation (Krutzinna & Floridi, 2019; Skatova & Goulding, 2019) by investigating how the risk and benefit trade-off can alter the negative effect of privacy concerns on data donation decisions. Third, we extend prior research on data donation by moving beyond intention-based measures (Skatova & Goulding, 2019), focusing instead on actual data donation behaviors.

## Theoretical Background

Information privacy is a complex and multifaceted concept, and its meaning and value depend on the context and time in which it is being applied (Acquisti et al., 2015, 2020; Alashoor, 2024 Alashoor et al., 2023a; Solove, 2021). Practically, privacy is often associated with activities with tangible consequences such as information collection, processing, dissemination, and invasion (Smith et al., 1996; Solove, 2006). Additionally, privacy is linked to ethics, as it is embedded within society’s moral values and justice considerations (Angst, 2009; Culnan & Bies, 2003; Culnan & Williams, 2009; Leidner & Tona, 2021). Recent research supports the multifaceted nature of privacy and the efficacy of using measurable proxies, such as privacy concerns, for theoretical and empirical investigations (Xu & Dinev, 2022).

## Privacy Concerns

Privacy concerns generally refer to “concerns about possible loss of privacy as a result of information disclosure to a specific external agent” (Xu et al., 2011, p. 800), such as a data donation app. Karwatzki et al. (2022) suggest that this concept reflects perceptions regarding the handling of personal information by others. Privacy concerns have been defined and studied in various ways and both unidimensional and multidimensional approaches have been taken towards the measurement of this construct (e.g., Dinev & Hart, 2006; Hong & Thong, 2013; Malhotra et al., 2004; Smith et al., 1996; Zhang et al., 2022).

In this study, we adopt a unidimensional approach to privacy concerns, focusing on the overall level of individuals apprehensions rather than on individual dimensions. We define privacy concerns as individuals’ apprehensions about potential privacy loss resulting from donating personal information for medical research. This definition aligns with the conceptualization by Dinev and Hart (2006), which provided one of the most widely used scales for measuring privacy concerns (Bartol et al., 2023). Additionally, our approach is consistent with previous research that emphasizes core theoretical constructs, including privacy concerns, risks and benefits, and disclosure decisions (Alashoor et al., 2023b; Xu et al., 2011).

Privacy concerns have been documented repeatedly in public polls and published research (Acquisti & Gross, 2006; Choi et al., 2015; Auxier, 2020; Auxier et al., 2019). These concerns have been shown to affect several outcomes (e.g., privacyprotective responses and disclosure intentions and behaviors) (for reviews, see Li, 2011; Li, 2012; Smith et al., 2011; Yu et al., 2020; Yun et al., 2014, 2019). The association between privacy concerns and these outcomes is also contingent on contextual factors (Alashoor et al., 2023b; Baruh et al., 2017; Anderson & Agarwal, 2011; Kehr et al., 2015; Mothersbaugh et al., 2012; Wakefield, 2013). Based on this literature, it can be reasonably inferred that privacy concerns have a negative impact on the disclosure of personal information. However, it is not known how this association is influenced by the privacy risk-benefit analysis, particularly in the context of data donation in which the analysis involves societal benefits. The prior literature is consistent regarding the positive (negative) main effect of perceived personal benefits (risks) on decisions to exchange personal information. Yet, how the perceived personal benefits, and particularly the societal benefits, moderate the association between privacy concerns and decisions to exchange personal information represents an area where further research is needed. We explore this in the context of data donation and use the privacy calculus model as our main theoretical lens.

## Privacy Calculus

The privacy calculus is a well-established model (Klopfer & Rubenstein, 1977; Laufer & Wolfe, 1977) that forms the foundation for a significant portion of contemporary privacy theories and empirical research (Acquisti et al., 2020; Culnan & Armstrong, 1999; Dinev & Hart, 2006; Dinev et al., 2015; Jarovsky, 2022; Leidner & Tona, 2021; Smith et al., 2011). It posits that privacy decisions are influenced by the perceived risks and benefits associated with disclosing or protecting personal information (Acquisti et al., 2016). This model has proven crucial for understanding individual-level privacy decisions (Culnan & Bies, 2003; Dinev & Hart, 2006), suggesting that people weigh potential privacy risks against expected personal benefits before making a privacy decision. If the anticipated benefits surpass the potential privacy risks, individuals are more likely to exchange personal information for a perceived benefit (e.g., sharing personal information on a fitness app to enhance personal health). Extensive research supports the privacy calculus, and this line of inquiry has significantly contributed to privacy theory (Acquisti et al., 2016, 2020; Baruh et al., 2017).<sup>8</sup>

In the privacy calculus empirical literature, perceived benefits are often assumed to be purely personal, while in reality, individuals sometimes share personal information to benefit society as a whole, with or without the expectation of direct personal gains (Kordzadeh & Warren, 2017; Son & Kim, 2008). In other words, previous research has typically focused on the trade-off between personal privacy risks and personal benefits (Table 1). This assumption may hold true in some contexts such as health fitness or dating apps (context #1), where individuals, despite potential privacy risks, share personal information primarily for personal gains, like improving their health or finding a partner (Barth & de Jong, 2017). However, this assumption is less applicable in other contexts like PatientsLikeMe or Open Humans (context #2), where individuals, despite potential privacy risks, share sensitive personal health information mainly for the greater societal good, such as helping patients or contributing to medical research (Angst, 2009; Kordzadeh & Warren, 2017). Privacy risks in context #1 and context #2 are relatively similar, and in each case, they fall upon the individual. In contrast, benefits in context #1 are more individual-centric, while in context #2, they are oriented towards the larger society. Moreover, potential personal benefits are more explicit and immediate in context #1 (e.g., finding a partner) but more implicit and distant in context #2 (e.g., experiencing warm glow feelings for helping researchers discover a potential cancer cure).

For more than two decades, researchers have predominantly studied privacy decisions in contexts where individuals receive personal benefits from sharing their information. Although some research supports the significant effect of social benefits (e.g., perceived reciprocity) on self-disclosure among online community and social media users (Posey et al., 2010; Yu et al., 2015), as shown in Table 1, most studies have tested the privacy calculus without accounting for the societal aspect. Wang et al. (2017) indicated that social capital gains (which are related to the societal aspect but still considered personal gains) are more powerful than purely personal monetary rewards in influencing disclosure intention. Furthermore, Choi et al. (2018) found that the effect of perceived risks and benefits interact with the level of privacy concerns in social media disclosures, highlighting the moderating role of the privacy calculus. Building on this literature, we apply the privacy calculus to data donation, a context in which the net benefits encompass a broader range, including both personal and societal gains.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

Figure 1 depicts our research model which predicts that privacy concerns impact data donation decisions, consistent with findings from the privacy and data donation literature (Krutzinna & Floridi, 2019; Smith et al., 2011). More specific to our research question, the model also predicts that the association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions will be influenced by factors involved in the privacy calculus (i.e., privacy controls and societal benefits).

Prior privacy research has shown that privacy controls are associated with perceived privacy risk and protection (Adjerid et al., 2018; Brandimarte et al., 2013; Cavusoglu et al., 2016; Dinev & Hart, 2006). Conceptually, privacy controls are integral to managing the risks associated with personal data exposure, aligning closely with the risk aspect of the privacy calculus. Therefore, we employ privacy controls to represent the risk aspect in our research model. Privacy controls and privacy concerns are distinct constructs (Xu et al., 2011).<sup>9</sup> However, their interaction effect is likely to be salient in the data donation context. For instance, privacy protection enabled through privacy controls may attenuate the impact of privacy concerns people have especially in prosocial contexts.

For the benefit aspect, we introduce societal benefit, which reflects the extent to which individuals perceive the societal benefits of data donation, either implicitly (e.g., through empathic concern) or explicitly (e.g., by directly informing donors about these benefits). Considering both implicit and explicit societal benefits is important for broadening our theoretical understanding of the benefit mechanism. Specifically, implicit societal benefits allow us to examine situations in which societal benefits are perceived indirectly, such as through empathic concern leading to actions beneficial to society (Batson, 2011). In contrast, explicit societal benefits allow us to examine situations in which people are transparently informed about the societal impact of their data donation. In summary, the research model draws on the privacy calculus and employs privacy controls and societal benefits as constructs that may offset the established negative association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions.

## Privacy Concerns and Data Donation Decisions

Several research studies provide evidence of the negative effect of privacy concerns on health information disclosures (Bansal et al., 2016; Fox & Connolly, 2018; Li et al., 2014). Based on a random sample of patients drawn from a medical center in Taiwan, Kuo et al. (2014) found that the negative effect of privacy concerns regarding collection, secondary use, and errors increase individuals’ intentions to pursue privacy-protective responses (e.g., intention to request removal of personal information from electronic health record (EHR) systems). Li et al. (2014) provided additional evidence on the negative effect of privacy concerns on intentions to adopt stand-alone personal health record systems. Further, recent COVID-19 research supports the negative impact of privacy concerns on the use of contact tracing apps (Chan & Saqib, 2021; Lin et al., 2021).

In our context, a data donation decision refers to an individual’s choice to disclose various types of personal information, such as medical, demographic, and behavioral data, and grant access to this information for the purpose of medical research. Organizations that seek to advance medical research and public health based on a data donation model cannot realize their goals unless individuals are willing to provide access to their personal information. Since health data represent one of the most sensitive types of personal information, individuals are likely to be highly concerned about how their data are used and shared by healthcare and non-healthcare entities (Libert, 2015; Westin, 2005). These concerns affect individuals’ willingness to have their health data digitized and accessed by health entities. Thus, individuals’ privacy concerns are likely to be an inhibitor in our context.

H1: Privacy concerns will be negatively associated with data donation decisions.

Testing H1 allowed us to assess the main effect of privacy concerns on data donation decisions. This effect is likely observable based on findings from existing literature. However, it remains unclear how this effect is moderated by the perceived privacy controls and, specifically, societal benefits arising from sharing personal information in the unique context of data donation. Although only a few studies have explored factors that moderate the association between privacy concerns and information disclosure in the health domain (Anderson & Agarwal, 2011; Angst & Agarwal, 2009), their findings support the context-dependent nature of privacy decisions (Acquisti et al., 2015; Xu & Zhang, 2022). Next, we develop hypotheses to examine the moderating role of privacy controls and societal benefits.

## Privacy Controls

Privacy control is defined “as a perceptual construct reflecting an individual’s beliefs in his or her ability to manage the release and dissemination of personal information” (Xu et al., 2011, p. 798). The ability to control personal information is a key component of information privacy (Solove, 2006; Westin, 2003). According to the literature, the potential loss resulting from revealing personal information can impact individuals willingness to share personal information (Smith et al., 2011). This potential loss can be mitigated through risk protection mechanisms such as privacy controls (Adjerid et al., 2018; Brandimarte et al., 2013).<sup>10</sup>

The level of privacy control is likely to be a significant factor in moderating the association between privacy concerns and data donation. In the absence of privacy controls, individuals make their private data vulnerable to unknown risks such as increased insurance rates and identity theft when they donate their data (Krutzinna & Floridi, 2019; Topol, 2015). Privacy controls, serving as a risk protection mechanism, may weaken the negative effect of privacy concerns on data donation decisions. This is because privacy controls are tangible measures allowing individuals to manage access, use, and sharing of their data, as opposed to privacy statements which are often not read or understood (Li & Slee, 2014). Therefore, a rational assessment of the level of protection provided by privacy controls can relegate the influence of stated privacy concerns to the background, making them less central in data donation decisions. Considering the conceptual relationship between privacy controls and privacy concerns, we posit that the more control individuals have, the less likely their privacy concerns will be associated with their data donation decisions because of the rational assessment of the provided control over donated data.

H2: The negative association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions will be weaker when privacy controls are provided to donors.

## Implicit and Explicit Societal Benefits

Societal benefits can be either implicit or explicit. Implicit societal benefits are perceived through subtle cues that can evoke the societal impact of data donation, such as when empathy is induced for a particular cause. Explicit societal benefits are perceived through transparent communication aimed at highlighting the societal impact of data donation. In data donation contexts like Open Humans, societal benefits are often more significant than personal ones. Therefore, the benefit aspect within the privacy calculus in this context is dominantly societal. Next, we discuss empathic concern as an implicit representation of societal benefits and informing individuals about the societal benefits of data donation as a more explicit method to emphasize these benefits. Then, we hypothesize the moderating role of implicit and explicit societal benefits on the negative association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions.

Empathic concern is a primary determinant of altruism (Batson, 2011; Batson et al., 2009). The theory of altruistic motivation posits that “feeling other-oriented emotion elicited by and congruent with the perceived welfare of another person in need (i.e., empathic concern) produces a motivational state with the ultimate goal of increasing that person’s welfare by having the empathy-inducing need removed (i.e., altruistic motivation)” (Batson, 2011, p. 29). This is the empathyaltruism hypothesis in which a motivational state with the ultimate goal of increasing another’s welfare is reached if an individual imagines a desirable change in the other person’s world and experiences a force to make some change (Batson, 2011). In a series of experiments, Batson et al. (2011) showed strong support for the empathy-altruism hypothesis by ruling out alternative explanations, such as self-benefit (for review, see Batson, 2011). When empathic concern is induced, individuals tend to be motivated to help the person in need from a purely altruistic motivation. We adapt the empathyaltruism hypothesis to our context, which involves helping multiple stakeholders such as patients and medical researchers through the donation of personal data.

Empathic concern leads individuals to consider the collective good (Batson et al., 1995; Velasquez, 2011), and research shows that individuals donate personal data for social duty reasons (Skatova & Goulding, 2019). We argue that societal benefits, perceived through an implicit means such as empathic concern, can weaken the association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions. The rationale behind this argument is that when individuals experience empathic concern, they are more likely to focus on the needs of others and the collective good (Toi & Batson, 1982; Williams et al., 2014), potentially diverting their attention from effortful processing of their own privacy concerns (Dinev et al., 2015). Consequently, the influence of privacy concerns on data donation decisions is weakened when empathic concern is at play, as the altruistic motivation to contribute to the greater good may offset individuals’ concerns about personal privacy.

Although empathic concern assumes pure altruism (Batson, 2011), the economic perspective on altruism presents an alternative view. In this perspective, individuals may experience both pure and impure altruism, with the latter involving self-benefit outcomes (Andreoni, 1990, 1995; Harbaugh, 1998; Nunes & Schokkaert, 2003; Stahl & Haruvy, 2006). For instance, while experienced donors exhibit pure altruism compared to first-time donors who show impure altruism, evidence still indicates that experienced donors experience warm glow feelings from donating, which reflects a self-benefit outcome (Ferguson et al., 2012). In relation to our context, empirical evidence demonstrates that the human brain’s reward system is activated when sharing personal information (Tamir & Mitchell, 2012), supporting the notion of impure altruism (i.e., the economic perspective) in privacy altruistic decisions. Therefore, we also consider this perspective in our research.

We approached the pure (Batson, 2011) and impure (Andreoni, 1990, 1995) perspectives by examining the impact of explicit societal benefits in relation to the effect of explicit personal benefits, which are the typical references in the privacy literature (Table 1). This allowed us to compare the moderating role of societal and personal benefits. In line with our reasoning for the moderating role of implicit societal benefits, we anticipate that explicit societal benefits will also weaken the negative association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions, as they explicitly highlight the prosocial aspect of data donation.

H3: The negative association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions will be weaker when donors perceive the societal benefits of data donation (a) implicitly or (b) explicitly.

## Privacy Concerns, Privacy Controls, and Societal Benefits

We also expect that privacy concerns will be most predictive of data donation decisions when the privacy calculus does not provide a means to maximize expected societal benefits and minimize expected privacy risks associated with the donation decision. Previous research supports the interaction effect of privacy concerns, privacy risks, and benefits on the odds of accepting friends on social network websites (Choi et al., 2018). In our context, consider a situation in which societal benefits are not perceived, and privacy controls are absent. In this case, the privacy calculus does not motivate individuals to provide personal information because the expected benefit of helping society through data donation is low, and the potential of losing control over personal information is high. Under such circumstances, we expect the negative association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions to be salient. The absence of privacy controls and societal benefits means that privacy concerns become the primary basis upon which individuals make their data donation decisions (Dinev et al., 2015).

In contrast, we predict that the association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions is attenuated when societal benefits are perceived and/or privacy controls are provided. When individuals perceive societal benefits, effective privacy controls, or both, they are less likely to base their data donation decisions solely on privacy concerns. Our reasoning stems from individuals’ ability to recognize the utility of data donations in contributing to societal welfare and/or the effectiveness of privacy controls upon making a data donation decision. As a result, individuals with high privacy concerns may still be inclined to donate data when they perceive substantial societal benefits, robust privacy controls, or both.

H4: The negative association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions will be weaker (stronger) in the presence (absence) of privacy controls and/or implicit or explicit societal benefits.

We conducted two experiments to test our hypotheses. In both experiments, we measured privacy concerns and data donations. Table 2 presents the definition of each construct and how they were operationalized.

## Method

We designed two data donation mobile apps for our experiments: DataDonors (a mockup used in Experiment 1) and Fight COVID-19 (a simulation used in Experiment 2). In our experiments, we manipulated privacy controls (Experiments 1 and 2) and either empathic concern (Experiment 1) or explicit information about societal benefits (Experiment 2). We conducted two distinct experiments to explore the impact of societal benefits on data donation decisions in varying contexts while replicating the impact of privacy controls.

The first experiment focuses on implicit societal benefits through empathic concern, utilizing both images and text to evoke emotional responses. Conversely, the second experiment emphasizes explicit societal benefits, informing participants directly about the societal impact of their data donations within a simulated app environment, “Fight COVID-19.” This approach enhances the ecological validity of our findings by mimicking real-world data donation scenarios. Together, these experiments offer complementary insights, with the first providing an understanding of the role of implicit societal benefits and the second offering a more practical examination in a realistic setting. Conducting two separate experiments also strengthens the methodological rigor and robustness of our study, ensuring the validity of our conclusions across different experimental setups and contexts. This multiple-experiment approach enriches our understanding of the nuances in data donation decisions, particularly in relation to privacy concerns.

In addition to measuring privacy concerns and data donations, we controlled for less tangible personal benefits (i.e., enhanced mood in Experiment 1 and the warm glow feelings in Experiment 2). Additionally, we measured several control variables, such as trust, altruistic personality, media exposure to health data misuse, privacy invasion experience, frequency of doctor visits, health status, and demographics since these factors can impact disclosure outcomes (Anderson & Agarwal, 2011; Dinev & Hart, 2006; Malhotra et al., 2004; Yun et al., 2014). Experiment 1 utilized a 2×2 factorial design with privacy controls (provided vs. not provided) and implicit societal benefits (empathy induced vs. not induced) as factors, while Experiment 2 employed a 2×2 factorial design with privacy controls (provided vs. not provided) and explicit societal benefits (societal vs. personal) as factors. As explicit personal benefits are typically considered in privacy calculus studies (Table 1), we used them as the baseline condition when creating the explicit societal benefits manipulation in Experiment 2. Each experiment tested H1, H2, H3 (H3a in Experiment 1 and H3b in Experiment 2), and H4. We collected data through Amazon Mechanical Turk.<sup>11</sup>

## Experiment 1

## Sample and Procedure

A total of 139 participants (mean age = 33.7 years; female = 49.3%; White = 66.9%) successfully completed the experiment. The experiment involved an app mockup called DataDonors, which included several screenshots for participants to view. After measuring their baseline mood and introducing the app and its features (where we manipulated privacy controls and empathic concern), participants were asked to select the data they wanted to donate (23 items, adapted from the actual DataDonors project that inspired our experiment, served as our measure for data donation). Participants chose data to donate from three categories: (1) Demographic data (gender, birth date, ethnicity, education, work experience, and sexual orientation); (2) medical data (height, weight, blood type, vaccination, sleep, benign chronic diseases, risky chronic diseases, family health history, drug use, surgeries, and allergies); and (3) behavioral data (drinking, smoking, exercise, social media use, diet, and emotions). Following the data donation selection, participants provided qualitative feedback about their decisions and completed scales for privacy concerns (Dinev & Hart, 2006), trust (Moody et al., 2017), post-donation mood (Dickert et al., 2011), altruistic personality, media exposure to health data misuse, privacy invasion experience, frequency of doctor visits, and health status (Anderson & Agarwal, 2011). Finally, participants were debriefed.

In this experiment, participants were led to believe that they would be required to provide actual data after making their data donation selections. This approach was employed to create a realistic proxy for actual decisions, rather than merely measuring donation intentions (Skatova & Goulding, 2019). Most participants took the scenario seriously and engaged in thought processes typically associated with actual data donations. Several examples demonstrate that participants behaved as though they were making actual decisions (see additional quantitative analysis in the transparency materials<sup>12</sup> for details):

“I didn’t want to go through the process [of providing the actual data].”

“I am a private person and don't like to give out too much information.”

“I think that it [data donation] will be beneficial and besides I have control over the privacy settings anyway.”

## Experiment 2

## Sample and Procedure

A total of 283 participants (mean age = 40.56 years; female = 46.3%; White = 78.1%) successfully completed the experiment, which involved a simulated app called “Fight COVID-19.” The app’s user interface was embedded in an online survey, allowing participants to interact with and browse the app as if they were using an actual mobile-based app. This provided a more engaging experience compared to the app mockup in Experiment 1. Additionally, unlike Experiment 1 in which participants only selected the data they wanted to donate, Experiment 2 involved data donation decisions in which participants donated data while interacting with the simulated app, enhancing the ecological validity of our empirical test.

After agreeing to participate and prior to engaging with the app, participants were given a brief tutorial about the app and its features. Next, participants were given the opportunity to interact with and make an actual data donation decision through the app. While interacting with the simulated app, participants were randomly assigned to either receive or not receive privacy controls.<sup>16</sup> They were also randomly assigned to be informed, via the app’s features, about the societal (vs. personal) benefits of donating data for COVID-19 research. Participants were able to proceed to the postexperiment survey after making their data donation decision.

The data donation decision consisted of 27 items; some were adapted from Experiment 1 and others were developed for this experiment. Participants were asked to donate: (1)

demographic data (age, gender, ethnicity, education, occupation, and sexual orientation); (2) medical data (height and weight, blood type, allergies, vaccination history, chronic diseases, medications, surgeries, mental disorders, frequency of testing for COVID-19, whether they had been diagnosed with COVID-19, and whether they had COVID-19 symptoms); and (3) behavioral data (sleep, drinking, tobacco use, marijuana use, exercise, social media use, frequency of wearing a mask, frequency of practicing social distancing, frequency of going to crowded places, and whether they had close contact with someone diagnosed with COVID-19). Participants had to decide whether to make a data donation decision (i.e., provide information) for each item. Participants were given an “I prefer not to donate” choice for each item.

## Manipulations and Measurements

In line with our research model and Experiment 1, privacy controls were either provided (treatment group) or not provided (control group) (Figure 4). Explicit societal benefits were manipulated by using a version of the app that explicitly informed participants about the societal benefits or personal benefits (Figure 4). As mentioned earlier, personal benefits serve as the standard reference in privacy calculus literature (Table 1). Hence, we used the personal benefits group as our baseline for examining the impact of the societal benefits manipulation. The screenshots of all manipulations used in this experiment can be found in the transparency materials.

## Discussion

Data donation holds significant potential for enhancing healthcare and tackling chronic illnesses (Bietz et al., 2019; Topol, 2015). Recognizing that large-scale data donations can substantially advance medical care and provide societal benefits, researchers and healthcare stakeholders are increasingly turning their attention to this practice (Krutzinna & Floridi, 2019). Despite the far-reaching implications of data donations, many individuals may be reluctant to donate their data due to privacy concerns. Our research provides evidence that privacy concerns play a key role in data donation decisions, supporting the arguments presented by scholars who have explored data donation (Mandl et al., 2015; Shaw et al., 2016; Skatova & Goulding, 2019; Taylor & Mandl, 2015; Topol, 2015). Building on the privacy calculus model, we hypothesized and empirically demonstrated that the negative association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions depends not only on the perceived level of control over donated data but also on the perceived societal benefits, a construct highly relevant to the context of data donation. In the following sections, we discuss how our research enriches privacy theory, informs healthcare practices, and raises social and ethical considerations.

## Theoretical and Practical Implications

While current privacy theories primarily focus on the assumption that privacy decisions involve direct self-benefits (Dinev et al., 2015; Smith et al., 2011), some research has also highlighted the role of societal benefits in privacy decisions (Angst, 2009; Kordzadeh & Warren, 2017; Posey et al., 2010; Son & Kim, 2008; Wang et al., 2017) and hence the broader range of benefits of privacy decisions. In many contexts, including data donation, individuals make privacy disclosures without seeking direct or explicit personal benefits. In these cases, they often consider societal benefits before making data donations (Skatova & Goulding, 2019). Our first objective in this research was to investigate privacy concerns and information disclosure within the context of data donation, where individuals may evaluate societal benefits with or without considering personal gains. In line with most empirical privacy research (Baruh et al., 2017; Yun et al., 2019), we present additional evidence supporting the negative impact of privacy concerns in both general data donation for medical research (Experiment 1) and specific data donation for COVID-19 research (Experiment 2). This finding substantiates the negative relationship between privacy concerns and privacy decisions in a societal-benefit-oriented context. It also emphasizes the importance of data donation organizations in terms of addressing privacy concerns through various means, including those we examined in our study.

Our second objective involved examining the negative impact of privacy concerns on data donation decisions under various conditions theorized by the privacy calculus model, which describes the risks and benefits of privacy decisions (Dinev & Hart, 2006; Klopfer & Rubenstein, 1977; Laufer & Wolfe, 1977). Given the limited research on the societal benefits aspect of privacy decisions, we argued for and empirically tested the moderating role of both implicit and explicit societal benefits alongside privacy controls—a recognized risk protection method. Privacy controls enable donors to manage the release and dissemination of their donated data (Xu et al., 2011). Findings across both experiments suggest that offering tangible privacy controls weakens the association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions. Theoretically, this implies that the impact of privacy concerns on information disclosure depends on the perceived level of privacy control and the associated risks of losing that control. In practice, data donation organizations should provide privacy assurances to potential donors by offering them control through granular privacy settings in an accessible format.

In relation to societal benefits, we manipulated empathic concern to evoke the societal benefits of data donation (Experiment 1) and explicitly communicated these benefits to participants through a simulated data donation mobile app (Experiment 2). Our findings across both experiments reveal that societal benefits weakened the negative association between privacy concerns and data donation decisions. This negative association was strongest when both societal benefits and privacy controls were absent, compared to scenarios where either one was present. While the association was still weakened when both societal benefits and privacy controls were present, this specific condition did not lead to the greatest weakening effect. This result, while initially surprising, can be explained through the lens of emotional impact on privacy decision-making.

For instance, empathy induction creates a strong emotional response that can significantly influence behavior, potentially overpowering rational considerations such as privacy concerns (Dinev et al., 2015). When individuals are deeply moved by empathic feelings, their focus shifts more towards the welfare of others (Batson, 2011), in this case, the potential societal benefits of their data donation. This shift in focus can diminish the salience of privacy concerns in the data donation process. On the other hand, the presence of privacy controls, while providing a sense of protection and reducing perceived risk, may not elicit a similarly strong emotional response. When both empathy and privacy controls are present, the decision to donate data may become more balanced between emotional and rational considerations. As a result, the presence of privacy controls might dampen the overriding influence of empathy to some extent, leading to less pronounced effects than when empathy is induced without privacy controls. This finding supports the notion of contextdependency in privacy decisions (Xu & Zhang, 2022), such that privacy-enhancing cues and tools might not necessarily lead to more disclosure (Acquisti et al., 2015).

Research supports the interaction between privacy concerns and privacy calculus factors in the context of social media (Choi et al., 2018). Our research examines this interaction and shows that societal benefits, particularly in the absence of privacy controls or assurances (Xu & Dinev, 2022), can outweigh personal benefits in the context of data donation. Additionally, we considered less tangible personal benefits (e.g., enhanced mood and warm glow feelings) that individuals might indirectly seek through prosocial behavior (Andreoni, 1990, 1995; Harbaugh, 1998; Ferguson et al., 2012). In both experiments, these personal benefits significantly influenced data donation decisions. Notably, Experiment 1 showed that both empathic concern (representing a pure altruistic perspective, Batson, 2011) and enhanced mood (indicating impure altruistic behavior, Andreoni, 1990) significantly predicted data donation decisions. This finding suggests support for both pure and impure altruism perspectives. In practice, this implies that data donation organizations emphasizing societal impact and leveraging implicit personal gains (e.g., a positive donation experience generating warm glow feelings or mood enhancement) could increase participation despite privacy concerns.

The recent privacy literature (Xu & Dinev, 2022) highlights the need for empirical research that examines the boundary conditions (Busse et al., 2017; Edwards & Berry, 2010) of the relationship between privacy concerns and disclosure decisions (Dinev et al., 2015). Investigating boundary conditions, especially in novel contexts (Johns, 2006), can help explain the malleability and contextual nature of privacy decisions (Acquisti et al., 2020; Xu & Zhang, 2022). In addition to highlighting the context of data donation in privacy research, we contribute to theory by exploring the boundary conditions surrounding the relationship between privacy concerns and privacy decisions. Our research extends prior literature (e.g., Posey et al., 2010) by operationalizing societal benefits in the data donation context, where the privacy calculus involves more than simply personal risks and benefits. Through the unique context of data donation, our research highlights the impact that a broader range of net benefits can have on privacy decisions.

## Social and Ethical Implications

Several social and ethical implications arise when considering data donations for medical research (Krutzinna & Floridi, 2019; Skatova & Goulding, 2019). Xu and Dinev (2022) highlight the bias inherent in data collection practices, as inferences made from donated data may be less applicable to individuals who are not willing to donate their personal data. Privacy invasion is another major concern, as sensitive health information may be revealed, potentially leading to stigmatization or discrimination based on medical conditions or genetic predispositions (Solove, 2006). Ownership and control of donated data raise ethical and economic issues, with questions surrounding who owns the data and how it can be used or monetized (Acquisti et al., 2020; Angst, 2009). Furthermore, data anonymization techniques, though often employed to protect privacy, may not be foolproof, raising concerns about the effectiveness of such techniques in safeguarding data donations (Ohm, 2010; Topol, 2015).

Data donation for medical research is a noble practice with the potential for significant societal benefits (Angst, 2009). However, respecting and safeguarding the fundamental human right to privacy (Brandeis & Warren, 1890) is essential throughout the data donation process. While the prosocial frame may alleviate subjective privacy risk perceptions due to the perceived societal impact of data donations, objective privacy risks might still exist or even increase due to multiple data exchanges (Adjerid et al., 2018; Acquisti et al., 2017; Krutzinna & Floridi, 2019). Data breaches involving sensitive medical information could have severe consequences, such as the exposure of personal health information, which might result in identity theft, fraud, or other forms of harm to individuals (Culnan & Williams,

2009). Thus, reducing privacy concerns can be challenging, especially without legal frameworks and robust infrastructure for data donation initiatives (Hummel et al., 2019). Our research demonstrates that the impact of privacy concerns was less pronounced when privacy controls were provided or societal benefits were perceived. Although this risk-benefit analysis may justify data donation despite privacy concerns, it is crucial to avoid designing data donation apps that manipulate or nudge individuals into overlooking their privacy concerns and preferences. Such manipulation, referred to as dark patterns in personal data collection (Jarovsky, 2022; Rosenberg, 2023), is considered theft in the information age (Sunstein, 2022). Regulatory frameworks, like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), as well as scholarly work, emphasize the importance of transparency (Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Karwatzki et al., 2017; Godinho de Matos & Adjerid, 2022). Data donation organizations should be transparent about any subliminal cues (e.g., psychological manipulations and choice architectures) that could affect individuals’ decision-making in ways they may not be aware of during privacy trade-offs. Advances in organizational privacy practices on the usability and engineering of privacy choices (e.g., cookie banners), can help address these concerns (Adjerid et al., 2019; Habib & Cranor, 2022).

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

First, our research is limited by the rational assumptions inherent in the privacy calculus (Klopfer & Rubenstein, 1977; Laufer & Wolfe, 1977). Specifically, we did not consider nonrational factors that may moderate the association between privacy concerns and disclosure decisions (Alashoor et al., 2023b; Dinev et al., 2015) or influence privacy decisions in general (Adjerid et al., 2018). Future research should investigate additional factors or mechanisms that could explain data donation decisions. Behavioral privacy research (Acquisti et al., 2020) and literature on donation decisions (e.g., Andreoni, 1990; Feeley & Moon, 2009) offer various theoretical perspectives that can aid researchers in understanding data donation decisions. Another intriguing area to examine is the choice architecture (Adjerid et al., 2019; Thaler, 2021) embedded in data donation applications. In Experiment 2, we asked participants to type their personal data, which required more time and cognitive effort (Dinev et al., 2015). The results, or at least the baseline donation amount, could significantly change if participants were to be presented with multiple-choice questions, which demand less time and cognitive effort to answer. This change could be attributed to the convenience of the donation process or the enjoyment associated with effortless prosocial behavior (Beurel et al., 2017; Feeley & Moon, 2009).

Second, we relied on the unidimensional conceptualization and operationalization of privacy concerns (Dinev & Hart, 2006). Despite its limitations, this holistic assessment of privacy concerns has been employed in recent privacy research (e.g., Alashoor et al., 2023b; Xu et al., 2011) and is appropriate to use when individual dimensions of privacy concerns are not a key focus and a more holistic assessment of privacy concerns is being made. While this was the case in our study, we acknowledge that exploring privacy concerns in a multidimensional fashion could unveil more nuanced effects associated with the different dimensions (Hong & Thong, 2013; Smith et al., 1996).

Third, our study design was limited to a one-time data donation decision, while data donation could be an ongoing process that occurs over time. In this regard, we should note that the effect of the empathy manipulation employed in Experiment 1 to evoke societal benefits could diminish over time with repeated app use. This limitation also applies to Experiment 2 as donors would need to provide their personal information over time, not just in a single donation instance, in order to obtain realistic societal or personal benefits. While recent research suggests that one-time rewards (i.e., monetary incentives) are ineffective at promoting long-term data donations (Fast & Schnurr, forthcoming), future research is warranted to examine the nuances of data donation decisions in a longitudinal manner.

Fourth, the images of patients we used in Experiment 1 to induce empathic concern were accompanied by text that encourages perspective-taking (e.g., with phrases like “put yourself in their shoes” and “imagine how their families feel”). Perspective-taking is a widely used method to induce empathic concern (Batson, 2011). However, our design could not distinguish whether empathic concern was induced because participants read the text and took the perspective of the patients and their families (i.e., empathic concern induced by perspective-taking), because they viewed the patients in the images (i.e., empathic concern induced by the images), or both. Although both explanations would be considered implicit ways to evoke societal benefits, further research is needed to tease apart the extent to which our results may have been influenced by perspective-taking.

Fifth, participants knew that they were part of a research study, which may have influenced their data donation decisions. This highlights the need for further research in more naturalistic settings where participants are not aware of being part of a study. This approach could help in understanding the nuances of data donation decisions in real-world scenarios, beyond the constraints of an experimental setup. Such research could also provide additional insights into data donation behavior across diverse contexts and populations, including specific groups like the elderly and chronic disease patients.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, this study explored privacy concerns and data donations for medical research, emphasizing the societal aspect of this prosocial privacy decision. Our findings revealed that increased privacy concerns lead to fewer data donations; however, this association is less salient in the presence of societal benefits. The moderation role of societal benefits is especially prominent in the absence of privacy controls, highlighting the role of altruism and privacy risk in privacy decisions. By understanding the interplay between personal privacy factors and those reflecting societal aspects, we can guide the design of information systems that respect individual privacy while promoting the greater good.
