# The enactment of significance: a unified conception of information, systems and technology

Paul Beynon-Davies

Cardiff Business School, Cardiff University, Wales

Correspondence: Paul Beynon-Davies, Cardiff Business School, Cardiff University, Aberconway Building, Colum Drive, Cardiff, CF10 3EU, Wales. Tel: 029 2087 6005: Fax: 029 2087 4556; E-mail: Beynon-DaviesP@cardiff.ac.uk

## Abstract

This paper introduces a new perspective that helps unpack the relationship between information, systems and technology within the nature of human organisation. We argue for an area of interest located at the intersection of signs and systems, which involves the enactment of forma, informa and performa. To demonstrate the utility of this perspective, we deliberately consider a ‘strange’ case from a different time, space and culture. We discuss the nature of human organisation among the Inka Empire and how this organisation was reliant upon the enactment of significance, particularly in relation to an artefact known as the khipu. We conclude with a discussion of the value of our framework in helping us understand the complex entanglement of information, systems and technology within human organisation. European Journal of Information Systems (2010) 19, 389–408.

## Introduction

In a series of editorials for the European Journal of Information Systems (Paul, 2007a, b, c), Ray Paul has discussed a number of challenges facing Information Systems as a discipline. These include: nobody seems to know who we are outside our community, demand from students for the subject is dropping, and our research publications do not appear to be publishing the right sort or content of research. Paul has also proposed that part of the problem may lie in our discipline’s lack of a clear conceptual framework upon which to build. He particularly raises the importance of defining the core concept of information system more clearly and distinguishing it from information technology (IT) on the one hand and business processes on the other (Paul, 2007a).

This paper attempts a response to two of the challenges raised by Paul: that of providing a coherent account in which the taken-for-granted concept of an information system is more clearly defined and located within the nature of human organisation. The aim of this paper is therefore to introduce a conceptual framework that we believe helps unpack the sociotechnical (Mumford, 2006) and sociomaterial (Orlikowski, 2006) nature of human organisation through the concept of enactment. To summarise our perspective: we maintain that our area of interest is or should be located at the intersection of signs and systems – what we refer to as the enactment of significance. This means that our sense-making (Weick, 1995) is fundamentally concerned with the enactment of signs through forma (the substance of a sign), informa (the content of a sign) and performa (the use of signs in coordinated action) (Dietz, 2006). Such enactment allows us to define more clearly and to relate three classes of systems important to human organising: activity systems, information systems and data systems.

Therefore, within this framework an information system is not considered as a system of activity (Alter, 2008), nor as a system of technology (Orlikowski & Barley, 2001). An information system is considered a special class of communication system, involving the use of signs within patterns of informative acts. As such, an information system is conceived of as a sociotechnical system that utilises a semi-formal ‘language’ (Agerfalk et al., 2006) mediating between activity systems on the one hand (social patterns of performative acts) and data systems on the other (technological patterns of formative acts).

To demonstrate the utility of this framework we rise to the challenge set by Orlikowski & Iacono (2001) who argue that the problem of conceptualising IT in relation to information systems and organisations ‘requires a shift of attention from taking IT artifacts for granted towards explicit theorizing about specific technologies with distinctive cultural and computational capabilities, existing in various social, historical and institutional contexts, understood in particular ways, and used for certain activities’. Therefore, within this paper, we deliberately consider a case of technology, communication and action from a different time, space and culture. We discuss the nature of human organisation among the Inka Empire (D’altroy, 2002) and how such organisation was reliant upon the enactment of significance, particularly in relation to an artefact known as the khipu (Beynon-Davies, 2007). This allows us to offer a new, broader perspective on the nature of the IT artefact. It also allows us to see more clearly the way in which the value of information technologies is accomplished within cycles of enactment, and how such enactment is critical to forms of human organising. This is the essence of what we suggest it means to think informatically (Bryant, 2008).

## Conceptual framework

In this section we introduce a conceptual framework (Figure 1) that revolves around the idea that data, information, information systems and IT are all located in the enactment of signs within various levels of system. As such, the framework takes as input ideas both from traditional reference disciplines (Keen, 1980) and from a range of disparate areas not particularly well-covered in the contemporary Information Systems literature.

Semiotics (Morris, 1946) and its application in Organisational semiotics (Stamper, 2001) provides the idea of signs and sign-systems. Organisational systemics (Jackson, 2003), and soft systems theory (Checkland, 1987) in particular, helps us formulate the distinction between activity systems and information systems. We use both information theory and communication theory as a baseline to expand upon the nature of human communication. Information theory (Shannon, 1949) contributes the classic model of the communication process while communication theory (Beniger, 1986) helps us expand upon the nature of IT and its basis in control. Evolutionary anthropology (Klein, 2002) informs the conception of homo sapiens as a symbol-manipulating ape, and evolutionary biology (Halliday & Slater, 1983) along with zoosemiotics (Sebeok, 1972) helps provide ideas relating to the nature of sensing and the idea of sensory modalities. Conceptual modelling (Tsitchizris & Lochovsky, 1982) helps us define the notion of ontology, that of a data model and the idea of formative acts. From the language-action tradition we adapt the idea of communicative acts (Searle, 1970) and we use symbolic interactionism (Blumer, 1969) and the dramaturgical perspective from sociology (Brisset & Edgley, 1990) to help inform the notion of performative acts.

## Signs and sign-systems

Very broadly, a sign is anything that is significant. For Pierce, signs relate people, objects and ideas: ‘A sign is something which stands to somebody for something in some respect or capacity’ (Pierce, 1931). For Morris, signs relate stimuli to behaviour: ‘If something, A, controls behaviour towards a goal in a way similar to (but not necessarily identical with) the way something else, B, would control behaviour with respect to that goal in a situation in which it were observed, then A is a sign’ (Morris, 1946).

A sign-system is any organised collection of signs and relations between signs. Everyday spoken human language is probably the most readily identifiable example of a sign-system. However, the concept of a sign-system is much broader than spoken human language. Signs exist in most forms of human activity, since signs are critical to processes of human communication and understanding. Signs are also important to non-human communication such as in animal communication and in man-machine communication.

The term semitotic/semiotics or semiology is used to refer to that area devoted to the study of signs and sign-systems. Pierce refers to semiotic as the ‘doctrine of signs’ (Pierce, 1931) and Morris describes semiotic as the ‘science of signs’ (Morris, 1946). De Saussure used the term semiology to refer to ‘a science which studies the role of signs as part of social life y’ (de Saussure, 1964), while Margaret Mead proposed the use of the term semiotics to cover the study of ‘patterned communications in all modalities’ (Mead, 1962). Eco, characteristically, defines semiotics as ‘the discipline studying everything which can be used in order to lie’ (Eco, 1977).

Following Pierce, Charles Morris maintained that three types of relations are important within any sign system: the relation between signs and actors; the relation between signs and objects; the relation between signs and other signs. This led him to propose three branches to semiotics: pragmatics, semantics and syntactics.

Stamper (2001) in his application of semiotics to issues of human organisation proposed the addition of an empirics level. This layered model of semiotics is sometimes referred to as the semiotics ladder (Figure 1), and is the approach adopted here.

Within this model, pragmatics is concerned with the purpose of a sign. Pragmatics is interested in the relations between signs and actors and considers the intentions of actors underlying communicative behaviour. Semantics is interested in the relations between signs and objects and is concerned with the meaning of a message conveyed in communication. Syntactics is interested in the relations between signs and other signs. It is therefore concerned with the structure of signs within their encompassing sign-systems. Finally, empirics can be seen as the relation between signs and matter and energy and is the study of the physical form or representation of a sign.

## Organisation and systems

Within human organisation we are particularly interested in the way in which signs are used to express intentions, make decisions and generate action. This is clearly the realm of the social world or social action. However, we are also interested in the relationship between signs and the physical world – particularly in the ways in which signs are represented using ‘technology’.

Signs take their shape within systems of various forms. Systems constitute the continuing patterning of order or organisation in the world. Therefore, within our framework, the term system is used not only to encapsulate the notion of the organisation or patterning of signs. It is also used to denote the organisation or patterning of activity, communication and representation. We further argue that signs interrelate between and within three different levels of system of interest to human organisation: activity systems, information systems and data systems. Thus, the semiotics ladder can be seen to conceptualise signs as sociotechnical phenomena.

At the level of ‘technology’ signs typically have a physical form and can be analysed in terms of features of representation and transmission. We therefore broaden the notion of IT or information and communication technology (ICT) to include a large variety of coding artefacts beyond modern digital computing technologies. In generic terms, we shall argue that, IT or ICT systems are best referred to as data systems since they consist of inter-related forms of data representation and data processing.

At the level of the social, signs are used within systems of action. We use the term activity system in the sense defined by Checkland. A human activity system, or activity system for short, is what Checkland refers to as a ‘soft system’ (Checkland, 1987): a type of social system that is ‘designed’ to meet certain defined objectives.

An information system is a mediating construct between actions (activity system) on the one hand and technology (data system) on the other. An information system is a form of communication system in which records represent and are processed as a form of social memory. In this sense, an information system can also be conceived of as a semi-formal ‘language’ which supports human decision making and action.

## Communication

Defining an information system as a class of communication system begs the question of the nature of communication. The most cited model of communication is one due to Shannon and Weaver (Shannon, 1949). In this model, communication is treated as an ‘engineering’ problem. As Shannon himself stated, ‘the fundamental problem of communication is that of reproducing at one point, either exactly or approximately, a message selected at another point’. In this model, a source generates a message. This message is then encoded and transmitted as a signal along some communication channel. The signal may be subject to noise in the environment, meaning that the received signal at the destination may not be identical to that transmitted from the source. Hence, the process of decoding a signal may involve correcting errors introduced by noise to produce the original message.

The Shannon and Weaver model has been criticised on a number of grounds as a model of human communication, primarily because it is explicitly designed to address only the empirics level of signs. To serve as a satisfactory baseline model for human communication, a number of elements have to be added to form an augmented model, which satisfactorily encompasses matters of pragmatics, semantics and syntactics. Within this augmented model, human communication is seen as a process involving interaction of a number of elements: actors, messages, signals and communication channels. Actors are the senders of communication or the receivers of communication. The intentions of a sender will be encoded in a message using elements from a particular sign-system. The message will be transmitted by the sender in terms of signals along some communication channel. One or more of the other parties will be a receiver, which have the ability to decode signals to reveal the meaning of the message (Figure 2).

Not surprisingly, there is an inherent relationship between elements of this augmented model of communication and elements of the semiotics ladder. A signsystem can be considered as a convention (Lewis, 2002) or a set of conventions that serves to control the communication process. This means that both sender and receiver must utilise a common system of signs for communication before such communication can occur (Eco, 1977). Within the communication process, pragmatics concerns the intentions conveyed in a message, semantics the meaning of a message, syntactics the symbolic formalism used to represent the message, and empirics the signals used to code and transmit the message.

However, even the augmented model is deficient as a generic model of human communication in a number of ways. First, the model assumes that communication is inherently linear (Burgoon et al., 1994), whereas human communication normally involves the use of feedback and is transactional in nature. Human verbal communication, for instance, typically occurs in the form of dialogue, discourse or ‘conversation’. Within such discourse, actors adjust their messages in response to a history of previous messages conducted within a particular dialogue (Clark, 1996). Second, the model tends to focus upon a limited subset of communication practice: dyadic communication. In contrast, human communication frequently occurs within and between groups of communicants. Finally, the model under-emphasises the role of records and record-keeping within human communication. We shall argue that, when human communication occurs between more than two people and particularly when messages have to be transmitted across time and space, the persistent record is an essential feature of much human communication.

## Enactment

We introduce the term enactment to relate systems of signs to systems of technology, action and communication. The term enactment has found use within organisation science, cognitive science and computer science. For Weick (1988) enactment is a social process, central to sense-making within organisations. For Varela et al. (1993), enactment is an individual process, used to describe the structural coupling between human and environment through means of human interpretation. Within computer science, the term enactment is used particularly to refer to the execution of business processes (OMG, 2007). Our view of enactment attempts a unification of these viewpoints. We try to unpack how enactment makes social, individual and computational sense through utilising the concepts of signs and systems.

Dietz (2006) uses the root of the Latin verb informare, from which the English word information is taken, to define three perspectives on the sign: forma, informa and performa. These three perspectives can be seen to act as the ‘glue’ between semiotics on the one hand and systemics on the other (Figure 3). Forma refers to the ‘substance’ that carries a sign. Forma deals with the physical nature of signs and therefore crosses the physical/technical and empirics levels on the semiotics ladder. Informa deals with the content and meaning of signs. It concerns the use of signs in communication and therefore crosses the syntactics and semantics levels of signs on the semiotics ladder. Performa refers to the use of communication to make decisions and to take appropriate social action. Performa therefore crosses the pragmatics and social levels of the semiotics ladder.

Figure 3 Forma, informa and performa.

Three symbols are used within Figure 3 to stand for forma, informa and performa. Forma is represented by a cycle of signal symbols to indicate the patterning of physical transformations required to communicate. Informa is represented by a cycle of speech glyphs familiar to the Aztec or Mexica peoples of mid-America. This cycle of informa represents the patterning of communication between two or more actors. Performa is represented by a cycle of entangled hands to indicate the patterned coordination of action. Coordinated action is the binding of one actor’s actions to those of others.

We would claim that the patterning of order characteristic of human organisation is enacted through three inter-related forms of action. Formative acts amount to the enactment of forma: acts of data representation and processing. Informative acts constitute the enactment of informa: acts of communication involving messagemaking and interpretation. Performative acts constitute the enactment of performa: the performance of coordinated action within human groups.

The distinction between formative acts, informative acts and performative acts is a convenient division, used to highlight the different roles associated with the more encompassing concept of a sign act. A sign act has intent (pragmatics), expresses meaning (semantics), has structure (syntactics) and has a physical representation (empirics). A given sign act bridges between the social world (activity system) and the physical/technical world (data system) through the mediating realm of communication (information system). Sign acts are significant acts because it is through such acts that we enact significance.

Our view of enactment can be seen to bear a resemblance to the constitutive cycle between agency and social structure defined in structuration theory. Structuration theory attempts to unify the traditional dichotomy between institutional and action theories of social life (Giddens, 1984). Giddens refers to structuration as a cyclical process in which on the one hand, the structure of social institutions is created by human action and is only evident in human action. Through human interaction, social structures are reproduced but may also change. On the other hand, human action is constrained by the way in which humans utilise institutional structure as a resource in interpreting their own and other people’s actions. Institutionalised patterns exist only as human actors enact them.

A sign-system can be seen as having many of the features of a social institution, since it is a necessary precondition for formative, informative and performative acts among a group of persons. In this sense, a signsystem can be seen to have an existence independent of the humans using it. It also means that the patterning of signs can be described and studied somewhat independently of the given enactment of signs. However, a sign-system is only really evident in actual sign acts. People use a sign-system as a resource for representation, communication and action. This sign-system is produced and re-produced through sign acts. Over time, sign acts may change the structure of the sign-system itself leading to a new basis as a resource.

Thus the enactment of significance has much synergy with Boland’s conception of in-formation (Boland, 1987). He states that ‘It is through dialogue that we accomplish and re-accomplish meaning, and thus bring order to the social world. Through dialogue we name objects and give them significance y . It is through dialogue that the symbolic order of our shared world is made real. Through dialogue we tell each other what is important and why. We search for the purpose, significance and meaning of our institutions and ourselves that is the social order’.

From our perspective, signs are enacted across the layers of the semiotics ladder and across the three forms of inter-related systems within organisations. Within Figure 1, the three levels of enactment are linked through cycles of influence. Within acts of performance, actors may communicate through informative acts. In turn, certain aspects of informa may be given persistence through acts of record-keeping – formative acts. In turn, such records may be used as an important resource within group communication, which in turn may be a resource for the coordination of performance among group members. Thus we would propose that the relationship between performance, communication and representation forms something of an inverted pyramid. Communication is typically a reduced aspect of performance and representation is a reduced aspect of communication. In other words, what I represent is always less than I communicate; what I communicate is always less than what I do.

## Activity system

Systems are evident through patterning. Activity systems consist of the patterning of acts of performance. Within the human sphere, such acts of performance are typically examples of symbolic interaction (Blumer, 1969). Human actors interact with each other by interpreting each other’s actions. However, the responses of two actors in interaction are not made directly to the actions of one another, but instead are based on the meaning that actors attach to such actions. Thus, human interaction is mediated by the use of signs within processes of signification and interpretation.

Hence, there appears an obvious link between the idea of performative acts and the dramaturgical perspective in sociology (Brisset & Edgley, 1990). Such a perspective uses a theatrical metaphor to understand the way in which people ‘perform’ in everyday interaction. There is also a clear relationship between the idea of a performative pattern and that of an organisational routine. Pentland & Feldman (2008) argue that organisational routines are generative systems that produce recognisable, repetitive patterns of interdependent actions, carried out by multiple actors.

Pragmatics is critical to human ‘performance’ because of its concern with the purpose or intentionality of communication. The term intentionality is often simplistically summarised as ‘aboutness’ or the relationship between mental acts and the external world. Searle’s concept of intentionality is an attempt to link the concept of mental states with behaviour: it is ‘the special way the mind has of relating to the world’ (Searle, 2000).

Searle (1970) further maintains that intentionality is expressed through illocutionary acts: acts of communication in which actors create and send messages in an appropriate context with certain intentions. The communication of such intentions is important for joint action – coordinated and collaborative action. It also underlies the process of organising itself, particularly in relation to the routinisation of joint work.

## Information system

A communicative act is some aspect of human performance designed by one actor, A, to influence the performance of some other actor, B. We would argue that information systems consist of recurring patterns of such communicative action. Such patterns arise from the ‘game-like’ character of human communication. A particular communicative act creates the possibility of usually a limited range of communicative acts as response. An information system can therefore be conceived as a semi-formal ‘language’ used in the generation of patterns of informative acts.

Searle argues that a given communicative act consists of two main elements: a propositional attitude or illocutionary force and a propositional content (Searle, 1975). Illocutionary force is used to represent the kind of attitude a speaker has when he says something and the direction of fit between the world and the propositional content of the communicative act (the word). Consider three speech acts: [Please take the customer order], [Will you take the order?], [You will take the order]. The propositional content of these three messages is the same – that you will process the customer order. The illocutionary force differs: the first is a request, the second a question and the third a prediction.

The term direction of fit was invented by Austin (1971) to refer to the fit between mental states (the word) and the world. Three directions of fit are proposed: word-toworld, world-to-word and null. A word-to-world direction of fit is intended to describe the world. A world-to-word direction of fit is intended to change the world. A null direction of fit occurs when having some mental state implies that some fitting to the world has already taken place.

Searle also refers to the context of some communicative act and uses this term to refer to the sender. receiver time, place and the ‘world’ within which the illocutionary act takes place. Hence, the context defines a message from a sender S to a receiver P at time T and in place P. The world is used to collect together a range of other features of context, which are important for understanding the meaning of a message, such as channel (speech, writing etc.), code (language style) and message-form (chat, debate, sermon etc.). We find it more useful to segment off these aspects of a communication into their appropriate semiotic category. For instance, channel is a matter of empirics, code is a matter of semantics and message-form relates to matters of pragmatics.

Searle (1975) identifies five key types of illocutionary act in these terms: assertives, directives, commissives, expressives and declaratives. Assertives are communicative acts that explain how things are in the world, such as reports and assertions. Such acts commit the speaker to the truth of the expressed proposition, as in, ‘Our orders have fallen by 10% this month’. As such, all assertives have a word-to-world direction of fit. Directives are communicative acts that represent the senders’ attempt to get a receiver to perform an action, such as requests, questions, commands and advice. As such, they are meant to cause the receiver to take a particular action Hence. an actor might ask of another actor: ‘Please ensure that our production target is met next quarter’. All directives have a world-to-word direction of fit. Commissives are communicative acts that commit a speaker to some future course of action such as promises, oaths and threats. They are communicative acts that represent a speaker’s intention to perform an action, as in, ‘I promise to write a letter or ‘I refuse to pay a bill’. All commissives have a world-to-word direction of fit. Expressives are communicative acts that represent the speakers’ psychological state, feelings or emotions such as apologies, criticisms and congratulations. They express a speaker's attitudes and emotions towards some proposition. Hence, an actor might state, ‘I am unhappy with Joe’s overall performance this month’. Expressives have a null direction of fit. Declaratives are communicative acts that aim to change the world through the communication itself, such as baptism, pronouncing someone husband and wife and sentencing a prisoner. Hence, they are usually communicated against a normative background and are frequently institutionalised, such as ‘This order has been fulfilled’. Declaratives have both a world-to-word and word-to-world direction of fit. ‘Declarations are unique among speech acts in that they actually make changes in the world solely in virtue of the successful performance of the speech act’ (Searle, 2000).

Searle distinguishes between the illocutionary force and propositional content of a communicative act using the notation F[p], where F stands for the illocutionary force of the communicative act and p its propositional content. He also introduces some symbols to stand for the particular illocutionary force, such as [p] for an assertion and ?[p] for a directive. We add to this scheme by including -[p] for a declarative, ![p] for an expressive and #[p] for a commitment.

Illocutionary acts presuppose the existence of a signsystem in which the signs used for the specification of propositions and illocutionary forces have an established meaning among a group of communicants. Semantics is concerned with the content or meaning of signs used within the message conveyed in a communicative act. A simple model of meaning is one in which a sign is broken down into three component parts that are frequently referred to collectively as the meaning triangle (Ogden & Richards, 1923). The designation of a sign refers to the symbol (or collection of symbols) by which some concept is known. The extension of a sign refers to the range of phenomena that the concept in some way covers. The intension of a sign is the collection of properties that in some ways characterise the phenomena in the extension, and is the idea or concept of significance.

The meaning triangle hence helps to distinguish systematically between data and information. A datum, a single item of data, corresponds to a symbol or a set of symbols in the meaning triangle. Information particularly occurs in the ‘stands-for’ relations between the symbol (designation) and its concept (intension) and the symbol and its referent (extension). However, the association between sign and meaning is not an immutable one. The structuration of a sign-system implies that meaning is an accomplishment and hence is everchanging. ‘Language is symbolic action, through which we search for meaning in the world’. But, ‘the search for meaning is a continuous search that is never completely or finally realized’ (Boland, 1987).

Syntactics is concerned with the form of a sign and with how signs relate within sign-systems. Boland argues that, ‘Information is an inward-forming. It is the change in a person from an encounter with data’ (Boland, 1987). He further argues that ‘y our images of information without in-formation lead to an ignorance of language and our human search for meaning which together deny the very possibility of human communication. The process of constructing the social world is a process of language and communication’ (Boland, 1987).

In its broadest sense, the term language refers to an agreed system of signs used to convey messages between a set of communicative actors. Language is important because as Stephen Pinker states, ‘a common language connects the members of a community into an information-sharing network with formidable collective powers’ (Pinker, 2001). This suggests an inherent relationship between information, communication, language and action.

Languages can be distinguished in terms of their modality or communication channel. They can also be distinguished in terms of their level of formality. Within the human domain, it is conventional to distinguish between spoken and written forms of languages. There are also the non-verbal or body languages, consisting of non-verbal cues and gestures that convey meaning within human interaction (Hall, 1959).

We can also distinguish between natural/ informal and artificial/formal languages. Certain languages such as spoken English are described as natural languages in that they have grown ‘naturally’ among a speech community and hence as a matter of course are continuously changing. Certain other languages are invented or created. Most natural languages have a complex, continuously changing syntax and semantics and as such are informal languages. Other languages are designed languages in that they have a highly specified and limited formal syntax and semantics. Hence, they are referred to as formal languages.

Information systems are systems for using signs in the sense that they act as a communication medium between different people, sometimes spatially and temporally distant (Beynon-Davies, 2009b). Therefore, the sign-systems of relevance to information systems are best described as semi-formal languages: some of their features are designed; some of their features emerge in continuous human interaction. Using this conceptual lens, information systems can be seen to consist of patterns of communication using semi-formal languages to make decisions and as a consequence to create, control and maintain social action. On the one hand, an information system is tied to patterns of instrumental action (performa). On the other hand, an information system is tied to a system of artefacts (forma). The ‘language’ of some information system includes formal messages that create, control and maintain social interactions in an organisational context. Such messages not only serve to make statements about the world. They are also used to give orders, make promises or commitments, classify things etc.

The idea of an informative or communicative pattern is similar in nature to the idea of a genre system (Yates & Orlikowski, 2002). Genre systems are described as, ‘interrelated sequences of communicative actions’ used to support group interaction through ‘organising the temporal, spatial and social dimensions of interaction’. Thus a genre system constitutes a set of expectations as to the why, what, who, how, when and where of communicative action. Why specifies the purpose of communication, what specifies the format of content, who specifies the actors appropriate for a communication, how specifies the medium of communication, when associates temporal expectations with communication, where associates spatial expectations with a communication. The why, what, who and how of communicative action can be seen to directly relate to elements of the semiotics ladder described above, namely, pragmatics, semantics, syntactics and empirics. However, the notion of a genre system supports Searle’s contention that communicative acts are events that take place within time and space. Communicative acts are situated actions (Suchman, 1994).

## Data system

Stonier (1996) argues for information to be treated as a basic property of the universe as are matter and energy. Just as energy is defined in terms of its capacity to perform work, so information is defined in terms of its capacity to organise a system. A ‘system may be said to contain information if such a system exhibits organisation y just as mass is a reflection of a system containing matter, and heat is a reflection of a system containing energy, so is organisation the physical expression of a system containing information’.

In Stamper’s original conception, empirics equates with the precepts of information theory as embodied in the Shannon and Weaver model of communication described above. In this sense, empirics refers to the physical characteristics of the medium of communication, particularly the study of the signals used to carry or code the signs of a message, as well as to communication channels and their characteristics. We prefer to define empirics in a broader sense as concerned with the physical characteristics of signs. This allows us to include a concern with how signs are represented or stored using various different media as well as the traditional concern with how signs are coded as signals that travel along various communication channels.

A communication channel is some physical channel for the transmission of messages using signals between two or more actors. It is possible to distinguish between such communication channels in terms of medium. For instance, human speech utilises sound generated by the human vocal organs and received by the human ear, whereas written English uses symbols written on the page transmitted to the human sight organ through the reflectance of light. The Shannon and Weaver model led to the consideration of a number of generic properties of all communication channels such as modulation, coding, capacity, direction and synchronisation.

Any form of energy propagation can be used for communication through signals: a signal being a physical pattern that travels along some communication channel. For instance, speech travels as a signal consisting of a pattern of sound waves. Modulation is the process by which variety is introduced into a signal. If we are unable to modulate the pattern of a signal then no information can be communicated between sender and receiver along a communication channel. Once we can vary the signal, then it becomes possible to code certain messages using variations in the signal. Coding is the translation of a signal from one medium into the same pattern expressed differently in some other communication medium.

There is a clear synergy between the idea of a communication channel and what within zoosemiotics (Sebeok, 1972) is referred to as a sensory modality. Within human communication a given communication channel effectively corresponds to a sensory channel. Symbols can be modulated and coded as signals using such a sensory modality. The physical makeup of homo sapiens determines a limited range of sensory modalities: sight, touch, smell, taste and hearing. These modalities determine what humans are capable of sensing: the range of phenomena that can be discriminated using a particular sense. This sets the cognitive background for what humans can use as symbols. There is thus a close relationship between symbols, signals and sensing.

Since a symbol is any aspect of the world that can be modulated as a signal and hence used in communication, symbols are both individual/cognitive constructs and group/social constructs. The range of features in the world that can be modulated is potentially infinite. Different cultures choose different aspects, features or facets of the world as significant. A finite set of facets derived from the infinite variety in the world is used to code a conventionally accepted set of symbols necessary for communication. In our conception, symbols as sensory constructs are equivalent to data.

Whereas human cognition determines what can be used as symbols, what is actually regarded as symbolic will be culturally determined. Symbols as social constructs rely on a shared ontology among a group of actors (Kishore et al., 2004): the context within which a group of signs is used in continuous communication by a social group or groups. Hence, a shared ontology is a necessary pre-condition for joint communication and effectively frames such communication.

Our conception of a data system can be mapped to the idea of a physical symbol system (Newell & Simon, 1976). A physical symbol system consists of physical patterns (symbols) that can be combined into structures and manipulated to produce new structures. We suggest a particular interest in those data systems in which the symbols have some persistence. By persistence we mean that symbols exist for some duration over and above the communication within which the symbols were used. Not all symbol systems demonstrate persistence. For instance, within human speech, words as sound symbols decay rapidly in air. In contrast, a clay token can act as a durable representation of the type and quantity of goods stored in warehouses (Beynon-Davies, 2009a). As such, persistent symbols become artefacts capable of independent consideration and manipulation by different people, situated in different spaces and at different times.

Such a system of artefacts, used for representing and processing persistent symbols, is what we mean or should mean by the term ‘information technology’. The advantage of defining ‘information technology’ in terms of primitives associated with some persistent data system is that this concept can be applied to a vast array of historical and cultural artefacts that are and have been used within human activity for the purposes of group communication.

The syntax of some persistent data system can be expressed in terms of a data model: a formal language for representing, organising and manipulating data. A data model therefore consists of two sets of primitives: representors and operators. Representors are primitives of data representation or organisation. Operators are primitives of data manipulation or processing.

In terms of data representation, a data model can be described at a high level of abstraction in terms of a hierarchy of data items, data elements and data structures (Tsitchizris & Lochovsky, 1982). A data item is the lowest level of data organisation. A data element is a logical collection of data items and a data structure is a logical collection of data elements.

In terms of data processing, it is useful to define a number of core types or classes of formative act from which all forms of such processing can theoretically be built: create, read, update and delete. Create or ‘write’ actions involve creating new data structures or representing new data elements within data structures (eod4). Update actions involve changing the value of data items in the sense that the symbols appropriate to the data item are changed (kod4). Delete actions involve removing data elements from data structures (dod4). Retrieval or ‘read’ actions involve accessing data from data items, data elements and data structures (mod4).

From these base or core classes of formative acts, more complex patterns of formative acts can be built. Hence, the transmission of data over some communication channel can be seen to consist of a combination of both a ‘write’ (send) and a ‘read’ (receive) action. Similarly, sorting can be seen to consist of a process of reading data elements from one data structure and creating their occurrence in an ordered sequence according to set criteria in some other data structure.

## The Inkas

Part of our aim in proposing the conceptual framework described in the previous section is to seek to challenge current conceptions of information, information systems and IT. Such conceptions are particularly embedded in certain contemporary theories of the ‘Information Society’ (Castells, 1996) in which the current historical period is seen to be substantially different in information terms from those that have gone before.

We would argue that current connotations of information, systems and technology are culturally entrenched and difficult to break down. This difficulty has resonance with a familiar problem experienced by social researchers such as social anthropologists who seek to understand the workings of a society of which they are a part. For instance, within texts on conducting ethnography (Agar, 1996), researchers are provided with a number of techniques to help them make the familiar strange. The most extreme forms of such are Garfinkel’s breaching experiments (Garfinkel, 1967) in which investigators deliberately seek to cause breakdowns (Bødker & Grønbœk, 1991) in the ‘methods’ people employ to act appropriately in particular social situations.

In this section we aim to free up some of the cognitive baggage we have associated with our current conceptions of information, systems and technology by attempting to make the familiar strange. To achieve this, we apply our conceptual framework to understanding the role of activity, information and data systems within a different culture and coming from a different historical period. This not only enables us to broaden current conceptions of terms such as IT, but also serves to illustrate the way in which our current technological frames (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994) are embedded in prior cultural history. This section is based upon a systematic collation and re-interpretation of extant scholarship on Inka civilisation. It also builds and expands upon some previous published work of the author (Beynon-Davies, 2007; Beynon-Davies, 2009c), which has considered this case.

We first need to consider the context within which this account of the enactment of significance had its place. The Inkas were a sophisticated society that existed in the high Andes of South America (D’altroy, 2002) for a comparatively short time (c.1200–1572 AD). This civilisation was remarkable in that it operated effectively without the benefits of a ‘written’ language or any mode of transportation based on the wheel.

The Inka created their empire, which they referred to as Tawantinsuyu (meaning ‘the four parts together’) through conquest. This conquered territory stretched across modern-day Peru and Ecuador, reaching the coast of modern-day Peru as well as extending into modern Colombia, Bolivia, Argentina and Chile. The four parts referred to were based in the Inkas’ conception of the way in which their world was divided. The political and cosmic centre of the empire was the city of Cusco. Radiating from this centre were the lands and peoples associated with the four parts: Chinchaysuyu, Antisuyu, Kollasuyu and Cuntisuyu. Chinchaysuyu encompassed the lands and peoples of the Peruvian coast, the adjacent highlands and the northern Andes. Antisuyu included the warm forests that lay to the north and north-east of Cusco. Kollasuyu formed the largest part of the empire and ran from Peru’s central highlands through the altiplano area to central Chile and Argentina. Cuntisuyu was the smallest part and included the stretch of land that ran south-west from Cusco to the Pacific Ocean.

The Inkas were a highly ordered society. At the top of the society was the Sapa Inka or ‘emperor’ who ruled by divine right as the son of the Sun. Worshipped as a living god, the Sapa Inka’s official wife was his full-blooded sister. The Sapa Inka also maintained a harem of concubines whose offspring held positions of power and influence in the Inka Empire. This hereditary aristocracy formed the upper class of Inka nobility, and was occasionally supplemented by Inkas who were assigned this rank through merit or exceptional service. Below the ruling class were the curacas who formed the lower echelons of Inka nobility. These individuals generally filled the administrative offices of government and supervised the large ‘bureaucracy’ of the empire.

Officials called the cacique were placed at the head of agricultural communities made up of a group of individuals and families known as ayllu. Land was redistributed by the state among the ayllu every year according to the number of active people. Ayllu also had rights to water and herds. The land at the disposal of each ayllu was divided into three unequal parts: the largest of which was given to the community to farm. The other two land parts were consecrated to the cult of the Sun and to the state. Communities also paid a tribute of textiles to the state and a periodic tax of labour. This labour tax, consisting of up to 3 months labour per calendar year for each active individual, was used for collective works such as building roads, monuments and irrigation canals.

## Khipu, khipucamayoq and chask

The organisation of activity within the Inka Empire relied on a number of critical elements: a large and efficient transport network, specialist information personnel (the chaskis and the khipucamayuq) and a method for recording data (khipu).

The Inka transport network consisted of thousands of kilometers of purpose-built roads and rope bridges that straddled Tawantinsuyu. Most of the roads were stonelined and in places extremely narrow, allowing only foot travel and transport of goods using llamas. However, the roads could not be used by everyone. Only those on official business, the chaskis, and the emperor and his armies were permitted to use the transport network.

The chaskis were highly trained runners employed purely in delivering messages throughout the Empire. A long series of small shelters stocked with food and water and known as tambos were arranged along the transport network. Individual chaski would transport messages by running between two such posts, and then pass them on to the next runner. A relay of chaski could transport a message in this manner up to 250 km in 1 day and it is claimed that a message could be delivered from Cusco to Quito, a distance of over 1500 km, within a week.

The Inka ‘bureaucracy’ sent and received many messages daily in support of the activity of the empire. Typically such messages contained details of resources such as items required or available in store houses, taxes owed or collected, census data, and the output of mines or the composition of particular workforces. Messages had to be clear, compact and portable. For this purpose a form of artefact known as the khipu was used, consisting of an assemblage of coloured, knotted cotton or camelid (llama or alpaca wool) cords.

The khipucamayuq (the keeper of the khipus) were responsible for encoding and decoding messages contained in khipu. Encoding or ‘writing’ a khipu involved tying together a complex network of cords of different materials and colours, and adding to them a series of different forms of knot. Decoding or ‘reading’ a khipu involved a khipucamayuq, both in visual inspection and running his fingers rapidly over the knots, rather like a Braille reader.

## Khipu

Several hundred (Urton, 2003) examples of khipu survive and typically vary from having a few cords to, in the largest case, being over 3 m long and having over 2000 cords. There is much argument as to whether khipu were merely used as mnemonic devices or acted as true information artefacts. As mnemonic devices they would merely be used to trigger the memory of an individual khipucamayuq. As an information artefact, they would need to encode signs from some mutually agreed sign-system used by the khipucamayuq.

Andean scholars (Locke, 1923; Ascher & Ascher, 1997; Conklin, 2002; Urton, 2003) believe that there is sufficient evidence to suggest that khipu were true information artefacts. This inference is based upon the historical record that suggests that khipu were rolled up and transported by chains of chaski over long distances. The creator and ‘sender’ of the khipu must therefore have been different from the reader or ‘receiver’ of khipu. This implies some form of encoding of data in such artefacts using a mutually understood ontology.

This interpretation suggests that khipu were used by the Inka as a three-dimensional sign-system in which data were recorded by tracing figures in space with pieces of cord. As a sign-system, khipu can be made sense of in terms of the four levels of the semiotics ladder and in terms of our tri-partite division between three forms of system characteristic of human organisation.

## Khipu as a data system

At the technical level and at the level of empirics, khipu can be considered purely as physical artefacts and analysed solely in terms of their methods of construction. A number of facets have been identified within khipu for encoding the variety (Beer, 1972) demanded of the construction of messages with a significant meaningful content (Figure 4). Five distinct types of knots can be identified within khipu: loop-hitch knots, single knots, ‘long’ knots, figureeight knots and figure-eight knots with an extra turn (Figure 5). Each type of knot may be tied in one of two ways (Urton, 2003). For instance, in terms of a hitch knot, the holding element of the knot may be either in front of the knot or behind the knot. The former Urton calls a recto hitch knot and the latter a verso hitch knot. In terms of single, long and figure-eight knots the orientation of the dominant diagonal axis of these knots is used to distinguish between what Urton calls ‘S’ knots and ‘Z’ knots.

A khipu thread has a particular colour. The threads and cords in khipu are frequently left with their natural cotton or camelid hues. However, a significant proportion of threads and cords, particularly those of cotton, are dyed a particular colour. The Aschers (Ascher & Ascher, 1997) identify 59 distinct colours, including white (neutral) and black, to describe the cords of extant khipu. On the basis of an analysis of colour naming used by native Quechua (the lingua franca of the Inka empire) speakers, Urton (2003) proposes a more restricted ‘palette’ of 24 distinct colours for cords or threads within khipu. Inspection of the palette suggested by the Aschers (Ascher & Ascher, 1997) suggests that approximately 30 or so of their 59 colours might fall roughly into those that occur naturally in unrefined cotton and camelid wool. This leaves some 30 or so colours that would demand some dyeing process. In this manner, Urton’s proposed 26–30 distinct colours seem to correspond with the group of dyed colours. Hence, it is possible to define a ‘palette’ of some 60 or so colours from which the threads and cords of khipu were composed.

It is therefore possible to consider khipu as a data system: a system for encoding and decoding symbols. Khipu ‘writing’ and ‘reading’ rely on the human visual and tactile senses. The relative positioning of elements on khipu such as pendant cords and knots is readily perceived by expert khipu makers. The colour of threads and cords are also clearly significant to a khipucamyuq. The ‘writing’ of a khipu involved a khipucamyuq in constructing a complex network of coloured cords and knots. The ‘reading’ of khipu relied on visual inspection, as well as touching elements of the physical network.

At the level of syntactics, physical elements of khipu can be considered as symbols and specified in terms of some data model (Tsitchizris & Lochovsky, 1982). The overall structure of a khipu is illustrated in stylised form in Figure 6. A main cord formed the ‘horizontal’ dimension of khipu and to this central axis a number of pendant cords are attached via hitch knots. Pendant cords hence form the ‘vertical’ dimension of khipu. Subsidiary cords are frequently knotted to a pendant cord via similar hitch knots. Such subsidiary cords could, in turn, have cords attached to them via hitch knots. Hence, a distinct hierarchy or tree-like structure of cords is evident within khipu. Pendant cords are frequently collected together on the main cord of a khipu in groups.

When this occurs, a top cord is typically attached to the group, but falling in an opposite direction to the direction of pendant cords. Their manner of attachment suggests that they bear a special relationship to a group of pendant cords.

Hence, there is evidence for the knot as being the fundamental symbolic element within khipu. The construction and positioning of knots relative to each other upon a pendant cord can be seen to constitute a datum. A related collection of knots upon a cord – a knot group – constituted a data item. The collection of knots within a knot group serves to value the data item. A group of pendant cords would constitute a data element and the entire assemblage of cords within a khipu constituted a data structure.

It is possible to use Backus-Naur-Form (BNF) (Knuth, 1964) as a way of expressing the representational elements of a data model for khipu artefacts. Part of a BNF specification, corresponding to a generative grammar for khipu described in previous work (Beynon-Davies, 2009c), is given below:

```txt
<khipu> ::= [<dangle cord>] <main cord>
<main cord> ::= <cord> <pendant group>
[<knot group>] [<marker>] end knot
<dangle cord> ::= <cord> dangle knot [<knot group>]
<cord> ::= <material> <spin/ply> <cord colour>
<cord length>
<material> ::= cotton | alpaca wool | llama wool
<cord colour> ::= <single colour> | <multi-colour>
<multi-colour> ::= <colour 1> <colour 2> <combination type>
<colour1> ::= <thread colour>
<colour2> ::= <thread colour>
<single colour> ::= <thread colour>
<thread colour> ::= [<colour hue>] <colour grouping>
<colour grouping> ::= <natural colour> | <dyed colour>
<dyed colour> ::= rose | yellow/orange | green | blue | plum/violet
<natural colour> ::= neutral/white | yellowish brown | brown | grey | grayish brown | grayish olive | reddish brown
<colour hue> ::= light | moderate | deep | dark | very dark
```

This BNF specification uses recursion to represent the structure of a khipu from basic elements. A khipu consists of an optional dangle cord, a main cord and an end knot. A main cord is built from one or more pendant cord groups. Each pendant cord group consists of one or more pendant cords and an optional top cord. Each pendant cord consists of a hitch knot and a possible subsidiary group and knot group. A subsidiary group consists of one or more subsidiary cords and a knot group consists of one or more knots. A subsidiary cord is also defined in terms of a hitch knot, possible knot groups and subsidiary groups. Top cords and dangle cords are also defined upon subsidiary groups and knot groups. Hence, the hierarchical organisation evident in many khipu can be generated through recursive application of this generative grammar.

From the point of view of data processing it is possible to consider khipu construction and use in terms of the four atomic types of formative action: create, read, update and delete. For instance, in terms of khipu, a [create] formative act might consist of constructing a pendant cord with its associated knot group and tying this cord to the main cord (d opendant cord4). In contrast, an [update] act might involve changing the configuration of a particular knot group upon the pendant cord (kopendant cord4).

## Khipu semantics and pragmatics

At the level of semantics, the meaning of symbolic elements within khipu needs to be established. The way in which data were encoded in khipu is still very much a matter of debate and investigation. Leland Locke in the 1920s established that 100 or so of the remaining khipus were used to store the results of record-keeping possibly in support of Inka imperial administration (Mann, 2005). Urton has recently shown how khipu acted as a complex accounting system that enabled census and tribute data collected in the empire to be synthesised, manipulated and transferred among different accounting levels within this administration (Urton & Brezine, 2005).

Evidence suggests that knot groups tied on pendant cords within certain khipu represent numbers to the base 10 (decimal). Particular knot types such as single, figure of eight and long knots and their positioning upon pendant cords signify distinct numbers. The closer the knot to the top of a cord: the higher the number. At the very top a single knot represented multiples of 10,000, then 1000, then 100, then 10 (Figure 7).

The Aschers (Ascher & Ascher, 1997) agree with Locke that knots tied to pendant, subsidiary or tertiary cords could be used to signify numerals or magnitudes of things. However, they also propose that numbers could be used as ‘labels’ denoting other referents. In this sense, they propose the use of knot groups as a more extensive form of coding. In this perspective, a given knot group on a khipu can be considered as similar in nature to a modern bar code, with individual knots substituting for the bars of the code.

The Aschers also suggest the usefulness of engaging in thought experiments in order to understand the potential capacity of khipu for the purposes of signification. For instance, consider such an experiment in which a khipu is used as a record of the tribute supplied to the Inka state in a particular year by a particular provincial area of the Empire. A schematic of the data structure for such a khipu is illustrated in Figure 8.

Within this khipu, the dangle cord and its subsidiary cord identify the particular tributary unit to which the records apply. The main body of this khipu is made up of four groups of five pendant cords each, in which a common pattern of knots is tied to these groups of pendant cords. The first pendant cord in a group (coloured moderate red) identifies a particular tributary unit of 100 people. The second pendant (coloured deep plum) signifies the amount of maize supplied to the Inka state by the tributary unit. The third pendant (coloured deep red) represents the number of llamas supplied as tribute. The fourth pendant (coloured deep green) records the labour tax paid. The final and fifth cord (coloured light green) in the group records the number of units of coca supplied to the state. This is followed by a pendant group recording specific elements of tribute: 185 units of maize, 21 llamas, 54 units of labour and 64 units of coca.

In terms of pragmatics, we would interpret khipu in terms of context: the information systems within which they were used and the activity systems they supported. Hence, at the level of pragmatics we need to consider the use of khipu in support of performative acts. The utilisation of khipu as a mechanism of data storage and transfer only makes sense in the context of the information specialists of the khipucamayuq, as well as the chaski and their use of the Inka transport network. It also is only significant in the context of the tributary systems of the Inka and their need to manage the distribution of labour throughout the empire.

## Activity and information systems of the Inka

There is evidence to suggest that the evolution of information systems in human cultures appears inherently associated with the rise of the state and its need to control the flow of goods and services in relation to its populace (Beniger, 1986). The Inkas faced the challenge of administering a populace that outnumbered them by about a hundred to one (D’altroy, 2002). Tawantinsuyu was divided into 80 provinces and was administrated by a provincial administration consisting of an umbrella of Inka officials supervising a hierarchy of ethnic lords appointed to state service.

As indicated above, various elements of tribute were collected from the provinces by the Inka state. The wealth of Inka society was based primarily upon agriculture and the herding of livestock. Mining, metalwork and textile weaving were also significant performative activities for the various ethnic groups subject to Inka control. Goods and artefacts produced from such activity systems were stored in vast storehouses distributed throughout various provincial areas. However, the main element of tribute paid to the Inka state was a labour tax in which each ‘taxpayer’ had to work a specified number of days each year on state projects such as road construction.

Most taxpayers were appointed to tributary units of 10–10,000 households. At the lowest level, tributaries were grouped into five accounting units of 10 members each. One person from each of these groups of 10 would serve as a Chuka Kamayoq or ‘organiser of 10’. Five groupings of 10, making a group of 50 tribute payers, would be placed under the authority of a Pichqa-Chunka-Kuraca or ‘lord of the 50’. Two groups of 50 would be combined into a unit of 100 tributaries led by a Pachaka Kuraca or ‘lord of 100’.

This hierarchical system of tributary units would continue up to the head of one of the 80 provinces of the empire known as a T’oqrikoq. These provincial lords were normally ethnic Inka and had overall responsibility for administering the population and lands of the province, as well as ensuring that the roads and bridges were in good working order. Provincial lords were in turn placed under the control of an appropriate lord of the four quarters, called Apu, which served directly the Inka emperor based in Cusco (Urton & Brezine, 2005).

Therefore, a number of activity systems of the Inka Empire such as tax collection, the administration of workforces in the building of collective works and the distribution of goods within the Empire relied on an effective system of communication. There is clear evidence of the use of khipu for the keeping of records within wider communication systems. Using the data recorded in khipu, ‘accountants’ in the Inka empire assessed levels of tribute due, recorded tribute paid and assigned aspects of performance to tributary units.

At the provincial level, the Inka also took censuses to keep track of the population. Census data included records of births, deaths, marriages and other changes of a person’s status. Individuals of each sex were assigned to one of 10 categories corresponding not with their chronological age but to their stage in life and ability to perform useful work. Separate khipus were apparently kept for this purpose by each province.

Lower down the administrative hierarchy, Urton proposes two major information flows reliant on the use of khipu. Information flow concerning tribute flowed up the administrative hierarchy and decisions detailing appropriate performance flowed down through the administrative hierarchy. He further claims that information would be partitioned based on the administrative units described above. Hence, local accountants would pass data as to completed tasks upward through the hierarchy. Data at each administrative level would represent the summation of accounts from the level immediately below. These data would eventually be used by imperial accountants based in the Inka capital of Cusco.

There is also evidence of data verification within Inka information systems. The governor of each province was required to keep a copy of khipu accounts so that ‘no deception could be practiced by either the Indian tribute payers or the official collectors’ (Urton & Brezine, 2005). Not surprisingly, historical accounts claim that even the smallest of Inka villages had as many as four khipucamayuq, and that the records kept by each khipucamayuq within a village were cross-checked.

## The enactment of significance

Mann (2005), citing Conklin, highlights the role that culture may have played in the development of the khipu as the primary IT artefact among the Inca. To modern, Western eyes the use of knotted strings for communication appears anomalous. However, the use of weaving and textiles for both tool-making and communication have deep roots in Andean culture. Andean peoples frequently solved basic engineering problems such as the construction of bridges through the manipulation of fibres. There is also evidence of the production of textiles in very early Andean societies for symbolic purposes alone and not for the purposes of clothing. In this cultural environment the use of textiles as a material base for ‘writing’ would have seemed entirely natural.

Orlikowski (2007) and others have tried to resurrect and re-position the nature of technology (particularly IT) within organisation studies. For this purpose she argues for the sociomaterial nature of organisational practice. She argues that ‘y materiality is integral to organizing y the social and the material are constitutively entangled in everyday life. A position of constitutive entanglement does not privilege either humans or technology (in oneway interactions), nor does it link them through a form of mutual reciprocation (in two-way interactions). Instead, the social and material are considered to be inextricably linked – there is no social that is not also material, and no material that is not also social’. Therefore, the sociomaterial is described in terms of constitutive entanglement between technology and humans.

Orlikowski’s examples of sociomaterial practice are located in modern digital computing technology; particularly in that form of IT that enables remote communication such as email, groupware and the use of blackberries. Rather surprisingly, she and others do not debate directly with the notion of an information system in defining the sociomaterial. As such, although they make a strong case for the sociomaterial, they do not offer a lens by which we can understand and unpack the entanglement of technology and action. Our purpose has been to step back and consider simpler IT and its entanglement within activity systems of a strange ‘organisation’ from a distant time. Utilising the concept of an information system directly (which is different from IT on the one hand and instrumental activity on the other) offers a useful way of unpacking the entanglement in cases such as the one considered. In other words, we would propose that the concept of an information system is central to understanding how the sociomaterial works through the enactment of significance.

Therefore. our key argument is that the enactment of significance is central to understanding the nature of the sociomaterial and can be seen to consist of the entanglement of performative, informative and formative action. Weick (1995) uses the term enactment to refer to the process by which individuals bring structures and events into existence and set them in action within an enacted environment. ‘y people enact the environments which constrain them’. Enactment therefore involves both a process and a product, an enacted environment. The enacted environment is described as ‘y the residuum of changes produced by enactment’. It is also described as a ‘material and symbolic record of action’. This suggests that Weick is struggling to encapsulate both technology and action within his conception of an enacted environment.

In terms of our conception we see the enacted environment as composed of the patterning of performance, communication and record-keeping. Such patterns form resources for performative, informative and formative action. People, on the basis of established understandings of the significance of things, will perform in some situation. Patterns of communication will be important to the coordination of such performance among actors. Groups may choose to make records of certain aspects of both communication and performance. One particular advantage of the use of records within communication is that the presence of such persistent artefacts facilitates one-to-many and many-to-many communication within a group of actors over a period of time and across space. It turns individual memory into social memory. Such social memory serves to encapsulate objects and events of significance to some group of actors. These records as social memory, combined with individual memory, will form the enacted environment for further cycles of the enactment of significance.

Consider an example of such enactment within the case of Inka. The entanglement of communicative acts with performative acts and formative acts within this case is illustrated in Figure 9.

As we have described above, a number of performative patterns were important to sustaining the Inka empire such as activities of agriculture, textile manufacture, mineral extraction, road building and census taking. Each of these activity systems was reliant on a complex network of communicative or informative patterns. Hence, within the account detailed above, there is evidence to suggest that khipu were used to reify certain aspects of a number of different types of illocutionary act. For instance, a particular pendant cord would be used to assert that a province had so many people of working age ( [this province has 2801 males of working age]). <sup>A</sup>Alternatively, a pendant cord might be used to record the commitment of a defined amount of labour from a tributary unit for use by the Inka hierarchy (#[this ayllu will pay 54 units of labour tax over the next year in mining]). Khipu might also have been created in formative acts to record opinions about the performance of some group of workers (![this ayllu is under-performing in the growth of foodstuffs]), document orders to specific tributary units (?[185 workers from this province will work for 3 months on repairing a given stretch of road]) and to declare events, such as when tribute had been fully paid by a tributary unit (-[all the textile tribute owed by this ayllu has been collected]). In each case, the data element produced by one khipucamayuq would be read by another khipucamayuq, normally displaced in time and space.

## Conclusion

The conceptual framework described in this paper is deliberately not an attempt to ‘re-invent the wheel’ but to synthesise thinking from a number of areas that are treated in a disparate manner, if at all, within the discipline of Information Systems. The framework centres an appropriate locus (Baskerville & Myers, 2002) for our discipline at the intersection between signs and systems; what we have referred to as the enactment of significance. This conception of enactment suggests a broadening of our sense-making (Weick et al., 2005) to consider the complex entanglement of significance within the nature of human organisation. We conclude with a summary of some of the key values that our conceptual framework supplies and which will form part of the basis for our continuing programme of work. In other words, what is significant about the enactment of significance?

First, it provides greater clarity to the nature of information, particularly the apparent paradox between the subjective, objective and inter-subjective nature of this phenomenon. Our conception of information can be seen to build and expand upon the work of both Dretske (1981) and Mingers (1995). Both criticise the established ‘engineering’ model of information in communication and wish to include semantics within any satisfactory definition of this concept. Both also lay claim that information is both an objective and subjective phenomenon. Information is objective in the sense of comprising physical signals. It is subjective in the sense of relating to interpretation and meaning. Mingers (1995) also importantly points to the inter-subjective nature of information because meaning is expressed in and conditioned by language, or in our terms, sign-systems. However, we believe that using the same term for each of these perspectives actually serves to obscure the multifaceted nature of information and as a consequence helps preserve many of the entrenched connotations of the term, which Boland (1987) challenged some decades ago. We have therefore attempted to demonstrate the utility of segmenting out the forma, informa and performa of significance. Forma can be seen to encapsulate the objective nature of information, informa its subjective nature and performa its inter-subjective nature.

Hence, knot groups within khipu are clear examples of forma. The physical structure of a given knot group has an independent existence over and above the actor who created it. The meaning of such knot groups was clearly a critical and continuing accomplishment within acts of intentional communication and interpretation (informa) in the Inka Empire. Such signification relied upon conventional or inter-subjective agreement concerning not only the intensions associated with knot groups but also the intentions supporting coordinated activity.

Second, the framework helps to breakdown (Bødker & Grønbœk, 1991) our established current conceptions of the nature of ‘IT’ and as such supplies a more historically encompassing and less culturally centric conception of such technology. It also allows us to relate the development of such technology to forms of embodied (Mingers, 2001) human communication. As we have seen, forma is a term we use to stand for the physical aspect of a sign. Hence, we can speak of the forma of human speech or the forma of non-verbal human communication. Such examples of forma are embodied in the sense that human actors communicate by manipulating aspects of their physical makeup directly, whether this be in terms of bodily movement or manipulating internal organs such as the larynx. Such embodied forma can be seen to involve ‘writing’ to a particular sensory modality such as the human auditory channel and ‘reading’ from this channel. However, restricting forma to the vehicles of embodied communication excludes consideration of ‘techne’ in sign-systems – the activities involved in producing signs using ‘tools’ that persist beyond the body and beyond any one communication. Such ‘technical’ activity involves manipulation of a persistent rather than a non-persistent data system. Defining the essence of ‘IT’ in the concept of a persistent data system allows us to make sense of a vast array of artefacts used across time, space and human cultures for physical symbol representation and manipulation.

Hence, within this conception, even though khipu would not traditionally be considered a form of ‘writing’, there is substantial evidence to suggest the use of this artefact as a form of persistent data system. Indeed, this allows us to push back the idea of IT to the first use of such persistent data systems. Marshack (2003) suggests that symbolism, such as scratches on antler horn from the Upper Palaeolithic (35,000–10,000 B.C.) period, may constitute some of the earliest use of a tally, related perhaps to calendar events such as the gestation period of a horse or the phases of the moon. In previous work (Beynon-Davies, 2009a) we have described evidence relating to the use of clay tokens dating back to 8,000 B.C. and the way in which such tokens were used within systems of the earliest accounting.

Third, we would suggest that the term information system as currently used within the discipline of Information Systems is overloaded. A common definition of this term suggests that it refers to the interaction between people, processes, data and technology (Kroenke, 2008). This encompassing, well-established and well-used definition initially served the purpose of highlighting that an information system was a much larger construct than mere IT. However, as Paul (2007a) suggests, use of this encompassing definition now limits the intellectual capacity of the term because it acts merely as a collection of things and does little to help us understand how people, processes, data and technology create significance. It is also probably because of the entangled and overloaded nature of this conception that much Information Systems literature does not address the concept of an information system directly, instead choosing to focus on the IT artefact and its impact upon organisations (Orlikowski, 1991).

Thus, paradoxically, much Information Systems research does not actually study information systems. Instead, it tends to take one of two polar positions. One method of interpretation. characteristic of technological determinism, tends to portray innovations in IT as a critical force or driver shaping innovations in social systems. Another method of interpretation, characteristic of the social construction of technology, tends to portray information technologies as fundamentally shaped by social forces. It should be apparent from our method of exposition that we believe that a more sophisticated and accurate view is to portray the relationship between IT (data systems) and activity systems as being much more dialectical in nature. Data systems and activity systems exist in a reinforcing cycle of interaction and creation through the mediating position of information systems.

In one sense our definition of an information system as a specialised form of communication system appears much narrower in scope than the encompassing definition and looks, at first glance, to provide little to understanding the exigencies of modern digital computing systems. In another sense, we would argue, that our conception of an information system, when placed within the wider conceptual framework described, actually provides more intellectual capacity and greater applicability than the encompassing definition described above. It not only allows us to provide a clearer conception of what an information system constitutes; it also addresses the problematic raised by Paul (2007a): how such systems differ from IT on the one hand and activity systems or business processes on the other. In doing so it provides preliminary answers to the questions provided in the previous paragraph. For instance, it allows us to appreciate the functional role that information systems play within human societies of various forms. As suggested within the Inka case, many societies (Beniger, 1986) invent various forms of information system and their associated technologies to address crises in the control of activity. In the case of khipu, the manipulation of such artefacts appears to have been critical to communication between dispersed and frequently different ethnic communities making up the Inka Empire. This communication was essential to ensuring the coordinated performance of dispersed groups across the high Andes.

Fourth, the framework provides a new rendering of information systems as sociotechnical systems (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977). Rather surprisingly, although digital computers have been applied for over 50 years within organisations, we still lack a coherent conceptualisation of the value that such technology has for organisations. This enduring problematic of IT and organisation has been highlighted in recent literature within other disciplines such as Organization Science. Zammuto et al. (2007), for instance, argue that ‘the relationship between technology and organizational form and function has been of interest to organization scientists for over 50 years y [However,] organization science’s interest in this relationship has declined significantly over the past thirty years, a period during which information technologies have become pervasive in organizations and brought about significant changes in them’. They further suggest that ‘a conceptual shift – from ‘‘organizational form’’ to ‘‘forms of organizing’’ – is needed y viewing the social and technological systems of organizations in concert, which was a critical part of sociotechnical systems theory in the 1950s, is a perspective that the field needs to rediscover because IT has become inextricably intertwined with social relations to weave the fabric of organization’.
