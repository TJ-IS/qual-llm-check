# The Assimilation of Enterprise Information Systems: An Interpretation Systems Perspective

## ABSTRACT

This research addresses the question of why enterprise-wide information systems are so difficult to assimilate. Conceptualizing organizations as interpretation systems, we posit that internal scanning of emerging organizational needs will have a direct positive effect on the firm’s level of enterprise IT assimilation, moderated by the extent of technology evaluation activities. Furthermore, we introduce the concept of information systems (IS) mindfulness, an elevated state of collective alertness toward IS clients’ needs, in this model. Our findings suggest that the presence of IS mindfulness increases an IS unit’s potential to identify and interpret opportunities to assimilate enterprise-wide IT within a firm.

Keywords: Mindfulness, Assimilation, ERP, Scanning, Evaluation, Enterprise-wide IT

## The Assimilation of Enterprise Information Systems: An Interpretation Systems Perspective

## 1. INTRODUCTION

When investing significant capital in enterprise IT, firms can fail to realize the expected payoffs for many reasons, ranging from unrealistic expectations to underestimating the technical difficulties associated with IT deployment [8, 44]. While technology failures, user resistance, and vendor problems can contribute to enterprise IT deployment failures, significant business risk also arises from the difficulties organizations face when attempting to move beyond initial deployment of enterprise systems and realize their full benefits by using them effectively to support, shape, and enable business strategies and value-chain activities [28, 66]. Even in situations where an enterprise technology is successfully deployed, individual firms differ in their ability to assimilate it; that is, to incorporate the technology into business practices in a way that effectively supports and enables the firm’s business goals, activities, and strategies [4, 49].

One arena where the challenges of enterprise IT assimilation can be clearly seen is ERP implementation. Enterprise resource planning systems are commercially available software packages that enable integration of transaction-oriented data and business processes throughout an enterprise’s supply chain [48]. ERP technologies constitute one of the most complex information technologies in the market today, and are deployed by many organizations as a foundation for efforts to improve overall performance and competitiveness [50]. However, organizations vary significantly in their ability to assimilate these technologies, with many ERP deployments failing to achieve the expected financial and operational impacts [1, 91]. Despite these difficulties, there is still evidence that effectively assimilated ERP systems constitute an important source of competitive advantage [33], leading ERP technologies to be embraced by many large and medium-sized companies [74].

Why are ERP technologies so difficult to assimilate? One important reason is that realizing the benefits of these systems involves more than simply implementing software and hardware. Assimilating enterprise technologies, such as ERP systems, requires extensive adaptation of both the technology and the organization [34, 4]. While some of these changes can occur during design and initial implementation of the system, ensuring effective ERP assimilation also requires that firms engage in ongoing adaptation of both the ERP software and the organization long after initial deployment has taken place [49, 48]. Yet despite the recognized need to support ERP assimilation, a significant portion of firms fail to achieve it, and hence leave many of the benefits of adopting ERP technologies unrealized [31, 68]. These missed opportunities suggest a need for a new perspective on how effective IS units manage the evolution of enterprise technologies and a need for additional research to better understand previously overlooked factors underlying the outcomes of efforts to facilitate IT assimilation [35, 5, 43].

In this study, we argue that ERP assimilation, and enterprise IT assimilation more generally, is facilitated by an IS management function that identifies, evaluates, and acts upon specific adaption opportunities in the post-deployment phase of the system’s life cycle. Using Daft and Weick’s [25] model of organizations as interpretation systems as the theoretical base, we focus on scanning and evaluation of enterprise IT adaptation opportunities as key management activities for promoting ERP assimilation. Augmenting Daft and Weick’s [25] work with

## 2. AN INTERPRETATION SYSTEM MODEL OF ERP ASSIMILATION

Enterprise Resource Planning systems are a particularly complex class of system that many organizations have found difficult to assimilate [59, 43]. ERP technologies include a variety of software, hardware, and infrastructure components, all of which must be integrated into an efficient, high-reliability system if they are to provide a useful platform for supporting critical business activities. Equally important are the challenges that arise from the deep and wide ranging consequences that ERP systems can have for organizational processes, structures, and even cultures [69]. Effective assimilation of ERP technologies is further complicated by the ever evolving nature of both the organizational needs and the capabilities of the underlying software and hardware. All of these features combine to make assimilating ERP a “wicked problem” [14]. Yet, it is exactly this problem that firms choosing to invest in ERP technologies must engage if they are to achieve the hoped for financial and organizational benefits.

#

Successful assimilation of any information technology requires mutual adaptation of both the technology and organizational practices [42]. While some of this adaptation can take place during initial design and implementation of the system, the complexity of ERP technologies and the breadth of their potential impact within the organization mean that ERP assimilation is always an ongoing, incremental process that continues long after initial deployment of the technology [49, 48]. New organizational needs develop or are discovered. Strategies and priorities change. Organizational units are restructured. Processes are redesigned. Lines of business are created or discontinued. ERP vendors develop new features and capabilities. Thirdparty software firms create add-ons that can be used in conjunction with the core ERP technology to analyze and disseminate operational data to business decision makers in new ways.

Together, emerging organizational needs and evolving technological capabilities create numerous opportunities to adapt an ERP system to better support business activities and objectives. ERP assimilation is achieved by taking advantage of these opportunities to refine the match between the firm’s needs and the available ERP features and capabilities [78]. Without effective, ongoing management of emerging ERP adaption opportunities, many aspects of the ERP technology are likely to be overlooked, necessary changes to how the technology is used within the business will not be made, and the ERP system will not be effectively assimilated by the organization [32, 43, 59].

The ERP assimilation process therefore depends on an organization’s ability to act on combinations of emerging ERP-related needs and new technologies, which create the conditions needed to advance the mutual adaptation of the system and the organization [34]. Identifying and taking advantage of these adaptation opportunities is a difficult learning process [48]. While existing work [7, 65] suggests that IT managers can significantly impact IT assimilation through their actions, it is unclear what practices and structures might be put in place within the IS function to facilitate ERP assimilation.

Daft and Weick [25], in their discussion of organizations as interpretation systems, argue that organizations must interpret events detected in their environment to be able to take proper action. Organizational activity is described in terms of three stages: scanning, interpretation, and learning. Scanning, which involves monitoring an environment and providing appropriate data to managers, can be done through formal or informal data collection systems. Organizational interpretation, or evaluation, is the process of translating events into the local context and developing a shared understanding of the potential action among organizational members. Evaluation includes identification of events as significant, recognition of phenomena as important, evaluation of possibilities as either desirable or undesirable, and determination of their worth or significance. Learning or action, the last stage, involves responding to or taking action based on the previous scanning and evaluation of identified opportunities [3]. Daft and Weick [25] also suggest that a strict linear sequencing of scanning and interpretive evaluation many be an over-simplification since scanning may interact with interpretive evaluation (instead of simply preceding it) to affect learning and actions.

## 3. RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESIS

## 3.1 ERP need scanning and assimilation

Daft and Weick’s [25] interpretation system view of organizations is grounded in specific premises about the nature, purpose, and consequences of information gathering and use within organizations [25]. The first premise is that organizations are open systems that must process information from and about their environments [73]. The environment is monitored to identify events that require a response from the organization [41, 16]. This implies that scanning the environment for information about changes that are likely to affect the organization is a foundational activity that underlies organizational activity and performance.

IT assimilation, and more specifically ERP assimilation, can be understood in terms of effectively managing emerging opportunities to adapt the ERP system and the organization [42, 48, 34]. ERP adaptation opportunities can arise from changes in the firm or the firm’s environment [26, 43, 59]. At the same time, vendors may develop new ERP features [89, 56]. Together, the evolving ERP-related needs of an organization, along with technological capabilities and innovations, create opportunities for advancing ERP assimilation. Yet, it is only when the IT function recognizes emerging ERP-related organizational needs and adaptation opportunities, and capitalizes on them, that an ERP system can be effectively adapted to support a firm’s goals [90, 39, 34].

The interpretation system perspective suggests that in order to facilitate ERP assimilation, the IS function must continuously collect information about potential opportunities created by new, or at least newly discovered, ERP-related organizational needs. This means that the IS function must scan other units or areas within the organization to recognize emerging organizational

#

needs. This information gathering, or ERP need scanning, corresponds to environmental scanning [16, 46]. It is through ongoing ERP need scanning that the IS function becomes aware of potential ERP adaptation opportunities that, if acted upon, may enable a more effective application of the ERP system supporting business strategies and value-chain activities. The IS function engaged in more ERP need scanning is more likely to become aware of emerging needs of business units or other functional areas and the opportunities they present for ERP assimilation [32]. IS personnel can be explicitly assigned the task of identifying new ERP-related organizational needs. IS management can also encourage individuals to interact with colleagues in other functional areas or business units in order to gather information about possible trends and emergent needs. Just as active environmental scanning is related to better organizational performance [16, 24, 91], it is posited that IS functions that engage in ERP scanning activities within their organizations will have higher levels of ERP assimilation [42, 39] because they are more aware of emerging ERP-related organizational needs. Conversely, IS functions that do not engage in ERP-related need scanning activities will have lower levels of assimilation because they are more likely to miss potential opportunities to adapt their ERP systems to support their business strategies and value-chain activities. This suggests:

Hypothesis 1: The extent of an IS unit’s ERP need scanning will have a direct positive effect on the level of ERP assimilation within an organization.

## 3.2 ERP solution evaluation and assimilation

The second premise of Daft and Weick’s [25] interpretation systems perspective on organization is that cognitive processes associated with interpretation of events are distributed throughout a

firm and not necessarily isolated with one individual. Interpretive activities, such as shared observations, discussion, debate, and deliberation, underlie an organization’s ability to bridge the gap between raw information about the environment generated by scanning activities and the design and selection of reasonable courses of action [23, 87]. While the information gathered by scanning provides a foundation, identification and evaluation of the opportunities implicit in that information are necessary conditions for effective organizational action. Similarly, while ERP need scanning can provide information about organizational needs, developing a shared interpretation of potential ERP adaptation opportunity and assessing their relevance for the organization’s objectives and goals (i.e. ERP solution evaluation) is a necessary condition for successful ERP assimilation.

ERP Need Scanning involves gathering information about emerging needs that constitute potential ERP adaptation opportunities. However, not all adaptation opportunities are likely to have the same impact on ERP assimilation. Among the adaptation opportunities will be some that significantly improve the degree to which the ERP system supports the business goals, and others which ultimately will have little or no impact on ERP assimilation. Others may not be aligned with organizational objectives or be infeasible given the available financial resources, personnel, and organizational climate. Collecting information about potential adaptation opportunities creates the possibility of advancing ERP assimilation, but effectively facilitating the assimilation process also involves directing limited organizational resources and attention toward the ERP adaptation opportunities that have the greatest potential.

#

ERP solution evaluation mechanisms involve identifying ERP adaptation opportunities as significant and worth acting upon. In many firms, informal processes determine what form incremental adaptation takes. Workarounds, shadow systems, and informally requested changes idiosyncratic needs and objectives. Through these processes, interpretation of the significance of ERP adaptation opportunities may be driven by coincidental co-occurrence of interests [19], local interests, needs, and agendas [22], improvisation [22], or even by accident [58]. While some ERP adaptation opportunities acted upon may result in greater ERP assimilation, others “selected” this way may not facilitate ERP assimilation and may even hinder effective adaptation. Opportunities for locally desirable adaptations may create operational distractions leading the organization to forego opportunities for greater ERP assimilation [47, 55].

Systematic ERP solution evaluation mechanisms contribute to ERP assimilation by increasing the likelihood that the most feasible and effective ERP adaptation opportunities for deployment are identified as the most promising. Formal ERP solution evaluation activities constitute coping mechanisms to assign significance to the potential ERP adaptation opportunities, designating some as desirable and others as less so [6]. These processes act as a filter, increasing the realization chances for those ERP adaptation opportunities that the IS function believes to be most feasible with the greatest contribution to the firm’s business objectives. On the other hand, underinvestment in formal ERP solution evaluation activities may lead to ERP adaptation opportunities being realized either by chance or idiosyncratic local agendas, resulting in lower levels of assimilation of the ERP system. This suggests that ERP solution evaluation has a moderating effect on the relationship between ERP need scanning and ERP assimilation:

Hypothesis 2: The effect of ERP need scanning on the level of ERP assimilation will be positively moderated by the extent of ERP solution evaluation activities.

## 3.3 IS mindfulness and ERP assimilation

The final premise of Daft and Weick’s [25] interpretation systems perspective is that organizations differ systematically in the mode or process by which they interpret the environment. Different organizations develop different ways of engaging their environments, and this leads to different courses of action (or inaction) which in turn results in different organizational outcomes [25]. The same scanning and evaluation activities, such as gathering documents or conducting a proposal review, can lead to higher or lower levels of performance depending on how the organization approaches the situation [36, 68, 85]. This implies that, in addition to being affected by the levels of ERP need scanning and ERP solution evaluation undertaken, ERP assimilation will also be affected by the way that the IS function approaches the challenges of interpreting and evaluating information about potential ERP adaptation opportunities [72].

While ERP need scanning can provide information about possibilities, effective ERP assimilation requires the organizational ability to recognize and engage emergent opportunities which deviate from, and even potentially disrupt, the stable, well-understood expectations embedded in ongoing operations. Weick et al. [87] have argued that the presence of collective

#

mindfulness, an elevated state of collective alertness<sup>1</sup>, leads to an enhanced ability to recognize and respond appropriately to unexpected events, those events that run counter to prevailing assumptions. Collective mindfulness focuses on a group’s ability to perceive cues, interpret them as being associated with significant phenomena, and respond appropriately. Extreme examples of contexts where collective mindfulness is needed are high-reliability organizations (HROs) that provide life-and-death services under tight constraints in complex environments such as hospitals, aircraft carriers, or chemical manufacturing facilities [88]. While few IS units operate under such life-or-death pressures, the centrality of ERP systems to the functioning and survival of a firm, combined with the need to support adaptation, create analogous challenges and a similar need for collective mindfulness [12]. In all cases, both the extreme and the more mundane, the common denominator is that the presence of mindfulness increases the group’s ability to recognize and interpret the cues that allow them to detect and adapt to the appearance of unexpected opportunities in a timely and reasonable fashion.

IS mindfulness is the ability of the IS function to be alert to its clients’ needs, recognize related unexpected problems and opportunities and to respond in a contextually appropriate manner [29, 38, 79, 87]. Mindfulness can be understood as “a rich awareness of discriminatory detail” [89]. Because of this, ERP need scanning activities in a mindful IS function are more likely to prompt managers to uncover and take action on ERP adaptation opportunities leading to greater ERP assimilation. Scanning activities in a less mindful IS function are less likely to facilitate the

discovery of and action on ERP adaptation opportunities that are the basis for high levels of ERP assimilation. Just as the ability to recognize and react to unexpected situations allows medical units to provide efficient, reliable services that are adapted to idiosyncratic patient needs [37], an IS function that is able to detect and interpret cues that indicate the presence of a high-potential ERP adaptation opportunity in the information that it gathers will be better able to lead and facilitate ERP assimilation [72]. Thus, firms in which the IS function is more collectively mindful will be better able to leverage their ERP need scanning activities to facilitate organizational assimilation of ERP systems. This suggests that IS mindfulness moderates the relationship between ERP need scanning and ERP assimilation:

Hypothesis 3: The effect of ERP need scanning on the level of ERP assimilation will be positively moderated by the degree of IS mindfulness.

## 3.4 Control variables

While ERP need scanning, ERP solution evaluation, and IS mindfulness are expected to significantly affect an organization’s ability to derive business benefit from its ERP technologies, they are not the only factors that affect ERP assimilation. To account for other aspects of the systems and environment that might affect ERP assimilation various controls based on the extant literature [59, 43, 53] are included in the model and analysis. IS functions in charge of larger

#

systems are likely to have more resources to support management and maintenance of their ERP systems potentially allowing for greater ERP assimilation. To control for this possibility, the number of ERP system users as a measure of size is included as a control variable in the model. Similarly, it could be argued that the longer the ERP software has been in place the more likely organizations know how to assimilate ERP technologies [43]. Thus, age of the ERP system as a control variable takes into account the accumulated organizational learning that facilitates assimilation [27]. To address this possibility, both the time since the first ERP module was deployed in production and the amount of time since the last ERP module was deployed in production are included in the model as control variables.

ERP assimilation may also be affected by the nature of the larger IT environment in which the organization operates. Prior studies of organizational adaptation have found that environmental turbulence can be a significant factor [61, 73] with organizations that operate in turbulent environments needing to innovate more frequently to be able to compete effectively [26]. IT turbulence is defined as the rate at which the information technology that is used within an industry changes in this study [13].

## 4. RESEARCH METHODS

To test the model and hypotheses, a survey instrument was created using a combination of existing and newly developed measures. Data were collected from a sample of firms with ERP systems in production using a combination of email and paper-based survey instruments.

## 4.1 Sample

The sample consisted of firms from a variety of industries in which ERP systems are likely to play a significant role. The primary source was the U.S. directory of firms from the Association for Operations Management (APICS). This list was chosen because professionals working on ERP systems are typically APICS members and it was expected that the large majority of APICS firms would have an enterprise resource planning system in place. Only members working in the IS function were surveyed. To ensure a broad sample of firms, the APICS contact list was supplemented with a sample of convenience drawn mainly from the first author’s MIS contacts. Out of 595 firms contacted (500 from the APICS list and 95 from the supplemental sample), 129 surveys were returned, with 121 (101 from the APICS list) providing complete and usable data (total response rate<sup>2</sup>: 20.3%). As seen in Table 1, the responding firms used a variety of different ERP vendors, varied in size, and typically had been working with their ERP system for two or more years. Approximately 47% of the firms were from the manufacturing and processing sector while the others were from sectors such as government, pharmaceutical, and services. Individual respondents were experienced IS managers in their organizations with most having middle and upper management positions. At the time of the survey, about 45% of respondents had been in their current position for more than five years (see Table 2).

## 4.2 Measures and instrument development

Whenever possible, construct measures were created based on previously validated survey instruments. In all cases, individual measures were averaged to obtain a single value for each construct. A 7-point Likert scale was used to assess the extent of agreement/disagreement with each statement in the questionnaire.

ERP need scanning items were drawn from prior studies of scanning [78, 46], which were then adapted for the specific context of this study. The items assessed the degree to which firms formally explored and collected data about ERP-related emergent organizational needs. The measure of ERP solution evaluation was created by drawing relevant items from Benamati and Lederer’s [6] study of the coping mechanisms organizations use to deal with technological change. The ERP solution evaluation scale developed for this study was obtained by selecting coping mechanisms related to evaluation, and adapting the item wording to the specifics of ERP management. The selected items assess the extent of a firm’s efforts to formally evaluate of the adaptation opportunity presented by ERP-related technological solutions.

ERP assimilation items were developed using measures from previous studies of IT assimilation, supplemented with additional items [4, 52]. For these items, respondents were asked to indicate whether or not the specific business activity was critical for the firm (coded as 1 or 0, respectively). Only the activities identified as critical were used to create the measure of ERP assimilation for each firm. This was done because some firms may not engage in all business activities. For example, being a low-cost producer may not be important for a firm that focuses on a differentiation strategy. For this firm, however, the critical activity would be enhancing its existing products and services. For the critical activities, the respondent was asked to assess the degree to which their ERP system supported that activity using a 7-point Likert scale. Next, the individual measures of the critical activities were averaged to obtain a single value for ERP assimilation.

The development of the IS mindfulness items proved to be more challenging because there has been little rigorous development of an instrument to measure collective mindfulness. Weick and Sutcliffe [87] suggested some practical questions managers could use to get a sense of the degree of collective mindfulness present in their organizations. More recently, Vogus and Sutcliffe [84] developed a safety organizing scale based on a mindfulness framework for use within a hospital, but it is based on mindfulness antecedents. Similarly, Mu and Butler [51] developed an instrument to measure mindfulness antecedent processes. Knight [36] attempted to measure the nomological network of organizational mindfulness; that is antecedent processes and collective mindfulness itself, but within the context of public swimming pools while Ray et al [67] did something similar within the context of colleges.

While the existing work provided some insight into measuring IS mindfulness, none of the existing instruments was directly applicable to the IS management context. For this reason, the items for IS mindfulness underwent a rigorous instrument development procedure to evaluate their validity. The original set of items for assessing collective mindfulness was subjected to two rounds of card sorting to assess face validity and clarity [81]. A panel of judges (four in each round) was asked to sort the items according to the construct definitions. In each round, items were dropped, modified, or added as needed. Once a consistent set was obtained, the preliminary version of the IS mindfulness measure was pre-tested with a sample of MBA students to ensure that the terminology and phrasing would be clear to managers and technical professionals.

Most control variables were measured directly as a single value. Specifically, the number of users in the ERP system, how long ago the first ERP module had been deployed, and how long ago the last ERP module had been deployed were each captured using a single question item. IT turbulence was measured as a construct comprised of four items.

The complete survey instrument was subjected to two rounds of pre-tests with two ERP practitioners in each round to ensure participants could understand and respond to the items [20]. After each pre-test, the surveys were revised and the wording modified according to the feedback provided by the participants. Together, the pre-tests verified that both the data collection protocol and survey instruments were adequate for the purpose of the study.

## 5. ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

Standard psychometric techniques were used to assess the reliability and validity of the scales. Multiple factor analyses were performed with all items to assess convergent and discriminant validity. Principal component analysis was conducted utilizing a varimax rotation. Items that had unacceptable cross-loadings (greater than or equal to 0.4) were removed. This resulted in the removal of two items from the scanning scale and three items from the evaluation scale. A second and final factor analysis (Table 3) showed that the refined measures were suitable for testing the proposed research model. All items load on the appropriate factors, and all cross-

loadings are less than 0.4, suggesting the measures exhibit both convergent and discriminant validity [77]. The final list of items used in this study is shown in Appendix A along with the items that were removed during this validation procedure. Cronbach’s alpha was above 0.7 for each scale [54]. Thus, the modified scales were deemed reliable. A correlation matrix with descriptive statistics and reliability for the final constructs is shown in Table 4.

The use of a single informant to provide organizational data introduces the possibility of common method bias. To control for this, standard procedural and statistical techniques were followed [62]. The questionnaire was designed so criterion and predictor variables were separated. Furthermore, the respondents were guaranteed anonymity. In addition, a Harman’s single factor test was performed. The exploratory factor analysis results showed an unrotated solution (eigenvalues > 1) where the first factor accounted for 29.9% of the total variance and the factors together accounted for 76.9% of the total variance. Since a single factor did not emerge or account for most of the variance, these results suggest that common method bias is unlikely to be a significant issue in this study [62].

Hierarchical multiple regression was employed to test both the main effect of scanning on assimilation (H1) and the moderating effects of mindfulness (H2) and evaluation (H3) on this relationship. ## 6. DISCUSSION

It has been argued that effectively managing ongoing assimilation is key for firms seeking to derive expected business benefits from IT investments in general [9] and ERP systems in particular [2, 30]. This study proposes that IT assimilation depends on an organization’s ability to recognize and respond to adaptation opportunities through the processes of scanning, interpretation, and action. Based on this view, we have hypothesized and empirically found that efforts by the IS function to scan for emerging ERP-related organizational needs has a direct positive effect on assimilation of the ERP system within the organization as a whole. Furthermore, although not hypothesized, collective mindfulness and solution evaluation both

have significant positive main effects on ERP assimilation, suggesting they play an important role on the extent of assimilation of the ERP system.

More interestingly, the model and the associated empirical findings indicate that collective mindfulness in the IS function (IS mindfulness) has a significant positive moderating effect on the relationship of ERP need scanning and ERP assimilation. This basic yet distinctive relationship reflects a fundamental tension associated with the IS function’s role in ERP assimilation: the tension between stability and adaptation. As critical organizational infrastructure, it is essential that ERP systems be managed as utilities with low risk of failure and minimal cost (14). Once a business becomes dependent on an ERP system, failure of that technology can have a significant, immediate business impact [45]. Because of this, IS management is pressured to focus available resources, personnel, and attention on maintaining stable, cost-effective, consistent systems which support existing business activities [70]. This pressure leads many IS managers to avoid making changes to ERP systems in order to minimize potential disruption of core business activities. In this regard, successful ERP system management is a matter of meeting expectations – of maintaining the status quo.

On the other hand, assimilation arguments imply that realizing the benefits of investments in ERP systems is highly dependent on the IS function’s ability to capitalize on adaptation opportunities – adaptation which requires potentially risky modification and changes to the systems, practices, and structures of the organization [57]. The IS organization must be able to recognize, imagine, and sometimes even create opportunities to challenge and change basic routines of the organization [17, 64].

Infrastructures, organizational practices, and work routines are valuable because they create maintaining what is normal and what is expected (i.e. low IS mindfulness) can constrain individuals’ and groups’ abilities to imagine alternatives, as suggested by our results. In the extreme, information gathering, as might be done as part of ERP need scanning, becomes an exercise in confirmation of expectations with anything that suggests “the need for change” being classified as irrelevant or impossible [18]. Hence, as indicated by the moderating effects of IS mindfulness, engaging in IS scanning without a mindful consideration of the organizational context can be counter-productive, resulting in lower ERP assimilation and less effective realization of the value of ERP investments (see Figure 2).

ERP adaptation opportunities are, by definition, situations in which there is the potential for changing the systems or the associated work practices and their integration into user knowledge sets (20) – situations that violate expectations [80]. Hence, effectively achieving ERP assimilation requires the IS function to also recognize and engage emergent opportunities which deviate from, and even potentially disrupt, the stable, well understood expectations embedded in ongoing operations. If the IS function is more mindful, the relationship between scanning and assimilation is positive, as seen in Figure 2. These findings suggest that actively gathering more information about the needs of the organization is only beneficial for facilitating ERP assimilation if the IS function approaches the process with careful attention to local differences and an awareness that new opportunities might challenge expectations and assumptions; that is, with a high degree of mindfulness.

On one hand, the idea of IS mindfulness is a logical extension of the long-standing admonitions that developers attend to the needs of business users [59, 91]. However, it goes beyond this to suggest that, at least in the context of ERP assimilation, more is involved than simply attending to user needs in planning and design of systems. This studies suggests that realizing the potential of enterprise systems requires that the IS units charged with working with these systems after their initial implement remain alert for and ready to respond to the possibility for previous unconsidered business needs.

## 7. IMPLICATIONS FOR RESEARCH

The results of this study have important implications for research. First, the IT literature has previously considered scanning as the set of activities to monitor the emergence of new information technologies in the firm’s external environment [46]. This study shows that it is also important to scan the firm’s internal environment, monitoring emerging needs within the organization. In particular, the reported results suggest that internal scanning of emerging organizational needs by the IS function is particularly important for organizations seeking to achieve greater levels of IT assimilation. These findings also raise the question of what type of mechanisms can be put in place to most effectively monitor and respond to emerging needs in the firm. While the items used in this work suggest some possibilities, including conducting formal reviews of emerging needs and assigning personnel official responsibility for gathering information about business needs in other areas, additional work is needed to best describe how the IS function can best perform active, internal scanning.

At the same time, while it was expected that formal ERP technology evaluation activities undertaken by the IS function would magnify the impact of ERP need scanning on assimilation, this hypothesis was not supported. Although there was a significant direct effect of ERP evaluation on ERP assimilation, the interaction of ERP solution evaluation and ERP need scanning was not statistically significant. For this reason, future research should consider gathering additional information about specific scanning and evaluation activities, perhaps with qualitative methods such as interviews or review of archival records, and potentially at the level of particular opportunities, to better understand the role of these activities within the larger process of managing both ERP and IT assimilation. On this point, Daft and Weick [25] warned also about the possibility of scanning and evaluation activities being hard to separate. After all, we look for (scanning) and notice only what we believe is important (evaluation) and we assess (evaluate) only what we have noticed; therefore, it is hard to separate these activities and this may explain the significant direct effect that both activities have on assimilation.

Lastly, the results of this research suggest that collective mindfulness, still a relatively new concept in the IS management literature, plays a key role in shaping the impact of scanning on the assimilation of information technology. In particular, the significant interaction of IS mindfulness and ERP need scanning suggest that activities, such as gathering information about emerging business needs, may have detrimental effects if they are not coupled to an appropriate collective mindset. Prior work on scanning in IS has applied as basic information processing model which assert that presence or absence of information about opportunities is the critical factor. However, the adaptation opportunities interpretation model and the empirical findings presented above imply that explanations and recommendations which do not take IS mindfulness into account are at best incomplete, and at worst misleading. More generally, the results of this study suggest that future studies of enterprise IT innovation and IT assimilation should carefully consider how constructs and theories of organizational cognition might be used to strengthen basic behavioral and activity oriented models.

Facilitating ERP assimilation, and IT assimilation more generally, is not simply a matter of completing projects successfully. IT assimilation is also a matter of interpreting emerging needs as adaptation opportunities that are possible and important for the organization. While there have been theoretical discussions of collective mindfulness [29, 87, 88] and some initial empirical studies [36, 83] in both management and in the IS literature [80], additional work is needed to develop the theoretical, methodological, and managerial tools needed to realize its potential value. What determines whether IS functions see emerging needs as threats or as adaptation opportunities? How do different IS governance structures affect IS mindfulness, and subsequently IT assimilation? Is it possible to create units which specialize in mindfully engaging potential opportunities or is it necessary for the entire IS function to be mindful? The work presented here is an initial effort to operationalize collective mindfulness in an IS context and link it to a significant IT management outcome. This takes a step toward opening the range of possibilities for future empirical studies into the role of mindfulness in IS management [12] and IS innovation [80].

## 8. IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE

Enterprise IT systems must be actively managed long after initial deployment to obtain and maximize expected business benefits. While ongoing management of the full ERP lifecycle has not been widely studied yet [5, 59, 42, 48], this study suggests the process can be viewed as a matter of effectively discovering, recognizing, and acting on adaption opportunities. Actively scanning for ERP-related needs is a necessary condition for engaging in the adaptation opportunity interpretation process. However, in addition to monitoring new ERP-related technologies, which is often done in the IS function, it is also important to develop the capability within the IS function to recognize and respond to cues about emerging internal business needs as opportunities to increase the value derived from existing enterprise systems. Like disasters and catastrophes in high-risk environments, emergent needs tend to appear subtly, suggested first by weak, noisy cues, only becoming “official” later when there is a confluence of factors behind them (and a corresponding urgency to fulfill them). IS units that actively seek information about internal needs and intentionally evaluate these opportunities, and do both of these activities in a way that reflects an alertness to local differences and an openness to the unexpected, will be better able to anticipate emergent organizational needs, leverage the opportunities to adapt the systems, and ultimately achieve a high level of IT assimilation. Organizations with high levels of IS mindfulness may take full advantage of the positive effect of scanning on IT assimilation. By encouraging IS employees to raise questions, consider non-standard approaches, brainstorm alternatives, and otherwise challenge the status quo, IT management can magnify the impact that their scanning activities have on ERP assimilation.

Perhaps equally important, the interaction between ERP scanning and IS mindfulness suggests that in IS departments with low levels of mindfulness, increasing scanning activities may be detrimental, resulting in reduced levels of IT assimilation. The results of this study suggest that IT units which do not take a mindful approach to managing IT assimilation should be aware of the possibility that this may be an area where the general idea that gathering more information “can’t hurt” may be incorrect.

From a practical point of view, IT managers must put in place management processes, such as scanning, to facilitate the assimilation of information technology. Often, firms disband or drastically reduce to crippling levels their ERP steering committees after the system enters production. However, the results of this study imply that having a formal structure to keep monitoring emerging needs is key for greater assimilation. More importantly, IT managers must be aware that these processes can only be effective in the presence of an appropriate level of collective mindfulness in the IS function.

## 9. LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSIONS

As with any study, there are limitations. This study is based on a cross-sectional survey, and therefore it is not possible to establish the direction of causality. While we have argued that the positive association between ERP need scanning and ERP assimilation is indicative of ERP scanning supporting ERP assimilation, it may be possible that past successes associated with using ERP systems may lead organizations to be more willing to invest time and resources to search for new ERP adaption opportunities. Further, while we believe our measures are sound, and they exhibited reasonable psychometric properties, it is undoubtedly possible to improve them.

The survey results reported here are also subject to potential biases because they rely on a single informant from each organization. However, the presence of significant interaction effects, and the use of middle and top managers as informants serve to minimize the likelihood that the informant biases negatively impact the conclusions of this study. In addition, tests for this bias, as discussed in the analysis and results section, suggested this bias was not a problem.

The measure that was used in this study for IS mindfulness focuses on the attentional aspects of IS mindfulness (i.e. noticing and being alert). However, there is also a significant action or behavioral element of collective mindfulness -- if a unit can "see" something, but cannot act in response to it, then it is unlikely to have the full effect. Future research, possibly qualitative, should carefully consider how the unit level process of actively seeking out, attending to, acting on information about evolving business needs relate to the outcomes of IT assimilation efforts.

ERP systems are representative of a much larger class of enterprise systems which also include technologies such as customer relationship management systems, supply chain management systems, enterprise data warehouses, and business intelligence infrastructures. In the context of healthcare, Electronic Health Records, computerized physician order entry systems, and other critical operational systems have similar characteristics. In the abstract, it is reasonable to expect that the adaptation opportunity interpretation process, scanning, and IS mindfulness would have similar consequences for these types of large scale, organizational systems. However, the focus of this study on ERP systems potentially limits the generalizability of the results. While the findings suggest that scanning, evaluation, and IS mindfulness are likely to be important parts of management strategies for facilitating IT assimilation, additional studies are needed to understand the degree and limits of these arguments. For example, it is not clear how scanning, evaluation, and collective mindfulness in the IS function would affect assimilation of less centralized technologies, such as personal productivity tools or personal mobile technologies that allow for more local experimentation and exploration.

We believe that our findings reflect a fundamental tension associated with the IS function’s role: the tension between stability and adaptation. While IT units must, and are pressured to, deal with performing day-to-day operations, it is necessary to be mindfully alert to the emergence of organizational needs and adaptation opportunities and to respond in a contextually appropriate manner to successfully assimilate enterprise-wide IT systems and realize their benefits.

Recognizing emerging IS clients’ needs and opportunities in the ERP post-implementation stage necessarily involves seeing things that are (at least in relative terms) novel since they were not included in the original planning/implementation. Another important contribution of this study is to explore how collective mindfulness, a concept derived from the study of high-reliable organizations, is applicable beyond this niche as has been proposed by several authors [12, 88, 89]. We hope that this study will spur additional research on the assimilation of complex technologies and the role of IS mindfulness in organizations.
