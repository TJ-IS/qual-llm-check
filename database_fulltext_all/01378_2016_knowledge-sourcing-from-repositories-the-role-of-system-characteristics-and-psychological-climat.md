# Knowledge Sourcing from Repositories: The Role of System Characteristics and Psychological Climate

## ABSTRACT

This paper draws on knowledge management literature to develop hypotheses relating key KR characteristics and psychological climate to KR knowledge sourcing behavior. These hypotheses are tested using survey data from 110 technical support agents from seven companies. Results reveal that searchability (finding knowledge in the KR), actionability (applying retrieved knowledge to problem solving) and support for knowledge contribution are characteristics of a KR that predict its use. Moreover, two of these relationships are moderated by the degree to which a knowledge worker perceives a climate that is conducive to KR knowledge sourcing (high autonomy, high innovation, and low work pressure).

Keywords: knowledge management, knowledge repository, searchability, actionability, psychological climate, survey

## INTRODUCTION

Today, many companies deploy knowledge repositories (KR) to support knowledge workers [1-3]. A KR is an information technology-based system designed to store codified knowledge for future reuse, including solutions to problems, best practices, and product knowledge [4, 5]. KRs hav the potential to promote deliberate knowledge sharing and reuse [6], enhance the transfer of best practices, improve decision making, and promote innovation through quicker access to new knowledge [7, 8]. However, these benefits are not guaranteed; it has been reported that a significant percentage of KR implementations fail [5, 9]. One notable driver behind this phenomenon is underutilization of the KR as a source of knowledge [10]. Naturally, an organization cannot achieve the return on its KR investment unless employees use the KR as a knowledge source, a behavior termed herein as KR knowledge sourcing [11]. Because KR knowledge sourcing is integral to knowledge management (KM) success, there is significant practical and theoretical incentive to better understand how and why knowledge workers decide to use (or not use) a KR as a knowledge source.

Research suggests that KM-related behaviors are driven by individual, organizational, and technological factors [12-14]. Accordingly, some studies have explored sociocharacteristics of knowledge seekers, job characteristics, and other organizational factors [e.g., 4, 7]. Other studies have focused on technological aspects, examining how perceived characteristics of a KR (e.g., ease of use and usefulness) influence overall satisfaction with the KR or general knowledge sharing activities [e.g., 15, 16]. However, evidence suggests that the influence of these technological factors on individual behaviors does not occur in isolation, but is instead shaped by the psychological climate (the user-perceived organizational environment) in which it is situated [17, 18]. In the KM domain, several studies have explored either antecedents of KR knowledge sourcing [e.g., 4, 19, 20] or the effects of psychological climate on general KM-related behaviors [e.g., 17, 18, 21]; however, there is a lack of research that combines these perspectives to examine how user perceptions of specific KR characteristics interact with perceptions of climate to influence the actual task of KR knowledge sourcing.

In this study, our objective is to develop greater theoretical insight into KR knowledge sourcing by examining whether and how it is influenced by the interaction of key user perceptions of KR characteristics together with the psychological climate in which it is embedded. Illuminating the nature of this interaction between perceived technology characteristics and climate carries important implications for both KM theory and practice. From a theoretical perspective, it is generally accepted that perceptions of technology characteristics (e.g., ease of use and usefulness) influence its use, and this relationship this has generally been substantiated within the KM domain [15, 16, 19]. Yet, research also argues that information technology is inextricably embedded within an organizational context that continuously shapes its use and the consequents of this use [22]. It is logical, then, to conclude that any effect of perceived IT characteristics must also be interwoven with perceptions of the climate in which it is situated [23]. Indeed, many KM studies have acknowledged the importance of climate in influencing KMrelated behaviors [4, 18, 21] and some have begun to explore interactions between climate perceptions and KR-related variables [e.g., 24]. However, this research has thus far yielded limited insight into exactly how these variable interact to influence actual knowledge sourcing behavior, leaving many unanswered questions for both theoreticians and practitioners. For example, do users’ perceptions of KR characteristics perceptions matter more in some environments than others? Are there certain climate characteristics that could used as lever or stymie the relationship between perceived KR characteristics and use? Answers to questions such as these have important implications not only for theoretical development, but for KM practitioners who are seeking to generate returns on their investments in KRs as a knowledge source.

This study offers two primary contributions to developing theory and practice around KR knowledge sourcing. First, we show that three enabling KR characteristics – searchability, actionability, and support for knowledge contribution – are important determinants of whether or not a KR is used as a knowledge source, with actionability being the strongest predictor of KR knowledge sourcing. Although other studies have explored the effects of certain perceived KR characteristics on other KM-related outcomes [15, 16, 25], the theoretical link between these perceived characteristics and reported use of the KR as a knowledge source has not yet been well established.

#

Second, we show that the relationships between these characteristics and KR knowledge sourcing change based on the climate in which they are situated. Many studies have identified climate as an important factor in KM [e.g., 26], but relatively few have studied it empirically [e.g., 17]. This paper shows that climate interacts with perceived characteristics of the KM system, thus providing more evidence that climate for a KM system must not be overlooked in ongoing theoretical development and practical implementation of these systems. Specifically, our findings suggest that autonomy, innovation, and work pressure are three KM-related climate variables that sourcing behavior. These results extend existing literature by offering a more nuanced, task-specific theoretical lens on the use of a KR as a knowledge source.

In an effort to develop theory surrounding knowledge sourcing behavior, empirical research has begun to explore factors that influence knowledge sourcing in general and knowledge sourcing from a KR in particular (see Table 1 for a summary). Much of this work draws from social-psychological theories that invoke various individual or contextual elements to account for knowledge contribution and retrieval. Such elements include intellectual demands of the job, individual learning orientation, work pressure, risk aversion, attitude toward knowledge seeking, social relationships, facilitating conditions, and knowledge reuse habits [4, 7, 27].

While the social-psychological perspective clearly contributes to our understanding of KR knowledge sourcing, it does not account for a set of factors that is likely critical to influencing KR use: perceived characteristics of the KR system itself. Research on information systems acceptance has consistently shown that individuals' choices regarding system use are based on their perceptions of the costs and consequences of using the system, which derive from judgments of system characteristics [32]. Moreover, the relationship between perceived technology characteristics and technology use often depends on contextual factors [23], which are commonly identified as a part of the climate in which the system and its users are embedded. Climate consists of a set of measurable properties specific to an organization and perceived by individuals who work in the environment [33, 34], and has been found to be a key factor in shaping information technology-related behaviors and outcomes [18, 21, 35, 36]. Climate can be conceptualized as organizational (a quasi-objective, collective summary assessment of the characteristics of the logical (an individual’s subjective assessment and interpretation of the work enviro ent) [37]. Because we explore individual-level behaviors in this study, we focus on psychological climate (hereafter referred to as climate) [37-39].

The KM literature features a handful of studies that have begun to explore how various KR characteristics (such as output quality and searchability), combined with climaterelated factors, influence individuals’ perceived usefulness of KR, individuals’ satisfaction with KR, and overall KM success [e.g., 5, 15, 25, 40]. However, with few exceptions [5, 29], this emergent work has not empirically examined the direct effect of perceived KR characteristics on actual KR knowledge sourcing behavior, or how this effect is moderated by perceptions of organizational climate among knowledge seekers. Moreover, studies that have attempted to link perceived KR characteristics with KR knowledge sourcing have generally focused on broad, generic characteristics of the system, most commonly perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use [e.g., 19, 27]. While these two factors offer a parsimonious account of general IT usage intention, the question remains as to “what kind of utilities ‘perceived usefulness’ represents to the users in different system contexts” [27, p. 827]. Literature on task-technology suggests that use of a technology for a specific task depends on the degree to which capabilities of the technology match the specific demands of the task [41], particularly in knowledge-driven applications [28, 42]. Thus, researchers [26, 43, 44] have called for a more granular exploration of how and why specialized systems (e.g., KRs) are used to support specific tasks (e.g., knowledge sourcing).

The present study expands theory of KR knowledge sourcing by exploring how individuals’ reported use of the KR as a knowledge source is influenced by perceptions of sourcing-related KR characteristics and climate in which this system is implemented.<sup>1</sup> We develop and test a research model that integrates three themes of prior research. First, based on technology adoption and success literature [45-50], we postulate that the degree of KR use as a knowledge source will be affected by user perceptions of certain KR characteristics that are germane to the knowledge sourcing task. Second, because our focus is on the task of knowledge sourcing, we draw from research on KM success [e.g., 15, 25, 49, 50] and knowledge system design [51] to identify KR-distinctive characteristics that directly enable the specific task of KR knowledge sourcing. Finally, we integrate research in both general technology adoption [e.g., 46, 52, 53] and knowledge management [e.g., 14, 18, 24] to propose that contextual (psychological climate) factors moderate the influence of KR perceptions on its ultimate use as a knowledge source.

## HYPOTHESES AND RESEARCH MODEL

## KR Characteristics

Over the years, a number of studies [e.g., 15, 25, 49, 50] have proposed theoretical models of factors that influence the success of general KM initiatives, which often include the implementation of a KR. We draw from this literature, as well as literature on the design of KR systems [5, 51], to identify the characteristics of a KR most likely to influence its use as a knowledge source. Most models of KM success have been derived from more general information systems success frameworks [e.g., 47, 48, 54], which argue that the ultimate success of any information system depends ultimately on the perceived quality of both the system itself and the information which it contains. Applying this general concept to a KR system, theoretical models of KM success point to two important KR characteristics that are likely to influence its overall success as part of a KM initiative [15, 25, 49, 50]. First, the KR, should exhibit searchability, defined as the degree to which the KR helps the user to find knowledge relevant to the problem at hand [25]. Because knowledge cannot be reused unless it is first located, searchability is a critical feature that enables a KR to be viable as a knowledge source [20, 55]. Second, the KR should support actionability<sup>2</sup>, defined as the extent to which the KR helps the user to apply the solution to solve the problem after retrieving it from the KR [5, 51]. Because application of knowledge is the ultimate objective of any KM initiative [26], a KR should facilitate not only locating the knowledge, but also applying it in a productive manner [20].

To illustrate the constructs of searchability and actionability, consider the hypothetical example of a technical support analyst (a common knowledge-work occupation) who has access to a KR and must resolve a customer’s problem with a software application. The KR provides support for searchability to the extent that it allows the anal o easily locate potential solutions to the customer’s problem. This may involve such aspects as speed of knowledge retrieval via indexing as well as robustness and accuracy of keyword search capabilities [2]. However, identifying potential solutions is only the first part of the knowledge sourcing task; the retrieved knowledge must also be applied. The KR provides support for actionability to the extent that it furnishes the necessary guidance to allow the analyst to apply the solution (i.e., assist the analyst to actually KR that provides the analyst with accurate, detailed instructions for solving the customer’s problem should be perceived as more actionable than one that offers only superficial, vague, or otherwise impracticable output [2, 3].

While scholars have identified KR searchability and actionability as important precursors to overall KM success, there is a paucity of research that empirically connects these perceived system characteristics to actual use of a KR as a knowledge source. Nevertheless, we believe that theoretical justification exists for such a connection. First, the findings of previous KM-success research have shown that perceived KR searchability [15, 25] and actionability [5] positively relate to users’ satisfaction with and perceived usefulness of a KR, both of which are likely to correlate with increased use of the KR as a knowledge source. This accords with the hypotheses of Kanhanhalli et al.

[5], who theorized that ease of use (focused mainly on finding knowledge in the KR) and perceived output quality would lead to greater KR use for knowledge seeking (only the latter hypothesis was supported). Second, research in the area of knowledge system design suggests that knowledge reuse from a KR is most likely to occur when the KR facilitates both online action (finding the necessary knowledge while “online” with the system) as well as offline action (applying the knowledge t solving a problem when “offline”) [51]. Based on reasoning rooted in these literatures, we theorize that a knowledge worker who perceives that KR helps her find relevant solutions (searchability) and apply them to solving problems (actionability) should be more inclined to use the KR as a knowledge source than one who perceives deficiencies in the KR in supporting one or both of these functions. Stated formally, we hypothesize the following:

H1: Perceived KR searchability is positively related to KR knowledge sourcing H2: Perceived KR actionability is positively related to KR knowledge sourcing

A third important characteristic of a KR is its support for knowledge contribution, which is defined as the extent to which it enables individuals to contribute knowledge to others [3]. KM research has recognized that “[KR] success depends on both knowledge contribution and reuse, [and] the same individual can be a contributor or a seeker at different points in time” [25, p. 536]. While our primary focus in this paper is on using the KR as a knowledge source, at least two lines of reasoning from the KM literature suggest that KR knowledge sourcing may be influenced by the degree to which the KR supports knowledge contribution. First, research suggests that the use of a KR as a knowledge source depends on the degree to which the knowledge it contains is perceived as relevant and up-to-date [56, 57], as a repository believed to contain inaccurate or obsolete knowledge is of little use to knowledge workers and will quickly be abandoned [58]. A KR that is perceived to facilitate knowledge contribution should be more likely regarded as a source of fresh knowledge, thereby increasing its perceived value to knowledge seekers and its use as a knowledge source [24]. Second, reciprocity theory suggests that knowledge workers are induced to contribute their knowledge to the KR based on the expectation that their action will be reciprocated; in other words, that their contribution will “lead to their future request of knowledge being met” [59, p. 122]. Under the tenet of reciprocity, a knowledge worker who contributes knowledge to a KR should be more likely to turn to the KR as a knowledge source as a form of reciprocation for the contribution made [20]. However, reciprocally-induced KR knowledge sourcing can only occur to the extent that the KR enables knowledge contribution [51, 58]. We therefore hypothesize that KR knowledge sourcing should depend in part on whether the KR is perceived to support knowledge contribution.

H3: Perceived KR support for knowledge contribution is positively related to KR knowledge sourcing

## Climate Characteristics

Climate is a broad construct with several sub-dimensions. Because a single study cannot accommodate all possible facets of climate, researchers have advocated focusing on specific dimensions expected to be most salient in the context of interest [60]. As shown in Table 2, prior knowledge management research has established a link between certain climate dimensions and knowledge contribution/sourcing behaviors. Drawing from this literature, we focus on three prominent climate dimensions examined in prior research that have been shown to contribute to KM-related outcomes: autonomy [18, 24], innovation [17], and work pressure [4]. The first dimension, autonomy, refers to self-determination of work procedures, goals, and priorities [38, 61], and is closely related to the concepts of self-management [62, 63] and control [64]. Support for autonomy in the workplace has been identified as a critical climate factor for knowledge workers [65, 66], and has been shown to contribute positively to organizational learning and knowledge sharing [36, 67, 68]. The second climate dimension, innovation, concerns the degree to which employees perceive that innovative and creative behaviors are supported and encouraged in the workplace. A climate for innovation has been shown to influence various KM-related behaviors including knowledge contribution [17] and knowledge reuse [24]. The third climate dimension is work pressure, defined as “the perception of time demands with respect to task completion and performance standards” [38, p. 273]<sup>3</sup>. The rationale underlying each of these climate dimensions and their anticipated effects is now discussed.

## Autonomy

In a knowledge work context, autonomy empowers the individual to pursue what she believes to be the best course of action for solving a problem, which may or may not include accessing the KR as a knowledge source. A person who is not required to utilize characteristics render it helpful in solving problems – hence, searchability, actionability, and support for knowledge contribution should become much more salient for a knowledge worker with high autonomy in determining whether she will engage in KR knowledge sourcing. This accords with literature that has theorized a stronger link rather than mandatory [23, 53, 71]. In contrast, a non-autonomous worker who is constrained to access the KR as a matter of protocol – a requirement common to many knowledge work environments [72] – may find the KR burdensome or unhelpful but still utilize it to comply with organizational mandate, thus attenuating the influence of perceived KR characteristics on its use as a knowledge source. Based on these arguments, we anticipate that the relationships posited in H1 – H3 will be stronger when knowledge worker autonomy is high and weaker when knowledge worker autonomy is low.

## Innovation

We theorize that a climate for innovation will strengthen the relationship between

#

perceived positive characteristics of a KR and its use as a knowledge source. The reasoning for this hypothesis is that a KR not only supports direct reuse of knowledge, but also enables “the creation of new, deeper knowledge that enhances the quality of the services/products offered by the firm” [73, p. 1443] by providing “exposure to greater amounts of on-line organizational information, both horizontally and vertically, than may previously have been the case” [26, p. 117] In other words, existing knowledge made accessible through a KR may serve as a catalyst for generating new knowledge [74]. Thus, in a climate that rewards innovation, a KR that facilitates finding, applying, and contributing knowledge should be perceived as a more useful knowledge source for sparking innovative solutions. We therefore anticipate the link between KR knowledge sourcing and perceived searchability, actionability, and support for knowledge contribution to be stronger in a climate where innovation is encouraged and weaker in a climate that discourages innovation.

## Work pressure

Research on knowledge sourcing behavior suggests that work pressure increases the cognitive demand placed on knowledge workers, which may in turn alter their knowledge sourcing behaviors [4]. Insights from cognitive psychology literature support this thesis, showing that information processing behaviors change under work pressure [75]. These changes usually take the form of load-reducing strategies that allow the individual to arrive at a (sometimes sub-optimal) solution within a specified time frame. For example, because work press may prevent a thorough and in-depth examination of all alternatives, individuals may accelerate their information processing or engage in forms of heuristic processing in which they consider only a subset of the relevant information (filtering) [75-77]. As noted by Kocher and Sutter [78, p. 378] work pressure “can result in the so-called ‘closing of the mind’ [79], meaning that people seek cognitive closure

#

and stop considering aspects of multiple alternatives.” In extreme cases, individuals may go beyond acceleration or filtration to change their information processing strategy entirely, which may include ignoring or bypassing important sources of information that would otherwise be consulted [75].

Unlike autonomy and innovation, we anticipate that increased work pressure will attenuate the positive relationships hypothesized in H1-H3. A KR facilitates information processing and application by enabling the knowledge seeker to identify all possible solution alternatives, analyze and choose between them, apply the chosen solution, and share what was learned [2]. However, work pressure induces heuristic-based processing modes that short-circuit this process, thereby making degree to which the KR supports each step less relevant [4]. In other words, individuals who have ample time to search for, evaluate, apply, and share solutions should be more likely to weigh these characteristics heavily in their decision to use the KR. Conversely, those who are simply focused on turning out a quick solution due to work pressure should be more likely to choose the quickest pathway to a solution, regardless of the presence or absence of perceived KR characteristics. It is important to note that we are not arguing that work pressure negatively influences KR use itself. Rather, we are hypothesizing that work pressure negatively moderates the already existing relationships between perceived KR characteristics and KR use. Stated differently, the perceived KR characteristics should have less influence in determining whether or not the KR is used as a knowledge source when work pressure is high (this leaves open the possibility that the KR may actually be used more under high work pressure even when it is perceived to have deficiencies in searchability, actionability, or support for knowledge contribution). Based on these arguments, we hypothesize that the relationship between perceived KR characteristics and knowledge sourcing behavior will be stronger in a climate of low work pressure, and weaker in a climate of high work pressure.

Stated succinctly, we anticipate that a climate for KR knowledge sourcing characterized by high autonomy, high innovation, and low work pressure will strengthen the relationships posited in H1-H3.

H4: A climate for KR knowledge sourcing (high autonomy, high innovation, and low work pressure) strengthens the relationship between perceived (a) searchability, (b) actionability, and (c) support for knowledge contribution and KR knowledge sourcing.

Figure 1 depicts the hypotheses in graphical form. In summary, we theorize that KR knowledge sourcing will be positively influenced by the degree to which the KR is perceived to support searchability, actionability, and knowledge contribution. Moreover, these relationships are expected to be stronger in a perceived climate of high autonomy, high innovation, and low work pressure, collectively defined as a climate for KR knowledge sourcing (C-KRS).

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY AND ANALYSIS

## Data Collection

The population of interest in this study included knowledge workers who use (or potentially use) a KR to support their work. One of the fastest-growing such occupations over the 2002-12 period is that of a help-desk analyst [80], also referred to as computer support specialist, service engineer, or technical support specialist. Help-desk analysts (hereafter referred to simply as analysts) provide “advice to users on changes to the product and/or how it is used for maximum benefit” [69, p. 416]. An analyst is responsible for resolving customer problems with large, complex, technical products (such as computer hardware or software) in a timely manner [70]. Analysts draw upon both “breadth knowledge” to support a wide variety of products and “depth knowledge” to solve specific customers’ problems [72]. From a KM perspective, analysts are best described as “shared work practitioners” who produce knowledge that they and their colleagues can reuse [2]. Such knowledge has historically been cataloged in printed documents or manuals to which analysts can refer. However, the rapidly evolving nature of hardware and software products renders formal documentation less useful as products and processes change. As articulated by one knowledge work manager:<sup>4</sup>

The best solution evolves – it’s not stable. You might think that you know the answer but in the meantime somebody could have come up with a better or faster way to solve the problem and you don’t know it. That’s why [employees] have to go [to the KR] every time they troubleshoot. But it’s difficult for them to understand this.

Moreover, searching such documentation can be prohibitively time consuming and often yields little useful support for the way that analysts actually carry out their work [81, 82]. Consequently, most technical support firms now rely on electronic KR software to facilitate the creation, storage, and retrieval of valuable technical support knowledge [83, 84]. Because analysts rely on such knowledge and have ready access to a KR in their daily work, they constitute an ideal population for examining KR knowledge sourcing behavior [72].

Our first step was to identify organizations that used a KR in a technical support context. Potential sites were identified by asking a KR vendor to provide names of organizations using its product to support analysts’ work. To ensure a critical mass of knowledge in the system, we only considered organizations that had used a KR for at least one year. The vendor identified 26 such companies that represented both ‘high’ and ‘low’ adopters of the KR. These companies received a letter from the vendor and from the researchers inviting them to participate in the study. Seven companies agreed to participate, of which two companies were in manufacturing, two in health care, and one in media/publishing, data communications and software development, respectively. All analysts were internal employees of their companies and no help-desk services were outsourced.

The primary means of data collection was a Web-based survey that was distributed to analysts at each company via email. Because each company used a different name for its KR and its various functions, the first step was to customize the language of each survey to ensure that the respondents would understand the questions. This was accomplished using input from 30-45 minute long interviews with total of two employees who worked as technical support managers or other personnel who interacted with the KR at each company. After the surveys had been finalized, the URL of the survey was emailed by the researchers to 150 analysts across the seven participating companies. Of these, 110 analysts completed the survey, for an response rate of 73%. Tabulation of respondent characteristics revealed that 68% of respondents worked as level I analysts (first contact with a customer) and 32% as level II (more experienced) analysts. Thirty-five percent were females and 65% males. The age of participants ranged from 26 to 64, with an average age of 42. Analysts had worked for their companies for an average of nine years (1 – 37 years), and had been at their positions for an average of five years (1 – 24 years). Hence, the sample comprised a wide range of analyst positions and organizational tenure.

To increase the response rate and reduce non-response bias, the following approach was utilized in soliciting participation. First, analysts received an email from their manager introducing the study. Within two days of this email, analysts received an emai from the researchers with the URL that invited them to fill out the online survey. Two reminders were emailed within the next month. In order to test for potential nonresponse bias, we split our data into three groups equivalent to the three emails (initial email from the researchers and two reminders). ANOVA was then used to perform a wave analysis in order to check for non-response bias covering all variables in the model. No significant differences were found (α <0.05). Therefore, given the high response rate (73%) and the not-significant results of the wave analysis, non-response bias was not deemed to be a significant threat to our analysis.

Given that the data were collected in seven different organizations, we tested whether individual-level analysis was appropriate. First, intraclass correlation for the climate was 0.19 (p=0.31) was non-significant and well below the 0.7 value recommended as a minimum for group-level analysis [85]. Next, single factor ANOVA confirmed that the difference in the dependent variable among the seven organizations was not significant (F=1.035, p = 0.407). Finally, SPSS mixed analysis was used to calculate fixed (Level 1) and random (Level 2) model effects for the climate variable (C-KRS) [85, 86]. The covariance parameter for the climate variable was not estimable in the model. Together, these results supported individual level analysis [87].

## Measures

Measures validated by other researchers were combined with measures created specifically for this study. In addition to the constructs described earlier, analysts’ gender, age and experience level (level 1, first contact vs. level 2, escalation level) were measured as control variables. All items except for gender, age, and level were measured on 7-point Likert scales anchored on 1 (Strongly Disagree) and 7 (Strongly Agree). The scale for KR knowledge sourcing was adapted from Gray and Durcikova [4]. Climate scales for autonomy, innovation, and work pressure were adapted from Koys and DeCotiis [38]. Following psychological climate literature [39, 88] and prior KM studies [e.g., 17], these three organizational climate dimensions were then used as indicators to create a summated psychological climate index (Autonomy + Innovation + Work pressure; work pressure was reverse coded to make it consistent in direction with the other climate dimensions). Finally scales for searchability, actionability, and support for knowledge contribution were developed for this study following Churchill’s [89] methodology. After creating a list of initial items based on the definitions in literature, a sorting exercise was used to determine face and discriminant validity. Ten doctoral students and knowledge workers from the participating organizations independently sorted the items into three groups. Any items that were not included on their intended construct at least 70% of the time (10% above the recommended threshold) were removed [90, 91]. The final set of items is shown in Table 3.

A climate for KR knowledge sourcing (high innovation, high autonomy, low work pressure) was conceptualized as a formative construct in our study. Following Jarvis et al (2003) and Peter et al (2007), we used four primary evaluation criteria for determining the formative nature of this construct. First, do the items define the construct or are they manifestations of the construct? In this case, each of the climate dimensions (innovation, autonomy, work pressure) makes a unique contribution toward determining the nature of the overall climate construct; therefore, the first criterion for formative constructs was met. Second, we examined the interchangeability of the measures, as measures for formative constructs should not be interchangeable. Removing any of the climate measures would yield a very different representation of climate. Omitting innovation, for example, would fundamentally alter our treatment and interpretation of climate for KR knowledge sourcing; thus, the second criterion for formative constructs was met. Third, we considered whether the measures should covary with one another. According to

Petter et al (2007), measures for formative constructs should not necessarily have strong correlations. While we expected some correlations, especially between innovation and autonomy, these correlations are not strong (<0.5), thus supporting the third criterion. Fourth, we considered whether the measures of the construct have the same antecedents and consequences. The climate dimensions conceptualized herein could have some antecedents/consequences in common but not all. For example, the reasons for perceived high innovation (such as the nature of interpersonal relation or the nature of hierarchy within a company) are likely to be different than those leading to perceived high work pressure [e.g., 92]. In sum, all four criteria for conceptualizing climate as a formative construct were satisfied.

Using a single data source to measure both dependent and independent variables can introduce common-method bias (CMB). Procedural remedies recommended by Podsakoff et al. [93] were implemented to control for this bias (e.g., proximal separation of independent and dependent variables, pretest and customization to reduce ambiguity, complete anonymity offered to subjects). In addition Harman’s single-factor test [94] and the marker variable test [95] were used to test for CMB. Harman’s single-factor test revealed five factors explaining 68% of the variance, with no single factor featuring significant (p<0.1) loading for all items (the first unrotated factor explained 25.5%). The marker-variable technique showed that after adjustment for the second smallest positive correlation, all significant correlations remained significant. Thus, CMB was not deemed a problem for our analysis.

## Data Analysis

We began our analysis by assessing reliability and validity for each construct in the model using SPSS 20.0. Reliability was assessed via Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability, with all constructs exceeding Nunnally’s [96] 0.7 guideline. Following Gefen and Straub [97], convergent and discriminant validity were assessed via two criteria: (1) average variance extracted (AVE), and (2) correlation of items with their latent variables. The AVE values (see Table 4) ranged between 0.58 and 0.78, which exceeds the minimum convergent validity threshold of 0.5 [98]. In addition, the square root of AVE for each construct (see Table 3) exceeded all respective inter-construct correlations, providing evidence of discriminant validity. Finally, as reco Gefen and Straub [97] all measurement items showed appropriate pattern of loadings, with correlations at least one order of magnitude higher with their latent construct than with any another construct (see Table 5).

Following Petter et al (2007), we validated climate for KR knowledge sourcing as a formative construct. First, we assessed construct validity by examining the factor weights produced by SmartPLS. The factor weights are as follows: 0.553 for Innovation (p < 0.05), 0.226 for Autonomy (n.s.), and 0.526 for Work pressure (p < 0.05). Research guidelines suggest that non-significant measures of a formative construct may be considered for removal, but only if such removal does not threaten the content validity of the construct [99]. Although the factor weight for autonomy was not statistically significant in our analysis, it was retained in the construct since it has been identified as an important climate dimension in this context and in prior KM studies [18, 24] and because removing it would undermine the content validity of the construct. Reliability for our climate construct was assessed via variance inflation factor (VIF) of the measurement items. The highest VIF was 2.28, which is well below the recommended threshold of 3.3 [100]. In sum, all constructs (reflective and formative) met the necessary reliability and validity criteria.

## DISCUSSION

This study seeks to understand how key enabling characteristics of a KR interact with characteristics of climate to influence its use as a knowledge source. Drawing on knowledge management research, we hypothesized that analysts would engage in higher levels of knowledge sourcing from a KR they perceived to support searchability, actionability, and knowledge contribution. In addition, we expected these characteristics would be especially salient in shaping KR knowledge sourcing behavior in an environment characterized by high autonomy, high innovation, and low work pressure.

The results of our study point to several interesting implications for knowledge management research. First, our results generally confirm that perceived characteristics of a KR do influence the degree to which knowledge workers utilize it as a knowledge source. Thus, while studies that focus on individual characteristics of the knowledge seeker [e.g., 7, 31] or contextual elements of the organization [e.g., 18] can yield insight into KR knowledge sourcing, a complete picture of this phenomenon must account for the characteristics of the system that enable or impede its use as a knowledge source. This confirms the centrality of the “IT artifact” [102] in the constellation of factors that surround organizational use of specialized systems such as

#

KRs, and highlights the need for additional inquiry into the facets of these systems that affect specific types of downstream use behavior.

Additional insights can be gleaned from the specific pattern of results we observed in our analysis. Prior work [5, 15, 25] has argued that supporting actionability (i.e., applying knowledge to solving a problem) is an important characteristic of a KR that is likely to influence its use as a knowledge source, and our results confirm this hypothesis. However, research has been somewhat equivocal on how much KR knowledge sourcing behavior is influenced by the degree to which it helps users find solutions to problems (i.e., searchability), with some studies emphasizing the importance of this feature [103] and others failing to substantiate a relationship between this feature and KR knowledge sourcing behavior [5]. Our results suggest that searchability is indeed a statistically significant predictor of KR knowledge sourcing; however, comparison of the main-effects only (step 2) standardized regression coefficients in Table 6 does indicate that the effect of searchability was smaller relative to that of actionability and support for knowledge contribution. Although not conclusive, this may suggest that knowledge seekers who have access to a KR are driven more by its capability to help them share their knowledge with others and apply it to solving problems than by its ability to help find knowledge per se. Stated differently, knowledge workers may be able to more easily overcome deficiencies in a KR’s searchability (e.g., by seeking tips from colleagues, turning to other sources, or even developing shortcuts/heuristics that mitigate poor KR search capabilities) than they can overcome deficiencies in supporting the exchange and application of such knowledge. Regardless, our results imply that while searchability cannot be ignored as a determinant of KR knowledge sourcing, future theory building might be justified in exploring actionability and support for knowledge contribution as more prominent determinants of KR knowledge sourcing.

Additional implications can be drawn from the pattern of results observed for the moderating role of psychological climate. As expected, a C-KRS climate characterized by high autonomy, high innovation, and low work pressure strengthened the degree to which perceived actionability led to KR knowledge sourcing (H4b). However, contrary to expectation, C-KRS weakened the relationship between searchability and KR knowledge sourcing (H4a) and had no significant impact on the relationship between support for knowledge tion and KR knowledge sourcing (H4c). What could account for this pattern? One explanation for the res observed for H4a is that when analysts believe they are in a climate of high autonomy, high innovation, and low work pressure, they feel free (and perhaps even encouraged) to pursue multiple sources of information to find information about solutions to problems [4]. Thus, such an analyst is less dependent on the KR for the retrieval phase of the knowledge sourcing process. In contrast, an analyst working in an environment characterized by high work pressure, low innovation, and little autonomy would likely feel more pr ure to quickly locate an existing solution to a problem [72]. In this case, searchability (i.e., knowledge retrieval) becomes much more salient in determining whether the analyst uses the KR as a knowledge source. Thus, for prior research that has identified KR support for knowledge retrieval as an important feature [103, 104], our results imply that the presence of this feature alone may not always enhance knowledge sourcing behaviors. Rather, its effect likely depends on perceptions of climate held by the knowledge worker.

Similar implications for research can be drawn with respect to H4b. Results for this hypothesis showed that a climate of high autonomy, high innovation, and low work pressure enhanced the importance of actionability (i.e., knowledge application) in determining KR knowledge sourcing. In other words, KR use by analysts who have the time and license to explore multiple solutions seems to depend mostly on the extent to which the KR helps them apply such solutions.

Indeed, one of the primary purposes of a KR is to provide detailed guidance on how to apply the solution in a specific case that may have been encountered before [2]. While other sources may be able to provide initial guidance as to the general solution, they may not provide the level of detail needed for successful knowledge application. To illustrate, a person seeking to troubleshoot a car problem may find information on the Web that suggests a solution to the problem but lacks the details necessary to implement it. Such detail may only be found in a more specialized source such as a repair manual or online auto repair forum. logously, although the analyst working in a C-KRS might be at liberty to seek information from many sources, the KR may provide a more customized level of problem-solving guidance than she is likely to find elsewhere. Thus, such an analyst’s use of the KR as a knowledge source is likely to depend more on its support for offline knowledge application than on its support for online knowledge retrieval. For research, this result implies that greater attention should be paid to the perceived KR actionability when KR knowledge sourcing is studied in environments of low work

Regarding support for knowledge contribution, our results clearly demonstrate that a KR that supports this capability is more likely to be used as a knowledge source (H3). However, we failed to find evidence that this effect is influenced by the contextual climate variables of autonomy, innovation, and work pressure (H4c). Although non-significant outcomes must be interpreted with caution, this might suggest that support for knowledge contribution operates differently than the more directly task-enabling characteristics of searchability and actionability. For example, as discussed earlier, a person who contributes knowledge to a KR should be more likely to use the KR as a knowledge source as a form of reciprocation for her contribution [20, 59]. It may be that such reciprocally-induced KR use is influenced more by other contextual elements [e.g., collaborative norms; 19] than by those that we have examined in this study.

Alternatively, it is possible that such KR use is immune to variations in climate perceptions because the motivation behind such use is based on an implicit social exchange contract (i.e., I use it because I have contributed to it) as opposed to direct task utility (i.e., I use it because it helps me solve a problem) [20]. This point resonates with the observation made by other researchers that there may be “other aspects that are at least as important to [KR] users as utility in finishing their tasks” [27, p. 827]. Further elucidating such aspects is an important avenue for future research.

Overall, an important implication of the results for H4 is that perceptions of system characteristics do not exert uniform effects on use behaviors, but rather that these effects can change in magnitude and even direction depending on the characteristic in question and the psychological climate in which it is situated. For knowledge management research, this means that the effects of KR characteristics on its use as a knowledge source should not be assumed static, but should be anticipated to shift as perceptions of autonomy, innovation, and work pressure change.

For practitioners, the results of this study emphasize that due attention must be paid to characteristics of the KR when seeking to manage or alter KR knowledge sourcing behavior within an organization. The need for the KR to support searchability and actionability is intuitive; however, less intuitive is the need to ensure that the KR supports knowledge contribution as an cedent to KR kno edg rcing. Our results suggest that managers seeking to promote KR create the technical infrastructure necessary to enable KRbased contribution of knowledge, as well as support for finding and applying solutions to problems. In addition, practitioners must consider how perceptions of climate may alter the focal characteristics on which efforts to influence knowledge sourcing behaviors should be based. Our results suggest that in a climate characterized by high autonomy, high innovation, and low work pressure, greater emphasis should be placed on managing perceptions of KR actionability to increase KR knowledge sourcing. On the other hand, in a climate of low autonomy, low innovation, and high work pressure, efforts to enhance KR searchability may be more fruitful.

As with any research, this study is subject to limitations. The use of a cross-sectional survey, while common in IS research, limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions about causal relationships among constructs. Although organizational constraints prevented us from collecting longitudinal data for this study, such data collected in future studies can both confirm causal relationships and yield additional insight into how these relationships evolve over time. Another potential limitation is the use of subjectively reported measures of all constructs, particularly KR knowledge sourcing. Only subjective measures were available from organizations participating in this study; however, incorporation of objective use measures in future work may serve to strengthen or challenge conclusions drawn using subjective use measures. Third, our operationalization of climate focused on three dimensions that are most likely to influence knowledge sourcing behavior, but other dimensions previously studied in KM research – for example openness and fairness [17, 59] – may also play a role and should be explored in future work. Finally, our study focused on the context of technical support - a domain in which KR systems have become widespread. While we believe that our results are generalizable to other contexts, future research should confirm this expectation by exploring how perceived KR characteristics affect KR knowledge sourcing in other types of knowledgeintensive environments. In addition, future research should explore how other contextual factors such as organizational memory, social networks, and specific KR search mechanisms enhance or detract from KR knowledge sourcing.

## CONCLUSION

As today’s organizations seek better ways of managing and reusing their knowledge, the deployment of KR systems will continue to proliferate. However, investment in such systems will be fruitful only to the extent that they are utilized by knowledge workers as a means of acquiring and leveraging knowledge from across the organization. This study develops and tests a model of KR knowledge sourcing based on technology adoption [45-48] and knowledge system design literatures [51], and extends these two literatures by theorizing the moderating effect of climate for KR knowledge sourcing (cl te characterized by high autonomy, high innovation, and low work pressure) on system characteristics. The ilts this study reveal that KR knowledge sourcing is influenced by all three KR characteristics, namely, searchability, actionability, and support for knowledge contribution. Moreover, climate for KR knowledge sourcing negatively moderates the relationship bet searchability and KR knowledge sourcing and positively moderates the relationship between actionability and KR knowledge sourcing.
