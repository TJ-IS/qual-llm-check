## 1. Introduction

Increasingly, information technologies (IT) have become an indispensable means to implement major public policy initiatives in the U.S. federal government. The Federal Health Insurance Marketplace (Healthcare.gov) is a case in point. It is one of the core components of the Affordable Care Act (ACA), the most profound policy reform of the Obama Administration, and enables the so-called “individual mandate” clause, which is the centerpiece of the ACA. Just as IT resources provide necessary organizational capabilities in the business sector (Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Wade and Hulland 2004, Banker et al. 2006, Rai et al. 2006, Rai and Tang 2010), so do government organizations rely on digital technologies for effective policy execution and public service delivery. Indeed, the U.S. federal government spends a considerable amount of tax revenues in IT every year. The Federal IT Dashboard (http://itdashboard.gov/) reports that in the past few years, the federal government has spent more than \$75 billion in IT annually. Hence, taxpayers would ask whether these investments are being well made.

From 2003 to 2016, on average, U.S. federal agencies have spent as much as 23.9% of their IT spending in new system development and the rest in ongoing maintenance of existing systems. However, there is wide variation across the agencies. For instance, the Department of Transportation (DOT) has spent more than 50% of its IT expenditures in new IT development, while the Department of State and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) spent less than 15%. This begs a question: What explains this variation in the IT investment profiles of the federal agencies? To our knowledge, information systems (IS) researchers to date have paid little attention to what influences IT investments and management in the government sector. This issue would be significant to policymakers and the public in general as well as to private-sector IT industries.

One reason that some agencies spend much less in new IT development than others is that these agencies are operating numerous legacy information systems (Konkel 2012). These systems are not only technologically obsolete and expensive to maintain but hamper ongoing business operations and effective fulfillment of mandated policy missions. Many federal government legacy systems are still operating on a 50-year-old COBOL, and federal agencies are struggling with recruiting new COBOL developers to replace a number of retiring federal IT workers who have been maintaining these systems (Robinson 2009, Konkel 2012). These outdated systems constrain the federal agencies’ ability to respond to emerging needs for public services in a flexible and agile manner (Louri 2013).

We theorize that the national politics significantly affects IT investment profiles in the federal government. Drawing on the political science theories such as separation of powers or political control of bureaucracy (Weingast 1984; Moe 1984, 1987; McCubbins et al. 1987, 1989; Wood and Waterman 1991; Hammond and Knott 1996), we discuss how the U.S. Congress influences federal IT investments. We posit that to make more capacity-building IT investments, a federal agency requires more policy directives, greater political support and legitimacy, and sufficient resource endowment from Congress. Based on this theoretical ground, we hypothesize that IT investment profiles of a federal agency are associated with the following three factors that indicate the degree of policy mandates and political support from Congress, i.e., (i) legislative approval for the agency head, (ii) federal government dividedness, and (iii) the agency’s ideological characteristics.

We test our hypotheses with a large-scale data set of IT investments in the U.S. federal government from 2003 to 2016. Our empirical analyses produce several intriguing findings. For instance, when the U.S. Senate and the House of Representatives are controlled by the President’s ruling party, the share of investments in new IT development is 8.32% higher than when the opposition party holds the majority in both chambers. We also find that when the head of a federal agency is not confirmed by the Senate for more than one year, the share of new IT development is 4.97% lower. Our analysis also shows that the more ideologically extreme a federal agency is, the more it spends in IT maintenance.

This study makes a unique contribution to the IS literature by examining the political antecedents for IT investments in the U.S. federal government. Several prior IS studies were devoted to identifying the predictors for the amount of IT investments and the decision processes for those investments (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al. 2002, Xue et al. 2008, Mithas et al. 2013). The literature in public administration (PA) has also studied what antecedent drives governments’ IT adoption (e.g., Pandey and Bretschneider 1997, Moon and Bretschneider 2002, Li and Feeney 2014). Little prior work in IS or PA thus far has studied how politics affects IT investments, adoption or management in public-sector organizations. Toward this end, we draw on the political science and PA literature, with which the IS discipline has had little intellectual collaboration.

This study is also new in the IS literature in that we study the antecedents for investment allocation between new IT development and maintenance of existing systems. Little, if any, IS research has examined a balance between the two. IT managers believe that adopting new technologies is crucial for organizations’ long-term success (Dignan 2010, Moore 2015), be they in the private or public sector. At the same time, however, the maintenance expenses of legacy systems limit investments in new, state-of-the-art technologies, and IT managers are struggling with how to contain mounting maintenance spending (Austin et al. 2009, Zetlin 2013). To our knowledge, ours is the first study to directly tackle this matter.

This study also offers several policy implications. We show that political gridlocks are detrimental to organizational capabilities of federal agencies by constraining capacity-building IT investments. If the federal government is to be agile, flexible, and efficient, so should federal IT management. Thus, we propose that the U.S. Congress pay more supervisory attention and provide more political support to federal IT management. We also argue that one of the reasons for the federal agencies’ reluctance to make capacity-building IT investments is that IT governance in the federal government is heavily decentralized. We believe that the rank of the U.S. Chief Information Officer (CIO) should be elevated to that of a cabinet-level secretary and their authority and responsibilities over government-wide IT management be expanded, so that the CIO can play a spearheading role in the digital transformation of the federal government.

## 2. Theoretical Development and Hypotheses

2.1. Political Influences on Federal IT Investments To explain the political impacts on federal IT investment profiles, we build our theoretical model based on four points. First, we present anecdotal evidence that IT is a key enabler for major public policy initiatives. Second, the U.S. Congress plays an influential role in formulation and implementation of public policies. These two points suggest that federal IT management is subject to Congressional supervision. Third, the IS literature documents that large-scale IT development projects entail substantial risks of failures. Fourth, we draw on the PA literature to posit that public-sector employees are risk-averse and value job security. Therefore, bureaucratic federal officials would not voluntarily commit to risky IT development in the absence of mandates from Congress. This leads us to propose that sufficient policy directives and political support from Congress are a prerequisite for capacitybuilding IT investments in the federal government.

Most of the recent major policy programs include the development of new, large-scale information systems, which provide necessary capabilities for program implementation and operation. For instance, in 2008, the U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement initiated the Secure Communities program, which aims to deport illegal aliens who are detained for criminal activities. Among the central pieces of this immigration policy is the Automated Biometric Identification System,<sup>1</sup> which integrates databases in the federal, state, and local law enforcement agencies to share information on criminal aliens. Another major policy project that heavily relies on IT is NextGen, in which the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) is developing a new, nationwide air traffic control system. The FAA is phasing out the decades-old radar-based air traffic control system and installing GPS-based control and digital communication systems in airports and control centers throughout the country. The goal is to improve the accuracy in monitoring and directing air traffic and to enhance air travel safety.

While federal agencies are in charge of implementation and execution of public policies such as Secure Communities and NextGen, the U.S. Congress exerts considerable influence on policy inception and formulation in various ways, according to the congressional dominance theory (Weingast and Moran 1983, McCubbins et al. 1989, Epstein and O’Halloran 1994, Seidenfeld 1999, Clinton et al. 2014). First, Congress enacts legislation, which gives federal agencies the formal authority to implement policy programs. For instance, NextGen is authorized by the Century of Aviation Reauthorization Act. Section 709-(c) of this law stipulates that the goals of NextGen are to “improve the level of safety, security, efficiency, quality, and affordability” of U.S. airspace. Another mechanism in which Congress influences policy execution is budget appropriation. The Budget and Accounting Act of 1921 provides Congress with the authority to approve the President’s budget proposals. All spending in the federal agencies, including IT expenditures, is bound by laws and appropriation bills enacted by Congress.

The IS literature extensively studies risk factors in large-scale IT projects and investments (Wallace et al. 2004; Kutsch and Hall 2005; Benaroch et al. 2007; Dewan et al. 2007; Dewan and Ren 2007, 2011). For instance, Dewan et al. (2007) find that IT capital investments affect risks in firm performance to a greater extent than other non-IT capital investments. As Healthcare.gov demonstrates, large-scale capacitybuilding IT investments in the federal sector entail a range of risks such as project delays, budget overrun, and technical failures. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) lists the management of IT acquisitions and operations as one of the high risk management areas and states that “federal IT investments too frequently fail to be completed or incur cost overruns and schedule slippages while contributing little to missionrelated outcomes” (GAO 2015, p. 37).

The public service motivation theory from the PA literature argues that government employees are riskaverse (Bellante and Link 1981, Pfeifer 2011, Buurman et al. 2012) and value stable income and job security (Houston 2000, Lewis and Frank 2002). For instance, Bellante and Link (1981) find that the more risk-averse a person is, the more likely they are to work in the public sector than in the private sector. Houston (2000) shows that public sector employees value job security more than their private sector counterparts. Aryee (1992) and Sousa-Poza and Hennberger (2004) also show that public employees have a significantly lower turnover rate. In addition, the theory on political control of bureaucracy posits that the primary interest of bureaucrats is to maximize their budget (Miller and Moe 1983, Banks 1989, Banks and Weingast 1992), the size of which they oversee represents their power, prestige, and legitimacy (Weingast and Moran 1983, Epstein and O’Halloran 1994, Olson 1995). Hence, government officials are afraid of budget cuts and the elimination of policy programs in which they are in charge.

This explains why many federal agencies cling to decades-old legacy systems. Federal officials are reluctant to replace old COBOL-based systems because “those COBOL systems handle such a huge part of the transactional load that they are not easy to replace when so much vital traffic passes through daily” (Robinson 2009). They are unlikely to proactively make substantial investments in risky capacitybuilding IT development, whose failure may lead to punishment against in such forms as budget reduction, elimination of programs or positions or congressional hearings that would tarnish their power and reputation. For instance, after a revelation of the \$170-million project failure in the Virtual Case File of the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), which was intended to modernize its case databases but produced no acceptable outcome, a high-ranking FBI IT official in charge of project management had to leave the agency (Goldstein 2005). It is also reported that after the Healthcare.gov launch, several officials in the Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) were intensely investigated by Congress and some of them resigned from their positions (Desouza and Smith 2014).

The prospect of sanctions following IT failures leads federal officials to forgo making necessary IT investments for organizational capabilities. Without clear

Congressional policy mandates, federal officials would not voluntarily seek to secure legislative approval and budget appropriation for capacity-building IT investments. Instead, they prefer maintaining the status quo by keeping existing legacy systems up and running, unless Congress demands otherwise. This is in accordance with Moon and Bretschneider (2002), who find that the perceived degree of digitization in governments is positively associated with the risk propensity of high-ranking officials.

In sum, the political influence on federal IT management can be explained by the four points discussed above. IT provides federal agencies with essential organizational capabilities for effective implementation of major public policies. The U.S. Congress holds dominant power over policy execution. A challenge for the federal agencies, however, is that most large-scale IT investments carry considerable risks of failure. Given that public officials tend to be risk-averse and have a strong desire for job security and political prestige, they would not voluntarily embark on major large-scale IT investments, whose failure would jeopardize their interests, unless Congress provides sufficient political support and compelling policy directives that require new capacity-building IT investments.

## 2.2. Hypotheses

Based on the theoretical foundation presented in Section 2.1, we identify three indicators that measure the degree of policy mandates and political support from Congress, i.e., legislative approval for the agency head, government dividedness, and agency ideology characteristics. Specifically, we propose that a federal agency receives more policy directives from Congress, thus making more capacity-building IT investments: (i) when its chief executive receives legislative approval for nomination; (ii) when the federal government is more united; and (iii) when it is ideologically moderate.

We argue that the IT investment profiles of federal agencies are affected by whether agency chief executives are appointed with legislative approval from the U.S. Senate. Most senior executives such as cabinet secretaries must be confirmed by the Senate to be sworn in (Gilmour and Lewis 2006, Lewis 2011, Gallo and Lewis 2012). A Senate confirmation is also required for many assistant-level or deputy-level secretaries and top officials of subagency bureaus such as the Census Bureau or the FBI. However, nominees often undergo a time-consuming and politically-charged confirmation process (Calvert et al. 1989, Wood and Waterman 1991, Lewis 2011). The Senate often delays or refuses confirmation of certain nominees for an extended period of time (Dull and Roberts 2009). For example, a nominee for the Director of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms, and Explosives (ATF) had not been confirmed by the Senate from November 2010 until July 2013. Likewise, the Administrator of General Services (the head of the General Services Administration (GSA)) remained unconfirmed by the Senate between October 2004 and July 2005 and between June 2008 and February 2010. In such cases, the position is assumed by a person on a provisional basis with an “acting” or “interim” title.

Congress is unlikely to offer strong political support and adequate policy mandates to a federal agency whose chief executive assumes their position without its approval. The political science theories posit that legislative approval for presidential appointees is determined by policy differences between the President and Congress (Calvert et al. 1989, McCubbins et al. 1989, Hammond and Hill 1993), and approval of a nominee by the Senate is difficult without political support and policy consensus. King and Riddlesperger (1991) find that the majority (79%) of opposition to presidential nominees by Congress from 1945 to 1989 was due to policy disagreement between nominees and senators. Hence, even when the President appoints an agency head unilaterally on a permanent basis despite Senate opposition, an approach called a recess appointment (Corley 2006, Black et al. 2007), Congress is unlikely to provide political legitimacy and support to the agency. When its senior official does not have sufficient legal and political authority, the federal agency would have more difficulty performing its duties and implementing policy. Congress would be unwilling to grant major policy programs, which would be supported by capacity-building IT investments, to an agency which, in its opinion, lacks formal leadership.

In addition, as discussed above, capacity-building IT investments carry considerable risks of failure. A chief executive who assumes a position without legislative approval would be more reluctant to initiate large-scale IT projects, whose failure could damage their career. A Senate confirmation takes a considerable amount of time and agency resources (Dull and Roberts 2009, Lewis 2011). Even in the case of IT failure, the President would find it difficult to dismiss an agency official who had been confirmed by the Senate. On the other hand, a chief executive who did not earn legislative approval for nomination would be easier to dismiss after a major IT failure. Furthermore, Congress is unlikely to approve a large amount of funding for major IT projects to an agency led by an official who is unconfirmed by the Senate. Therefore, with legislative confirmation, the agency official can be more proactive in making major capacity-building IT investments.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> Capacity-building IT investments by a federal agency are positively associated with legislative approval of its chief executive.

We propose that federal agencies spend less in capacity-building IT development when federal government is more divided. Such division means that the legislative and the executive branches are controlled by different parties (Huber et al. 2001).

The theory on separation of power posits that under a divided government, with different ideological preferences and divergent policy directions, the two branches constantly restrain each other to advance the political agenda of each and to block the counterpart’s policy proposals (Moe 1984, Weingast 1984, McCubbins et al. 1987, Wood and Waterman 1991, Bawn 1995, Seidenfeld 1999). Consequently, under a divided government, major policy proposals are less likely to be codified into law. If Congress passes a bill for policy initiatives that are at odds with the President’s political agenda, the President can veto the bill, thus blocking it from being enacted. Likewise, Congress is less likely to approve legislative proposals put forth by the President; if a policy program is created by an executive order unilaterally, Congress is unlikely to fund the program. Thus, under a divided government, there will be fewer policy mandates that warrant large-scale IT investments for policy execution capabilities.

In addition, under a divided government, federal agencies are under more intense Congressional scrutiny, and sanctions against federal officials for failures in large-scale IT investments are more severe. When the opposition party controls Congress, it is more likely to use IT failures as a political advantage, and federal agencies may endure more intense scrutiny, larger budget cuts, and greater damage to their political legitimacy. Consequently, under a divided government, federal bureaucrats would become more risk-averse and are likely to refrain from making capacity-building IT investments. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> Capacity-building IT investments by a federal agency are negatively associated with federal government dividedness.

Next, we theorize that a federal agency makes more capacity-building IT investments when its ideology is not too extreme, i.e., neither too liberal nor too conservative. Studies in the political science literature measure the ideological characteristics of federal agencies (Nixon 2004, Clinton and Lewis 2008, Bertelli and Grose 2011, Clinton et al. 2012) with such information as its primary policy responsibilities, the party that created it, and expert opinions. For example, agencies such as the Department of Labor (DOL) or the EPA, both of which aim to advance social or regulatory policies, are evaluated to be liberal (Clinton and Lewis 2008). Agencies such as the Department of Homeland

Security (DHS) and the Department of Defense (DOD) are considered to be conservative.

In Congress, while the minority party has weaker political power than the majority, it still exercises considerable influence on policy formulation and budget allocation (Gailmard and Jenkins 2007). For example, any U.S. senator has the power to indefinitely suspend a legislative process at any given time, a tactic called a filibuster. The Senate rule requires that 60% or more of the senators agree to break a filibuster and move the process forward. Therefore, unless the majority party achieves a “super majority” (60%) to break a filibuster by itself, it still needs support or consent from the minority party to pass a bill. Even in the House of Representatives, in which passing a bill requires a simple majority, members of the minority party still exert some degree of power that influences legislative procedures and budget appropriation.<sup>2</sup>

Therefore, whichever party holds the majority in Congress, it is challenging to enact ideologically extreme policy agendas that would provide policy imperatives to very liberal or very conservative agencies. Major policy initiatives that would empower ideologically moderate agencies are more likely to be enacted cooperatively by both parties, while initiatives that would be implemented by liberal or conservative agencies are likely to face stronger opposition from either party. This leads ideologically extreme federal agencies to invest less in capacity-building IT projects. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> Capacity-building IT investments by a federal agency are negatively associated with extreme ideological characteristics. An agency is less likely to make more capacity-building IT investments when it is ideologically too conservative or too liberal.

## 3. Empirical Methods

This section explains our measures, data sources, and analytical approaches. The unit of analysis in our study is U.S. federal agencies and bureaus (Table 1). Agencies include cabinet and independent departments. Cabinet agencies supervise several bureaus, such as the FAA or the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA), which perform more specialized functions. Thus, our observations include the cabinet and independent agencies and the bureaus under the cabinet agencies. More information on the sample population is available in Online Appendix A (available as supplemental material at https://doi.org/10.1287/ isre.2016.0665). We built an unbalanced panel data set of 1,596 observations from 135 federal agencies and bureaus.

DME investments include “costs for projects leading to new IT assets/systems and projects that change or modify existing IT assets to: substantively improve capability or performance; implement legislative or regulatory requirements; or to meet an agency leadership request.”<sup>3</sup> The rest of the IT investments are for operations and maintenance. We test our hypotheses with the following measures. For legislative approval for a chief executive (H1), CONFIRM is a dummy variable that is equal to zero if a chief executive position does not require a Senate confirmation or its nominee had not been confirmed by the Senate for more than one year at the time of the Presidential budget proposal in February.<sup>4</sup> CONFIRM is one otherwise. We chose a one-year window as it represents a leadership vacuum for an extended period of time. We checked whether a position requires Senate confirmation from the U.S. Government Policy and Supporting Document published by Congress.<sup>5</sup> For a position that requires confirmation, we used two sources, i.e., the Presidential Nomination Records maintained by the Library of Congress and the Federal Vacancy Records from the GAO. We coded government dividedness (DIVIDED, H2) as follows: DIVIDED is 0 when the President’s ruling party holds the majority (more than 50% of the seats) in the Senate and the House of Representatives. DIVIDED is coded 1 if the opposition party holds a majority in the Senate or the House. It equals 2 if the opposition holds the majority in both chambers.

We use the agency ideology measure (IDEO) of Clinton and Lewis (2008) for H3. They devised the ideology measures of 82 federal agencies and bureaus, and collected ideology evaluations of several political experts from academia, media, and Washington, D.C., think tanks. Combining their perceived ideology of federal agencies with objective information such as the agencies’ primary functions and the party and the President that created them, Clinton and Lewis (2008) estimate the latent ideology scores using a Bayesian method. Their measures are widely used by many studies in political science and PA (e.g., Stone et al. 2010, Lavertu and Moynihan 2013, Clinton et al. 2014, Hollibaugh et al. 2014). The more positive a score, the more conservative an agency is; the more negative, the more liberal it is.<sup>6</sup> For instance, the ideology scores of the DOL (a liberal agency) and the DHS (a conservative agency) are −1043 and 0.88, respectively.

Because the dependent variable (DME\_INV) is fractional, ranging between 0 and 1, a linear regression that does not account for this range such as ordinary least squares (OLS) can be biased and inconsistent (Papke and Wooldridge 1996). In addition, we needed to control for agency-specific unobserved heterogeneity 4v 5 in our estimation. Hence, we followed the approach of Papke and Wooldridge (2008) to estimate a panel model with a fractural dependent variable. Equation (1) was estimated by a generalized estimating equation (GEE; Liang and Zeger 1986) with a logit transformation and a binominal-family function for residuals. This approach is widely used for panel estimation with a fractional dependent variable (e.g., Phelps 2010, Adegbesan and Higgins 2010, Eickelpasch and Vogel 2014). We included year dummies 4w 5 in Equation (1) to account for nationwide changes in political and economic environments. More details on the GEE estimation are available in Online Appendix A. Because this is a non-linear estimation, it could be sensitive to parametric assumptions; thus, interpreting coefficients is not straightforward. Hence, we also present the results of linear regressions (OLS and random-effects) in Section 4.

The coefficient of CONFIRM (H1) in Column 4 demonstrates that when the head of a federal agency is confirmed by the Senate, the agency is predicted to invest 4.97% more in DME than otherwise. It could be that the residuals in Equation (1) are serially correlated, as IT investments in federal agencies can be path-dependent; capacity-building IT investments in prior years could require more maintenance spending in subsequent years.<sup>9</sup> To account for this possibility, we estimate the model (Equation (1)) with a random-effects regression with autocorrelation in residuals (AR1) (Table 4, Column 5) and a feasible generalized least squares (FGLS) for panel-specific autocorrelation (Column 6). Details are available in Online Appendix B.

To investigate how the absolute amount of IT investments is affected by political factors, we regressed a logarithm of total IT expenditures, DME spending, and maintenance (O&M) spending on the explanatory and control variables. Random-effects estimations with these alternative dependent variables, presented in Online Appendix C, show that among the three explanatory variables, only the coefficient of DIVIDED in Column 2 is negative and significant; others are found to be insignificant. This finding is in accordance with our theoretical proposition that Congress has a significant impact on federal IT investments in a way that influences risk-averse officials’ behaviors in allocating IT budgets between new system development and maintenance. It implies that under unfavorable political environments, federal officials shy away from risk investments in new IT systems and allocate more budgets toward regular maintenance and upkeep.

## 5. Discussions and Conclusion

## 5.1. Key Findings

While some U.S. federal agencies are actively investing in advanced digital technologies for new public policy programs, why are others struggling with maintaining aging legacy systems that stifle flexibility, agility, and innovation? Our empirical analyses demonstrate that national politics has a significant impact on their IT investment profiles. We find that a federal agency is more likely to make capacity-building IT investments when its chief executive is appointed with legislative approval, when the federal government is more united, and when it is ideologically more moderate. In sum, our study supports the central proposition that to invest more in major capacity-building IT development, federal agencies need to secure compelling policy mandates and political legitimacy from Congress for implementation of strategic policy initiatives.

We believe that our findings are applicable to other government systems. For instance, the U.S. federal government and the state governments share similar political systems. In state governments, the governors and the state legislators are directly elected by constituents, as is the case in the federal government, and the state legislatures perform comparable functions to those of Congress. Hence, the federal and the state governments are under similar political environments, which we expect to similarly affect state IT investments. In 2014, the total spending of the U.S. federal and state governments combined was more than 30% of the U.S. gross domestic product. This demonstrates the value and significance of our findings. We expect that our results also hold true in other national governments with a presidential system including Brazil, France, and South Korea.

## 5.2. Contribution to the Literature

The U.S. federal government remains unchartered territory for IS researchers, even though its policies and public services profoundly affect the U.S. economy and the daily lives of all citizens. As in the private sector, well-functioning IT systems are indispensable for effective government operation and public service delivery. As noted in Section 2.1, a range of anecdotal evidence illustrates that IT is a key enabler of major policy implementation. At the same time, however, outdated legacy systems can disrupt ongoing business operations and limit flexibility and responsiveness in the federal sector, costing a significant sum of tax revenue without much value-added. Therefore, it is a legitimate issue for IS researchers to examine how the federal government invests in IT and what antecedent affects its IT investment profiles. The IS literature to date has not shed as much light on IT investment profiles in public-sector organizations as on the business sector, even though public-sector organizations are among the largest IT consumers with substantial impact on private-sector IT industries, one of the key audiences of IS research.

We believe that our study is one of the first to regard politics as a factor that influences IT investments. To understand the role of the U.S. Congress in federal IT investment decisions, we draw on various theories from the political science and PA literature and couple those theories with IS research. This is our unique theoretical approach that few prior IS studies have attempted. We also contribute to the IS literature by addressing the issue of a balance between IT investments for new capabilities and IT maintenance for ongoing business operations, which has received scant attention in the IS literature. Investing in new technologies while maintaining existing systems has been a continuing dilemma for IT practitioners in the public and private sectors. They express a concern that while development of new IT systems is critical for organizations’ success, IT maintenance limits their ability to secure adequate capacity-building IT investments (Dignan 2010, Konkel 2015). We believe our study fills this critical gap in IS literature.

## 5.3. Policy Proposals

We offer several implications for policymakers and practitioners in the public sector. Our findings illustrate that political gridlock limits federal agencies’ organizational capabilities, as shown by a decrease in capacity-building IT investments under a divided government (H2) or the absence of legislative approval for chief executives (H1). Public sector organizations are increasingly required to be flexible, entrepreneurial, and innovative in addressing emerging societal and economic challenges (Moore 1995, Stoker 2006, Alford and Hughes 2008). To do so, government agencies need to develop necessary organizational capabilities, for which advanced digital resources are pivotal. Our study shows that political stalemates are detrimental to federal agencies’ endeavors to achieve such goals.

This study also shows that the U.S. Congress exerts substantial influence in federal IT investments. Therefore, we maintain that effective federal IT management must be among Congressional responsibilities. Toward that end, we call for the creation of legislative committees in the Senate and the House of Representatives dedicated to supervising federal IT investments and management.<sup>10</sup> Given that IT is a critical means for effective policy implementation, it is incumbent on Congress to ensure that IT plays an enabling, not hindering, role in the federal government. We also recommend that Congress approve multi-year budget appropriations for capacity-building IT investments. Currently, although federal IT budgets must be approved every year, it is reported that this requirement hinders long-term, strategic planning for largescale IT development projects (Konkel 2015). Federal officials are reluctant to initiate multi-year IT investments when they are uncertain whether they can secure budgets in the coming years. Thus, we endorse a proposal for multi-year budget approvals for IT development and modernization, which would facilitate more capacity-building IT investments.

Federal IT management must also be streamlined. Currently, IT management and governance in the federal government is heavily decentralized. The Federal Information Technology Acquisition Reform Act of 2014 has substantially increased the authority and power of CIOs in individual federal agencies with respect to IT investment, strategic planning, and procurement; we believe this is a step in the right direction. However, it stopped short of empowering the U.S. federal CIO, who is an administrator of the Office of E-Government and IT, a suboffice of the Office of Management and Budget (OMB). Consequently, the federal CIO has a very limited legal and hierarchical authority to oversee IT investments and projects in peer federal agencies, to enforce government-wide IT policies, and to drive modernization of legacy IT systems across the federal government. For instance, the U.S. CIO was unable to get involved in the development of Heathcare.gov until its technical failures had come under intense political scrutiny in 2013. It became fully operational only after he brought in an emergency team of technical experts from the private sector who fixed the system in a few months (Brill 2014). In 2009, the Obama Administration issued an executive directive that encourages federal agencies to adopt cloud computing. However, as of 2014, the spending in cloud computing is only 1% of total federal IT investments (GAO 2014). This is due to the U.S. CIO’s lack of power to enforce the directive.

We propose expanding the legal authority and control of the U.S. CIO over federal government-wide IT management and elevating the rank of that position so that it is on par with secretaries of cabinet agencies or directors of independent agencies. Just as the OMB, the OPM, and the GSA are independent agencies with government-wide responsibilities over financial, personnel, and acquisition management, respectively, so should the Office of E-Government and IT become a standalone agency that oversees IT throughout the federal government. With expanded statutory and hierarchical power, the U.S. CIO could compel federal officials, who might otherwise be too bureaucratic and risk-averse, to increase capacity-building IT investments. The CIO should be able to exercise more control in managing high-risk, high-stake development projects such as Healthcare.gov.

We also urge the federal government to attract more IT talent from the private sector. To effectively manage risky capacity-building IT investments and mitigate IT project failures, the federal government must improve its IT management capabilities. Hence, it is imperative to hire IT officials who have strong expertise in state-of-the-art digital technologies, experience in leading large IT organizations, and access to knowledge and professional networks in private-sector IT industries. One of the obstacles, however, is a rigid salary scale in the federal government. According to the federal pay scales mandated by the OPM, annual base salaries of senior executives including agency CIOs in 2016 range between \$150,000 and \$205,000.<sup>11</sup> This figure is substantially lower than salaries of several CIOs in Fortune 500 companies (Bednarz 2015) and thus acts as a hindrance to attract private-sector IT professionals to the public sector (Wood 2011, Sternstein 2014). We advocate for more flexible pay scales and sufficient incentives for IT professionals in the public sector.

## 5.4. Limitations and Future Research Directions

This research is by no means free of limitations. First, even though our data set includes 14 years of IT investments in federal agencies (2003–2016), it covers only two administrations (Bush and Obama). Thus, a data set with a longer time frame would have allowed us to draw a more comprehensive picture of political influences on IT investments. Second, to test H3, we use the agency ideology measures of Clinton and Lewis (2008), who did not measure the ideology of some subagency bureaus. As explained, we instead used the parent agency’s ideology scores, since we had no reason to believe that a federal bureau would perform functions that are ideologically distant from the parent agency. Third, our panel of 135 federal agencies is unbalanced due to missing data and recent reorganizations of some federal agencies. A more balanced data set would have produced more reliable estimates. Last, despite our efforts to rule out possible alternative explanations in our estimations, there may still be unaccounted for factors or unobserved heterogeneity that may affect federal IT investments, potentially leading to omitted variable bias. For instance, we were unable to obtain granular information on the amount of existing IT stock, which requires maintenance for ongoing operations.

The public sector offers abundant future research opportunities for the IS community. For instance, IS researchers can examine the performance effects of federal IT investments, i.e., how federal IT spending improves agency performance and the kinds of value IT investments create for the public. As discussed above, IT projects in the government sector are subject to high risks of failures and mismanagement. Taking into account unique factors in the public sector such as political environments, bureaucratic administration, and pursuit of diverse values (Caudle 1991, Molina and Spicer 2004), research on IT project management, IT outsourcing, and IT governance in the federal government context will produce interesting findings and fruitful theoretical insights. Future research can also examine the impact of public-sector IT investments on specific public policy areas such as education, national security, environmental protection or healthcare. How does the emergence of new digital technologies affect formulation and implementation of major policy programs? These efforts will expand the influence of IS research toward the political science and public policy disciplines and bring new audiences to the IS discipline.
