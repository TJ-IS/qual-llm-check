## Highlight

 We integrate users into visualization and analysis of an ICT ecosystem with user data

 We propose a simple method of showing evolutionary trends in the ICT ecosystem

 We broaden the ecosystem layer model by using users’ media repertoire concept

 The evolutionary trends of firms and layers help practitioners make a strategic decision

## Abstract

We integrate users into the visualization and analysis of an information and communication technology (ICT) ecosystem by using demand-side data. We also broaden the ecosystem layer model by using a media repertoire concept and propose a clear method of showing evolutionary trends. Consequently, we discover the nature of an evolutionary path in an ICT ecosystem. This path becomes more rigid and centralized as it matures, a finding that agrees with prior studies’ results that used supply-side data. Further, we analyze the trends of firms changing layer positions and suggest multiple approaches of visualizing and analyzing interfirm relationships for practitioners.

Keywords: ICT ecosystem; Visualization; Ecosystem Layer Model; Network analysis; Media repertoire; Evolutionary trajectory

## 1. Introduction

In order to adapt to the rapidly changing, highly complex business ecosystem, practitioners should find an effective way to make decisions about their company’s role in the ecosystem, especially in the Information and Communications Technology (ICT) field [1, 2]. Visualizing an ICT business ecosystem is one of the most effective methods for practitioners to understand the entire picture intuitively and establish strategies under the given conditions in a timely manner [3, 4]. In this study, we employ user-level data to build on the ICT ecosystem theory, visualize it, and analyze its evolutionary trajectory.

Fransman [5] introduced the ecosystem layer model (ELM) to analyze an ICT ecosystem’s structure that consists of the content, platform, network, and device layers (C–P–N–D). Unlike traditional ecosystem concepts used in the strategic management field [6, 7], the ELM to this model, an ICT ecosystem’s value is created by its own growth efforts in functional jobs; moreover, innovation is created in each layer’s symbiotic relationship within the ICT ecosystem [5].

The most distinguishable point of the ELM is its emphasis on the users’ role in innovation. ELM regards users as among the most important actors in supporting an ICT ecosystem’s sustainable growth. First, a large number of users contribute to the ICT ecosystem. Because of the demand-side network effect, the high amount of users amplifies the value of the ICT ecosystem [2, 8]. Second, users lead an ICT ecosystem’s innovation. As stated by Moore [9], innovation occurs because of customers who build demand and capabilities and suppliers

who design new products and provide various services. In the process of delivering value from suppliers to users, the latter create their own combinations while consuming a significant amount of products and services. Occasionally, users also actively participate as suppliers in an ICT ecosystem (i.e., users can provide a service, as in Uber and Airbnb) [5, 10].

Scholars have empirically studied ICT ecosystems’ characteristics by various methods; for instance, by comparing each layer’s actual efficiency and an entire ICT ecosystem’s potential efficiency [11-13], by analyzing a macroeconomic input–output table to identify the role of each layer in an ICT ecosystem [14], by reviewing the literature about an innovation ecosystem to determine the roles at each phase of the innovation ecosystem’s lifecycle [15], and by visualizing an ICT ecosystem’s network structure [3, 16-18].

Although scholars have been attempting to understand ICT ecosystems’ evolution, the method of visualizing and quantifying ICT ecosystems’ dynamics is still lacking [17]. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, there is a lack of empirical studies about the ICT ecosystem that consider the user layer, as well as the four layers (C–P–N–D) that also play important roles. Users participate in an ICT ecosystem’s evolutionary trajectory by creating various combinations in the ecosystem. The choice itself is a selection process of the ICT ecosystem that drives the overall ecosystem toward a more innovative and healthier status [19]. In order to address the research gap, this study utilizes the concept of the users’ media repertoire to visualize the ELM and analyze an ICT ecosystem’s evolutionary trajectory.

This study makes three contributions. First, we suggest a new method of visualizing and analyzing an industry-level ecosystem. Second, we propose a simple way of displaying the evolutionary trends of each firm, layer, and content genre in an ICT ecosystem. This proposal could help practitioners in the rapidly changing business environment. Third, we provide empirical evidence of Fransman's [5] ELM, extending the work of Basole and Karla [16] and

Basole, Park, and Barnett [17]. We also expand the model by introducing the users’ media repertoire concept.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the theoretical foundations such as ecosystem’s evolutionary stages, the ICT ecosystem concept, and media repertoire. Section 3 explains the various methodologies that we have employed in this study and includes descriptions of the data and variables. Section 4 shows the analytical results, and Section 5 provides discussions and conclusions.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Evolutionary stages of a business ecosystem

Moore [20] defined the evolutionary stages of a business ecosystem in terms of the following four phases: birth, expansion, leadership, and self-renewal. The first phase, birth, is started with a seed innovation; the leading firms then make the seed innovation attractive enough for follower firms to cooperate with them [20]. As Dedehayir, Mäkinen, and Ortt [15] mentioned, the birth phase is the most chaotic of the four phases because of the high level of uncertainty. Moreover, further studies to find the unknown factors that give birth to a new ecosystem are required [15]. Lee, Kim, and Lee [21] described the birth stage of a business ecosystem as follows. A few firms create a seed innovation. Firms in the same industry then try to benefit by imitating the seed innovation or building new compatible products or services. Finally, during this repetitive cycle, a business ecosystem is naturally created.

The second phase is expansion. In order to enter this phase, the business ecosystem needs to increase the size of the cooperative firms until the critical mass is reached [20]. During this phase, the core technology’s standardization in key market segments is a critical strategy in order to expand the ecosystem because network effects prevail [8, 22]. From the perspective of the leading firms, developing good relationships with cooperative firms and increasing the ecosystem’s brand value are more important than developing new products or services because inferior technologies can sometimes be adopted by the relevant association for standardization and by consumers when the influence of such inferior technologies is more powerful.

When the ecosystem has reached the critical mass level, a few competitive ecosystems encourage the follower firms to participate in the ecosystem’s stabilization [20]. At this point, the leading firms’ leadership style affects the ecosystem’s fate. According to Lee, Lee, and Hwang [12], the leading firms’ strong control of the cooperative firms achieves short-term efficiency more effectively but is not good for the ecosystem’s long-term health. The reason is that allowing the cooperative firms to have autonomy may lead the ecosystem toward the self-renewal phase, which is the last phase of the evolution, by preparing for the next innovation.

## 2.2. The concept of an ICT ecosystem and the ELM

Since 2007, Apple’s iPhone has broken the prior paradigm and created a new business concept, a so-called platform business model, producing a completely new ecosystem in the ICT industry. This phenomenon has inspired scholars, including Fransman [5], who suggested the ELM to explain the newly built ICT ecosystem, which consists of four layers (C–P–N–D). The content layer contains content (or application) providers (CPs), characterized by diverse media genres including music, books, games, films, television broadcasting, cultural spaces, software, and advertising [23]. The platform layer is a system that supports complementary economic activities and shapes symbiotic interaction between the other layers. These activities and the interaction are conducted by platform providers (PPs) such as the mobile operating system (OS) platforms of Google and Apple [5]. The network

layer contains network providers (NPs), such as AT&T, that are responsible for controlling, managing, and operating the communication network in order to provide an improved service for all connections [24]. The device layer includes device providers (DPs), such as Samsung Electronics, that design and assemble various types of handset. In the ICT ecosystem, content, created by CPs, flows from PPs, through NPs, to personal devices manufactured by DPs. These four layers affect each other because they form the behavioral rules that repeatedly influence the layers’ actions [5].

The ELM’s goal is to assist practitioners and policy makers with the analysis of the ICT sector and the development of an appropriate strategy or policy. The ELM views the ICT sector as a system that includes semiconductors, computers, software, consumer electronics, telecommunication equipment, network operators, broadcasting, media, newspapers, books, music, and advertising as subcomponents. In this regard, the ELM contributes to making it easy to understand the interdependency, complex interaction, and power of evolution in the system.

Since Fransman [5] suggested the ICT ELM, the concept has been widely adopted to the ratio of the maximum potential efficiency that the entire ICT ecosystem may possess for each ICT ecosystem layer [11–13]. Battistella, Colucci, De Toni, and Nonino [18] presented the network structure of a digital imaging industry ecosystem, including manufacturers, service providers, and technologies. In addition, Suh and Lee [14] identified the role of each actor in an ICT ecosystem and visualized the relationships by using interindustry analysis. Further, Kang and Downing [25] empirically investigated the competition between two different ecosystems built by telecommunication technology.

Even though scholars strongly emphasized the importance of an ecosystem perspective, they knew only little about the means to explain many of the relationships between the

multiple actors in a complex system until the network analysis method was introduced. Basole and Karla [16] performed the first trial of an ELM’s empirical visualization. Their study considered a mobile platform as an ecosystem and visualized the structure and evolution of the mobile ICT ecosystem that previously existed only as a concept. They developed an ICT ecosystem based on models and theories of complex systems, innovations, the mobile device ICT ecosystem.

Despite their significant contribution to the area of ICT ecosystem research, Basole and Karla [16] focused only on examining the relationship between two layers, the PPs and DPs, and omitted the CPs, which comprise a layer that is nevertheless important. Without the CPs’ data, we can understand only part of the entire network structure and the interdependencies between each layer. In order to overcome the limitations of a mobile-centric view, Basole, Park, and Barnett [17] used a longitudinal data set that contained information about the relationships between ICT-related firms. However, the data used by Basole, Park, and Barnett [17] contained only the published relationships between large conglomerates and ignored the role of small firms and users in an ICT ecosystem’s innovation. We address the gap in the literature by employing user-level longitudinal data that can detect implicit relationships between large conglomerates, small-sized firms, and even users’ preferences for products, services, and genres in each layer in order to show an ICT ecosystem’s evolutionary trajectory from a different perspective.

## 2.3. Media repertoire as a key component in an ICT ecosystem

Jenkins [26] defined “trans-media” as the unconstrained flow of content through diverse media, cooperation between several media industries, and media users’ new behavior in exploring the many ways of consuming content. In a trans-media environment, multiple

#

content based on diverse media platforms creates a new value [26]; moreover, each user has his or her own media repertoire that combines several user options. According to Hasebrink and Popp [29], media users may have their favorite combinations of options. By using such combinations, researchers can determine an effective way of advertising or marketing to these users and can analyze the business ecosystem beyond the firm or industry level. In addition, market share and the relationships between the firms in an ICT ecosystem are identified more accurately from the demand-side by using the bottom-up method. These useful properties of media repertoires enable us to measure users’ behaviors when they select their own preferences across content, platforms, networks, and devices and to understand further the relationships between the four layers (C–P–N–D) in an ICT ecosystem. Thus, we adopt the network analysis method to portray an ICT ecosystem, which is represented by media combinations based on user preference.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Data

We use data from the Korea Media Panel (KMP) of the Korea Information Society Development Institute (KISDI). The KMP is an annual longitudinal survey about the media behavior of nationally representative households and their members. In 2010, the KMP selected 3,085 households by using stratified sampling. Since then, the KMP has noted changes in the same sample’s sociodemographic information, media devices, subscriptions to ICT and media services, expenses on telecommunications and any type of media content, media usage patterns, and behavior annually [30]. The KMP asks about the CPs, PPs, NPs, and DPs that the respondents mainly use; consequently, in this regard, we can establish the respondents’ media repertoire depending on individual preference. With regard to CPs in particular, the KMP identifies and updates annually almost all CPs that users can access for

their content. In this survey, CPs consist of broadcasting stations, media outlets, application providers, web portal service providers, cloud computing service providers, and so on. Data on combinations of CPs, PPs, NPs, and DPs are required in order to form an ICT ecosystem network.

We examine data from 2010 to 2015 and restrict the sample to mobile telecommunication subscribers. In order to maintain the data’s consistency over the six-year period, we select items from the survey that the KMP asked each year. ## 3.2.Formation of ICT ecosystem network

After building the final data set at a personal level, we create an edge list by connecting

each respondent’s media choices for the purpose of network analysis. Network analysis emphasizes each actor’s position that is already embedded in a specific form of network. This position can influence an actor’s behavior, benefits, or other social factors [31]. Among various reasons, the one that has frequently caused recent research to adopt social network theory is that many relationships between consumers (users) and firms, between firms, and between firms and governments can be described by the relationships discussed in social linking patterns between firms. Because information in the social network structured by firms is an important source for obtaining competitive advantage, firms may build a closed and strongly tied network for exclusive access to, and sharing of, such information [32, 33]. Thus, an industry’s structure is regarded as a result of competition between many networks, and an industry’s ecosystem is regarded as the set of such structures.

We create the ICT ecosystem network based on the users’ choices among the various CPs, PPs, NPs, and DPs. For example, we propose a hypothetical situation with the media repertoire of User 1, which is shown in Figure 1. User 1 mainly uses Facebook, downloaded from the iOS mobile platform, through the long-term evolution (LTE) communication network provided by SK Telecom, with a personal Apple-branded smartphone. In this case, User 1 chooses a CP (Facebook), a PP (Apple), an NP (SK Telecom), and a DP (Apple). Consequently, these four layers are User 1’s media repertoire. At this step, we can capture the relationship between the four layers as a media repertoire set. As depicted in Figure 1, User 1’s choices of Facebook (content), iOS (platform), SK Telecom (network), and Apple (device) become the nodes, and links are formed between the four choices because they are grouped by User 1’s media repertoire.

User 2 prefers the following: Facebook (CP), Google (PP), LG Telecom (NP), and Samsung Electronics (DP). User 1 and User 2 share similarities because they use Facebook as their content layer, especially in terms of a social networking service (SNS). In this case, two media repertoire groups are connected by Facebook (CP). ## 3.3. Analysis

## 3.3.1. Visualization analysis

There are a number of methods to analyze a network; however, the importance of visualizing and examining sample networks cannot be overlooked. Illustrating a sample in order to gain new insights can be helpful to researchers. The visualization method can be accomplished by identifying the nodes and linking them together, as shown in Figure 1. We use the Gephi 0.9.1 program to visualize the network.

## 3.3.2. Analysis of the network metrics

Visualizing networks is helpful for gaining intuition; however, it is difficult to follow every node and link when the network is large. Network metrics enable us to understand the characteristics of a complex network.

The number of nodes represents the sum of each user’s media repertoire. The large number of nodes shows that the number of possible choices regarding CPs, PPs, NPs, and DPs is large. The number of links represents the amount of links between the collected samples. A large number of links means that the users’ media repertoires are diverse. If the number of nodes remains the same but the number of links increases, the connectivity between the nodes also increases.

The graph density is estimated as the actual number of links in the network divided by connectivity. “High density” has two meanings in this research. First, the PPs, NPs, DPs, and CPs supply diverse products on the supply-side; second, the users attempt to combine their media repertoires in various ways on the demand-side.

Modularity is an indicator that shows the number of modules that are formed inside a network [35]. It is possible for a large network to contain groups of small networks. Modularity is the probability indicator used for determining the quantity of groups of networks that can be found in a large network. Forming groups inside a network is a natural phenomenon; however, extreme grouping prevents interactions between the nodes, and rare grouping makes the protection of each piece of information difficult.

The average path length is an indicator that represents the mean of the minimum distances between every pair of nodes [36]. A low value for this indicator means that the diffusion of information is highly efficient. If there are many actors in a bridge position, the average path length is short in an ICT ecosystem; however, if there are several intermediate local hubs but fewer bridges, the value is large.

## 3.3.3. Trend analysis based on positioning each node using centralities

Centrality analysis is one of the most representative metrics in network analysis. We use degree centrality and betweenness centrality to identify the positions of the nodes in the ICT ecosystem. Degree centrality, or the number of links that a node has, is measured for each node. If the direction of the links has different implications for incoming and outgoing links, in-degree and out-degree centrality should be measured separately. However, we do not consider in- or out-degree centrality because the direction of the links has no meaning. If a node clearly has a higher centrality than other nodes, it is defined as a hub-platform or a hub content.

Betweenness centrality indicates the extent to which corresponding nodes are linked to other nodes. It is also measured for each node. A node with the highest betweenness centrality is the most effective node for transferring information that also influences other nodes. Betweenness centrality is measured by the number of shortest paths that pass through each node.

In order to determine the trend of the positions of each node, we introduce a matrix based on weighted degree centrality and betweenness degree centrality (see Figure 2). Weighted degree centrality is used rather than degree centrality because it reflects market power more effectively. Because users select a node more often when it has a greater number of overlapping linkages, weighted degree centrality is better able to detect hubs in an ICT ecosystem network. As aforementioned, high betweenness centrality means a higher possibility of connecting two random nodes. Thus, if a node has both high weighted centrality and high betweenness centrality, the node is likely to be the global hub of the entire ecosystem because the platform or content has a high market share and even a higher possibility of overlapping with each user’s repertoire. However, if a node has a high weighted centrality but low betweenness centrality, the node is likely to be the local hub of a community or a module because it has a high market share but only in a specific community that shares a similar repertoire. In addition, if a node has low weighted centrality but high betweenness centrality, the node is likely to be a bridge that links communities. This situation means that the node has little market share; however, many users from different communities have begun to include the node in their repertoires.

Facebook, though, has been growing rapidly and now plays the bridge role in the ICT ecosystem. Daum–Kakao has relocated from the peripheral region, through the bridge role, to the global hub position.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

## 5.1. The evolutionary trajectory of an ICT ecosystem

Using Moore's [20] four phases of an ecosystem and the results of network metrics, we observe that the expansion phase in an ICT ecosystem in Korea existed from 2010 to 2012. During this period, the number of edges, graph density, and modularity increased. This finding means that the primary characteristics of the expansion phase are the new relationships between actors and the expansion of new applications. An explanation could be that users expanded their media repertoire because content providers created many new products and services that were driven by innovation in network and device technology. Since 2013, there is no significant difference in network metrics; thus, the ecosystem seems to be mature. This finding means that the ICT ecosystem developed into a leadership phase where consolidation, establishment, and stability are easily found [20]. According to the network metrics, the modularity that increased at first decreased sharply after 2013. The explanation could also be that the ICT ecosystem has become stable and more centralized. Specifically, the number of segments has reduced, a finding that is consistent with the research of Basole and Karla [16], who showed that the rate of convergence no longer increased as segments in the ICT ecosystem converged. Our finding can be regarded as evidence of the leadership

phase.

## 5.2. Practical implications

By using the trend analysis of the network position in the ICT ecosystem, we can forecast the dynamics of the ICT ecosystem and provide some strategic implications. First, the NP, SK Telecom, was forced toward the local hub position. This finding has implications for NPs. Because the number of subscribers has stopped growing, and the profits from the voice and short message services have been decreasing, many NPs have been experiencing a decrease in their average revenue per user [38]. Consequently, they have attempted to launch their own platforms and content; however, many NPs have failed because when new mobile OS platforms emerged, the NPs have had less capability in terms of all types of value-added service. Although the NPs may keep trying to build their own platforms and content, as several studies have suggested (see [39, 40]), the strategy may not be cost-effective because two powerful platforms (Android and iOS) are already in the global hub position and continue to build their capabilities to secure that position. Hence, we suggest that NPs need to continuously create another gatekeeping role related to their own capabilities. This role could be killer content and applications that offer incentives for both developers and users to use the NPs’ own application stores [41], or localization strategies that create alliances with influential local CPs.

Second, the mobile communication and smart-device technologies become rapidly obsolete when new technologies come out. In 2010, feature phones with 2G and 3G networks were in the global hub position. However, this position was rapidly taken by smartphones with 3G networks, which in turn were replaced by smartphones with LTE networks as soon as LTE technology was developed. This finding shows that NPs cannot avoid investing in

#

research and development (R&D) for next-generation communication technology even if they have enjoyed above-normal profit stability. In addition, DPs should always develop new devices that adopt next-generation communications in order to ensure sustainable growth.

Third, we can determine long-term trends and short-term fashions from the trend analysis of the changing position of the preferred content genre. If the genre shows an increasing trend the genre related to SNSs is a long-term trend. According to Figure 7, this genre has been increasing more steadily than other genres. This finding means that CPs should consider SNS platforms when they create their applications, games, software, and any type of content. In this context, Suh and Lee [14] discussed the importance of utilizing advanced SNS platforms such as Daum–Kakao in the content service market. The gaming genre is also likely to be a long-term trend. The genre has been in the peripheral box but has been fluctuating toward a bridge position. Considering the direction and speed, the gaming genre should be more popular for all age groups than it is currently. “Gamification” has also received significant attention from other application areas such as health care and education [42, 43]. Thus, CPs must focus on the gamification factor and the SNS genre when developing a new service.

Finally, we analyzed the changing position of SNSs in an ICT ecosystem. SNSs started as social media applications but are now also types of PP. A large amount of content is posted on SNSs. They also secure a significant number of users, which places them in the role of platforms. Hence, analyzing their position has implications for small- and medium-sized CPs that provide content to SNSs and to the SNS firms themselves. With regard to an SNS, the bridge position is an intermediate phase as it moves toward the global hub because it can connect diverse types of media repertoire. However, if an SNS grows toward the local hub, it is difficult for it to be positioned in the global hub area. In the local hub position, an SNS could have stable profits; however, when the short-term fashion has passed, the SNS has no

power to expand its market. Facebook has significant potential to grow toward the global hub because Kakao has moved to the global hub position through the bridge role. Thus, the CPs that want to provide their content to SNS firms should choose SNS platforms that relate to their target markets. If their target markets are in locally isolated groups, the CPs would do best to choose the SNSs in local hub positions; however, if their target markets are sparse in several communities, the CPs would do best to choose the SNSs in bridge positions.

## 5.3. Contributions, limitations, and future research

The ELM in an ICT ecosystem provides significant usefulness for researchers. In an ICT ecosystem, four layers consisting of the content, platform, network, and device are interconnected and influence each other. However, it has been difficult to analyze the relationships between all four layers (C–P–N–D) and their dynamics. The difficulty arises in obtaining information on all firms in the ICT ecosystem; moreover; using published information does not provide a representative relationship between firms. Finally, and most significantly, prior studies have ignored the role of users, who are important drivers of innovation in the ICT ecosystem. Thus, we used data based on users’ media repertoires in order to overcome the limitations when using data from the supply-side. By using the bottom up method, we could include all firms related to the ICT ecosystem and obtain the underlying relationships between them by considering the users’ choices. In addition, we showed an ICT ecosystem’s dynamics and how the ecosystem has evolved by using panel data empirically, an approach that had been lacking in prior studies.

In this regard, the current study makes scholarly contributions to the ICT ecosystem literature. First, we developed Fransman's [5] ELM by adding the empirical evidence of prior studies and converging the model with the users’ media repertoire concept. While ELM highlighted the users’ role, mainly in terms of consuming the products and services in the ICT

#

ecosystem, this study expanded the role whereby users take the lead in the evolution of the ICT ecosystem by building their own media repertoires. Moreover, unlike Basole, Park, and Barnett [17], who focus on the competition and convergence between existing actors, this study showed that the user layer can also be a main part of the ICT ecosystem. Second, we showed the dynamics of the framework, whereas prior studies have simply shown static snapshots of the framework.

This study also has practical contributions. First, we suggested a new bottom-up method employing user-level data for visualizing and analyzing an industry-level ecosystem. Official survey data from multiple years were integrated into the analysis of an industry-level ICT ecosystem, illustrating the actual relationships for each actor. From the results, we found that visualization based on demand-side data is as powerful a method to show the interfirm relationships in an ICT ecosystem as using supply-side data. While Basole and Karla [16] used firm-level data and Suh and Lee [14] employed industry-level data to analyze ICT ecosystems, our study visualized an ICT ecosystem by using data from users’ media repertoires to show the evolutionary trajectory of the ICT ecosystem over six years. Second, we proposed an understandable method of showing the evolutionary trajectory of each firm, layer, and even the content genres. In a rapidly changing business environment, timing is an important factor for decision-makers when they reach a critical point. The method of visualizing an ICT ecosystem that we suggested could be helpful for practitioners, especially those who manage a business in a highly complex and highly changeable industry. Our method allows such practitioners to obtain ideas and intuition from our method of business ecosystem analysis because demand-side data are more accessible than supply-side data.

This study is not free from limitations. First, we failed to control individual sociodemographic characteristics such as gender, age, and educational attainment that relate to individual media repertoires. However, because these individual factors do not generally

#

change over time, we ignored the bias by using longitudinal data that track the same people over time. Second, the implementation of the network position in the centrality matrix by using network metrics was not performed with absolute values. We used relative position, which could create bias in determining the exact position in the ICT ecosystem. Finally, in terms of external validity, our findings are more applicable to countries with rapidly diffusing ICT and a business-friendly environment.

In terms of further research, we could expand this study by incorporating the concept of coevolution. Competition with an external ecosystem, the direction of connection and interaction, self-organization, and communication between social, political, and economic factors can be incorporated to investigate the dynamics of more complex systems from a coevolutionary perspective. Such additional network metrics would be useful to investigate the relationships and trends of each actor from a micro-level perspective. This approach would also lead us to a better understanding of an ICT ecosystem’s dynamics.
