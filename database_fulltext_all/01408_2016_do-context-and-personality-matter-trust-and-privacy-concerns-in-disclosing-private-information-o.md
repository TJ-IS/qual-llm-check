Do Context and Personality Matter? Trust and Privacy Concern in Disclosing Private Information Online

## ABSTRACT

Research and business practice have already established the central role of trust in business, particularly in online interactions. Many online business exchanges require the disclosure of sensitive personal information on a regular basis. At the same time, customers are reluctant to disclose private information online due to concerns about privacy. Thus, trust plays a critical role in such disclosures. However, a number of factors could influence the extent of customers’ trust and their willingness to disclose their private information. In this study, we explore the critical roles of two sets of factors: the sensitivity of the context within which the private information is disclosed and the customer’s personality. Our theory building is based on the contextualization of theory of reasoned action (TRA-Privacy) and its synthesis with Prospect theory. Our theory argues that context sensitivity as a moderator and individuals’ salient attributes in terms of personality types and privacy concern are critical factors impacting trust and the willingness to disclose personal information. Our results indicate that context moderates the parameters and path structure of the trust model, and that the influence of personality on privacy concern and trust depends on the nature of the context. Adding these “who” and “where” aspects of theory building, the study opens a new avenue of context-related research, which can increase the specificity and richness of trust and privacy research. It also provides a new perspective in trust scholarship, requiring the examination of three inter-related components: trustee, trustor and the context of trust.

Keywords: Trust, Personality, Privacy, Context Sensitivity, Theory of Reasoned Action, Prospect theory, TRA-Privacy.

## INTRODUCTION

Trust has been the fundamental pre-requisite for the progress of commerce and prosperity in human societies [46]. Gefen [49] was among the first to show the similarly critical role of trust in ecommerce. Trust is the willingness to depend on another person or institution based on the belief in the integrity, ability, and benevolence of this other [47, 91, 94]. Trust is a central determinant of behavior across situations because it determines the extent to which people are willing to depend on others [91]. Trust is a social phenomenon, which can downplay the social complexity involved in assessing others’ motives and behaviors and thus can be valuable in social interactions [54, 86]. Research in traditional business interactions has demonstrated the significant role of trust [26, 36, 47, 79]. Research on online purchasing activities has demonstrated the singularly critical role of trust in online business transactions [49, 52, 69, 78, 87, 94, 107, 109].

#

While the central role of trust as a major social capital in online activities is well-established [34, 46, 125], there is inadequate research about the role of context and trustee attributes in the formation of this critical social capital. Context and getting to know your customers are now considered among the main principles of forward-looking businesses at Google [119]. The business mantra “getting to know your customer” requires learning what motivates and concerns them. Trust is formed and modified within the business interaction context and influenced by customers’ attributes. Various personality traits have emerged as salient attributes in doing business in the online social environment [65, 136].

While context and customer attributes in terms of their personalities, concerns, and experiences are of great interest in the industry literature, there has been inadequate systematic investigation about the role context and customer attributes play in online business interactions. This paper aims to address this gap by answering the following question: what are the roles of context and personality traits in the formation of trust and the willingness of individuals to disclose their private information online? To answer this question, we develop our theory based on the contextualization of the theory of reasoned action and its synthesis with Prospect theory [76] to examine the contextual role of personality (Big Five) on privacy concern and trust. We test the resulting conceptualized model through a lab experiment, which contrasts high and low sensitive contexts: finance versus ecommerce, with health acting as a validity check. Personality types and privacy concern are used as salient customer attributes.

The contribution of this study is in introducing personality and context to trust and privacy when disclosing personal information online. In their seminal paper Mayer et al. [91] suggested that propensity to trust, an aspect of personality, determines trust. Thus, a major contribution of this paper is setting Trust theory in a new perspective by showing that personality is indeed a significant determinant of trust. The study adds the who and where—previously neglected aspects—to the theory of trust and privacy, opening new avenues of research, and showing that managers need to consider context and personality in dealing with their customers and personalizing websites.

In what follows, we discuss the theoretical framework and model conceptualization, followed by methodology, data analysis, and discussions. Conclusions and limitations are presented last.

## THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

Theory development based on context specificity is an emerging area of theory building in the IS literature. The word context in its Latin root means knitting together or making a connection [124]. A more salient definition of context is given by Mowday and Sutton [99, p. 198]: “context encompasses stimuli and phenomenon that surround and thus exist in the environment external to the individual.” In his seminal work on what constitutes theoretical contribution, Whetten [146] presents four components of theory building, three of which are commonly present in all theories—what (salient variables such as x and y), how (relations such as xy), and why (the logical reasons for relations). The fourth component of theory building consists of when, where, and who, which constitute the context of the theory. This context component sets the boundaries for the relationships posited through the first three components, and is commonly missing from general theories. Whetten [146] encourages scholars to ask whether their theories remain valid over time (when), in all places (where), or for all people (who).

Context could relate to time, location, or individual attributes. For example, examining individual behaviors before and after a major traumatic event could be a contextual study where the context variable is time (when). A comparison of individual behaviors across occupational domains such as professionals versus students could be categorized as a contextual study where the contextual variable is demographics (who). The context variable in a study of factors impacting job performance in the urban and rural settings is location (where). Context can moderate the nature and extent of relationships. People understand what is going on by understanding where and when it is happening. In order to understand a phenomenon, it is important to analyze its context, since meaning is derived from the context [58].

Trust research has examined some aspects of the what, how, and why. While the when aspect has been studied by Zahedi and Song [149] in their dynamic analysis of trust, there has been inadequate investigation of the who and where aspects, Bansal et al. [10] being an exception. Addressing this need, we focus on the where (context) and who (personality) aspects of online trust.

Johns [70] argues for the inclusion of context in organizational research for a number of reasons, the most important of which is the ability to explain anomalies that otherwise would remain unexplained.

#

Later, Johns [71] laments the lack of adequate recognition of the importance of context in organizational scholarship and argues for the contextualization of theories in organizational studies. Whetten [146] calls for contextualized theory building in order to provide sharper boundaries and deeper understanding about generalization, explains inconsistencies caused by context, and provides an appreciation of the interplay of person and situation, making research more relevant to industry [70]. Theory contextualization could thus allow for the development of a more realistic theory of trust with a broader and more holistic view of trust relationships, making current scholarship more relevant.

Extending theory contextualization to IS, Hong et al. [63] provide guidelines for context-specific theorizing in the IS research. Contextualization of a theory could be done in a number of ways, including specifying context-specific antecedent variables, specifying context-specific consequent variables or using context as a moderator of path relationships. In this study, our theory contextualization includes all three—context-related antecedents (who—users’ personality and experience), a context-specific outcome variable (context-specific disclosure of private information), and context sensitivity as the moderator of path relationships.

Theory contextualization in the IS literature is in its early stages. A recent example is the contextualization of the theory of planned behavior (TPB) [4] for studying teenage behaviors in virtual worlds [90]. The general theory in our research is the theory of reasoned action (TRA) [5, 42], on which the technology adoption model (TAM) was built [29]. TAM applied a simplified version of TRA by excluding subjective norm (beliefs about the opinions of other people important to an individual). In this study, we too focus on belief, attitude, and behavior intention as the core constructs for theory contextualization. Privacy concern constitutes the salient dispositional belief, which impacts behavior intention directly and indirectly as mediated by trust attitude. The behavior intention in this study is the disclosure of context-specific private information. Furthermore, synthesizing with Prospect theory and following Devaraj et al. [32], we extend TRA to include antecedents of privacy concern as users’ personality and their previous experiences of privacy invasion.

#

Prospect theory [76] suggests that people’s behaviors in making choices under uncertainty depend on the utility and disutility of outcomes, and are impacted by the context of such choices [140]. In this theory, it is shown that the influence of regret/negative feeling of loss is greater than the joy/positive feeling of winning [76]. Moreover, utility and preferences for outcomes depend on individuals’ personal attributes, such as risk aversion, potential changes in their status, and extent of regret they may feel about loss [140]. Relying on a synthesis with Prospect theory, we show how context sensitivity acts as the moderator of the TRA paths, thus completing our theory building through the contextualization of TRA. We refer to this contextualization of TRA as TRA-Privacy.

## Salient Belief—Privacy Concern

Privacy is defined as the right to control and decide what personal information is transmitted to others [120, 126]. Private personal information varies in the degree of its sensitivity. Sensitive private information is a distinctive type of private information whose public disclosure could harm the individual financially or socially [98]. For this reason, some sensitive information, such as individuals’ health records or students’ grades, is protected by law. While a person’s name is private, it is not sensitive in that its disclosure generally does not harm. However, the public disclosure of the individual’s social security number or bank information could lead to financial and identity theft, causing major harm. The concern over exposure to such harms causes the belief that one may be in harm’s way in the online environment.

The cost of data breaches has been on the rise globally, with the U.S. having the highest per capita cost of data breach, as reported in a Symantec study [117]. Privacy concern is an increasingly important issue for individuals in the online environment. With the advent of technology that enhances data collection, data sharing, and data mining techniques, the concept of Internet information privacy has become as ubiquitous as the data itself. Users across the world are increasingly wary of Big Brother, and more so of Big Business as businesses amass and scrutinize more and more data about users [67]. It is observed that people have become increasingly reluctant to disclose their personal data and even resort to fabricating such information in order to mitigate the risk of abuse [83]. Research on privacy has modeled privacy concern in several different ways [131]. Privacy has been examined as being in control of information, calculus of information exchange, right of being left alone, and also as a state of privacy [131]. Privacy has also been related to a belief that the collector of the information will behave appropriately [80]. Privacy concern is also considered as a personal disposition and belief [7, 35, 88]. Privacy has also been modeled as situation-specific, where an individual’s situational cues enable a

person to assess the consequences of information disclosure [81, 148]. In this study, privacy concern is the salient dispositional belief related to the disclosure of personal information online.

## Trust Attitude

The need for trust arises when the trustor depends on the trustee, being vulnerable to its actions but unable to control its behavior [91]. Disclosing sensitive private information online is such a case [52]. It involves vulnerability to monetary loss, social embarrassment, and psychological violation of private space. It has been shown that trust plays an important role not only in ecommerce [14, 88], but also in online health care [138] and banking [41, 62]. We focus on trust as the salient attitude in disclosing private information online, since trust determines the extent to which people are willing to reveal sensitive information [52] to the business entity that owns the website [8].

In presenting an integrative theory of organizational trust, Mayer et al. [91] asserted the role of context, arguing that context impacts antecedents of trust (ability, benevolence, and integrity). Since then, however, little conceptualization and empirical investigation has been done on the role of context in online privacy concern and trust. We address this gap here. Recently Smith et al. noted that “general privacy—its conceptual understanding, rigorous definition, and the intensity of the individual and cultural beliefs it informs—is so dependent on the specific context that it is impossible to develop a one-size-fitsall conceptualization of general privacy” [131, p. 1002].

Sensitivity of information disclosed heightens the loss of control and increases the perceived privacy risk belief [82]. In traditional markets, sensitive private information is shared with those whom individuals trust based on intimate familiarity or on the assumption that the penalty for breaking trust is such that it will prevent abuse. For example, physicians by law and professional ethics are bound to keep patients’ sensitive private health information confidential. Similarly, banks could be liable if they abuse their customers’ sensitive financial information. These penalties, however, do not apply as easily to the case of providing private information online. The impersonal nature of websites and the inadequate knowledge about the physical location, the legal entity behind the website, and multiple layers of possible security breaches could make customers wary of providing private sensitive information online, hence the need to manage customers’ online trust.

## Context Sensitivity

The US Government Accountably Office (GAO) defines personally identifiable information (PII) as “(1) any information that can be used to distinguish or trace an individual’s identity, such as name, social security number, date and place of birth, mother’s maiden name, or biometric records; and (2) any other information that is linked or linkable to an individual, such as medical, educational, financial, and employment information” [48, 92]. Context sensitivity depends on the sensitivity level of the PII type and the regularity of its exchange. For example, the social security number is a more sensitive PII than a person’s first and last name. We define sensitive context as an environment in which individuals’ sensitive information is collected, stored, and communicated as a matter of routine business activity. We investigate two types of context: ecommerce and finance, which involve different sensitivity levels for PII in business interactions, allowing us to investigate the impact of customers’ personality, experiences and privacy concern on their trust in multiple contexts involving different types and sensitivity levels of PII. Hence, this investigation examines how customers’ traits in various contexts modify their trust.

The argument that both individual factors and situation impact behavior is called interactionism [113]. “Most personality psychologists are now interactionists, in the sense that they emphasize both person and situation variables when defining behavior” [113, p. 352]. Here we need to draw a distinction between context and situation. Context is defined as “stimuli and phenomena that surround and thus exist in the environment external to the individual” [99 p. 198]. Situation has a number of different definitions. Some have argued that situation is an internal cognitive state [13]; others have viewed situation as a cognitive state which depends upon one’s interaction with the social environment [60]. It is also argued that situation is the concrete environment in itself [28, 137]. While a situation could be external or internal, context is generally external [99]. Accentuating the role of context in privacy choices, FTC emphasizes the importance of context and data sensitivity in choosing a method of privacy protection [40].

#

Based on a literature review [122, 130, 134, 139], Stone and Stone [135] have argued that the degree of perceived invasiveness should differ depending on the type of information. While previous research examined the level of privacy concern for different types of information, such as contact details, credit card number, social security number, and health information [95, 114, 129], no previous research has examined how such sensitivity and related privacy concern impact the reliance on trust across various types of websites. Nonetheless, extrapolating from Mayer et al. [91], reliance on trust in providing private information on websites should increase as the inherent risk in the context increases. Thus, it is critical to understand why context sensitivity pervasively moderates the TRA paths.

Prospect theory [141], an extension of Utility theory [77], can shed light on how the sensitivity of context moderates the paths leading to intention to disclose private information. Based on Prospect theory, in making choices individuals balance the utility and disutility of various aspects of each choice, and the utility-disutility balance depends on the personal preferences of individuals and their utility functions, which are influenced by their knowledge, experience, and personality. This utility-disutility balance could also vary across contexts, with an increased reluctance to engage when there is a higher degree of perceived vulnerability [37, 44, 141, 142]. Prospect theory suggests people dislike losing more than they enjoy gaining. Loss has a steep convex function, suggesting that people generally are loss averse. For most people the smallest gain that they need to balance an equal chance to lose \$1 is around \$1.5 - \$2.5 [75], and this coefficient increases as stakes rise [75]. This theory also suggests that the degree of loss aversion varies from person to person [75].

Acquisti and Grossklags [1, 2] argued that Prospect theory is the most appropriate lens to study privacy. Bansal et al. [11] have argued that the service a website provides has positive utility and that sharing sensitive private information has negative utility, or disutility, since people prefer to keep such information private. They contended that this disutility may be aggravated or reduced depending on privacy concern (disutility aggravator) and trust in the websites (disutility reducer). Applied to the case of website types, an individual’s decision to provide sensitive private information online should depend,

therefore, on factors that increase or decrease the disutility of disclosing private information, moderated by contextual sensitivity. To study this change in disutility due to contextual sensitivity, we examined financial and ecommerce contexts, with the latter containing less inherent contextual risk. Based on Prospect theory, building on the established importance of trust in disclosing private information online [52, 94], and applying the implied increased importance of trust in more risky contexts [91], we argue that trust is an antecedent of intention to disclose private information online. And, in the more sensitive contexts, the presence of trust is even more critical in the disclosure of private information online. Hence, we posit that:

Hypothesis 1: (i) Trust is positively associated with intention to disclose one’s private information online; and (ii) context sensitivity increases the extent of this association.

There are several reasons consumers may wish to keep personal information private, among which are invasion of privacy, disclosure (publicly disclosing embarrassing facts), false light (false public portrayal), and appropriation (use of a person’s name without permission) [95, 104, 118]. These privacy concerns negatively impact both trusting beliefs [88] and the willingness to provide personal information on the Internet [35]. Based on Prospect theory, since increased privacy concern increases disutility, higher privacy concern should lower intention to disclose private information. Indeed, previous privacy studies have found that consumer willingness to disclose personal information depends on the nature of the information requested [88, 95, 103, 114, 129, 145], and hence on the associated privacy concern. Echoing similar thoughts, Milne [95] has argued that when obtaining information from people, compliance rates vary according to the type of the information requested. Applying Prospect theory, with its increased reluctance to engage as disutility increases and its context dependency, to the above observations implies:

## Hypothesis 2: (i) Privacy concern for a given type of information is negatively associated with intention to disclose that type of information online; and (ii) context sensitivity increases the extent of this association.

Privacy concern may also influence trust. Privacy concerns reflect the worries one has about the opportunistic behavior of the online vendor related to information handling. These worries lower trust in the online vendor [11] in the same way that distrust tends to reinforce and perpetuate further distrust [130]. People act in accordance to the confirmatory bias—those with negative prior attitudes tend to actively distrust and vice versa [23]. In the same vein, a high degree of privacy concern is more likely to be associated with low trust in the online vendor. This is in line with the argument made by Malhotra et al. [88] that “one’s tendency to worry over information privacy will influence how the person perceives a specific situation in which an online marketer requests personal information (i.e. trusting and risk beliefs) (p. 341).”<sup>1</sup> Hence,

Hypothesis 3: (i) Privacy concern for a given type of information is negatively associated with trust in a website asking for that type of information; and (ii) context sensitivity increases the extent of this association.

## Personality

Another hypothesized source of differences in intentions to disclose sensitive private information is related to the personality traits of the individual. According to Prospect theory, individuals differ in their utility functions as well as in their personality traits. Personality relates to the “who” aspect of theory building [146]. The conceptualization of the trustors’ traits in previous empirical research has been limited to propensity to trust and risk beliefs, as in [49, 68, 91, 94, 111, 133, 149]. Nonetheless, there is good reason to argue that personality differences affect trust because personality traits are related to how people seek information and to their behavior. Such links have been well-documented in the extensive research relating personality and job performance [12], personality and job satisfaction [73], personality and sustained attention and workload [121], and personality and career success across life span [72]. Ajzen and Fishbein [5] have observed the importance of personality in forming individuals’ beliefs and motivating their behaviors. Wrightsman [147] has reported the relationship between personality and willingness to trust under different game conditions. Devaraj et al. [32] have reported the influence of

five-factor personality traits in perceived usefulness and ease of use in TAM. Junglas et al. [74] report the impact of personality types on privacy concern in the context of location-based services.

Metrics for personality traits have been investigated extensively in psychology since the 1930s when Allport and Odbert [6] identified 4,500 descriptive terms for personality traits [3, 6, 15]. Since then, there have been numerous attempts to reduce traits to a manageable and meaningful structure. Briggs has provided an extensive review of the research in personality and concluded that the five-factor model (FFM) of personality traits has emerged as the “model of choice” (p. 282). There are two widely used models for FFM—NEO five-factor inventory [23, 25] and Goldberg’s Big Five factor [61].

The personality trait classification applied in this study is Goldberg’s Big Five factors. We use Goldberg’s Big Five factors since their items match closely with trait lexicons and are publicly available from published research. The Big Five factors include extroversion, agreeableness, emotional instability, conscientiousness, and intellect. Neuroticism and openness in NEO have been shown to correlate with emotional instability and intellect in the Big Five factors [15] . Using the Big Five factors makes it possible to theoretically capture the essence of individuals’ personality [33]. Indirect support for the need to include personality traits in the study of trust comes from previous studies of online trust, which suggested that the personality trait of trusting disposition impacts trust e.g., [94]. Previous research, however, has not studied the impact of the Big Five personality traits on online trust.

Extroversion reflects people-orientation and pleasure in social interactions. Descriptions for this trait include being talkative, bold, assertive, sociable, and demonstrative [61]. Extroverts enjoy being the center of attention. On the other hand, introverts prefer solitude—significant correlation has been found between introversion and a preference for isolation [112]. Since social interactions often involve sharing personal information, extroverts are less likely to be concerned about their privacy. Ajzen and Fishbein [5] have also observed that extroversion could influence beliefs and behaviors when it comes to social norms. Stone and Stone [135] noted that, compared to extroversion, introversion has a higher association with privacy concern. Extroverts are more likely to trust in others since they derive more enjoyment from social interactions. However, in most cases, and particularly in what is studied here, social interactions

require trust. The threshold for trust should vary between extroverts and introverts. To accommodate their needs for social interactions, extroverts should have lower thresholds for trust [20, 144]. At the same time, the extent of privacy concern and trust depends on how damaging the consequence of abuse of personal information is. Thus, applying Prospect theory to the case of the extroversion personality trait implies that:

Hypothesis 4a: (i) Extroversion is negatively associated with privacy concern; and (ii) context sensitivity reduces the extent of this association. Hypothesis 5a: (i) Extroversion is positively associated with trust; and (ii) context sensitivity reduces the extent of this association.

Agreeableness is a personality trait that reflects social conformity. This trait is described as being warm, kind, cooperative, trusting, generous, flexible, considerate, and agreeable [61] . Agreeable individuals have a better sense of wellbeing and are more successful in getting along with others [24, 73]. Agreeable individuals emphasize proper social behavior and are apprehensive about deviant behaviors [20]. They consider such behaviors risky. Since privacy invasion is a deviant social behavior, we argue that individuals with this trait are more concerned about their privacy than others are. Based on Prospect theory, the extent of privacy concern should depend on the sensitivity of the context.

On the other hand, agreeableness “involves getting along with others in pleasant, satisfying relationships” [106 p. 340]. Hence, agreeable individuals should be more trusting, particularly when the context is non-sensitive and implies social interactivity. Furthermore, the social conformity of agreeable individuals requires more interactions with others than those with less agreeable personalities. Hence, agreeable individuals need to lower their trust threshold in order to satisfy their needs for social conformity and therefore should display more trust. Thus, based on Prospect theory and considering the nature of the agreeable personality trait, we posit that:

Hypothesis 4b: (i) Agreeableness is positively associated with privacy concern; and (ii) context sensitivity increases the extent of this association.

Hypothesis 5b: (i) Agreeableness is positively associated with trust; and (ii) context sensitivity reduces the extent of this association.

Emotional instability is a trait that reflects neuroticism and the extent of individuals’ reactions to stressful conditions. This trait is described by items such as being anxious, depressed, stressed, suggestible, volatile, and fearful [61]. Neurotic individuals experience more negative affects, such as job dissatisfaction [73]. Chauvin et al. [20] have observed that emotional stability involves more fearlessness in hazardous situations, whereas emotional instability makes it more difficult to form trust, since suspicion and fear are not suitable conditions for trust formation [31]. Individuals with a higher level of anxiety and fearfulness should be more nervous about disclosing their personal information and have more privacy concern. Indeed, Pavlou and Gefen [111] have shown that when online buyers experience what they perceive to be a contract violation, they feel increased fear and anxiety, which makes the institutional mechanisms in the website less effective in building trust. Therefore, this concern should increase with the rise of context sensitivity. Furthermore, being fearful and anxious about other people makes such individuals more aware of the reasons for not trusting others [111], implying that they might have a higher threshold for trusting people. The sensitivity of the context increases the fear factor, leading to higher levels of privacy concern and an increased trust threshold. Hence, the application of Prospect theory to the above arguments leads to:

Hypothesis 4c: (i) Emotional instability is positively associated with privacy concern; and (ii) context sensitivity increases the extent of this association.

Hypothesis 5c: (i) Emotional instability is negatively associated with trust; and (ii) context sensitivity increases the extent of this association.

Conscientiousness represents volition, which is described by terms such as being organized, dependable, thorough, and exacting [61]. It is related to precaution and foresight [20]. Conscientious individuals are detail-oriented or take action to perform the required steps to accomplish a task. Such people are less willing to take risk or to get involved in hazardous situations [108, 143]. They see deviant behaviors as more risky and hazardous [20]. Since privacy invasion is a deviant behavior, individuals with high conscientiousness should regard online disclosure of information as a more risky behavior in sensitive contexts. To date, there has been little evidence or reasoning about how conscientious individuals form their trust, since this trait is less concerned with social interactions and is more about

orderliness and foresight. Nonetheless, since online information could easily be misused, such individuals should be more suspicious and watchful of the circumstances. Being more cautious and foresighted should lead to heightened privacy concerns. Being more suspicious means having a greater perception of risk, which makes individuals more hesitant to trust and to have a higher threshold for trust [91]. Hence:

Hypothesis 4d: (i) Conscientiousness is positively associated with privacy concern; and (ii) context sensitivity increases the extent of this association.

Hypothesis 5d: (i) Conscientiousness is negatively associated with trust; and (ii) context sensitivity increases the extent of this association.

Intellect is a personality trait that reflects individuals’ intellectual abilities, such as imagination, curiosity, broad-mindedness, open-mindedness, and artistic sensitivity [12]. In this research, we focus on intellect in terms of being quick-minded, reflective, and imaginative [61]. Individuals with the intellect trait are more rational and open to new ideas and possibilities. They have a “live and let live” attitude [20 p. 174]. Focusing on the rationality and reasoning power of such individuals, one can argue that these individuals may have higher privacy concern only when the context is risky to them. These individuals use their logic and rationality to understand sensitive contexts better than others do and are therefore better able to assess risks. In non-sensitive contexts, such individuals should be more willing to share their information because they do not see any reason to worry about their privacy. Individuals with the intellect personality trait should also form their trust through the lenses of reason and rationality. Rationality requires caution in sensitive contexts and demands a higher trust threshold for disclosing sensitive personal information. However, in contexts with a lower possibility of loss, such individuals should realize, more than others, the advantages of providing the information, and so should have less need to withhold their trust. Hence, their trust threshold should be lower in contexts that are less not sensitive. Hence:

Hypothesis 4e: (i) Intellect is positively associated with privacy concern in sensitive contexts; and (ii) intellect is negatively associated with privacy concern in less-sensitive contexts.

Hypothesis 5e: (i) Intellect is negatively associated with trust in sensitive contexts; and (ii) intellect is positively associated with trust in less-sensitive contexts.

## Individual Experiences

Another aspect of individuals’ characteristics is their context-related experiences. When customers provide their information to online vendors, a “psychological” contract is created. This contract implies an assurance that the vendor behind the website will undertake the responsibility to manage customers personal information properly [19, 28, 96, 114]. Customers might consider such an implied “psychological” contract breached if they think their privacy has been invaded [111 p. 372]. In the contex of online marketplaces, Pavlou and Gefen [111] have demonstrated that to an individual customer, a single online seller’s psychological contract violation leads to the perception of contract violation by the entire community of online sellers. Thus, online privacy invasion, being a psychological breach of contract, could generate feelings of betrayal, resentment, anger [123], and injustice [107]. Culnan [27] and Stone and Stone [135] have also argued that previous encounters of privacy invasion could affect individuals’ concerns for privacy. Indeed, previous experiences of privacy invasion negatively impact one’s willingness to be profiled online [7]. Thus, experiences of privacy invasion should increase the disutility of disclosing sensitive personal information online. Being more aware of undesirable consequences based on experience, individuals who have encountered privacy invasions should have higher privacy concern. And, applying Prospect theory, context sensitivity should increase this effect, thus:

Hypothesis 6: (i) Previous online privacy invasion is positively associated with privacy concern; and (ii) context sensitivity increases the extent of this association.

Previous research supports the notion that prior positive experience is associated with increased trust in a website. Familiarity and positive previous experience with a website increase consumers’ trusting behavioral intentions [49, 50, 51, 56, 68, 94], while negative experience lowers trust [111]. In the context of health infomediaries, Song and Zahedi [133] have shown that past positive experience leads to favorable trusting beliefs. Based on Prospect theory, building trust in more sensitive contexts should require more extensive positive experiences.

Hypothesis 7: (i) Prior positive experience with a website is positively associated with trust in the website, and (ii) context sensitivity reduces the extent of this association.

Along the same lines, prior positive experience with a website should also reduce inhibitions to disclose private information online. Based on Prospect theory, positive experiences with a website should lessen the disutility associated with disclosing one’s information based on experience. Furthermore, per Prospect theory, context sensitivity should reduce the impact of this association, since individuals need more extensive positive experiences in order to overcome their reluctance about disclosing their private information. This reasoning is in line with results obtained by Gefen [49] that familiarity increases intentions to interact with websites.

Hypothesis 8: (i) Prior positive experience with a website is positively associated with intention to disclose private information on the website; and (ii) context sensitivity reduces the extent of this association.

## METHODOLOGY

The model was tested using controlled lab experiments with online access. We used financial websites as contexts with high monetary sensitivity, and online travel websites (ecommerce) as contexts with low monetary sensitivity, as argued by Malhotra et al. [88]. We also added a third category of websites, health websites, to validate the findings obtained from comparing the first two sets of websites. The health websites involve the least monetary sensitivity and normally entail more social interactions with health service providers. The protocol included nine websites (three for each context: finance, ecommerce, and health). The specific websites were Finance: chase.com, huntingtonbank.com, bendigobank.co.au; Ecommerce (travel): orbitz.com, shermantravels.com, bahamabeachclub.com; and Health: webmd.com, everybody.co.nz, healthscout.com. The websites were chosen such that they differed in terms of trust and reputation. The nine website stimuli were selected based on the URL ranking as a measure of reputation and trust. The rankings were based on Alexa.com (owned by Amazon.com) for category of health, finance and online travel (used for the ecommerce context) at the time of data collection. We considered an Alexa ranking of less than 5,000 as high reputation, 5,000-100,000 as medium reputation and above 100,000 as low reputation. Based on the trust literature [9, 69], reputation and trust are highly correlated. Therefore, we expected trust to vary based on the ranking of websites within each category, as shown in

Table B-9. Participants were randomly assigned to view three websites (one finance website, one ecommerce website, and one health website). Even though the websites did not ask for any specific type of information, the participants were primed about the type of information by measuring their sensitivities related to financial information, personal information, and health information. The order of assignment of each type was also randomly determined. After viewing the website, the participants were first asked to answer a set of questions about the contents of the website to ensure that they had examined the ebsite and were then requested to respond to the instrument. A total of 367 observations were collected.

Data analysis was carried out in multiple steps. First, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to examine the discriminant validity of the model in each context. Some items were removed in order to increase convergent and discriminant validity and avoid high cross loading. Tables B1-B3 in Appendix B summarize these EFA results and indicate the appropriate discriminant and convergent validity of the constructs. Table B-4 in Appendix B reports the results of reliability analyses. The Cronbach Alpha values are well above the recommended cut-off point of 0.70 [105], and the composite factor reliability values are greater than the recommended threshold of 0.70 [127]. The AVE values for the constructs exceed the recommended threshold of 0.50, indicating that the constructs have captured appropriate levels of variance, except for intellect, which had a borderline value of 0.48 [43]. Together, these results support the reliability of constructs. We also compared the square of AVE with correlation among latent variables (as reported in Tables B-5 to B-7 in Appendix B). The square values of AVE we greater than the pairwise correlations, indicating reasonable discriminant validity [43, 57]. We also estimated the measurement model for the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Factor loadings for all items were high (Table B-8 in Appendix B). The t-values were greater than 10.63, supporting the statistical significance of factor loadings and the convergent validity of constructs [100].

We checked for common method variance (CMV), which refers to “the variance that is attributable to the measurement method rather than to the construct of interest” [115, p. 879]. We analyzed CMV using several methods. First we used Harman’s single factor test. This test involves exploratory factor analyses of all items to “determine whether the majority of the variance can be accounted for by one general factor” [115, p. 890]. The first factor for finance and ecommerce contexts extracted only 8.7% of the variance in each context. This indicated a lack of significant CMV threat [66]. Furthermore, “there was no general factor accounting for more than 50% of the variation” [97, p. 752]. Since the Harman test is considered a weak test [89], we also carried out the partial correlation test, as suggested by Podsakoff and Organ [116] and Pavlou and Dimoka [110], wherein the variable with the highest explained variance from the factor analysis was added as an additional independent variable. The addition of this new variable did not noticeably alter the variance explained in the dependent variables. Finally, the study collected data in two phases. Personality items were assessed in Phase 1. In Phase 2, participants examined the websites and answered questions pertaining to websites such as trust, experience with the website and intention to disclose. Collecting data in different stages reduces the threat of CMV [115]. Collectively, the above procedures suggested that CMV was not a significant threat.

To check for the success of context manipulation, we included items that measured the sensitivity of information. On a scale of 0-10, the general sensitivity of finance information was significantly higher (average 7.37, standard deviation 2.28) than ecommerce information (average 3.18, standard deviation 2.85). The paired t-test for finance-ecommerce general information sensitivity was 23.52 (p value<0.001). This indicated manipulation success for the sensitivity of monetary information.

In multi-group analysis, testing measurement invariance is important to establish that items measuring constructs remain invariant across groups [17, 21, 102]. We examined for measurement invariance following four steps suggested by Muthén and Muthén [102]. First, we estimated and analyzed the measurement model separately. Fit indices for finance and ecommerce showed good fit (Finance: Chisq/df=1.68, CFI=.946, TLI=.939, RMSEA=.043, SRMR=.047; Ecommerce: Chi-sq/df=1.65, CFI=.950, TLI=.943, RMSEA=.042, SRMR=.046). Results of this analysis showed that there was a good fit for the two groups, since these statistics are within the accepted guidelines for MIS research [53]. Second, we estimated CFA using multi-group analysis, allowing all factor loadings to be freely estimated (unconstrained). Third, we estimated CFA using multi-group analysis by constraining factor loadings of all items for each construct to be equal across the groups (constrained). Finally, we tested the invariance by computing the difference in CFI of the constrained and unconstrained CFA estimations. Since the difference in CFI was less than the suggested threshold of 0.01 [22], the results supported measurement invariance.

The fit indices for the measurement models are reported in Table 3. The fit indices were within their respective thresholds, providing further support for model fit [64]. Model estimations were carried out using the multi-group structural equation modeling approach in Mplus (version 7.2) and the maximum likelihood estimation method with the robust standard errors (MLR) algorithm [101]—an algorithm that

All fit indices were within the accepted thresholds (Table 3). The estimation results of finance-ecommerce are reported in Figure 2 and in the first four columns of Table 4. Supporting H1(i), trust is positively associated with intention to disclose information. Table 4 shows that the relationship is significant in both finance and ecommerce contexts. The validation in the health context supports this finding. H1(ii) posited that the relationship between trust and intention to disclose is stronger in more sensitive contexts. This was not supported in the finance-ecommerce context. However, in the ecommerce-health validation, we observe that the path coefficient for the more sensitive context (health) is significantly higher than that for the less sensitive context (ecommerce). Both the t-test and chi-square confirm the context sensitivity as hypothesized in H1(ii) at p < 0.05 level for ecommerce-

health, however the context sensitivity was not supported for the finance-health comparison. The results suggest that in a more sensitive social-based health context as opposed to monetary (finance) or ecommerce contexts, trust plays a significantly more important role.

In H2, we posited that privacy concern is negatively associated with intention to disclose private information and that context sensitivity increases this negative association. The results in Table 4 show that the relationship between privacy concern and intention to disclose information is significantly negative in both finance and ecommerce contexts (at $\mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ . The validation in health context supports this relationship as well (at $\mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 $ ), thus providing a strong support for H2(i). The path coefficient privacy concernintention to disclose increases in magnitude as the nature of information changes from less sensitive (ecommerce) or sensitive monetary-based (finance) to sensitive social-based contexts (health). However, the difference was not large enough to be statistically significant. Thus, H2(ii) was not supported.

In H3, we hypothesized that privacy concern is negatively associated with trust and that context sensitivity increases the extent of this negative association. The path was significant in the finance context, but was insignificant in ecommerce. This analysis shows that context not only impacted the value of path coefficients (the intensity of the relationship), but also influenced the significance of the relationship (the structure of the relationship). In the sensitive context of finance, privacy concern negatively influenced trust, whereas in the ecommerce context, this relationship did not exist. Thus the results showed a structural difference in this path coefficient, indicating the significant context-dependency of this relationship. The comparison in the health context validated this result. The H3 path in Table 4 was statistically significant for finance, but not for health. Thus results showed a structural difference in the path coefficients of finance vis-à-vis ecommerce and health, thus providing a strong support for the moderating role of context moderation, indicating that privacy concern influenced trust only in sensitive monetary contexts.

In H4(a-e) and H5(a-e), we postulated the influence of personality traits on privacy concern and trust. In what follows, we discuss the influence of each trait on privacy concern and on trust. In H4a, we

postulated that extroversion is negatively associated with privacy concern and this negative association is higher in a less sensitive context. We found partial support for this hypothesis. Extroversion had a significant negative coefficient in ecommerce, but was not significant in finance. However, contextdependency was fully supported in that there was a structural difference in the coefficients of finance (not significant) and ecommerce (negative and significant). The validation supported this finding in that the coefficient was not significant in the health context either. Hence, the results indicated that extroversion negatively impacted privacy concern only in less sensitive contexts.

Continuing with the impact of extroversion, in H5a we posited that extroversion is positively associated with trust and the extent of this positive association is higher in a less sensitive context. The relationship H5a path was significant in both finance and ecommerce contexts, hence supporting the hypothesized positive impact of extroversion on trust. The relationship was significant at p<0.05 level in sensitive finance context, and at p<0.01 level in less-sensitive ecommerce context. However the difference in path coefficients was not large enough to be statistically different. In the validation process for H5a, we compared the finance and ecommerce contexts with the health context. This path coefficient was not significant in the health context in either of the two comparisons. The structural differences in the path coefficients provided support for the context-dependency of the impact of extroversion. Furthermore, it showed that in the case of socially-sensitive contexts (such as health), extroversion does not modify trust.

In H4b and H5b, we hypothesized about the impact of agreeableness on privacy concern (H4b) and on trust (H5b). For H4b, the results supported the positive association between agreeableness and privacy concern and showed that the path coefficient was significant in both finance and ecommerce. The test of difference between the path coefficients did not support context dependency. The validation using the health context also provided a full support for the impact of agreeableness on privacy concern, but did not support context dependency.

In H5b, the impact of agreeableness on trust in finance was marginally significant at p < 0.10 level, but the relationship was not significant in the ecommerce context. The validation with the health context

#

showed that agreeableness had a significant positive impact on trust (p < 0.01). Overall, the findings showed that the impact of agreeableness on trust was highly context dependent—it had marginal structural moderation in finance vs. ecommerce, no difference in finance vs. health, and had structural moderation in ecommerce vs. health. Overall the results showed that underlying socially-sensitive contexts such as health strengthen the impact of agreeableness on trust.

H4c and H5c posited that emotional instability positively influences privacy concern and negatively impacts trust, and more so in the more sensitive contexts. For H4c, emotional instability was positively associated with privacy concern in finance and ecommerce and was validated in the health context. Thus this path was strongly supported. The tests of context dependency had mixed results. The path had similar coefficient values in the finance and ecommerce contexts; hence this context dependency was not supported. Validating comparing finance vs. health, there was a large difference in coefficient values, but the test result was not significant. However, in the ecommerce vs. health contexts, there was a marginal context sensitivity at p<0.10.

The path from emotional instability to trust (H5c) was insignificant in both the finance and the ecommerce contexts as well as in the validation health context. Thus emotional instability had no significant impact on trust. Others have also found the relationship between emotional instability and affective commitment to be insignificant [38]. Since trust could also be considered an affective commitment [18], the validation also supported this lack of significance in the health context.

H4d and H5d posited that conscientiousness impacts privacy concern (H4d), and trust (H5d) and that such impact is heightened by context sensitivity. We found no support for the influence of conscientiousness on privacy concern or on trust in the finance, ecommerce, or health contexts. Conscientiousness did not influence privacy concern and trust in our data. Similarly, H4e and H5e hypothesized that intellect has a positive (negative) influence on privacy concern (trust) in sensitive contexts and a negative (positive) influence on privacy concern (trust) in less sensitive contexts. There was no support for H4e and H5e. The path coefficients of H4e and H5e were insignificant in the finance and ecommerce contexts. This finding was validated by a similar lack of significance in the health

context. Erdheim et al. [39] have also argued that openness to experience (intellect) should not be related to affective commitment. The lack of support for the influence of conscientiousness is in line with findings by Erdheim et al. [39].

In H6, we hypothesized that previous negative experiences resulting from online privacy invasions are positively associated with increased privacy concern (H6i), and that context sensitivity heightens the extent of such associations (H6ii). The results showed that these paths were statistically significant as hypothesized in both finance and ecommerce contexts, and this finding was validated in the health context. However, the path coefficients were not significantly different across the contexts, indicating that experience with privacy invasion increases privacy concern regardless of context.

In H7 we hypothesized that prior positive experiences are positively associated with trust and that context sensitivity lowers the extent of this relationship. Results affirmed the significant relationship between prior positive experiences and trust in all three contexts. However, there was not a significant difference in the path coefficients in the three contexts. Hence, context dependency was not supported.

In H8, we hypothesized that positive prior experience has positive association with intention to disclose (H8i) and that context sensitivity reduces this association (H8ii). The hypotheses was supported in both finance and ecommerce contexts, as well as in the validation using the health. The path coefficient was significantly higher for finance as compared to ecommerce. The test result showed strong support for context sensitivity— t-test at p <0.01 and by chi-square test at p<0.05. The validation results supported this finding. The difference was statistically significant in finance vs. health and was marginally significant in ecommerce vs. health. The results indicated the importance of first-hand knowledge in all contexts, especially in monetary-based sensitive contexts. However, the path coefficient for ecommerce was significantly higher than that in the health context, which indicates the prevalence of more first-hand knowledge in the ecommerce context than the health context.

## DISCUSSION

The broad objective of this study was to investigate the impact of context and personality on salient privacy-trust constructs when dealing with the disclosure of private information online. Based on the contextualization of TRA (which we called TRA-Privacy) and its synthesis with Prospect theory, we argued that personality traits should influence the way individuals balance between the utility gained by disclosing personal information online in order to receive needed service and the disutility of the adverse individuals’ privacy concern and trust formation. Our results indicated that a number of personality traits influence the formation of privacy concern and trust when disclosing private information online. Based on TRA-Privacy, its synthesis with Prospect theory and the assertion by Mayer et al. [91], we argued that context matters. Prospect theory holds that context, particularly when dealing with monetary issues, introduces biases and differences in individuals’ decisions. This argument was applied in the conceptualization of privacy- and trust-related relationships in multiple contexts. We investigated contextual moderation, in which context sensitivity influences construct relationships, thus moderating the effect of personality and individual experiences on privacy concerns and trust.

We compared two types of contexts: finance websites for high context sensitivity and ecommerce for low monetary context sensitivity. The assertion pertaining to the moderating role of context on privacy concerntrust was supported. There were distinct structural differences in the antecedents of trust. The importance of trust increased in the more sensitive financial context when compared to the less sensitive ecommerce context. In high context sensitivity, privacy concern plays an important role in trust formation, whereas in low context sensitivity, the structure of this antecedent changes and its role becomes insignificant. Furthermore, context played a significant moderating role in the path trustintention to disclose information online in socially-based sensitive health context as compared to ecommerce context. While supporting current trust theory, these results add new context and personality aspects to the theory.

## Personality Traits

We investigated the influences of personality traits on privacy concern and trust in three different contexts. Of the five personality traits, extroversion, agreeableness, and emotional instability were influential in forming either privacy concern, trust or both in the multi-group analysis of the three types of contexts we investigated. In what follows, we discuss evidence from the literature that provides reasons for the lack of support for the last two personality traits. We then provide an overall assessment regarding the influence of personality traits on privacy concern and trust when disclosing private information online.

Conscientiousness and Intellect did not have significant impacts on either privacy concern or trust. Our finding is in line with studies that show a lack of association between trust and conscientiousness. Sharif et al. [128] found no significant negative relationship either between conscientiousness and trust and or between emotional instability and trust. Similarly, the findings are consistent with the results of Zhou and Lu [150], who found no relationship between conscientiousness and trust in mobile commerce. McCrae and Costa [93] proposed that being open to experiences (high on intellect) leads to an increase in all emotions, both positive and negative. DeNeve and Cooper [30] argue that intellect should not relate to affective commitment because research has noted that it is a “double-edged sword that predisposes individuals to feel both the bad and good more intensely” (p. 199), and hence it is hard to understand its influence on trust and privacy concern. Similarly it has been discussed that “although the Big Five is widely researched, the fifth factor [intellect] is the least understood of the five factors” [30, p. 221]. Perhaps the lack of specificity about this factor in general is the reason for the lack of significance of paths from intellect to trust and privacy concern.

Putting our findings related to personality traits in a broad perspective, the lack of support for the influence of conscientiousness and intellect indicates that the personality traits that influence privacy concern and trust are the ones that involve social interactions. Privacy concern and trust arise in the context of social interactions with others, and as such they are social phenomena. As shown in Appendix A, conscientiousness was measured by items such as being prepared, paying attention to details, getting chores done right away, liking order and following schedules, all involving few direct social contents. Intellect was measured by being quick to understand, spending time for reflection, and being full of ideas. Again, these items contain little social content. We argue that trust and privacy concern are formed in interactions with others. Conscientiousness and intellect are traits related to individuals’ own activities and performances, and so pertain less to interactions with others. Our results indicate that traits might be categorized based on the extent of focus on social interactions with others as opposed to ones’ own

internal processes. Therefore, personality traits that have less direct social relevance (conscientiousness and intellect) show no significant influence on privacy concern and trust.

Extroversion and agreeableness, on the other hand, are traits that are by definition directly related to social interactions. Extroversion was measured by items such as being life of the party, enjoying being the center of attention, being comfortable around people, and easily starting conversations with them—all arising when an individual interacts with others. Agreeableness was measured by the extent of an individual’s sympathetic feelings for and behaviors with others. The items included sympathizing with others’ feelings, being soft-hearted, taking time for others, and feeling others’ emotions. Hence, agreeableness too has direct social contents. These two traits influence both privacy concern and trust, and the extent of their impact on privacy concern and/or trust is influenced by the sensitivity of the context.

Emotional instability has some social content. It was also measured with items such as getting stressed, irritated, disturbed, and upset. These emotions at times arise in social interactions. Our results showed that emotional instability influences privacy concern in varying degrees depending on the nature of the context. However, the influence of emotional instability on trust was not supported.

Hence, our findings differentiate between socially-oriented personality traits and non-social personality traits. Our results indicate that socially-oriented personality traits have significant influence on privacy concern and trust formation, and their influences vary depending on the context within which private personal information is disclosed online. Furthermore, our results show that socially-oriented personality traits could be related to positive extrinsic aspects of relationships such as trust, or to negative intrinsic aspects of relationships such as privacy concern. The impacts of personality traits on positive and negative aspects of social relationships vary depending on the extent of social focus in such personality traits.

## Context Sensitivity and Experience

We found that the influence of experience (direct experience with the website) on intention to disclose information depended on the context. While the role of direct experience on intention to transact with an online business has been well established [49], we found that individuals relied on their knowledge base in different ways. When the context was sensitive and monetary, direct experience with the website played a greater role in the intention to disclose information online.

## CONTRIBUTIONS

We begin this section by noting the limited generalizability of our results. All participants were college students living in the Midwest. Our investigation of context was limited to three types. All the items were self-reported, including the intention to disclose private information. While we recognize these limitations, which are common in a number of other trust studies (e.g, [52, 85, 94]). Our participants’ profiles correspond with the gender distribution of Internet users and represent the younger population of Internet users. Replication with older populations, other contexts, and actual behaviors is needed.

## Implications for Theory

A central part of the theory of trust is the role of context in influencing antecedents and consequents of trust [91], yet this central aspect is rarely examined. We contextualized TRA-Privacy and synthesized it with Prospect theory in order to investigate factors that influence customers’ decisions to disclose their private information. From a theoretical perspective, the study shows that contextualization provides valuable insights into the structure of trust, and is first to apply this approach in studying the moderating impact of context on relationships that involve privacy concern and trust in the online environment. As such, this study makes a novel theoretical contribution to privacy and trust investigation.

The findings in this study highlight the context-dependency of privacy-trust relationship. The finding that privacy concern influences trust only in sensitive monetary contexts indicates that the conceptual structure of trust is context-dependent. While some antecedents of trust may be universal (such as prior experience), others influence trust only in certain contexts. This has important implications for theory since it opens the door to the examination of the “where” component in trust theory—certain antecedents and consequents are local and significant only in certain contexts. Our findings suggest that context

sensitivity (defined in terms of financial sensitivity or socially-based health sensitivity) is one of the determinants of the universality and locality of trust antecedents.

Furthermore, even when an antecedent is universally significant in all contexts, its relative importance may change depending on context. These results open up a new avenue for the exploration of context features (sensitivity being just one example) that influence the strength of trust relationships. Such analysis by nature calls for context analysis, which is relatively novel in MIS research.

Another theoretical contribution of this work is the synthesis of TRA-Privacy with Prospect theory to identify salient personal attributes in trust and disclosure of sensitive private information. The conceptualized model could be used in other contexts in which business transactions require exchange of customers’ sensitive private information on a regular basis. The results suggest the need to include individual personality in studies of privacy concern and trust. The findings support the argument by Whetten [146] that “who” and “where” are major components of theory-building. The inclusion of these two components, through website context and individual personality, provides a rich insight into the model structure of the theory of trust.

This personality extension has potential importance to MIS because personality is a central theme in social psychology; and its inclusion in the MIS trust theory is an important avenue for future research. The lens of social psychology theories and their implications as they apply to personality could shed light on additional aspects of IT adoption and why failure rates continue to be as high as they are, thus moving personality traits into the mainstream of MIS research through the trust and TAM connection [52].

Moreover, in the TRA [5] and its successor, TPB [4], beliefs are a central determinant of behavior and as such have been incorporated into the much cited TAM as the ease of use and perceived usefulness constructs [29]. Such beliefs have been taken for granted by MIS research, assuming they relate to the technology itself as proposed by TAM. Although as Gefen and Straub [55] noted, gender also affects these beliefs, and as Gefen et al. [52] explained, so does trust, most MIS research has treated these beliefs as independent of users’ personality. This study, by adding personality to the theory of trust, suggests an interesting expansion of these theories. Recall that trust itself is a set of beliefs about the integrity, ability, and benevolence of another person or entity [52, 94]. If people do indeed form their beliefs based on circumstances as well as on their personality, this should not be ignored in TRA, TPB, or TAM.

Most research into trust in MIS has ignored the importance of personality, with the exception of [11]. The significance of extrovert and agreeable personality factors in this study is novel and important. Our work shows that extroverts and agreeable people are more trusting, but how trustful they are depends on the context. This is revealing not only because the idea of personality impacting trust is new, but also because it reveals a lot about Trust theory itself. To trust means to be willing to depend on another when that other can readily take advantage of the situation [91]. As the data tell us, people who are more comfortable interacting with others are more willing to take that risk and be trusting. This finding moves Trust theory in a new direction. The reason trust plays such an important role in all business interactions could be that it depends not only on what a trustee does that makes people set aside their concerns about being taken advantage of [52], but also on who the trustor is and the context in which the trust operates. In other words, trust is a social phenomenon, and the study of trust should be analyzed with salient attributes of three interacting components: trustor, trustee’s sociability and context—addressing the question: “who is trusting whom in what context?”

## Implications for Practice

This contribution has a practical side too. This study also shows that managers should expect variations in trust building outcomes depending on the context of their websites as well as on the personality traits of their web customers. Exploration of trust relationships within different context types makes trust research more relevant to managers. Our work suggests that managers of websites where customers are required to disclose sensitive private information may wish to consider investing more in trust creation tactics. Furthermore, managers need to be aware that positive direct experiences constitute a significant factor in disclosing information online in highly sensitive contexts and that privacy concern should be particularly managed in sensitive monetary contexts.

Managers are already aware that no one strategy fits all business circumstances. Systematic studies in differentiation between universal and contextual antecedents as well as the consequents of trust provide the knowledge that allows managers to develop trust-building strategies that fit the type and context of their online business. Awareness of the role of customers’ personality is also critical for managers of online businesses. A number of approaches could assist in inferring customers’ personality traits, including direct self-evaluation questions and inference based on the similarity of customers’ behaviors. Knowledge of customers’ personality assists in developing personalized webpages in which the interactions are designed specifically for dealing with a given personality type. Opportunities for social interactions on context sensitive websites could bolster trust in some customers, depending on their personality, while strategies to reduce information sensitivity (such as legal and emotional assurances) could counter personality traits that reflect other customers’ heightened personality-created anxieties.

## CONCLUSION

This research contextualized TRA and synthesized it with Prospect theory to develop TRA-Privacy theory and to address the “who” and “where” aspects of trust theory. Our results indicate that context and personality are salient components of trust formation and are critical in disclosing personal information online. This paper shows that personal characteristics—personality traits and salient experiences and concerns (who), and online context (where) are critical in disclosing personal information online. Extensions of this line of investigation could provide richer theoretical content and increased empirical relevance for studies of online privacy and trust. The incorporation of personality type into Trust and privacy theories and the dependency of its impact on context present trust in a new light—as a social phenomenon—and provide a potentially new and promising avenue in trust research.
