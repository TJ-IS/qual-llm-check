# An investigation of moderators of the link between technology use in the supply chain and supply chain performance

Leo R. Vijayasarathy \*

College of Business, Colorado State University, Fort Collins, CO 80523-1277, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 30 May 2007 Received in revised form 16 April 2010 Accepted 26 June 2010 Available online 20 September 2010

Keywords: Technology use in the supply chain Supply chain performance Process innovation Partnership quality Uncertainty

## A B S T R A C T

We studied the moderating effect of process innovation, partnership quality, and uncertainty on the relationship between technology use in a supply chain and their effect on supply chain performance. A Web-based survey was used to collect data empirically from 276 manufacturing organizations. An analysis of the data showed that the effect of technology use in the supply chain on its performance was moderated by process innovation, partnership quality, and competitive uncertainty. In addition, partnership quality was the only variable that had a direct effect on supply chain performance.

## 1. Introduction

The goal of supply chain management (SCM) is to facilitate the efficient and effective movement of products, services, finances, and/or information from a source to a consumer. SCM is not only essential for effective production, distribution, and logistical performance of today’s companies, but can also influence their strategic well being.

IT can play a significant role in supply chain operations. The research strategies for examining the relationship between SCM and IT range from surveys [e.g., 10], to case studies [e.g., 4], and simulations [e.g., 16]. A comparison of research models shows that some [e.g., 25], have looked for direct links between IT use in the supply chain and performance gains, while others have modeled the presence of mediators [24] and moderators [3,23] as qualifiers of the relationship between the two constructs.

There has also been a difference in the choice of research variables. The exogenous variable of interest, the technology used in the supply chain, has been seen as the use of generic IT systems [22], adoption of specific technologies such as electronic data interchange (EDI) and point of sale systems, overall information processing capability, and the amount of data exchanged. There have also been differences in the dependent variable (supply chain performance) including buyer–supplier cooperation and collaboration [28], cost and cycle-time reduction, better inventory control and improved customer service, and overall supplier network performance. Finally, the list of moderating and mediating variables has included level of integration, the initiator of the supply chain linkages [19], cross-country differences, industry clockspeed, type of systems used [2], category of products exchanged [21], information processing need, and the quality of the relationship among trading partners.

The diversity of the research models and the choice of constructs may partially explain why some studies that have investigated the presence of a direct relationship between IT use and supply chain performance have found support for the link [e.g., 1], while others have not [e.g., 12]. The impact of the organizational context, as emphasized by structuration theory and the institutional view of technology [15], could also explain some of these contradictory findings.

Empirical studies have found that the relationship between technology use in the supply chain and supply chain performance is typically moderated by factors such as relationship commitment, partner criticality, coordination, collaboration, and uncertainty [13]. In our study, we focused on process innovation (innovations such as cross docking that are intended to integrate and enhance exchange processes among supply chain participants), partnership quality (trust and commitment among supply chain partners), and uncertainty (unpredictability in price and supply of inputs and competitors’ actions). While process innovation is mentioned as a critical complement to technology use, its moderating effects have been little examined [6]. Similarly, only a few studies have investigated the influence of uncertainty on the relationship between technology use and supply chain performance, and partnership quality has been more often studied as a mediator or outcome factor, than as a moderator. We addressed these gaps in the literature by explicitly modeling and empirically analyzing the moderating effects of these three factors.

## 2. Prior empirical research

Our review focuses exclusively on empirical studies that have examined the relationship between technology use in the supply chain and supply chain performance.

## 2.1. Direct relationship

Empirical support for a direct relationship between technology use in the supply chain and supply chain performance was offered by Sanders and Premus [27], who reported that organizations that use IT more than the norm in their industry, achieved more operational benefits; such as reduced costs and cycle time. Similarly, Mukhopadhyay et al. [20] found that the use of EDI in supply chains benefited Chrysler Corporation by at least \$220 million in annual savings as a result of improved information exchange and reduction in operating cost. Dehning et al. examined the impact of IT-based SCM systems on the operational and financial performance of 123 manufacturing companies, and found a strong relationship between them. The results reported by Lai et al. [14] are more mixed; they found that the positive association between electronic integration and logistic performance was confined to cost reduction and did not extend to service improvements.

## 2.2. Mediators and moderators

A number of studies have identified geographical, environmental, intra-organizational and inter-organizational factors that mediate or moderate the relationship between technology use and SCM performance. The environment in which the supply chain operates can also be a factor, as discussed by Guimaraes et al. Their hypothesis, that the relationship between effective IT use and supplier network performance is moderated by industry clockspeed, was supported by data collected from 135 manufacturing organizations.

At the intra-organizational level, studies have examined the type of systems used and the fit between information processing needs and information processing capabilities. The proposition that certain types of IT systems are more relevant for SCM was tested by Narasimhan and Kim. They showed that production and process control, price management, customer service, customer management, inventory, and warehouse management systems had a positive effect on SCM performance, while IT systems used for logistical operations did not.

As use of IT in a supply chain usually involves several organizations, a number of studies have examined inter-organizational factors as mediators and moderators. For example, the status or role of the primary initiator of the supply chain was examined by Mukhopadhyay and Kekre, who showed that a supplier can derive significant strategic benefits if the company enhances the EDI linkages initiated by its customer. Another finding was that EDI linkages have to be integrated with internal business processes for both supplier and customer to achieve operational benefits.

Chae et al. conducted case analyses of five pairs of suppliers and retailers and discovered that existing relationships between the channel partners moderated the ability of IT to influence interorganizational collaboration. Similarly, Fawcett et al. [9] found that while information sharing capability positively influenced operational performance, the impact was stronger for those firms that reported higher levels of both connectivity and willingness to share information. This suggested that implementing IS for promoting connectivity among supply chain partners required a commitment to exchange information to realize better performance.

Research by Kent and Mentzer also investigated the existence of mediating variables between investments in IT and long-term supply chain relations. Their results indicated an association between IT investment and commitment and between the latter and logistics efficiency. However, a hypothesized link between logistical performance and long-term orientation was not supported.

The proposition that IT integration is a critical moderator has been empirically examined by a few studies including Chatfield and Yetton and Craighead and Laforge [5]. The former study showed that the extent to which EDI was embedded moderated the relationship between EDI use and derived strategic benefits, while the latter concluded that integration was critical for realizing supply chain improvements. Devaraj et al.’s [7] study, which also focused on integration, found that eBusiness technologies supported customer and supplier integration. Further, supplier integration had positive impacts on cost, quality, flexibility, and delivery performance, but there were no associations between customer integration and performance indicators.

Sanders [26] investigated the role of operational and strategic coordination as mediators of the relationship between IT use and supply chain performance. The study, which used data collected from 241 OEM suppliers in the computer industry, showed that the link between IT use for exploitation and operational benefits was mediated by operational coordination, while the association between IT use for exploration and strategic benefits was mediated by strategic coordination. Kim et al.’s study also explored the role of coordination as a mediator by gathering data through a survey of logistics managers. They concluded that the adoption of IT to support supply chain communication systems had a positive influence on both intra- and inter-organizational coordination, and that inter-organizational coordination had an effect on firm performance. Moreover, the criticality of the partner firm had a moderating influence on the relationship between intra-organizational coordination and firm performance.

Myhr and Spekman’s research showed that the relationship between electronically mediated exchange and collaboration was stronger for standardized products while the association between trust and collaboration was more significant for customized products.

## 3.1. Technology use in the supply chain

Over the last two decades computing and communications technologies have developed and merged to offer organizations a wide variety of tools for enabling and documenting transactions, supporting and enhancing decision making, and seamlessly automating and speeding up information sharing. By using proprietary systems (e.g., the SABRE Reservation System) and market-based systems (e.g., Covisint), organizations have learned to improve their inter-organizational relationships.

In our study, we were interested in the supply chain relationship between a focal organization and its supplier; we defined technology use as the extent to which specific information technologies (e.g., EDI, RFID) are used to manage the supply chain between an organization and its supplier. Therefore, our technology use in the supply chain construct was intended to represent the extent to which specific ITs were used to support the flow of goods, services, finances, and/or information between the respondent organization and its supplier.

## 3.2. Process innovation

SCM was seen as coordinating such business functions as forecasting, production, procurement, logistics, distribution, order processing, marketing, and sales. The successful coordination of these functions often requires the introduction of new processes and/or the reengineering of existing processes to facilitate the efficient and effective flows of products, information, etc. among supply chain partners.

## 3.3. Partnership quality

One of the key measures in our study was the quality of the relationship between the organizations. This is important because the way that partners perceive and interact with each other has been found to determine whether or not desired outcomes are achieved [11]. For our study, we decided to examine partnership quality as a reflection of trust and commitment, the two dimensions that have been identified as key antecedents to successful relationships [18].

## 3.4. Uncertainty

Organizations rarely operate in an environment that is placid, certain, and benevolent. Rather, they face varying levels of uncertainty with respect to the laws and economic policies of the countries in which they operate. Uncertainty is both an impetus for the formation of inter-organizational linkages and a determinant of inter-organizational performance. We included uncertainty in our study to assess its moderating influence on supply chain performance.

## 3.5. Performance

In our study, we focused specifically on operational effectiveness (the achievement of specific objectives) and efficiency (the means by which specific objectives were attained), which have been recognized as direct and observable goals of SCM practice. Organizations in a supply chain achieve operational efficiency and effectiveness by lowering costs, reducing inventory, promoting flexibility, ensuring on-time deliveries, and minimizing stock-outs. These objectives relate to both parties in a buyer–supplier dyad, and therefore, can determine the performance of the overall supply chain.

## 3.6. Hypotheses

The timely and accurate flow of data is a necessity for successful supply chain operations. Technology is intended to facilitate and enhance information exchange. Therefore, IT use in a supply chain can be expected to produce positive results.

H1. There is a positive association between technology use in the supply chain and supply chain performance.

A primary objective of our study was to determine whether technology use alone could deliver the desired SCM results or whether, its effect was influenced or moderated by relevant environmental and inter-organizational factors such as process innovation, partnership quality, and uncertainty. We therefore hypothesized:

H2. The relationship between technology use in the supply chain and supply chain performance is moderated by process innovation (i.e., the effects of technology use in the supply chain on supply chain performance will vary with different levels of process innovation).

The level of trust and commitment in a partnership is indicative of how the member organizations perceive each other in terms of reliability and integrity and their resolve to carry on a long-term relationship. If the partner organizations enjoy mutual trust and commitment, they are more likely to share sensitive information and less likely to engage in opportunistic behavior. Given the critical role of partnership quality, it can be expected to influence the effects of technology use in the supply chain on supply chain outcomes.

H3. The relationship between technology use in the supply chain and supply chain performance is moderated by partnership quality (i.e., the effects of technology use in the supply chain on supply chain performance will vary with different levels of partnership quality).

From a resource dependence perspective, organizations can be expected to form inter-organizational alliances to combat uncertainty. Further, the environment in which firms operate can have a direct bearing on the internal structures they adopt and the strategies they choose to pursue. Besides influencing an organization’s choice of internal structures, alliances and strategies, environmental uncertainty can also have an impact on SCM performance. Indeed, the relationship between IT use and supplier network performance is moderated by industry clockspeed (or uncertainty).

H4. The relationship between technology use in the supply chain and supply chain performance is moderated by uncertainty (i.e., the effects of technology use in the supply chain on supply chain performance will vary with different levels of uncertainty).

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Data collection procedures

Data were collected through an online survey of supply chain executives and managers. Contact information for the target respondents of the study was obtained from the membership list of an international organization of supply chain professionals. The organization, which was willing to share its membership list for academic research wished to remain anonymous; they provided the names, addresses, and e-mail addresses of 5000 randomly chosen members from the following industry sectors: manufacturing (4463), wholesale/retail (437), and construction (100).

An online questionnaire was developed for use as the survey instrument. It was pre-tested in an iterative manner among a convenience sample of 10 colleagues and supply/purchasing managers. These respondents were asked to provide comments on the relevance and wording of the questionnaire items, length and layout of the survey, and time taken to complete it. Based on this feedback, some of the items were dropped. Further, the questionnaire layout was modified, and the wording of some questions was changed to improve their clarity.

An e-mail soliciting participation in the study was sent to each of the 5000 target respondents. The e-mail explained the purpose of the study, offered assurances of anonymity and confidentiality, and included a link to the online survey. Respondents who wished to receive a summary of the study’s findings were asked to send a separate e-mail; thus ensuring that no identifying information was collected as part of the survey. As a reminder, a follow-up e-mail encouraging participation in the study was sent to all respondents 2 weeks after the initial mailing.

A total of 396 responses were received, but 21 had to be removed because they had missing data for more than 30% of the questions. Thus the overall response rate was 7.5%. Of the 5000 original e-mails, 860 were returned while 152 respondents stated that they could not participate. The most common causes for the returned e-mails were (a) not having permission to send to the recipient (e.g., rejected by spam blockers) and (b) invalid e-mail addresses (e.g., recipient name was not recognized or the e-mail account did not exist). Typical reasons for non-participation included time constraints, personal and company policy against survey participation, and job change. When the returned e-mails were removed from the original sample, the effective response rate was 9.1%, which is typical for electronic surveys. For our study, we chose to restrict the sample to the manufacturing sector and thus avoid having to control for industry differences. Therefore the data analyses included only 276 from manufacturing companies.

## 4.2. Non-response bias analysis

Non-response bias analysis was conducted by comparing the characteristics of early respondents and their responses with those of late respondents; we considered responses received prior to the e-mailing of the reminders as early and those received after as late. This resulted in 153 early respondents (55.4%) and 123 late respondents; t-tests were conducted to check for differences between the two sets of respondents on key attributes. As shown in Table 1, there were some differences between the two groups. It appears that the early respondents had more experience (16.5 years) than the late respondents (14.1 years). However, since the late respondents had a relatively high level of experience, we do not believe this discrepancy to be a real source of bias. However, it appeared that the respondent pool contained more small companies (in terms of annual revenues) than our non-respondent group did, and the results have to be evaluated with an assumption of some bias.

## 4.4. Measures

Each variable in the study was measured using multiple-item scales. Technology use in the supply chain and process innovation was measured by asking respondents to indicate the extent to which their organization used ten technologies and eight process innovations to support SCM with their selected suppliers. The items for these two scales were identified from relevant articles and trade publications. While some researchers have measured technology use as the extent to which IT, in general, is used in the supply chain, we decided to gauge this variable by asking respondents to indicate the extent to which specific technologies (e.g., EDI, RFID) were used to support supply chain operations. Partnership quality between the respondent organization and its selected supplier was assessed with six items primarily derived from Doney and Cannon [8] and Wilson and Vlosky [30], and perceived uncertainty was captured with six items adapted from Miller [17]. Finally, supply chain performance was assessed by asking respondents to rate the performance of the supply chain between their organization and the supplier relative to that of their top competitors. The seven items used for this measure were obtained from the Supply Chain Council’s Supply-Chain Operations Reference (SCOR) model [29]. A complete listing of all items is provided in Appendix A.

## 4.5. Data analyses and results

In order to assess convergent and discriminant validity, the items used to measure partnership quality, uncertainty, and supply chain performance were subjected to principal components analysis with varimax rotation. The Bartlett test of sphericity was significant (chi-square: 2509; significance: 0.00) and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy (index: 0.87) was satisfactory, confirming the appropriateness of proceeding with our analysis. A four-factor solution that cumulatively explained 64.1% of the variance with Eigen values greater than one resulted. As shown in Table 4, the six items for channel quality and the seven items for supply chain performance variables loaded on their respective factors. However, the six items for the uncertainty variable loaded onto two separate factors. Based on the items making up the two uncertainty factors, one was labeled product uncertainty – reflecting the uncertainty associated with product acquisition, price and demand – and the other competitive uncertainty – reflecting the uncertainty associated with remaining competitive in the industry.

Additional checks on the validity of the scales were conducted through reliability analysis, which lead to the dropping of one item for the supply chain performance factor (Perform 6) that had a low item-total correlation. Table 5 lists the research variables along with relevant descriptive statistics. Technology use in the supply chain and process innovation was assessed by asking respondents to indicate their company’s use of our list of technologies and innovations. Each of these were considered to be formative of the two constructs and assumed not to be correlated with each other since the extent to which they were used can be expected to differ within the same company. Therefore, neither factor nor reliability analyses was appropriate for them.

The hypotheses were tested by first classifying each respondent organization as being either low or high on each of the five independent/moderator variables (using the median-split approach), and then conducting an ANCOVA. We controlled for the variability in the importance of the chosen product and supplier by introducing the percentage of the total revenues accounted for by the chosen product and the percentage of the product (or its raw materials/parts) supplied by the chosen supplier as covariates in the ANCOVA model.

The results presented in Tables 6a and 6b show that there was no support for hypothesis H1. There was, however, support for hypotheses H2 and H3 as the two interaction effects between technology use in the supply chain and process innovation and technology use in the supply chain and partnership quality were significant. Further, the significant interaction effect between technology use in the supply chain and competitive uncertainty showed partial support for hypothesis H4. A graphical representation of the interaction effects is shown in Fig. 2a–c. In addition to the interaction effects, the main effect of partnership quality was also significant, suggesting that partnership quality had a direct effect on supply chain performance.

## 5. Discussion of results and implications

There were two important findings. First, the influence of technology use in the supply chain on supply chain performance was moderated by the environment, the quality of the channe relationship, and the implementation of process innovations. Second, the quality of the channel relationship was an important determinant of supply chain performance.

## 5.1. Process innovation

From the significant interaction effect between technology use in the supply chain and process innovation, technology use has to be coupled with investments in process improvements to ensure a gain in supply chain performance.

## 5.3. Uncertainty

The items used to measure uncertainty loaded on two separate factors. The results of the ANCOVA indicated that competitive uncertainty has a moderating influence on the relationship between technology use in the supply chain and supply chain performance. Specifically when there was a higher level of technology use in the supply chain, the performance was better if there was less uncertainty. Therefore, organizations that operate in environments in which their competitors’ actions are more predictable enjoy greater returns from the use of technology in the supply chain.

It appears that technology use in the supply chain does not have much impact if there is higher competitive uncertainty, and therefore, organizations operating in such environments cannot expect dramatic rewards from their use of technology. However, in more competitively stable environments, organizations are more likely to reap higher benefits from technology use in their supply chains.

## 5.4. Limitations and conclusion

As with most empirical field studies, our research has some shortcomings. First, in the interest of keeping our questionnaire at a reasonable length, we limited the list of technologies in SCM. Further, those we chose were not mutually exclusive. For example, the use of VPNs may be related to the extent to which the Internet is also used in the organization. Second, the data for the study was collected from a single respondent from each organization. However, based on the respondents’ titles and years of experience, it appeared that they were well qualified to answer the survey. Third, data was gathered from only one half of a buyer–supplier dyad.

Fourth, while a response rate of 9% may be the norm for online surveys, it is still lower than rates for traditional mail surveys. The non-response bias analysis appeared to indicate that our respondent pool may consist of more smaller companies (in terms of annual revenue) thanournon-respondent groupwithin our target sampleof 4463 manufacturing companies. Fifth, even though the factor loadings for the items that measured competitive uncertainty were high,thealphascoreforthisvariable(0.68)wasmarginallybelowthe recommended minimum of 0.70. Finally, as a cross-sectional field survey, the study does not capture the dynamics of technology use.

Our study used a reasonably large sample to examine the relationship between technology use in the supply chain and supply chain performance by introducing three relevant moderator variables. The results indicated that technology use in the supply chain was moderated by the corresponding implementation of process innovations and the quality of the relationships between manufacturers and their suppliers. This suggests that organizations should be aware of the need to make adequate and appropriate changes to supply chain processes and focus on improving their relationship with their suppliers if they hope to increase the benefits of technology use in the supply chain. Further, organizations that operate in an environment that is characterized by more competitive predictability will tend to reap more positive returns on their use of technology in the supply chain.
