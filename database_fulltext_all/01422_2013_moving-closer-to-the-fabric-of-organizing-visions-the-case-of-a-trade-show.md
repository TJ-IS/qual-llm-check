# Moving closer to the fabric of organizing visions: The case of a trade show

## a b s t r a c t

This paper examines the fabric of authorized discourses about Information Technology (IT), i.e. of ‘‘organizing visions’’ (OVs), through the investigation of the discourses, practices, and sociomaterial contexts that make up their micro-social underpinnings. The case of a trade show allows us to explore the production of everyday discourses and practices about IT by gathering many of the parties involved in the fabric of organizing visions. Through a combination of direct observations, interviews, pictures, documents and a survey, we identify and analyze a number of micro-social practices related to the fabric of IT discourses. The trade show we studied was not a context in which new IT buzzwords and concepts emerged, but, rather, it was a setting where existing discourses about IT were repeated, refreshed, and materialized. Our three main findings reveal intriguing relationships between practices, discourses, and sociomaterial contexts: (1) practices and artifacts contribute to enclose the production of discourses; (2) practices and discourses aim at refining and updating an existing discourse about IT; and (3) many actors in the trade shows engage in discourses and practices that materialize the IT artifact. This research adds to the conceptualization of the dynamics of OVs through a better understanding of how they are affected by the relationships between the discourses and practices of multiple actors and by the sociomaterial context in which they take place. It draws several strategic implications concerning the dynamics of OVs at the industry level, how actors influence the fabric of OVs, as well as the roles and combination of artifacts, discourses and practices to build OVs.

## 1. Introduction

A recent focus on practices has provided fascinating insights for management research, in particular in the Information Systems (IS) (Walsham, 1993; Desanctis and Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 1992, 2000) and strategic management disciplines (Jarzabkowski, 2003; Whittington, 2002, 2006; Regnér, 2008). A practice perspective helps researchers to understand more thoroughly the dynamics of technology in organizations (Orlikowski, 2000), the shaping of strategy through everyday practices (e.g. everyday discourses) (Whittington, 2006) and the dynamics of resources that sustain competitive advantage (Regnér, 2008).

Practices correspond to ‘‘the way we do things around here’’ (Whittington, 2002, p. 114) or ‘‘shared routines of behaviors including traditions, norms and procedures for thinking, acting, enduring things’’ (Whittington, 2006, p. 619). Practice-based views examine how actors involved in the organizational process “act and interact with the social and physical features of the context in the everyday activities that constitute practice’’ (Jarzabkowski, 2003, p. 23). Practice is also essential to the maintenance, reinforcement and/or transformation of social structures (Bourdieu, 1977). The practice perspective has been extremely helpful in shedding light on social aspects of Information Technology (IT) within organizations or a specific IT project (likely to involve external stakeholders), and, less frequently, between organizations (see Orlikowski, 1992, 2000; Walsham, 1993; Desanctis and Poole, 1994; Levina and Vaast, 2005; Sarker et al., 2006).

At the institutional level, Swanson and Ramiller’s (1997) organizing vision (OV) framework has been especially useful for understanding the diffusion and dynamics of authorized ideas about technologies between organizations (see e.g. Wang, 2001; Firth, 2001; Currie, 2004). Even though Swanson and Ramiller’s (1997) framework originally applied OVs to fads and fashions, the concept can be applied more generally to the discourses and practices that emerge due to the diffusion of technology. As in Wang (2010b), in this research we refer to IT OVs as ‘‘the authorized ideas about technology.’’ Empirical studies that have examined (IT) OVs (e.g. Wang, 2001; Firth, 2001; Gorgeon and Swanson, 2011) have so far adopted mostly ‘‘macro’’ perspectives. They have thus replicated the prevalent dichotomy between micro (focused on everyday practices using case studies, for example, see Styhre, 2010) and macro-social research (e.g. when targeting aggregate effects of these practices by identifying key buzzwords in the IT press)

The everyday, industry-wide practices that generate, support or transform OVs thus still need to be more fully examined (Currie, 2004; Carton et al., 2007). In other words, we have yet to unearth the micro-social underpinnings of OVs and the everyday discourses and practices that sustain them. Part of what accounts for this gap in the literature is methodological; it is difficult to grasp the practices taking place throughout entire industries. Such an elusive research object involves a large number of stakeholders (e.g. IT vendors, IT managers, CIOs, IT journalists and consultants) and a wide variety of practices (e.g. communication practices of IT vendors, adoption practices of firms, IT journalists’ analysis practices, management practices of CIOs and IT practitioners). Thus, despite their significance for theory and practice, the micro-social underpinnings of IT OVs have yet to be explored.

Understanding better the micro-social underpinnings of IT OVs holds strategic implications. IT adoption and the arguments that justify it can leverage the legitimacy of an organization in its industry and can also affect that organization’s reputation. Legitimacy and reputation are themselves strategic assets (Oliver, 1991; King and Whetten, 2008; Regnér, 2008; Nevo and Wade, 2010); yet, following authorized ideas too blindly might also damage competitive advantage.

In this paper, as we undertake an exploration of the micro-social dynamics of IT OVs, we focus our attention on a context in which the everyday discourses that build or sustain OVs seem to be especially visible and accessible: an IT trade show. We examine the following research question: How are OVs produced, reproduced or refined through everyday (material and discursive) practices in trade shows? In particular, this research investigates how practices and discourses taking place in the sociomaterial context of a trade show are involved in the fabric of authorized ideas about IT.

The word ‘‘fabric’’ comes from the Latin term ‘‘faber’’ meaning workman or artificer. It represents an area possessing few mechanical tools, but the know-how and processes sufficient to shape raw materials into a relatively simple manufactured product (Golsorkhi, 2006; Rouleau, 2006; Johnson et al., 2007). Trade shows are privileged places where salesmen exchange their explicit or tacit know-how and promote their businesses; therefore, IT trade shows can be viewed as a fabric of ideas and practices that transform reality and strongly incite companies to adopt and implement new IT. The fabric metaphor, borrowed from practice-based studies of strategic management (Golsorkhi, 2006; Johnson et al., 2007), not only highlights the local roots of a broader social phenomenon, but also underlines the professional sub-cultures of the various stakeholders involved in trade shows.

This paper is structured as follows. We first review the OV framework and argue for the importance of examining the discourses and practices produced in the context of a trade show to further the understanding of the dynamics of OVs. We detail our research setting (an IT industrial trade show that took place in France) and methods. We then present our main empirical findings. In particular, discourses and practices in the investigated trade show were oriented toward refining pre-established technical concepts and existing buzzwords about the technology. In addition, we highlight the importance of the sociomaterial context of the tradeshow in the development of practices and discourses about IT. Our findings extend the OV framework by highlighting: the practices and artifacts involved in enclosing a discourse about IT in a tradeshow; the importance of the refinement of existing discourse and OVs; and the importance of materializing discourse and technology in a trade show. We conclude by summarizing the paper and acknowledging its limitations. We also identify promising areas of future research and articulate the strategic implications of trade shows as an essential locus for the shaping and reshaping of IT OVs.

## 2. The organizing vision framework

In this section we first present key ideas associated with the OV framework (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997; Ramiller and Swanson, 2004; Wang and Ramiller, 2009). We then argue for the importance of further examining several important aspects of this framework. In particular, we contend that examining micro-social underpinnings of OVs can help us understand better the roles and relationships between discourse, practices, and artifacts in the dynamics of OVs.

## 2.1. Building and sustaining shared ideas around IT

Swanson and Ramiller’s concept of OV illuminates the question of the emergence and diffusion of shared ideas about technologies. They propose a framework that encompasses the stakeholders of the ‘‘social structure’’ of an innovation as collectively involved in developing and maintaining ‘‘interpretive–discursive activities’’ at the source of OVs. The concept of OV, i.e. ‘‘a focal community idea for the application of information technology in organizations’’ (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997, p. 460), provides some indication of the degree to which the implementation of particular technologies is socially constructed, as well as of the conditions necessary for gaining added value from these technologies, and of the organizational changes that they may bring about (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997).

## 2.2. Multiple stakeholders jointly build OVs

The concept of OV brings to light a number of processes that underlie decisions to adopt specific technologies, as well as the actual implementation of these technologies in organizations. OVs change over time due to the everyday discourses of a broad inter-organizational community that is made up of among others CIOs IT managers IT vendors, and journalists (Ramiller and Swanson, 2003). As such, OVs tend to be abstract representations of IT, and can be seen to represent what IT is, what it is meant for and the potential changes that it can bring about. In turn, as these changes are often unstable, they must be illustrated and considered with respect to particular customers and situations. Thus, OVs must be constantly fleshed out and reinvented, which explains our interest in the ‘‘fabric’’ of OVs and in how the discourse and practices of multiple actors lead to the conception and evolution of OVs.

Swanson and Ramiller (1997) suggest that OVs become more sophisticated through the multiplicity of discourses that take place in different bazaars and forums. ‘‘Bazaars’’ (i.e. ‘‘physical places in which the OV is given elaboration and exposure’’ (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997, p. 461)) are places where technology can be seen and bought. A trade show, such as the one we present here, is a key example of a bazaar: trade shows are contexts in which such processes are especially likely to be made visible. Other examples include conferences, private events for club members and workshops. In addition to bazaars, ‘‘forums’’ are places where ideas are shared and where the dynamics of the OVs that employ virtual media are fed, e.g. IT newspapers, websites, social bookmarking and virtual trade shows. From this perspective, ‘‘forums for carrying out the discourse expand or contract according to the community’s need for them’’ (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997, p. 463); in other words, they participate in the evolution of the OV’s content.

To represent OV framework, Swanson and Ramiller have proposed a multilayered analysis, from ‘‘practical activities’’ to ‘‘cultural resources’’ (see Fig. 1).

Cultural-linguistic resources constitute the first layer of the framework. These resources are dependent on the specific knowledge and characteristics of various adopting companies in terms of their linguistic resources and culture. Culture can be influenced in different ways: national, types of crafts and professions. Cultural-linguistic resources are produced and reproduced through everyday discourses and everyday practices (described by both layers 2 and 4 of Fig. 1).

The second and third layers, ‘‘interpretive discursive activities’’ and ‘‘social structure’’, describe both the specific set of practices involved in the use of OVs (interpretive discursive activities) and the social structure that is at the core of the broad inter-organizational community involved in the production and reproduction of OVs. Both lavers appear to be direct refer: ences to structuration theory (Giddens, 1979, 1984); however, the OV framework does not provide the OV community with content. In particular, the identities of the different stakeholders associated with OVs, as well as their relationships with one another, remain implicit. Moreover, due to its being relatively ‘‘high level,’’ this model does not situate OVs in specific temporal, social or spatial contexts.

Finally, the fourth layer ‘‘practical activities and objects’’ is not fully defined nor positioned with regards to interpretivediscursive activities at layer 2. This is partly because layer 4 is implicitly mostly about practices of IT adoption, implementation, and use rather than about the dynamics of the OV itself. However, a practice perspective suggests that practices and artifacts are actually involved in the emergence and refinement of the OV and not only in the use of the technology. We therefore see this as an opportunity to develop more empirically grounded investigations of the practice- and discoursebased dynamics of OVs.

All in all, then, the OV framework is very helpful in conceptualizing the dynamics of IT buzzwords, as it can focus on the discursive nature of authorized ideas between organizations as well as help one to understand the conceptual links between IT adoption and diffusion. This framework encompasses all stakeholders, provides concepts for following the process of adoption and diffusion of authorized ideas on IT and finally, highlights the importance of ‘‘discursive activities’’ and ‘‘objects’’ for OVs. This framework, however, has not yet much examined the social underpinnings that articulate the discourses, practices and artefacts that create and sustain OVs.

## 2.3. The fabric of OV, grounded in discourses, practices, and sociomaterial contexts

Despite the theoretical breadth of the model and convincing calls from prominent researchers in the field to further examine and refine it (see Swanson and Ramiller, 1997 as well as Currie, 2004; Carton et al., 2007; Ramiller et al., 2008 or Wang, 2010a), existing empirical research on OVs has not yet fully tackled the operationalization of the different layers of the model and relationships between these layers. Research on OVs has identified and analyzed the different layers and explored their drivers and the modalities of their emergence as well (Currie, 2004; Ramiller et al., 2008). Nonetheless, the relationships between practical activities (including material artifacts) and discourses (two key layers of the OV framework) remain to be examined. One way to examine the articulation between these two layers is to mobilize a sociomaterial perspective.

Thus the sociomaterial dimension of discourses about IT is still under-explored in the MIS literature (see in particular Välikangas and Sevon, 2010 or Orlikowski, 2007), as well as in the broader literature of social science (Austin, 1962; Foucault, 1974; Muniesa and Callon, 2009). In addition, the processes of diffusion of IT and of ideas about IT (the OVs) are often considered separately, rather than jointly. Research has mostly focused on IT adoption and implementation or on IT discourses but there is less work that has attempted to articulate these processes. As a consequence, the importance of the sociomateriality in the fabric of OVs has remained under-examined and there is a need for a better conceptual understanding of the connections between sociomaterial contexts, discourses, and practices in the dynamics of OVs.

To contribute to this understanding, we examined a trade show. Indeed, trade shows can be viewed as microcosms in which multiple members of the community involved in IT are present (layer 3). During trade shows, discourses are produced and reproduced (layer 2) and objects and practices related to the technology are on display and used (level 4). Trade shows are thus places where multiple written or spoken discourses about IT become visible. Moreover, many of the practices and artefacts devoted to the communication and demonstrations of IT are situated in a single context. Trade shows are thus settings in which discourses and practices related to IT are highly visible and may become ‘‘embodied’’ sociomaterially. The material underpinnings of discourses about IT can be grasped through the analysis of marketing brochures, forms and colors of the stalls, and the investigation of informal discussion between clients and vendors (e.g. Fig. 1). The study of trade shows and the discourses that they produce can therefore help us develop a better understanding of OVs’ fabrics, i.e. their microsocial and material underpinnings. In what follows, we present how we undertook this unpacking by detailing our methods and findings.

## 3. Methods

We relied on the case of the INDTS trade show (a pseudonym) to examine the content and context of discourses and practices that constitute the fabric of OVs. We first present some information about our research setting. We then detail our data collection and analysis process that helped us to clarify the context of visitors’ practices and to shed light on actual trade show practices.

## 3.1. Case setting

INDTS, a trade show organized alternately in Lyon and Paris. France, targets small and medium-sized industrial firms from the mechanics and robotics industry. It is composed of three sub-sections: machine tools, robotics and IT. In March 2009, we visited the INDTS organized in Lyon. The official obiectives of INDTS are to provide information to visitors and to facilitate connections between IT vendors and clients rather than to encourage business deals within the trade show context itself. We focused our attention on how the INDTS trade show spotlights IT innovations and brings together a multitude of actors from the IT in the mechanics and robotics industries. To do so, we especially examined the space inside INDTS dedicated to IT companies and to mechanical companies with IT offers. Most visitors worked for small- and medium-sized industrial firms and were looking for Computer Mediated Tools for modeling and designing parts.

This context was opportune given our interest in the micro-social underpinnings of OVs. In this physically bounded space, it was possible to access an OV ‘‘community,’’ to examine many of the actors that made up the community (e.g. clients, IT vendors, consultants, trade journalists and events organizers), and to observe how they acted and reacted toward each other. Investigating INDTS therefore enabled us to compare the discourses and practices of these actors, including those of the visitors, trade show organizers, companies that have invested in different stalls to present their IT products and services and other corporate actors.

## 3.2. Data collection

Consistent with the interpretive stance of the OV framework (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997), we relied on an in-depth case study approach consisting of an investigation of actors’ interpretations of IT-related discourses. We aimed at capturing key micro-social practices that were involved in the fabric of OVs in the context of a trade show. To do so, we relied on a multimethod approach to build our case narrative; we made use of observations (systematically recorded in observation checklists), semi-structured interviews, questionnaires, documents and photos. We relied on these diverse sources of data in order to optimize our access to the many stakeholders that are involved in the OV building process. We see this approach as coherent with the OV framework and the status that we have given to that framework (e.g. more a theoretical perspective than a model awaiting operationalization). We collected both secondary and primary data. A team of four researchers collected an extensive set of data that included interviews, written media, photographs, attendance at conferences, and informal conversations in stalls. The variety of these data sources helped us gain an overall understanding of everyday discourses related to IT buzzwords.

At INDTS, we met exhibitors (IT vendors and consultants) and visitors (in particular IT customers, students and consultants) and characterized their profiles and their ‘‘[relationships] to external information’’ by means of interviews (for IT vendors), a survey (for IT customers) and observations (also including journalists and politicians) (see Table 1). Qualitative data was collected during the trade show, whereas quantitative data (an on-line survey dedicated to IT customers, see table below) was collected 1 month after the trade show. We thus did our best to cover the interpretations and discourses of most OV stakeholders involved in the trade show through our data collection process.

## 3.2.1. Qualitative data

We conducted 15 semi-structured interviews during the INDTS, in the stands with exhibitors (14) and with the organizer ‘‘INDORG’’ (1) (see Appendix A).

Interviews were semi-directed and covered motivation for participating in the trade show, evaluation of the trade show (benefits and limits for exhibitors), IT selling processes, organization and business arguments in the stands, and the appearance of authorized ideas among IT products and services. Approximately 40 exhibitors were dedicated to IT products. We interviewed the biggest ones, exhibitors who had the more original communication or stalls, and also according to other criteria such as the size of the stall, the size of the exhibitor, the type of activity, the availability of the interviewees. All in all, we interviewed 15 exhibitors (37.5%) and stopped adding new interviews once we reached saturation on the categories of our thematic analysis (see Appendix C).

We also collected secondary discourse data that provided us with discourses from many different actors: leaflets, IT journals, IT reviews, gadgets, even DVDs distributed about some conferences (see Appendix B).

## 3.2.2. Observations and observation checklist

We took extensive notes from our observations as participants in INDTS. During each of the 4 days of the trade show, two researchers observed interactions, attended sessions, and transcribed discourses. The field researchers collected leaflets from the stands and took magazines and publications offered at the trade show entrance, as well as DVDs and brochures (see Appendix B). We also took 45 pictures of stands, participants and event promotion over the course of the trade show.

## 3.2.3. Questionnaire

Quantitative data was obtained via 400 questionnaires we distributed 1 month after INDTS to IT customers using a mailing list provided by INDORG. The survey was distributed by email to the 400 companies that had participated in the IT area of the trade show. Survey questions, elaborated in continuation to our interviews, aimed mainly at contextualizing customers visits. We attempted to understand participants’ IT purchase processes and to determine if visiting companies felt that IT fashions existed in their field, and that if so, at which stage of the IT purchase process they occurred. We also asked about information gathering, in particular which media and actors were used to scan the IT environment. The latter set of questions (and media or actors it included) was mainly based on the OV literature. Modalities describing the profile of respondents, their activities and purchase processes were derived mainly from our interviews (as these aspects were discussed by exhibitors) and observations. These results helped us to put a number of direct observations or discussions that we had with customers during the trade show into perspective. We received more than 140 responses (response rate of thoroughly 35%) of which 70 were fully exploitable (see Appendix D). We compared the demographics of our respondents with the population of companies visiting INDTS, and this comparison revealed that our sample was fairly representative of the customers visiting the section of INDTS devoted to IT.

## 3.3. Data analysis

As recommended by Mingers (2001), we adopted a ‘‘pluralist’’ method by combining qualitative (thematic coding and lexicometric analysis) with quantitative (descriptive and cluster analysis) treatments. We believed that such multi-method approach was especially well suited both to our theoretical interest on the fabric of OVs and to the diverse set of data that we had collected and that required different analytical instruments.

## 3.3.1. Qualitative data analysis

Each interview transcript was analyzed by means of thematic coding (Miles and Huberman, 1994) summarized in Appendix C. This codification highlights six main categories (interviewee, industry, firm, product, trade show and stand) and several sub-categories to describe each phenomenon more precisely. Two researchers double-coded the data to validate the categories. We also used a lexicometric analysis to identify buzzwords (often embodying an OV, see Swanson and Ramiller, 1997) in our interviews. This technique describes quantitatively textual sequences of a corpus (Guilhaumou, 1986; Bolden and Moscarola, 2000). It relies mainly on counting occurrences or co-occurrences of words used in a text (without adverbs or prepositions). For example, here below, are presented IT objects found in interviews with the number of occurrences in brackets: ‘‘CAD (27), CAM (18), software (12), MPM (11), 3D (9), ERP (5), interface (3)’’. Pivot analysis was also used to see how IT objects were used in discourses (words just before or after IT objects).

Moreover, written notes from participant observations were condensed and treated by means of a grid (a checklist of observations, see Appendix F) completed at the end of each day (days 1–4) by all researchers attending the trade show. Three or four researchers had observed the trade show each day, thus allowing us to compare observations and grids on a daily basis. Observed items mostly concerned typical behaviors and dress codes of stand visitors (particularly customers), typical itineraries, the most frequently asked questions we heard, the most frequent answers, buzzwords we heard, most frequented areas, the way documents and objects were used for promotion, and any other behaviors significant for our research or the event we were attending. With regards to typical itineraries, we followed a dozen people from their entrance in the trade show to their departure. Our observations of the most frequented areas were mainly based on our cross observations. We did our best to keep moving (physically) throughout the trade show. Our list of frequently asked questions was the result of our observations at stands (the issue was also covered in our interviews).

## 3.3.2. Quantitative data analysis: descriptive and taxonomic treatments

The general aim of our quantitative analyses was to clarify the diverse profiles of customers visiting INDTS and to shed light on the organizational contexts of their IT adoption practices (see Fig. 2). We wanted to elaborate a taxonomy of visitors’ IT adoption practices, particularly with regard to external information. To do so, we used our survey responses to develop a cluster analysis based on OVERALS (Valette-Florence, 1998) and canonical treatments. The OVERALS algorithm was first described by Gifi (1981), and Van Der Burg et al. (1988). Similar to multiple regression and canonical correlation analysis,

OVERALS focuses on the relationships between sets. Any particular variable contributes to the results only inasmuch as it provides information that is independent of the other variables in the same set. Thus, OVERALS requires original data to be structured into sets of variables (see Appendix E.2), and the results of OVERALS are the identification of dimensions, based on coordinates of modalities (see Appendices E.3 and E.4). Moreover, it enables cluster analysis on initial variables that are heterogeneous and numerous. We used cluster analysis (see the results in Appendix E.5) to search for structuring elements in our survey responses, in particular with regards to IT purchases and relationships with external information (see Appendix E for further details). We wanted to know if there were typical prospective behaviors among the companies represented at the trade show as this could help us understand better the industry and the dynamics of reception of OVs.

Coordinates of modalities on the dimensions resulted from the data structuration through OVERALS, and were then used as input data for the cluster analysis (Kmeans type).

In short, our research combined both quantitative and qualitative techniques. Our quantitative techniques helped us to grasp the context of INDTS, industry dynamics and the average profile of visiting companies. It covered elements related to layer 1 of the OV framework (see Fig. 1). Our qualitative techniques helped us to shed light on everyday discourses and their relationships with material artifacts, and were therefore precious in understanding the relationship between layers 2 and 4 of the OV framework.

## 4. Discourses and practices at INDTS

During INDTS, mostly already established OVs were expressed through the discourses of the gathering community. We did not see much evidence of emerging buzzwords and technologies, but rather, more complex and refreshed versions of pre-existing discourses about technologies (in particular CAD and CAM technologies). In addition, we observed the presence of unexpected stakeholders in the fabric of OVs and the importance of materiality in the reproduction or refinement of discourses about IT. Numerous artifacts (even IT itself) contributed to making the process more or less locally effective in the discourses of inter-organizational stakeholders.

## 4.1. Contextualizing the demand-side of the trade show

The survey enabled us to characterize IT customer profiles and practices in terms of company size, job profiles, and customer behavior with regard to discourses about IT (in particular OVs) and more generally, with regard to external information. We interpreted the results based on cluster analysis, observation grids and the interviews conducted during the trade show (as recommended in Ansari et al. (2010)).

With regard to the overall profile of INDTS visitors, most of our respondents were young (83% respondents were less than 44 years old). The majority of them had technical backgrounds (in mechanics and electronics) both in terms of education and work experience. They worked within a technical and industrial culture, and half of them (52.1%) were newcomers to INDTS and were mainly there to meet suppliers (52.4%), to observe key technological trends (21.4%) or to search for precise information about a product (21.4%).

More than half of our respondents worked in small and medium sized firms (SMF) with fewer than 50 employees (58.2%). Due to the small size of their organizations, 43% of our sampled respondents did not have a dedicated IT department. When there was an IT department, 42.9% had fewer than 3 employees, 33.9% between 3 and 10 employees, 14.3% between 11 and 50 employees and only 8.9% more than 50 employees.

For 51.1% of the sample, IT purchases were decided by the Chief Executive Officer, only 33% by the Chief Information Officer, usually there was no specific and autonomous IT budget in the company. ## 4.2. A taxonomy of clients

The cluster analysis revealed three groups of clients that were variously receptive to external information about IT and that exhibited different adoption practices. Each group can be described by analyzing the meanings of the different modalities associated with each group, as the cluster analysis puts each modality of the questionnaire in one group (see Appendix E.5.3).

The first group of respondents considered that they made decisions purely on the basis of their companies’ needs and without taking into account external information (modalities ‘‘Not agree at all’’ and ‘‘Not agree’’ for the variable Followfashion). They thought that their companies were not influenced by external environment such as forums, trade shows, consultants as well as suppliers (modalities ‘‘Not agree at all’’ and ‘‘Not agree’’ for the variables Influenceforum, InfluenceTS, Influenceconsultant, and Influencesupplier).

The second group of respondents acknowledged the existence of fashions in IT investments (modality ‘‘Absolutely agree’’ for the variable Followfashion). However, they claimed not to be influenced by the external environment, particularly by web and service provider (modality ‘‘Not agree at all’’ for the variables Influenceweb and Influenceserviceprovider).

By contrast, the third group of respondents was characterized by its recognized openness to external discourse on technology. They acknowledged that they sought out information on IT on a regular basis from various media: e.g. publications, forums, mailing lists (presence of the modalities ‘‘Yes every week’’ for the following variables ISdeptmagazinesinfo, ISdeptforums, ISdeptmailinglist). These companies considered that their external environment – notably their customers, suppliers, consultants, tradeshows, and forums – had a strong impact on their IT investment portfolio (modalities ‘‘Absolutely agree’’ and ‘‘Agree’’ for the following variables: Influenceforum, InfluenceTS, Influencemagazines, Influencesupplier, Influencecustomers). Moreover, managers acknowledged being influenced by IT fashions (modalities ‘‘Agree’’ and ‘‘Relatively agree’’ for the variable Followfashion). In these companies, formal strategic intelligence activities were often implemented (variable techno logyscanning), whether by IT personnel or by a dedicated department.

In short, the descriptive and cluster-based analysis of our survey findings gave us some information regarding the industrial context of INDTS. Visitors came mainly from small and medium-sized firms, and had very concrete expectations about ITs. We believe that these expectations shaped by the crisis that the machine tool industry was experiencing in France at the time. Moreover, most visitors came to INDTS to search for information in order to convince their CEO/CIO about the need for IT purchases. As discovered in the group suggested by our cluster analysis, visitors were quite skeptical about IT products and IT fashions (see groups 1 and 2); they came to INDTS both to get precise information and to socialize with IT salesmen.

## 4.3. Shaping OVs during the trade show

On the supply side of the community, we consider both the role of INDORG, the organizer of the trade show, and the role of the exhibitors.

## 4.3.1. INDORG’s key role in the elaboration of a sociomaterial setting

INDTS took place in March 2009, a period that was characterized by reduced participation in industry trade shows and a global economic crisis for industrial activities. Trade publications distributed at the trade show largely publicized this recessionary context.

While most of the physical space in which the trade show took place was used by mechanical and machine tool oriented companies, about 20% of the space was dedicated to IT companies. The space dedicated to IT attracted exhibitors from rather small-sized companies from both the IT and mechanical sectors. The pathways within the IT area at INDTS were relatively narrow; therefore, people often went to the bar, located at the entrance of INDTS, to socialize. Certain larger stalls, directly equipped with bars of their own, incentivized visitors by offering them alcohol and appetizers.

Exhibitors mentioned that for the past 5 years, visitors had not come to INDTS with precise objectives in mind, but rather, in order to meet IT vendors and to get general information on mechanical and electronic tool innovations. We observed very few actual buying negotiations. Rather, INDTS facilitated encounters among various actors. During an interview with the IN-DORG Chair, we noticed that he did his best to position INDTS as the central place and event in time where discourses were constructed, ‘‘the place and time where things happen.’

The 4 days of the actual trade show event were but one step in the life cycle and activities of the trade-show organization. The INDORG Chair explained: ‘‘Trade shows are now a marketing package where the event itself is only the visible part of the iceberg. We are accompanying our customers before and after the event via an Internet platform, other events at other tradeshows, business meetings, conferences, industrial talks. IT is commanding a growing part in trade show strategies.’

In preparation for the trade show, INDORG sent exhibitors documentation to encourage them to prepare for and communicate about the event. They also sent promotional information to potential visitors, companies and public figures, and communicated in trade publications.

During the trade show, INDORG tried to maximize the possibilities for various actors to meet, and to this end, created opportunities for interaction and exchanges, including lunches with public figures and conferences held by mechanical companies or consultants. After the trade show, INDORG continued to broadcast information through their website and sent satisfaction surveys to all visitors. INDORG engaged in fierce competition with other trade shows and media, in particular with the trade publications that promoted discourse on IT.

## 4.3.2. Artifacts, discourses and practices at the trade show

Covers of trade publications and exhibitors’ leaflets gave off a production line atmosphere, consistent with the industry covered in INDTS. See, for example, the two following trade publication covers (see Fig. 4)

Exhibitors and customers discussed functionality, global offers and prices, but also technical problems that actors faced in their operations. Most importantly, industrial activities, in their material dimensions, were often part of sale strategies. As an exhibitor stated, for instance: ‘‘You can learn mechanics, but the culture of our job, you can’t learn it in three minutes. Our customers belong to the mechanical industry. To sell IT software, you need to know their work, to talk their language. When we do a demo, we don’t ask our customer: ‘How do you make this part?’. We do not hesitate to go inside the workshop, to touch machine tools. . . you see, it is more or less a horse dealer’s job!’’ (Exhibitor [5] in Appendix A, GP, p. 4).

Moreover, at times the different parties concretely illustrated the potential added value of the software by live testing it during the trade show. We observed several customers who visited INDTS with a spare part they wanted modeled by specific software.

Finally, we also met with a popular software vendor whose booth was not located in the IT area, but in the tool machine area of the INDTS. The president of the company explained that the combination of machines and IT was at the core of his business model. His company jointly offered machines (exhibited at INDTS) and software (mainly ERP for SMF). He told us that his experience in the design and production of machines brought a great deal of legitimacy to his IT activities and that he presented his software in the tool machine area on purpose

Among notable non-IT artifacts observed during the trade show were robots used in demonstrations (see Picture 1), software visualizations (using big LCD screens and microphones, see Picture 2), as well as the attractive setting of stands, activities (e.g. CADCAM demos and contests) and the flow of food and beverages aimed at enticing visitors.

Demos of IT products entered into the specificity of their customers’operations. IT vendors used very technical vocabulary. Videos displayed on exhibitors’ LCD screens showed IT-supported machine tools involved in, for instance, turning, milling or spinning activities. As an exhibitor said: ‘‘This demo is about simultaneous milling. First, we see multiple task machine tools, then, after that, we see the B axis milling, which turns with rotating tools. As you’ll see, it is really impressive.’’ (Exhibitor [7] in Appendix A, FR, p. 2).

In regards to the key IT buzzwords we observed (identified by our lexicometric analysis of interviews), we found mainly specific applications, in particular CAD (27 occurrences), CAM (18 occurrences), software (12 occurrences), and MPM (11 occurrences). The field covered by IT buzzwords was heavily production-related and fairly standard for the industry rather than new and emerging. Moreover, our own observations of the IT area did not result in the identification of emerging innovations.

In regards to the broader material setting, INDTS was very noisy, combining the background hum of machines, large LCD screens, and the tirades of exhibitors in their stands. The loudness of this environment was in fact familiar to INDTS visitors who came from equally blaring work environments. Some exhibitors used noise as a means to attract visitors and make them feel like they were in their work environment. Some IT vendors notably came to the trade show to broadcast widely their business slogans: for instance Exhibitor [7] proposed a contest in which the person who visited INDTS and yelled the catchphrase ‘‘’[Name of the product of the company], the right choice’’ Exhibitor [7] in Appendix A, FR, p. 2); the loudest won a skateboard. Through this contest, this IT vendor enrolled other actors to relay its discourse broadly throughout the tradeshow.

Moreover, customers sought to materialize IT, or in other words, to make sense of what the vendors were offering in the context of their daily operations. In fact, most trade show attendees had received a technical degree (49 of the 66 respon dents to the questionnaire) and were constrained by a limited budget dedicated to IT investments

## 4.4. Practices and discourses at INDTS

On the visitors’ side, we observed a broad range of practices and discourses. INDTS appeared mainly to be a place for socializing and sharing practices. It was also a place to meet some unexpected (peripheral) OV stakeholders.

## 4.4.1. A place to where multiple stakeholders meet

We observed the massive presence of more peripheral and unexpected stakeholders in the fabric of OVs. Many high school teachers, university faculty members, and students from industrial disciplines, particularly engineering, attended the trade show. They gathered information about innovations and looked for job or internship opportunities. Faculty members were customers as they acquired IT products to demonstrate and use in their own classes, In turn, some of their students eventually worked with or became customers of these same IT vendors.

The main trade association for the mechanical engineering industry had its own booth and held information sessions encouraging the use of specific software. It also aimed to spotlight all jobs related to the industry sector; students were thus strategic targets for the association

On another note, the French Industry Minister visited the trade show. His visit was particularly strategic for INDORG. During his talk (in an overcrowded room), he did not specifically mention technology, but rather insisted on the potential of the French industrial sector. He mentioned support by the French government for the industry in order to help it overcome the economic recession.

## 4.4.2. A place for multiple actors to interact and access discourse about IT

Despite the presence of these unexpected stakeholders, INDTS was primarily a place for gathering information and social izing rather than for negotiating or closing deals. This observation was very consistent with our survey responses: 52% of our respondents (who were INDTS visitors) attended this trade show mainly because they intended to meet vendors and collaborators, rather than to look for a specific product or service. Only 21% of them had such a precise objective in mind while visiting the trade show. This was also consistent with visitors’ profiles. Visitors were not decision makers in the IT purchasing process. CEOs, the main decision makers for IT investments in small to medium companies, did not usually visit the trade show. Actual INDTS visitors represented their company in the capacity of design office engineers, technical managers, CAD studies service providers, or plant managers. Some of them were looking for information and arguments as to the benefits of a specific IT solution to help them convince their CEO to make the purchasing decision. Many of them had already zeroed in on which IT to adopt prior to their visit to INDTS, and were looking to rationalize and legitimize their choice by gathering pre-made arguments based on OVs circulating at the trade show: ‘‘The people that come to the trade show do have projects. The turnover inside the trade show has certainly decreased during the last years but we still meet a lot of people here. An opportunity with the Internet is to automatically clock in visitors that come into our stall. And you also have the informal meetings around bars.’’ Exhibitor [7] in Appendix A, FR, p. 7. These stakeholders favored ideas (OVs) that could convince their managers of the added values of specific IT offerings. They looked for pre-packaged ideas or argumentative strategies likely to appeal to the average boss of a small or medium-sized firm in the robotic or machine tool industry.

In short, most visiting customers expected IT vendors to ‘‘enter into contact with people, inside companies and perform demos’’ (Exhibitor [7] in Appendix A, p. 5) rather than to sign contracts. For example, from exhibitors’ point of view, visitors were interested in the following arguments: ‘‘This specific product (a CAD software) is easy of use, powerful and ergonomic.’’ (Exhibitor [13] in Appendix A) or ‘‘Visitors have the same issues: whether they are in an IT investment phase or not, the issue is the same: productivity. They want IT product to be profitable, they want to control use costs, CAD software automate recurrent activities.’’ (Exhibitor [12] in Appendix A, p. 5).

## 4.4.3. A place to socialize and share practices

At INDTS 2009, this search for rationalization was also partly the result of the economic recession, a subject that, in addition to being on everyone’s mind, headlined many of the numerous trade publications distributed at the trade show’s entrance. The recession also reinforced the need for social contacts to reassure customers and show them that vendors were still in business and that they were economically stable.

Among IT exhibitors that were direct competitors, we noticed seemingly very friendly relationships. Exhibitors from different companies enjoyed long coffee breaks together and had extensive knowledge of each other’s products. In fact, many ‘‘competing’’ salespeople knew each other because they had previously worked together, as the turnover among salesmen is very high. ‘‘I am recent in the company. I was in the company until 2003. I left and then I came back seven months ago’’ (An exhibitor: Exhibitor [13] in Appendix A, p. 1). The trade show was thus an open space where technologies were shown and discussed and where competitors met with one another to scope out the industry mood as well as to catch up with one another on a more personal level. This may well have accounted for a relative homogeneity of communication and sales techniques.

Such homogeneity was also perceptible among other stakeholders, for instance between consulting companies and IT vendors that frequently met and had discussions. As an exhibitor explained: ‘‘XX [name of a major company] has implemented a consultants’ club in which we meet regularly consultants firms: we discuss about our products, our past projects and about feed backs they gave to us on CAD projects. These are people who are in charge of specification elaboration in our client companies’’ (A major exhibitor: Exhibitor [1] in Appendix A, TO, p. 3). Through similar formal and informal meetings, IT suppliers (consultants, editors, service providers) built the discourses that they then offered to customers based on past experiences, thus leading to a relative homogeneity of discursive practices. Stories on past implementation were told to other competitors. The process of adaptation of core technology (component of layer 4 of the OV framework) was simply repeated.

## 5. Trade shows, discourses, practices and artifacts in the dynamics of OVs

Our objectives in this paper were to examine the microsocial underpinnings of OVs. In particular, we wanted to understand better how OVs are (re-)produced through practices and discourses taking place in a trade show and through this to examine how various practices and discourses that unfold in the sociomaterial context of a tradeshow affect the fabric of OVs. In the case of INDTS, this fabric mostly led to a refining of existing OVs in ways that combined discourses, practices in a specific sociomaterial context (see Fig. 5).

Our case revealed three findings that extend the OV framework and that bring crucial insights onto the fabric of OVs. These findings deepen the understanding of the relationships between its layers 2 and 4 (see Fig. 1) through the joint examination of discourses, practices, and artefacts involved in the dynamics of OVs during a trade show.

Practices and artifacts involved in enclosing the discourse: non-IT artifacts clearly delimited the conditions of production of discourses about IT to articulate in a focused way the IT artifacts and their potential (arrow 2a and 2b). The VIP room, the stand, a small stall behind the stand, a glass of champagne with appetizers, a leaflet, music, etc. served as both the context and the medium of a particular discourse. They set a physical and social boundary that forced visitors to listen to the standard discourse about IT; the setting was therefore both social and physical. The epitome of this was the French ‘‘aperitif’’, which had social (a ritual) and material (made of various objects, e.g. champagne and appetizers) dimensions. It was at times merely the opportunity to socialize with existing customers visiting INDTS, but it also often provided a way to create a pleasant and closed (from the noise, other advertisements,<sup>2</sup> etc.) setting for the presentation of a technology.

![](/api/attachments/2PRMDP8U/fulltext/images/c82511cade2686ef224dce4f7a651e49611e0cd6b9d95054a7445108dd6807e2.jpg)  
Fig. 5. Discourses–artifacts relationships observed at INDTS

Refining the discourse about IT artifacts: these were perhaps specific to INDTS and the industry it covered. INDTS simply ‘‘refreshed’’ well-known technical concepts, re-enacted them and re-imagined their potential. For example, an established technology (ERP) was purported to ‘‘[produce] more quickly, cheaply and in a cleaner way’’ (from the trade publication ‘‘Industry and Technology Review’’), which was important in the context of an economic recession. INDTS showcased familiar buzzwords about well-known technologies such as CAD, CAM, ERP. These existing technologies and buzzwords were then ‘‘re-explored’’ and re-interpreted by IT-vendors in the context of the crisis (e.g. in view of the efficiency they can bring).

Discourses and practices of materialization of IT artifacts: IT artifacts were discussed, visualized (see arrow 1a) through situated discourses (i.e. demos or sell pitch) that focused on the usual material artifacts (arrow 1b) of the industry by the customer or the vendor. IT can seem abstract and yet it is expected to do ‘‘something’’ for people and companies that adopt it. For this ‘‘something’’ to become clear, vendors at INDTS materialized IT through various practices and the reliance on the use of artefacts most software offerings were illustrated through demos of machine-tools and robots, and their adaptability, rapidity or precision (see our example of the spare parts or the robots doing penalties in Picture 1).

These three main findings combine and reveal a joint refinement of the OV and materialization of IT. In other words, the refinement of the OV can rely upon the (new) materialization of possibilities associated with the technology in question. We might speculate the reasons for the predominance of existing OVs’ (in particular CAD and CAM technologies) refinement over the emergence of new OVs and their development via the discourses of the stakeholders involved in INDTS. Firstly, the broader economic context of the crisis caused clients to ask for tangible, immediate, and visible added values of IT. Secondly, the industry targeted by the TS (small and medium sized companies of the machine-tool and robotic) was likely to correspond to a specific culture favoring a material relationship with cognitive technologies. Lastly, the commercial strategy of IT vendors themselves (in comparison to the economic context) could favor profitable existing technologies and existing communication strategies in the context of a recessionary economy (see upper part of Fig. 5).

## 6. Contributions and avenues for future research

This research added to the OV literature by moving closer to the micro-social underpinnings of authorized ideas about IT through an investigation of various key practices and discourses that developed in a trade show. There are several contributions to our work in particular as it examined everyday discourses, practices and the sociomaterial context in which they take place. Our three main findings uncovered relationships between discourse, practices, and artifacts involved in trade shows and revealed how these relationships affect the fabric of OVs: we observed that practices and artifacts contributed to enclose the production of discourse; that the trade show was a setting in which existing OVs were mostly refined rather than generated; and that the trade show was a context in which artifacts and practices were used to materialize the technology and the discourse about the technology.

Our research also illustrated how the (re-)production of OVs through discourses and practices is grounded in specific sociomaterial contexts. We came across spare parts from booth to booth as well as depicted on the leaflets used to present software and anecdotes about the machine-software business model. These examples revealed how IT and its effects are materialized in typical industry artifacts in order to ‘‘make sense’’ for industry insiders, to convince clients in the mechanica industry and to promote socially accepted ideas about needs that are made visible and that demonstrate the impact of IT on performance and productivity. ‘‘Practical activities and objects’’ (layer 4 of the OV framework) thus have very tight relationships with “interpretive-discursive activities" (laver 2). At INDTS, linguistic resources were highly embedded in common artifacts of the machine tool industry. IT rhetoric and IT enacted-practices had to be as close as possible to the machine tool universe in order to be convincing. In contrast, other, more service-oriented, industries can be expected to rely on more abstract or idealized demonstrations focused on processes, activities or business models. Additional fieldwork would be useful to develop cross-industry comparisons, but our work constituted a first step towards answering Chiasson and Davidson’s (2005) call to more carefully consider the importance of the industry in IS dynamics. It also illustrated the relevance and significance of taking in consideration sociomateriality in industry-wide settings such as trade shows

This paper thus provided a thorough examination of strategic aspects of IT-related dynamics at an industry level. As Gable (2010) stated current IS literature has not given much attention to this important level of analysis so far. Understanding better strategic contexts and processes involves not only taking in consideration the focal level of the firm, but also opening up investigations to the industry level. One of the most striking, and somewhat unexpected, findings of our research was the importance of industry-level dynamics at play in the trade show. These industry-level dynamics were essential to understand how OVs emerged and circulated during the trade show and beyond it.

Moreover, our research brings to light several intriguing findings regarding important stakeholders of OVs. The impact of inter-organizational IT discourses on customer decision-making depends on their relationship with external information and their purchase processes. Our cluster analysis in particular revealed that certain clients were more open than others to external information and to the influence of various IT vendors. Most visitors had very reflexive relationships with IT discourses. We note that our research did not examine how visitors actually used the IT discourses they were the targets of and witnessed at the trade show in the context of their organization during the adoption process. Future research could tackle this important process in the dynamic of OVs.

Overall our study, with its micro-social focus, focused on the role of trade shows in the dynamics of OVs Trade shows appear to be a central place in which communities supporting ideas about IT diffusion and adoption are reproduced and maintained. Visibility (as suggested in Baskerville and Myers (2009, p. 2)) was a key objective for exhibitors. As many vendors told us, orders were signed at later dates during business meetings at client locations. Our findings also suggest that INDTS (perhaps in contrast to other trade shows and other industries) was more a place where discourses were refined or reproduced than a space for innovation. This finding thus supports Swanson and Ramiller (1997), who consider the trade show as more of a bazaar than a forum. In this way, our observations also support the idea of the trade show as a fabric of IT OVs.

This study has several limitations. It is exploratory in that it represents a first attempt to examine trade shows as a context in which OV dynamics take place. Additional investigations would be useful to extend our conclusions about the dis courses involved in the fabric of OV and their relationships with practices, discourses and the sociomateriality specific of diverse industries. Moreover, while we believe that one of the key implications of our work is a better understanding of the relationships between discourses and practices involved in the (re)production of OVs, we are also convinced that future research could further unpack these relationships.

In terms of data collection, we did not systematically record the sessions and presentations occurring at the trade show. In a future research project, we plan on examining such discourses along with those that came out of our interviews. Furthermore, some of our interviews were cut short for practical reasons (i.e. INDTS’ busy and noisy environment). Lastly, our analysis did not focus on layer 3 (social structure/community) of the OV framework. In other words, we observed the different stakeholders involved in the trade shows (e.g. exhibitors, clients, students), but did not delve into the undoubtedly complex relations within and among them For instance, we noted that exhibitors were friendly toward each other but they were also competitors. How these agreeable yet rival relations unfold in discourse and practices would be very interesting to examine. Finally, consistent with our interpretive epistemological stance, our case narrative itself was a carefully built and empirically substantiated interpretation of our data (Geertz, 1983; Walsham, 1993, 2006; Walsham and Sahay, 1999). However, we did not much question the potential impact of our presence as researchers on the dynamics of OVs and the practices of the actors we met.

Overall. we suggest three main avenues for further research. Firstly, other case studies about IT tradeshows in other industries and other countries could further explore the micro-social underpinnings of authorized ideas about IT and their local and national specificities. Secondly, our results provide insight about trade-show discourses and their relationship with material artifacts (IT or non-IT); thus, the sociomaterial nature of the inter-organizational discourses that sustain IT diffusion could be pushed further. Austin’s (1962) work on performativity and performative utterances, or that of Orlikowski (2007) or Orlikowski and Scott (2008) about sociomaterial practices could be illuminating in this regard Thirdly, future research could further examine the implications of our research for IS strategy theory and practice. In this regard, we see much promise in multi-level research that would explore the relationships between OV dynamics at the micro-social level, the strategic value of IT and competitive advantage. In particular, Regnér’s (2008) work on the dynamics of the resources that sustain competitive advantage (using a practices-based view) or that of de Vaujany (2008) about IT strategic values (particularly legitimacybased values) could be extended and clarified by means of further examination of trade shows. How do firms arbitrate and differentiate between the search for up-to-date, legitimating technologies? What is the degree of ‘‘mindfulness’’ (Swanson and Ramiller, 2004) of IT managers, CIOs and CEOs in the process? What do differences among industries mean for the pro cesses through which OVs get generated and sustained? For these questions, trade shows could offer, once more, an inter esting context in which to observe and interview these stakeholders and to analyze their IT adoption practices.

## Appendix E. Cluster analysis

## E.1. Description of the global roll out of the cluster analysis

The constitution of variable sets was achieved by means of several successive iterations making it possible to highlight five sets among the 18 variables corresponding to 73 modalities. These sets were identified based on the meanings of the variables: some variables were used to define the sources of external information mobilized by companies (Set 1), others describe perceptions of external influence on IT investments (Set 2, Set 3, Set 4), and some specifying company behaviour vis-à-vis the external environment (Set 5). Each set is explained in Appendix E.2. The results of the cluster analysis highlight three main ‘dimensions’ obtained by the overall cluster analysis structuring our population by means of canonical analysis. Choosing the number of relevant dimensions for a cluster analysis was carried out by means of a scree test, identifying three dimensions (with an inflexion point after these three dimensions). These three dimensions correspond to 51% of the explained variances (see Appendix E for further details). Based on the canonical analysis (coordinates of modalities along the three dimensions), a cluster analysis helped us put forward three groups of companies, their related behaviours and influence of external information on their IT investments. These three groups were then validated by means of an ANOVA and discriminant analysis prior to final interpretation. The three groups identified by means of our cluster analysis (Kmeans method, see Appendix E.4) are described in the following way in view of the modality coordinates. The diagram in Appendix E.4 shows the distribution of the 73 modalities into three main groups. There is relative heterogeneity in the distribution of modalities within these three groups. Thus, there are 49 modalities for Group 1, 3 for Group 2 and 21 for Group 3. A summary of the 73 modalities is found in Appendix E.
