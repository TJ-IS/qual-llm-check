# INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY INVESTMENT AND COMMERCIALIZED INNOVATION PERFORMANCE: DYNAMIC ADJUSTMENT COSTS AND CURVILINEAR IMPACTS<sup>1</sup>

Prasanna P. Karhade

Department of Information Technology Management, Shidler College of Business, University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa, Honolulu, HI, U.S.A. {karhade@hawaii.edu}

John Qi Dong Trinity Business School, Trinity College Dublin, University of Dublin, Dublin, IRELAND {john.dong@tcd.ie}

Firms’ investment in information technology (IT) has been widely considered to be a key enabler of innovation. In this study, we integrate prior findings on the augmenting pathways (where IT investment supports innovation) with a new theory explaining the suppressing pathways (where dynamic adjustment costs associated with large IT investment can be detrimental to innovation) to propose an overall inverted U-shaped relationship between IT investment and commercialized innovation performance (CIP). To test our theory, we analyze a unique panel dataset from the largest economy in Europe and discovered a curvilinear relationship between IT investment and CIP for firms across a broad spectrum of industries Our research presents empirical evidence corroborating the augmenting and suppressing pathways linking IT investment and CIP. Our findings serve as a cautionary signal to executives, discouraging overinvestment in IT.

Keywords: Information technology investment, overinvestment, dynamic adjustment costs, commercialized innovation performance, digital innovation, business value of information technology, curvilinear relationships

## Introduction

Recent years have witnessed a proliferation of digital innovation research on a positive relationship between firms’ investment in information technology (IT) and various innovation outcomes (e.g., Bardhan et al. 2013; Gómez et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2020; Karhade and Dong 2021; Kleis et al. 2012; Ravichandran et al. 2017; Saldanha et al. 2017; Trantopoulos et al. 2017; Xue et al. 2012). Although prior studies have documented various benefits stemming from IT investment for innovation, there is also some evidence on the limits to these benefits (e.g., Chircu and Kauffman 2000; Sherif et al. 2006). In particular, Aral and Weill (2007) find no impact of IT investment on commercialized innovation performance (CIP)—sales of new products or services that are commercialized in the marketplace. It is unclear how to resolve this empirical puzzle given the mixed findings in the digital innovation literature.

Not every investment for digital innovation is advantageous because they may expose firms to unwarranted risks (Grover and Kohli 2013; Kohli and Grover 2019). For instance, more than 50% of large IT projects reportedly experience significant failure and cost overrun (Bloch et al. 2012), as such endeavors often require costly adjustments before any innovation outcomes can be realized. Since large IT projects may require organization-wide adjustments to business processes (Karimi et al. 2007), they tend to be significantly riskier (March and Shapira 1987; McFarlan 1981). These discussions imply that a systematic understanding of hindrances that curtail the innovation benefits of IT investment is much needed. Our research motivation is to reconcile the mixed findings in prior research by theorizing the overall relationship between IT investment and innovation with an integrative view incorporating both the augmenting and suppressing pathways through which IT investment influences CIP. Next, we theorize the suppressing pathways linking IT investment and CIP by introducing dynamic adjustment costs (DACs) to the IS literature.

In prior strategic management research, Tan and Mahoney (2005) maintain that, from a resource-based perspective, a firm that alters its resources for developing capabilities to meet market changes adaptively is likely to incur DACs because adjustments of resources can often disrupt current operations. In our theory, embedded in the context of IT investment and innovation, we define DACs as the costs that firms incur in making frequent and costly adjustments stemming from their investment in IT resources for developing innovation capabilities to meet market changes adaptively. Although DACs stemming from resource alterations such as investment in human resources and acquisition of external resources have been investigated in strategic management research (e.g., Argyres et al. 2019; Kor et al. 2016; Penrose 1959;<sup>2</sup> Tan and Mahoney 2005), the understanding of DACs associated with IT investment is still scant. While systematic theorizing of DACs is missing, prior IS literature has pinpointed some costs associated with IT investment that may contribute to DACs. We discuss these potential sources of DACs below.

Costly adjustments may be required for IT investment because new IT systems have to be integrated within the existing ecosystem of IT systems. Firms incur DACs when systematically integrating new IT systems with existing IT systems or upgrading incumbent IT systems to make them compatible to new ones (Sikora and Shaw 1998). Similarly, as IT investment often leads to the deployment of new IT systems or significant upgrades of incumbent IT systems, it may also require employees within the firm to redesign or reinvent their work routines and corresponding social subsystems (Ryan and Harrison 2000) before the IT investment yields the intended benefits. Additionally, as the investment in IT increases, it requires firms to deal with larger amounts of data, or big data. Data lifecycles can be difficult to manage, often causing the costs of managing data to swell up dynamically, as firms not only have to manage data storage costs but also have to deal with costs of conducting data backups and other information management costs for ensuring business continuity (Goodhue et al. 1992; Tallon 2010). In the big data age, firms are also struggling with the problem of information overload associated with using IT for innovation (Karhade and Dong 2021). It might be tempting to leverage outsourcing as a solution to cut costs, but the act of IT outsourcing itself involves a wide array of hidden costs in transitioning to the vendor and re-transitioning back from IT vendors (Barthélemy 2005). When IT projects are large, complex coordination across multiple stakeholders is often necessary and difficult (Williams and Karahanna 2013). Although the above potential sources of DACs have been discussed in prior IS literature, systematic theory development relying on DACs is still missing.

In the digital innovation literature, calls for research highlight the need to investigate hindrances that plague the innovation outcomes of IT investment (Kohli and Melville 2019). Strategic management scholars emphasize that DACs need to be in the foreground when theorizing and understanding how investments in resources (in our case, IT) lead to hindrances to innovation (Argyres et al. 2019; Kor et al. 2016). Therefore, we integrate research on digital innovation with theory on DACs by incorporating both the augmenting and suppressing pathways linking IT investment and CIP. Since CIP captures the sales of new products or services in the marketplace, theory on DACs is particularly suitable for the purpose of our theorizing as it examines resource investments for developing capabilities to meet market changes adaptively. We theorize that when firms make a large IT investment, they are likely to incur high DACs and the overall relationship between IT investment and CIP is likely to be an inverted U-shape.

To test our theory, we constructed a panel dataset consisting of 3129 firm-year observations from Germany between 1997 and 2004. Analysis of this unique dataset corroborates the hypothesized curvilinear impacts of IT investment on CIP. Since DACs are latent and at best can be conjectured ex post (Kor et al. 2016), we developed a three-pronged ex post validation strategy to validate our DACs-based theory. First, when firms make a large IT investment, they incur high DACs as evidenced by high innovation costs. Second, when firms make a large IT investment, they incur high DACs as proxied by a summative index that we created based on different sources of DACs. Third, we measured innovation cost savings in the subsequent period after IT investment was made and found consistent curvilinear impacts of IT investment on innovation cost savings, which indirectly corroborates our DACs-based theory. Overall, this study makes important contributions to the digital innovation literature by deepening the knowledge about the IT innovation link by incorporating both enablers and hindrances stemming from IT investment for CIP and by unveiling the nonlinearities of the relationship between IT and innovation.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, we present the research background and motivation, followed by theory development, which brings DACs into the foreground when investigating the relationship between IT investment and CIP. We then report our empirical methodology and results, and elaborate on a three-pronged research design for ex post validation of our DACs-based theory. Finally, we discuss the implications and limitations of our study and outline directions for future research.

Investigating how IT contributes or hinders the innovation activity is central to IS research. What We Have Learned: An Overview of Relevant Literature

## The Augmenting Pathways: How IT Supports Innovation

IT investment can be beneficial for innovation and we group prior literature to represent the augmenting pathways linking IT and innovation. IT plays a critical role in coordinating various business processes (Chang and Gurbaxani 2012; Gurbaxani and Whang 1991) and assisting firms in the innovation activity (Bardhan et al. 2013; Nambisan et al. 2017; Sambamurthy et al. 2003) along three primary dimensions.

First, IT enables firms to better understand customer needs in developing desirable new product features (e.g., Pavlou and El Sawy 2006; Saldanha et al. 2017). Second, IT assists firms to better forecast market demand for effectively meeting the demand of new products or services (e.g., Gómez et al. 2017; Rai et al. 2006). Third, IT investment helps firms better identify opportune times to launch their new product or service offerings (e.g., Joshi et al. 2010; Tambe et al. 2012). In summary, there is empirical evidence supporting a positive linear relationship between IT investment and innovation outcomes such as patents (e.g., Gómez et al. 2017; Kleis et al. 2012; Ravichandran et al. 2017; Saldanha et al. 2017; Xue et al. 2012) and new product development and introduction (e.g., Banker et al. 2006; Joshi et al. 2010; Pavlou and El Sawy 2006; Tambe et al. 2012). With regard to CIP, however, prior research documents no significant impact of IT investment (Aral and Weill 2007).

## The Suppressing Pathways: How IT Hurts Innovation

Although prior research highlights how IT augments innovation outcomes, findings on how IT may hurt innovation remain relatively scant in the literature. We group these findings to represent the suppressing pathways linking IT and innovation. Firms face various barriers when deploying new IT resources that may limit the benefits of IT investment. In particular, a large investment in IT may not always contribute to developing stronger capabilities for innovation (Aral and Weill 2007). Costly interventions and organization-wide adjustments are necessary for value creation from large IT projects (Sherif et al. 2006), leading to disruptions in the innovation activity along the following dimensions.

First, firms may incur high integration costs for technological systems and social systems because a large IT investment can disrupt existing operations in the innovation activity and the broader social ecosystems within which new IT systems operate (Ryan and Harrison 2000; Sikora and Shaw 1998). Second, firms may incur high costs associated with data management challenges when heavily investing in IT (Tallon 2010), leading to big data and information overload problem in the innovation activity (Karhade and Dong 2021). Third, in the IT outsourcing context, hidden costs may also be prohibitively high when firms outsource IT operations for innovation initiatives. Firms may be forced to internalize IT operations that come with high costs if relationships with vendors break down (Barthélemy 2001). Finally, a large IT investment often involves multiple stakeholders in the innovation activity, resulting in high costs of governance and coordination (Williams and Karahanna 2013). All these findings suggest that a large IT investment may require adjustments and cause disruptions in the innovation activity. Our motivation is to investigate hindrances associated with IT investment for innovation and incorporate these insights into our study of the relationship between IT investment and CIP.

## What We Need to Learn: Integrating the Augmenting and Suppressing Pathways

Recent research suggests that DACs need to be in the foreground for theorizing and understanding the hindrances to innovation (Kor et al. 2016). As discussed above, while prior research provides some insights into the costs and disruptions caused by IT investment, systematic theorizing of both enablers and hindrances from IT investment for innovation and a largescale econometric analysis of the overall relationship between IT investment and CIP to reconcile mixed findings are missing. We address this gap by integrating augmenting and suppressing pathways through which IT investment influences CIP in our theory development. In addition to augmenting pathways, we theorize suppressing pathways and suggest that a large IT investment is likely to induce high DACs and reduce CIP.

## Theory Development

## The Augmenting Pathways: Moderate IT Investment Strengthens CIP

We propose that increasing IT investment from low to moderate levels can introduce various benefits for CIP—i.e., sales of new products or services that are commercialized in the marketplace. First, firms aspire to develop new products or offer new services with a desirable set of features for consumers. IT investment enables firms to gather and learn from market-facing information about shifting consumer needs and develop new products or services with desirable features (Pavlou and El Sawy 2006; Saldanha et al. 2017). More desirable features can enhance the sales of new products or services in the marketplace. Second, succeeding in commercialization also requires firms to produce and offer new products or services in appropriate quantities. IT investment can support a focal firm’s coordination with suppliers and customers to jointly forecast market demand (Karhade and Dong 2021; Rai et al. 2006), allowing the firm to produce and deliver the optimal volume of new products or services, thereby meeting market demand and generating more sales. Third, the market launch of new products or services has a temporal dimension in commercialization. IT investment enables firms to identify opportune times for introducing new products or services (Tambe et al. 2012)— for example, identifying and leveraging seasonal trends based on customer data—which can promote sales of new products or services in the marketplace.

In summary, increasing IT investment to moderate levels enables the development of innovation capabilities that are vital for developing and commercializing new products or services (Joshi et al. 2010), leading to superior CIP in the end. However, innovation benefits are unlikely to increase proportionally to the increase of IT investment because all resource investments are subject to the law of diminishing returns in production (Samuelson and Nordhaus 2001). Adding one more IT system may generate greater per-unit benefits when IT resources are limited than when IT resources are plentiful, especially if a new IT investment generates slack and loose discipline of resource deployment for innovation (Nohria and Gulati 1996). Thus, we expect that increasing IT investment strengthens CIP with diminishing marginal benefits.

The theoretical mechanism that shapes the overall impacts of IT investment on CIP is driven not only by the benefits for innovation but also the costs associated with IT investment in the innovation activity. The costs of managing IT resources for innovation often increase with the size of investment (e.g., McFarlan 1981), as a large IT investment is inherently difficult and costly to coordinate. When firms do not make a large investment in IT, the disruptions associated with coordinating low to moderate levels of IT investment are not major. With moderate levels of IT investment, we expect that the aforementioned three benefits of IT investment outweigh its DACs to the largest possible extent. In the absence of costly adjustments, increasing IT investment from low to moderate levels is therefore likely to strengthen CIP.

## The Suppressing Pathways: Large IT Investment Reduces CIP

From a DACs-based perspective, knowledge workers involved in the innovation activity are boundedly rational (Cyert and March 1963; Penrose 1959). In other words, knowledge workers have a limited ability to envision how the innovation activity will unfold over time because they only have access to limited information about the market and, more importantly, have limited attention when engaging in the innovation activity. Furthermore, the outcomes of the innovation activity are inherently uncertain and unpredictable (Nelson and Winter 1982). Because of bounded rationality, knowledge workers are required to adjust their operations for innovation in changing information environments (Argyres et al. 2019), and these adjustments may be frequent and costly (e.g., Kor et al. 2016). The nature of adjustments that might need to be undertaken—and the corresponding DACs—are not known ex ante and only manifest dynamically as the innovation activity unfolds over time.

We propose that, when firms heavily invest in IT, they likely incur high DACs greater than the benefits of IT investment, reducing CIP. First, as IT investment expands from moderate to high levels, the digital information channels available to a firm and the diversity of information the firm is exposed to rapidly increase (Gómez et al. 2017; Trantopoulos et al. 2017). Knowledge workers are boundedly rational and, given their limited information processing ability and attention span, they may find it increasingly difficult to utilize abundant information for developing new products or services. High diversity of information can also be a source of high DACs and can distract the limited attention of knowledge workers, who may be overwhelmed by information overload in the innovation activity (Karhade and Dong 2021). Owing to the inability of boundedly rational knowledge workers in identifying and processing marketfacing information for innovation, firms may develop new products or services with less desirable features and inferior sales. As IT investment increases to high levels, the limits on the cognitive capacity of knowledge workers make it costly and even disruptive to adjust the innovation activity in an information-rich environment (e.g., Tambe et al. 2012). As a result, high DACs stemming from a large IT investment are likely to outweigh its benefits for innovation, leading to lower CIP.

Second, when IT investment increases from moderate to high levels, firms may incur high costs if new or upgraded IT systems from the new investment need to be integrated into the ecosystem of incumbent IT systems (e.g., Sikora and Shaw 1998). This often requires firms to redesign their business processes, leading to disruptions such as inefficiencies in demand forecasting. Particularly when firms invest in large IT projects, the broader sociotechnical ecosystem in which the new system operates can be disrupted, forcing firms to incur nontrivial adjustments of labor skills and work practices (Ryan and Harrison 2000). The necessity of redesigning organization-wide as well as boundary-spanning business processes (Karhade and Dong 2021) can be a source of high DACs. If the new investment is not fully integrated within the existing sociotechnical ecosystem, the benefits of IT investment may be curtailed and outweighed by the disruptions it causes (Nevo and Wade 2010), resulting in lower CIP when benefits are eaten away by high DACs.

Third, when IT investment expands from moderate to high levels, the costs of governing large IT projects involving multiple stakeholders can rapidly increase (Williams and Karahanna 2013) because the massive coordination of stakeholders needed in the deployment of new IT resources can be difficult to govern. Disruptions resulting from governance and coordination challenges in any stage of the innovation activity may delay the introduction of new products or services. Given the strategic necessity of welltimed market launches for successful commercialization (Tambe et al. 2012), delayed launches may force firms to slash their prices, leading to lower CIP. Although the importance of timing a new launch varies across industries, in most marketplaces, well-timed launches generally promote sales (Tambe et al. 2021). The costs of governing large IT projects with high investment thus represent another source of high DACs and may outweigh the benefits of IT investment for innovation. Delays in market launches caused by the difficulty of governance may be detrimental to CIP, as late-market entrants cannot command high price markups (Lieberman and Montgomery 1988).

In summary, as firms increase IT investment from modest to high levels, they will likely incur high DACs, and CIP will likely decrease because (1) leveraging market-facing information for developing desirable new product features can be difficult, (2) integrating new IT systems to the existing ecosystem may lead to inefficiencies in demand forecasting, and (3) governing and coordinating complex groups of stakeholders in large IT projects may cause delays in market launches. For all these reasons, the benefits of CIP are likely to be eaten away by high DACs from a large IT investment. It is worth noting that DACs may note increases proportional to the increase in IT investment, as a large investment in complex IT systems requires much more costly adjustments to the innovation activity and can cause considerable disruptions to current operations. Thus, when IT investment increases from moderate to high levels, DACs may increase exponentially and become inevitably high.

Integrating the augmenting and suppressing pathways, we propose that CIP likely increases with more IT investment from low to moderate levels and likely declines with more IT investment from moderate to high levels. The above two latent theoretical mechanisms with diminishing benefits and exponentially increasing costs can additively lead to an inverted U-shaped relationship (Haans et al. 2016). Therefore, we hypothesize that IT investment has an inverted U-shaped relationship with CIP.

## Methodology

## Data

To test the relationship between IT investment and CIP, we constructed a large-scale panel dataset from the Centre for European Economic Research’s (ZEW) Mannheim Innovation Panel (MIP) database (ZEW 2013). ZEW follows the Oslo Manual (OECD 2005) to annually collect innovation data ensuring rigorous methods and valid instruments for data collection. Germany, the largest economy in Europe, is a suitable context for our study because it is an innovative country, ranked comparably to the U.S. (Hollanders and Arundel 2006). Every year since 1993, a random sample of firms with at least five employees from a broad spectrum of industries in Germany are surveyed for constructing the MIP database. Table 2 describes our sample distribution across different industries.

Voluntary mail questionnaires were used as the primary means for data collection. For each year, questionnaires were sent to firms in early spring with reminders later in the spring and early summer. An advisory board closely monitored data collection, ensuring high data quality. We were able to ascertain the high quality of our data by replicating prior findings in the digital innovation literature. For example, Bardhan et al. (2013) focus on large U.S. firms and find a positive interaction effect of IT and R&D investments on Tobin’s Q. We also found a positive interaction effect of IT and R&D investments on CIP when examining large German firms. Gómez et al. (2017) report diminishing returns on R&D investment for Spanish manufacturing firms. We also found diminishing returns on R&D investment for CIP when examining German manufacturing firms. As will be reported later, we found no significant linear relationship between IT investment and CIP, consistent with prior findings from Aral and Weill (2007). Overall, these replication results validate the high quality of our data.

Based on the data availability for IT investment, we used the data from the MIP database between 1997 and 2004. This time span makes our study comparable to prior studies—for example, Bardhan et al. (2013) use data from U.S. firms in 1997-2004. In our data, IT and control variables are available in 1997, 1999, 2001, and 2003. Following a research design for measuring CIP with a one-year time lag, we used CIP data in 1998, 2000, 2002, and 2004. For variables that were measured on interval scales, we converted the values to the midpoints of intervals (e.g., Rai and Patnayakuni 1996). After removing missing values, our dataset consisted of a sample with 3129 firm-year observations from 2402 unique firms spanning four years between 1997 and 2004.

## Operationalization of Variables

CIP: We selected a widely used measure for CIP—sales of new products or services (e.g., Laursen and Salter 2006; Leiponen and Helfat 2010). We computed the natural logarithm to reduce the skewness of this variable. A similar measure has been used in IS research (e.g., Aral and Weill 2007). Sales of new products or services is a fine-grained measure of innovation performance, compared to a count measure indicating the number of patents or new products (e.g., Gómez et al. 2017; Joshi et al. 2010; Kleis et al. 2012). It is an appropriate measure, particularly because not all industries have a high propensity for patenting. Our sampled firms had an average of 0.887 million Deutsche marks sales of new products or services, or 12% of their total sales. Only 9 observations (0.3%) reported that all their sales were attributed to new products or services in a specific year.

IT investment: We measured IT investment as the total expenditure in IT hardware, software, services and personnel, which is widely used in past IS research (Bardhan et al. 2013, Gómez et al. 2017; Ravichandran et al. 2017). We computed the natural logarithm to reduce the skewness of this variable. To address concerns stemming from the outliers in making IT investment, we winsorized this variable at 5% level. Winsorized IT investment of our sampled firms has the mean as 1% of total sales, with a maximum level of 9%.

Control variables: We controlled for several factors that may influence CIP. First, we controlled for R&D investment which was measured by computing the natural logarithm of a firm’s R&D expenditure (Bardhan et al. 2013). Second, we controlled for firm size by computing the natural logarithm of total sales (Kleis et al. 2012). Third, we controlled for firm age by constructing a binary variable indicating whether the firm was a new entrant to the industry within three years prior to data collection (0 = no, 1 = yes). Fourth, geographical location may offer advantages for innovation (Lahiri 2010), so we controlled for this effect by including a binary variable indicating whether a firm was located in eastern or western Germany (0 = Western, 1 = Eastern). Finally, we included industry dummies based on NACE Rev. 1 two-digit codes and year dummies to control for industry and time fixed effects. ## Analysis Strategy

To test the relationship between IT investment and CIP, we followed guidelines from prior literature on testing curvilinear relationships (Haans et al. 2016; Lind and Mehlum 2010; Xue et al. 2011). Equation (1) presents our empirical model. To mitigate concerns stemming from the endogeneity of IT investment, we used a firm-fixed effects model to control for time-invariant unobservable characteristics. We also used a one-year time lag between IT investment and CIP. In Equation (1), ln(Sales of New Products or $S e r v i c e s _ { i t + 1 } )$ indicates CIP of firm i in year t + 1, ln(IT<sub>it</sub>) indicates the IT investment made by firm i in year t, $X _ { i t }$ represents the vector of control variables, u<sub>i</sub> is timeinvariant unobservable characteristics of firm i, and $e _ { i t }$ represents the error term. If the hypothesized inverted Ushaped relationship does exist, three criteria need to be fulfilled: (1) $\beta _ { 1 } > 0$ and $\beta _ { 2 } < 0 ; ( 2 )$ the slope must be steep on both sides, before and after the turning point, and (3) the turning point needs to be within a feasible data range (Haans et al. 2016, Lind and Mehlum 2010).

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln (S a l e s o f N e w P r o d u c t s o r S e r v i c e s _ {i t + 1}) = \beta_ {0} + \\ \beta_ {1} \ln (I T _ {i t}) + \beta_ {2} \ln (I T _ {i t}) ^ {2} + \mu X _ {i t} + u _ {i} + e _ {i t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

To examine the first criterion, we estimated the fixed effects regression model as shown in Equation (1). To examine the second criterion, we conducted the U test following Lind and Mehlum (2010). To examine the third criterion, we calculated the turning point to ascertain whether it lies within the data range of IT investment (Haans et al. 2016).

## Results

## Main Analysis

Table 4 reports fixed effects regression results. We first estimated a control model, followed by a linear model with IT investment. We then added IT investment squared to estimate a curvilinear model. To compare the model fit of different models, we used two complementary information criteria—Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). AIC is often used to determine the most adequate model among alternatives, while BIC looks for the true model (Vrieze 2012). By comparing AIC and BIC between the control model and the linear model, we found that the linear model demonstrates greater AIC and BIC, indicating a worse model fit. However, the curvilinear model shows a smaller AIC and BIC and therefore a better fit than the control and linear models. Thus, a curvilinear model is the most suitable. In the linear model, we failed to observe any significant effect of IT investment on CIP, consistent with prior findings from Aral and Weill (2007). In the curvilinear model, however, we found that IT investment has a statistically significant and positive effect on CIP $( \beta _ { 1 }$ $= ~ 0 . 8 4 7 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 1 )$ and IT investment squared has a statistically significant and negative effect on CIP $( \beta _ { 2 } = -$ 0.453, $p \ < \ 0 . 0 1 )$ . Both of these findings meet the first criterion of testing an inverted U-shaped relationship.

We then conducted the U test (Lind and Mehlum 2010), to evaluate whether the slope is steep on both sides, before and after the turning point. This test confirmed that, on both sides, the positive and negative slopes are statistically significant (t $= 2 . 7 8 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ), meeting the second criterion for validating an inverted U-shaped relationship. Finally, we found that the turning point appears when IT investment is 0.935 within our data range (see Figure 1), meeting the third criterion. All these results support an inverted U-shaped relationship between IT investment and CIP (Haans et al. 2016).

## Robustness Checks

We conducted multiple tests to examine the robustness of our results. Although we controlled for time-invariant omitted variables by using a fixed effects model and assuaged endogeneity concerns stemming from reverse causality by using a one-year time lag between IT investment and CIP, endogeneity caused by time-variant omitted variables remained an open question. To further address the endogeneity of IT investment, we used two complementary approaches. First, we used instrumental variables available in our dataset to address endogeneity. We considered (1) mergers and acquisitions (M&As) as a radical change of resource bundles that can lead to significant shifts in IT investment (Benitez et al. 2018), (2) geographical location in eastern or western Germany, which we used as a proxy for national IT infrastructure impacting firms’ investment in and value creation of IT (Melville et al. 2004), (3) an industry dummy indicating a high or low industry clock-speed driving IT investment made by firms (Mendelson and Pillai 1999; Ray et al. 2009), and (4) a year 2000 (Y2K) dummy indicating a year before or after 2000, capturing the temporal radical changes in firms’ IT investment driven by the Y2K problem (Anderson et al. 2006). We dropped 93 observations from this test because of missing values in the instrumental variables. We found that our instruments significantly correlate with IT investment (Cragg-Donald Wald F = 7.864, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and, more importantly, pass overidentification test suggesting that they are valid instruments (Hansen J = 4.345, p = 0.114). Using these instrumental variables, two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression with clustering standard errors by firm show that the fitted value of IT investment has a statistically significant and positive effect on CIP and the fitted value of IT investment squared has a statistically significant and negative effect on CIP, supporting an inverted U-shaped relationship (see Table 5).

Second, we used an alternative approach to address endogeneity. Following Shaver (1998) and Bharadwaj et al. (2007), we used a two-stage Heckman model to control for endogeneity. In the first stage, we created a binary variable with a value of 1 if a firm’s IT investment is greater than the mean in our sample, and 0 otherwise. Therefore, this variable indicates that a firm’s IT investment is high or low relative to the sample mean. We then estimated a Probit model by regressing this new binary variable on the exclusive restriction as the industry average IT investment—which is expected to influence a firm’s IT investment in the industry (Mithas et al. 2013)—as well as all control variables. The Probit model explains 42% variation in the likelihood that a firm’s IT investment is at the high or low level. Industry average IT investment has a statistically significant and positive effect (?? = 2.452, p < 0.05). Based on the results of the Probit model, we then calculated the inverse Mills ratio (IMR), which captures the endogenous variation in IT investment. In the second stage, we tested the relationship between IT investment and CIP, using the IMR as an additional control. Although the IMR is statistically significant—implying the necessity of controlling for endogeneity—we still found support for an inverted U-shaped relationship between IT investment and CIP after controlling for endogeneity (see Table 5).

We also examined the sensitivity of our results by using alternative measures. We used an alternative ratio measure for CIP as the percentage of sales of new products or services over total sales (Laursen and Salter 2006; Leiponen and Helfat

2010). Accordingly, we also created ratio measures for IT and R&D investments as IT and R&D expenditures scaled by total sales respectively (e.g., Bardhan et al. 2013; Mithas et al. 2013). We estimated a random effects double-censoring Tobit model (Laursen and Salter 2006; Leiponen and Helfat 2010), because (1) the ratio measure of CIP has a lower bound of 0 and an upper bound of 1, and it is therefore more appropriate to use a Tobin model with censoring in both directions, and (2) fixed effects are not available for the Tobit model and only radon effects are feasible. We found support for an inverted U-shaped relationship between IT intensity measure and CIP as a percentage of total sales (see Table 6). Although we also found support for a linear model by using ratio measures, AIC and BIC indicate better model fit for a curvilinear model.

Furthermore, we considered industry differences in firms’ IT investment. <sup>3</sup> Firms in IT-intensive industries may systematically make a larger IT investment, albeit still below the break-even levels, than firms in other industries. To address this concern, we created a deviation measure for IT investment by subtracting the industry average IT investment from a firm’s IT investment (e.g., Ho et al. 2017; Mithas et al. 2013). This alternative measure partials out industry differences and only captures a firm’s IT investment that deviates from the industry average level. Again, we found support for an inverted U-shaped relationship between the IT deviation measure and CIP (see Table 7). Also, the curvilinear model demonstrates the best model fit.

## Validations of Theoretical Mechanism

We conducted three tests to comprehensively validate our theory. Table 8 maps our DACs measurements, in relationship with prior research. First, we used a binary variable indicating the presence of DACs in the innovation activity experienced by a firm based on whether the firm incurred very high costs for innovation (0 = no, 1 = yes) throughout the same year in which IT investment was made. Second, we created a summative index using both internal and external sources of DACs based on whether the firm had internal organizational problems for innovation and whether the firm was operating on missing external marketfacing information for innovation (0 = no at all, 1 = yes for either question, 2 = yes for both questions) throughout the same year in which IT investment was made. These two concurrent measurements of DACs are appropriate because recalling hindrances to innovation after time lapses can be difficult. Finally, we measured innovation cost savings as the percentage of costs reduced by innovation in the subsequent year after IT investment was made. This ratio measure of innovation cost savings in the subsequent time period is in alignment with our cost-based theorizing and allows us to examine the cost-based outcomes of the innovation activity. The implicit argument is that if IT investment and innovation cost savings also have an inverted U-shaped relationship, that would indirectly support our theoretical explanation that firms incurred higher DACs as they invest in IT from moderate to high levels. We did not have access to the information about total costs and used the ratio measure of innovation cost savings. The three tests for validating our DACs-based theory are complementary, as the first test used a cross-sectional, overall measure of DACs (i.e., high innovation costs). The second test used a cross-sectional, summative index based on both internal and external sources of DACs. The third test used a lagged, ratio measure of innovation cost savings.

Our first test sample was 2907 firm-year observations because 222 observations had missing values in the first proxy of DACs. We found that 44% of our sampled firms experienced high DACs in the form of very high innovation costs. Since our first measure of DACs used a binary scale, we conducted a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and t-test. We examined whether firms making a large IT investment incurred greater DACs compared to firms making a small IT investment, and used both the mean and median of winsorized IT investment as a threshold to divide our sample into two groups: (1) firms making a large IT investment above the mean/median IT investment, and (2) firms making a small IT investment below or equal to the mean/median. As Table 9 shows, using the mean of IT investment as the threshold, the ANOVA results indicate that the DACs incurred by firms with a large IT investment are significantly greater than the DACs incurred by firms with a small IT investment $( \mathrm { F } = 4 . 9 9 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The t-test results consistently indicate that the DACs incurred by firms with a large IT investment are significantly greater than the DACs incurred by firms with a small IT investment (t = 2.233, p < 0.05). Using the median of IT as the threshold, the ANOVA results indicate that the DACs incurred by firms with a large IT investment are significantly greater than the DACs incurred by firms with a small IT investment $( \mathrm { F } = 6 . 4 9 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The t-test results consistently indicate that the DACs incurred by firms with a large IT investment are significantly greater than the DACs incurred by firms with a small IT investment (t = 2.548, $p < 0 . 0 5 )$

However, our results indicate that R&D investment does not have a statistically significant effect on the likelihood of firms incurring high DACs. Taken together, these results suggest that IT investment, rather than R&D investment, is likely to induce high DACs in the innovation activity.

We further used an ordered logit model to regress the second proxy for DACs on IT investment, because the summative index has an ordinal scale that goes from 0 to 2. Because fixed effects are not feasible for the ordered logit model, we used a random effects model in regression analysis. Again, we controlled for R&D investment, firm size, firm age, and industry and year dummies. Geographical location was omitted and inclusion of this variable generated qualitatively similar results (?? = 0.364, p < 0.1). As Table 11 shows, IT investment has a statistically significant and positive effect on internal and external sources of DACs (?? = 0.440, p < 0.05), suggesting that a greater investment in IT correlates with a higher likelihood of incurring high DACs from internal and external sources. Again, our results indicate that R&D investment does not have a statistically significant effect. Consistent with previous results for the first proxy for DACs, these results also suggest that IT investment, rather than R&D investment, is likely to induce high DACs.

In the third test, we measured innovation cost savings as the percentage of costs reduced by innovation in the subsequent year after the IT investment was made. Thus, complementing concurrent measures for DACs in the previous two tests, we indirectly captured the cost-based outcomes in the innovation activity following the IT investment. Our third test relies on a ratio scale that is superior to the binary or categorical scale used in the other two tests. We followed prior research (Laursen and Salter 2006; Leiponen and Helfat 2010) to use a random effects Tobit model with double censoring to examine the relationship between IT investment and innovation cost savings with a ratio scale varying between 0 and 1. The missing values in innovation cost savings decreased our sample size to 2073 observations. Since innovation cost savings could be correlated with CIP, we further controlled for a firm’s prior CIP. Since prior CIP and R&D investment are highly correlated $( r = 0 . 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , we excluded R&D investment from the model to avoid multicollinearity issues. To corroborate our theory, we expect an inverted U-shaped relationship between IT investment and innovation cost savings—as IT investment increases, innovation cost savings are likely to be eaten away when firms incur higher DACs in the innovation activity.

As Table 12 shows, AIC suggests that a curvilinear model has the best fit while BIC indicates the best model fit with control variables only; neither information criterion favors a linear model. Since AIC selects the model that most adequately describes the unknown reality and BIC seeks to find the true model among alternatives, AIC could be more appropriate to indicate model fit when the analysis is exploratory like ours (Brunham and Anderson 2002). Our results show that IT investment has a statistically significant and positive effect on subsequent innovation cost savings $( \beta = 0 . 0 8 0 , p < 0 . 1 )$ and IT investment squared has a statistically significant and negative effect on subsequent innovation cost savings $( \beta = - 0 . 0 3 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ We also found that, on both sides, the positive and negative slopes are statistically significant $( t = 2 . 1 7 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and the turning point appears when IT investment is 1.111 within our data range. These findings corroborate our theory that as firms make a large IT investment, they incur high DACs reducing innovation cost savings.

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Theoretical and Managerial Implications

Two key theoretical implications follow from our research. First, our study integrates research on digital innovation (e.g., Kohli and Melville 2019; Nambisan et al. 2017) with theory on DACs (e.g., Kor et al. 2016; Tan and Mahoney 2005) to deepen our understanding of the complex impacts of IT on innovation. Along with the role of an enabler for innovation, our research theorizes the important role of hindrances stemming from adjustments associated with IT investment. Accounting for both the augmenting and suppressing pathways can deepen the understanding of the dual latent mechanisms through which IT influences innovation. In particular, theory on DACs is a powerful lens for understanding the suppressing pathways and should be considered when examining the impacts of IT investment on innovation.

Second, our research augments prior findings on the positive linear relationship between IT and innovation (e.g., Gómez et al. 2017; Ravichandran et al. 2017; Saldanha et al. 2017; Trantopoulos et al. 2017) by demonstrating a curvilinear relationship between IT investment and CIP. Consistent with prior findings from Aral and Weill (2007), we do not find support for a positive linear relationship between IT investment and CIP. Instead, strong empirical evidence corroborates a curvilinear relationship with an inverted U-shape. Such findings suggest that discovering limits to returns on IT investment for innovation can likely reconcile the mixed findings in extant digital innovation literature and, more broadly, can deepen our understanding of the nonlinearities underlying the ITinnovation relationship.

Our research also provides important managerial implications. IT payoffs are central to IS research and practice, and attention has been recently shifted from financial performance to innovation performance (e.g., Bardhan et al. 2013). Our empirical findings on IT payoffs in innovation can guide managers in their decision-making for IT investment. First, given the advancements in IT, there could be a tendency for managers to think of IT as the panacea for all performance problems. Undeterred by the high failure rate of large IT projects, managers continue to heavily invest in IT (e.g., Anderson et al. 2003; Ho et al. 2017). Our research reports evidence on the possible detrimental effects of IT investment for innovation, which should serve as a cautionary signal, encouraging managers to curb overinvestment in IT.

Second, we analyzed data from a large number of firms in Germany—the largest economy in Europe—and investigated the impacts of IT investment on CIP across a broad spectrum of industries in this national context. Our findings thus serve as a sound basis for managers in many industries to benchmark their IT investment decisions for innovation purposes. Furthermore, our finding on the curvilinear impacts of IT investment on CIP suggests that firms should first experiment with small pilot projects before making a large IT investment. Such a stepwise, experimental and incremental approach to making progressively a larger IT investment could mitigate costly adjustments and minimize disruptions to current operations as the innovation activity proceeds.

## Limitations and Future Research

The contributions of our research must be viewed in light of its limitations. First, we analyze a large-scale panel dataset from firms in a particular country. Future research could collect data from other innovative countries to examine the generalizability of our findings. Second, our research design employs an aggregate indicator of IT investment and does not allow us to tease out the impacts of IT at the system level.

Since examining the entire IT resource bundle in line with the resource-based perspective in which DACs are grounded—which are arguably incurred as new IT systems are required to be integrated into the existing ecosystem with incumbent IT systems—would be of great interest, future research should examine specific IT systems (e.g., supply chain management or customer relationship management systems) to refine our findings.

To assuage DACs associated with IT investment for innovation, firms can adopt one or a combination of the two strategies. First, firms might consider investing in their internal agility by training their employees for replenishing their digital skills to overcome bounded rationality. This internally focused strategy could prove beneficial because knowledge workers with replenished skills are likely to adjust the innovation activity more efficiently. Second, skilled employees could also be brought in from the external environment to increase the flexibility of an organization for dynamic adjustments. However, skilled employees might still need time to acclimatize themselves to the routines of a focal firm before they are productive in the innovation activity (e.g., Tan and Mahoney 2005). Future studies could investigate the comparative efficacy of the internally and externally focused strategies outlined above. Research exploring boundary conditions for these strategies could also yield interesting insights into mitigating DACs associated with IT investment for innovation.

## Conclusion

We investigate the curvilinear impacts of IT investment on innovation by accounting for both the enablers and hindrances associated with IT investment for innovation. We hypothesize an inverted U-shaped relationship between IT investment and CIP owing to the augmenting and suppressing pathways, especially high DACs incurred by firms as they make a large investment in IT. We analyze a large-scale panel dataset from German firms and discovered nonlinearities in the relationship between IT investment and CIP across a broad spectrum of industries. Our findings on the curvilinear relationship between IT and innovation should serve as a cautionary signal to managers and discourage them from overinvesting in IT.
