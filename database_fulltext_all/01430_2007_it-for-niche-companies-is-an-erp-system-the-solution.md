# IT for niche companies: is an ERP system the solution?

Kai A Olsen\* & Per Sætre<sup>†</sup>

\*Molde University College and University of Bergen, Norway, email: kai.olsen@himolde.no, and <sup>†</sup>Molde University College, Norway, email: per.satre@himolde.no

Abstract. Niche companies are per definition idiosyncratic. They survive in a competitive world by mastering a small market niche, providing what their customers need. This often requires a flexible organization, and the ability to customize products. To be more efficient, many of these companies rely on extensive use of IT, often by installing general Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems. These systems have grown from isolated systems that handle planning based on incoming orders and the component structure of the various products, to systems with ambitions to embrace the total functioning of the company including vendor and customer relation management. In this paper, we present four case studies. One company is a part of a large enterprise, but performs niche functions within this enterprise. The other three are small- or medium-sized enterprises. Each of these performs in small niche markets. Common to all is the fact that they encounter problems with the utilization of their ERP systems. The major problem seems to be that the ERP system has an inherent business model that may not conform to the needs of the company. Without a good understanding of the underlying models and the constraints under which the fundamental algorithms operate, it is difficult to use these systems correctly. Even excellent systems may give bad results if they are applied to situations where they are not suited. Further, the monolithic structure of an ERP system, with a rather complicated parameter setting, is often insufficient to mould the system to the needs of a niche company. We discuss these problems based on our four case studies, and offer alternative approaches that may be considered.

Keywords: niche companies, SME, ERP, core functions, proprietary software, development

## INTRODUCTION

Companies that compete in the world market have a drive to be better, to make processes more efficient and to improve the quality of their products. For many years, IT has been seen as a tool for achieving all these aims. As a starting point, the management has used computerized planning systems, the administration has used word processing and accounting systems, the design department has used advanced drawing and modeling tools, and production has used numerically controlled programmable machines. While successful in this approach, most businesses realize that there is a limit to how far isolated systems can be used. More sophisticated systems are needed to solve problems of handling and disseminating complex information, to automate processes within procurement, to get an overview of production, to optimize production, etc. To avoid duplication and to utilize data as best as possible, the need for integrated systems has been widely appreciated. Often, however, this has been interpreted to imply that one large integrated system is the only solution.

These companies therefore turn to the marketplace where a large number of ERP systems are available. The term was initially used for a few large systems that could take care of all functions within a large enterprise (‘company in a box’), but is today used for most ‘all-in-one systems, also for the systems that are tailored for one type of industry. An ERP system is offered as a holistic solution, one complete system that can take care of all phases of information management. Since the term has a strong foothold in the market, nearly all commercial systems that handle administrative tasks term themselves ERP. In this paper, we shall restrict ourselves to using the term for the holistic, all-in-one systems that aim at handling all administrative functions within a company.

The systems are offered as customizable, that is, they can be accommodated to the special needs of the customer. This is achieved both by a modular approach, allowing the customer to chose the modules that are going into his system, and by extensive parameterization, where the ERP system can be tuned to the company. It is not surprising that European surveys show that the fit between system and the company business procedures is by far the most important selection criteria for the information system (IS) used in the company (van Everdingen et al., 2000).

The introduction of an ERP system is known to be a costly undertaking, and serious customers perform a throughout reviewing of the most promising systems before they sign a contract. However, there is often little understanding as to how an ERP system works, and what premises and constraints are inherent in the underlying models. There may be a common understanding that the ERP systems embody best business practices, but general reference models may not be suitable, especially not for a niche company that thrives on its idiosyncrasy. As Soh et al. (2000) point out, there may be a considerable mismatch between the policies and practices embedded in the ERP systems and that of a particular company, industry or country (Kumar & van Hillegersberg, 2000). These mismatches have been the downfall of many ERP implementations (Davenport, 1998).

There is also often a weak metaview in many small- or medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) of their own business, their position in the marketplace, the type of business they represent, market opportunities, and so on. This lack of an overall view does not affect day-to-day operations in marketing, dealing with customers or developing the next version of a product, but becomes important when a new computer system is to be chosen and installed. Then it is required that one can evaluate the reference model of the ERP systems that are considered with regard to the needs of the company, and to have a very good understanding of where the firm is today and where it wants to be in the future.

The sales people that promote their ERP systems are often of little help. Their aim is to sell the system, in practice to show how the customer can utilize their system and how their solution can be applied to the problem. Vendors concentrate on and have knowledge of the possibilities, not on the constraints of the ERP system. While a larger business has the ability to launch a large-scale requirements engineering phase even before a selection is made, for example, using vendor-specific processes (Daneva, 2003), this is seldom possible for smaller- and medium-sized companies.

Then, when the chosen system has been implemented and the promised effects do not materialize, the customer will be told that they do not use the system extensively enough, that such a holistic system will work best when it handles all functions. Added to this is a deep belief that the customer has to adjust to the system, that any other possibility is inefficient and too expensive.

In this paper, we will take the reader through a set of real-world cases, where SMEs try to cope with their ERP systems. Based on these case studies, we will discuss their problems, and offer our analysis of what went wrong in each case. We also offer a presentation of alternative solutions for these companies.

## THE INVESTIGATION

The object of this paper is to report and discuss the effects of ERP installations in a set of case companies. Qualitative studies of IT investments, such as the study reported here, are complex and cannot be performed without understanding the social-technical structure of the organizations involved ( Holland, 1995; Farbey et al., 1999; Vaast & Walsham, 2005; Howard et al., 2006). This includes knowledge of business ideas, markets and competitive advantages, as well as processes and IT functionality. In this study, we have been allowed to work intimately with four companies. In three of the cases, the funding came from a governmental research programme to stimulate cooperation between academia and industry. In the last case (Case 4), we were hired by the company as consultants. A major concern for all companies was to enhance the use of IT to improve business performance. The companies are all niche-oriented with between 100 and 200 employees. All operate in a global market, exporting a major part of their production. They are, in all respects, good representatives of the SMEs that we find in many European countries.

Traditional research, whether positivist or interpretative, seldom allows researchers to go deeply into the cases, and to spend a long time with each company. However, backed by our role as consultants, we were allowed to work closely with employees on all levels and were allowed to access confidential company data on processes, computer systems, requirement analysis, specifications, and so on. Hence we have achieved a thorough understanding of the companies, the properties of their niche, their view of the future, down to business processes and how their current IT systems handle these. Our experience of the above is presented here as a study of the phenomenon of selecting an ERP system in a real-life context.

We are aware of the difficulties in reporting objectively when being deeply involved in the case companies (Hirchheim et al., 1996). Our method of investigation may at best be described as action case, as defined by Braa & Vidgen (1999). They define an idealized framework for IS research identified by the points prediction, understanding and change as illustrated in Figure 1. Prediction is the aim of the systematic reduction of a positivist approach, understanding the aim of an interpretive approach, and, finally, change is connected to an interventionary approach. Braa and Vidgen position action case as indicated, with both an interventionary and interpretative element. This is based on the realization that a case study often has a significant interventionary aspect. However, it should be noted that while our work has an interventionary aspect, it has never been our aim to study the effects of these changes as in the more formal approaches to action research (Susman, 1983; Checkland, 1991). The research performed here has more in common with what has been labelled participative action research (Whyte, 1991).

Action research is based on the understanding that theory can be built on practice itself (Hult & Lennung, 1980; Argyris & Schön, 1991; Checkland, 1991). This is, of course, how much of our scientific base has been built up, by creative practitioners seeing above and beyond their daily tasks. Action research has the advantage that it allows in-depth studies. Our case companies were willing to give us access, and to spend time giving us the information we needed, in the expectation that they would have direct benefit from this work. The disadvantage is that we, as researchers, may need to adhere to more than one master. Formally, this is only a potential problem in one of our four cases, because funding has come from research agencies in the other three. Nevertheless, we were not bounded or restricted by any of the companies. They wanted our open advice as experts on IT.

However, where we give advice and propose solutions, these may become our ‘babies’ and as so may come in conflict with the need for an objective evaluation. Braa & Vidgen (1999) argue that all research performed in an organizational setting will have an interventionary aspect, whether explicit or implicit. That is, as researchers we will always have an influence on the organizations where we conduct our research, perhaps more profoundly the more time we spend and the deeper we go. Thus, this ‘bias due to involvement’ may be something that we should be aware of in any setting and, of course, especially where there is an interventionary element.

Information was gathered by participant observation, by individual interviews and by discussions with personnel on all levels, with operators, foremen, managers and top leadership. We used a free-form style, and had many discussions with each group. The aim of these discussions was not only to gather information, but also to help subjects to get a metaview of their work. On the average, we spent 90 days in each company, spread over a period lasting from 3 months to 3 years. Extensive field notes, including transcriptions, were taken at each visit. These were then used as a basis for constructing subsequent narrations that were sent to the relevant respondents. Finally, these narrations or memos were edited into a final ‘recommendation’ report that was submitted to each company.

Our initial endeavour was to get a thorough knowledge of business ideas, markets, customers and basic operational parameters. Interestingly enough, we found that none of these SMEs had a clearly defined business idea. It seemed that their day-to-day activity never demanded an explicit description of what they were or what they wanted to be. For example, one of our case companies executed approximately 50 orders per year. Each order, better described as a project, had a value of several million US dollars. Many of the orders were won because of the SMEs’ ability to customize the product, to adapt to the requirements of demanding customers. Still, a major policy for the company, as expressed by top management, was to standardize. Even if it was clear that this policy would have come in conflict with the needs of important customers, the balance between the explicit standardization policy and the more implicit customization practice had never been discussed. In fact, we found that the customization rather was viewed as a nuisance in the company since it set some additional demands on the production department. Neither had the potential advantages of a ‘standardization’ been assessed. In this case, we were able to show that the potential benefits of standardization were mostly fictitious, and that the customization was perhaps their most important business advantage.

Second, we studied their IS. We performed this part by studying the functionality of the systems, mainly by going through user manuals, by interviewing users and observing the daily use of the systems. Both manual and computerized IS were included on this process. Third, our aim was to look for process improvement. We suggested ideas that were discussed with employees on different levels, and helped them to put forwards their own ideas on how to improve both the administrative and production side of the business. Even if some of our suggestions went beyond IT, the basis for our work was to see how the companies could make better use of modern IT, either by utilizing existing systems or by procuring new systems. In the latter case, we worked along with company personnel on all levels to specify requirements for new IT systems.

Four cases are not a basis that is large enough for any quantitative study. We feel, however, that Walsham (1993) has a point when he states:

If one adopts a positivist epistemological stance, then statistical generalizability is the key goal. However, from an interpretative position, the validity from an extrapolation from an individual case or cases depends not on the representativeness of such cases in a statistical sense, but on the plausibility and cogency of the logical reasoning used in describing the results from the cases, and in drawing conclusions from them (p. 15).

We show that all the case companies have problems with their ERP systems. Although each company felt that it selected a mainstream solution, it did not get the system to work as expected. We shall try to show that the reason for their problems is that the ERP systems did not support the niche orientation within each company. That is, a general system forces the companies into using general business processes that may come in conflict with the processes needed in each company. Further, because these processes are hidden within a large system, they are not easy to change. Thus, small and flexible organizations become more rigid than necessary.

While we find different symptoms in each case, we argue that all of their problems are due to the fact that the companies operate as niche-oriented SMEs. Being small- or mediumsized offers the possibility of being flexible and customer-oriented, while the niche market orientation often supports the expansion of idiosyncratic business processes. Thus, we expect that similar problems will occur in other niche companies trying to adjust to a general ERP.

If the mainstream solution, going for an all-in-one system, does not work, are there alternatives? We shall answer this question in discussing each case. We shall see that none of these companies has considered the possibility of developing its own customized software. Based on bad experience with software development from years back, it seems that management has eliminated this solution without a more careful analysis. We will argue, based on experience from other companies that proprietary development is a feasible solution today, because:

1 There is no need to develop everything. Proprietary development can be limited to core functions. Off-the-shelf products can be used for everything else.

2 Existing interconnectivity standards can be used to integrate proprietary systems and offthe-shelf products into one system.

3 Modern development tools, from application generators to open source code, will simplify software development.

Thus, our aim with this paper is to make readers aware that there may be problems by enforcing a standard ERP system on a ‘no-standard’ niche company, and that there are alternatives that should be carefully examined.

## CASES

In this section, we shall discuss four different companies, all of which are running an ERP system. Three of these are small- to medium-sized niche companies operating on the world market. One is a division within a large oil company. Here the decision to go for an ERP system has been taken on a higher level, but apart from this they are in a very similar situation to the other three firms.

## Case 1: is MRP what they need?

Our Case 1 company produces heavy mechanical equipment. Its existence in the market is based on the commercialization of a patented invention. They manufacture one product, a dump truck with several different models and a set of add-on options. The product is quite complex and has many thousand parts, some are minor bolts and nuts, others are expensive items, some of which have to be ordered at least months in advance. Because of the long lead time for these major components, the production load and mix are very stable. These components are effectively setting the metronome for the production plan. Because most of the manufacturing is done in house, production is also limited by total capacity.

We were involved with this company in their search for an ERP system. The requirements specification had already been developed, and defined the needs for a Material Resource Planning (MRP) based system. MRP is a method for scheduling production and procurement. The method requires a detailed description of the products, decomposed in a component structure – the Bill-of-Material. The Bill-of-Material tells how a component is made up of subcomponents. Subcomponents may then be split into lower-level components in a recursive manner. For example, a truck may be decomposed into the components engine, frame, chassis, etc. If the engine is bought from a supplier, there may be no need for any further decomposition, while other components may be decomposed until we are describing basic parts. For example, the frame can be decomposed into parts such as steel bars, nuts and bolts. Based on incoming orders, for top-level products, the MRP algorithm will calculate the need for producing or procuring subcomponents, taking into account the number of items in stock or what is already ordered. Results are presented as lists with component number, the date to be ordered or produced, delivery or finished date, the date for assembly, and so on. These are then used as a basis for manual or automatic procurement or job handling.

Our task was to aid in the selection process, finding the system that best suited their needs. However, it is a moot question whether an MRP-based ERP system is what this company really needs. The problem is not that MRP cannot handle their acquisition and production planning, but more that MRP seems to be both an overkill and a straitjacket. MRP works at its best when there is a high number of components and an unstable product mix, ie when the complexity is too high for handling manually (Gelders & van Wassenhove, 1985; Maes & van Wassenhove, 1991). However, in our case company, the long-term contracts for major parts (among these a diesel engine and a transmission unit) set severe constraints for production flexibility. Instead of viewing these constraints as a disadvantage, they should be used as a basis for creating a planning strategy. While MRP can work within these constraints, it cannot help the business to utilize the constraints to a maximum.

Given the stable production plan, the situation is ideal for ‘just in time’ planning where replenishment of both in-house production and procurement is based on ‘power of two’ strategies (Maxwell & Muckstadt, 1985). Such repeatable policies have the advantage that they can be tuned. After each period, one can discuss if there are ways to increase efficiency even further. This is quite simple for repeatable tasks. When a job is performed again and again on the same day of the week, perhaps in the same order in a sequence of jobs, it is easier to see how it can be performed more efficiently. For example, with predetermined pick-up points, route planning algorithms can find an optimum, with regard to both distance and load utilization. Error frequency can also be reduced. If the truck driver expects to find two pallets at the supplier load area every Monday morning, she will probably go in and ask the day she finds only one. Such a system could operate with a high degree of automation, allowing management to focus on the important parts, setting up long-term agreements with suppliers, handling exceptions, and so on.

Of course, MRP-driven replenishment might also benefit from the stable production plan, but it adds an unnecessary complexity to the process. MRP tends to focus on lot sizes and lead times rather than on periodicity leading to more ‘nervousness’ in the job shop production (Vollman et al., 2005). For example, small changes in the underlying data (a scrapped item, a supplier that cannot deliver the full lot in this period, a delay in production, etc.) may have a large impact on the output from MRP calculations. Of course, these exceptions must also be handled within a cyclic policy, but this can be accomplished within the constraints of keeping the schedule, for example, by adjusting the size of the next batch instead of moving the order in time as an MRP-based system would do.

We were able to influence this company to do a set of tests using more cyclic ordering of goods from outside suppliers. However, in spite of highly promising test results, the manufacturer decided to go along on the MRP path, probably because this seemed to be what others did. By interviewing core personnel, we found that there was an expectation that the system would keep track of what was needed at any time, but there was no clear understanding of how this rather complex algorithm worked. What they did not see, according to our view, was that there were clear differences between themselves and some of their reference companies and that the standard solution was not very well suited for their needs.

## Case 2: can MRP handle procurement of all types of papers?

Case 2 is quite different from Case 1, although there are superficial similarities. Both companies produce mechanical equipment of their own design, both are medium-sized niche companies and both serve the world market. However, where Case 1 has a serial-oriented production, Case 2 has a make-to-order production philosophy. While the Case 1 company delivers a complete product, the Case 2 company delivers products to be implemented in largescale systems. For a given model, the customer specifies key attributes. In addition, a typical order also requires some engineering. However, it would still be an exaggeration to call this an engineer-to-order production company, because most variants are determined by setting parameter values within a generic Bill-of-Material structure.

The company has an ERP system in place. We were called in as a result of the problems with delays, mainly caused by some purchased components. After analysing the production structure and important procurement parameters, we separated components into three groups: A, B and C. To take Group C first:

Group C are the simple components, i.e. cheap components with short lead times.

Group A are the complicated components, the components that were causing trouble for the company because of long and unstable lead times.

Group B are the rest, i.e. components above a given cost but with known and stable lead times.

Based on this simple characterization, the problems that the firm has encountered become more apparent. They tried to force all procurement, of A, B and C items, through the MRP part of the ERP system. For group B this was uncomplicated, but not so for the other two groups.

The C papers come in high numbers (bits and bolts) and are quite inexpensive, and it is not easy to tell operators that they need to register any exception, e.g. a scrapped bolt, in the computer system. Thus, items in stock will never be as indicated by the computer. The firm had already a two-container system (where a supplier takes away and fills up the empty box) in place for some of these components, and our recommendation was to increase the use of these simple mechanisms for avoiding administrative overhead and the implications of bad data. If two-container systems are used for more expensive components, we get the drawback that such a system may force unnecessary procurement, for example, by filling up a container of a component that will no longer be in use. We have therefore proposed an ‘intelligent twocontainer system’, where the foreman takes the decision as to whether an empty container is to be filled or not. Background data for making this decision will be offered by the system. Note that this will always be up to the discretion of the foreman – if no action is taken, the containers will automatically be filled. This may be seen as a Kanban system, but is here applied only to a part of the chain.

Group A is more difficult to handle. These components are expensive, and the cost of keeping these items in inventory is therefore high. A further impediment is the requirement that these components have to be certified by a class agency (such as Lloyds), where it is the discretion of the customer to choose the agency. An inventory solution therefore requires storing items for each relevant classification agency. In practice, this is impossible. Thus, the firm has to rely on the MRP-based system. However, because some of these components are procured on a project-oriented basis, lead times are unstable. This reduces the accuracy of the MRP calculations. In itself, this problem can be reduced by operating with excessive lead times, but this diminishes the ability to react to market demands.

It was interesting to note that the production delays were quite recent, although the firm had been using the same ERP system, the same suppliers and similar components for many years. Earlier, procurement was handled manually by a very experienced person who overrode the system when necessary. Because of the retirement of their experienced procurer and a more complex product mix, it was found necessary to use the ERP system for procurement of all papers. The company was convinced that all of their problems would go away when the ERP system handled everything. They trusted in the ‘all-in-one’ philosophy, expecting that the system would work at its best when everything was included.

One way of solving the problems with regard to the class agencies is to hold the classification process until the last possible moment. This can be achieved by stocking more generic parts at the suppliers. In one case, this was implemented by requiring the supplier to keep an inventory of (raw) material at their site, for each basic dimension and for each class agency. Orders for items manufactured from these materials could then be processed faster. In a second case, suppliers received two types of orders: first, one order to manufacture a generic ‘class-less’ component; then when the actual class agency was known, an order to let a class agency certify the item. In practice, production and classification orders were independent as the manufacturing and classification order could have different sets of items. Thus, lead times were reduced at the same time as stock levels were kept to a minimum, without significant burdens on behalf of the supplier.

In practice, we found a way to reduce practical lead times for each of the type A components, often, however, with a different policy for each component. The problem, of course, was that these special cases were difficult or impossible to handle within the ERP system. For example, the last example discussed above, sending both a generic production order and a classification order, was, understandably, not a part of the functionality of this system. To handle these cases, we proposed a separate procurement system to handle type A components.

The aim of this semiautomatic system is to mimic what the retired expert did manually, i.e. it should have the ability to keep track of all data pertaining to part orders, their status, delays, and so on. Such a system could store documents and email messages as well as more formalized data. For example, when examining the procurement process for a part, the user should be able to retrieve any information pertaining to this component, such as the initial order, the confirmation from the supplier, an email message from the supplier notifying the company of a delay, and so on. The system could further present tabular and visual presentations of supply and demand on a time scale, showing when new parts were scheduled to arrive and when they were needed in production. These overview displays would permit the procurer to monitor the supply situation for these parts on a daily basis. Delays that could effect production would be indicated, and the procurer would then be in a position to enforce the necessary actions in cooperation with the system. That is, this system can be seen as an implementation of the concept which Zuboff (1988) coined ‘informate’, using IT to produce new information.

While the company management clearly saw that such a system would give the information they needed, it was decided to try to handle matters within the ERP system. They were afraid of violating the ‘all in one system’ principle that their software vendor had stressed so strongly.

## Case 3: was an ERP system the solution?

The Case 3 company is a division within a large oil company. The enterprise encompasses all functions such as oil exploration, refining, delivery and marketing. Their customers are power companies down to ordinary customers buying gas for their car. The task for the division that we present here is to supply North Sea exploration and production rigs with everything they need, such as drilling equipment, chemicals and food. They also handle all waste from the rigs. In order to do this job, they have a large base on the north-west coast of Norway and operate a fleet of supply vessels. These are special-purpose vessels that can handle large loads (containers, pipes and fluids), which can be loaded and offloaded when the vessel operates close to a rig – even in bad weather. The goods are shipped from other sites in Norway to the supply base by truck or boat. This part is executed by a few national carriers having a contract with the oil company.

Like many other large enterprises, the parent company had the problem that a huge number of different IT systems were used in all parts of the organization, and those data available in one system were not available to others. To solve this problem, it was decided to remove most existing systems, replacing them by a large ERP system. The approximate \$150 million project was well planned. The installation kept to budget, with regard to both costs and time limits.

In their thesis, two master students looked at ways to optimize the running of the supply vessels. An earlier thesis had shown that there was a potential of utilizing each supply vessel better, for example, by improved loading of the vessel and by better route planning. Input data to the optimization process would be information on loads that were to arrive on the supply base, e.g. size, weight, contents and the destination rig for these loads. We expected that our students would get all the data they needed from the ERP system. However, this was not the case.

A more thorough study showed that the ERP system could only supply a fraction of the essential data. One reason was that some of the rigs were operated by other contractors. These used their own systems to procure supplies, and data were therefore not available in the ERP system of our oil company. But important data were also missing from the rigs that were operated by the oil company itself. The reason was that rig personnel used many communication channels in order to procure goods, e.g. fax, telephone and email. In a hectic situation, the data on these orders were not entered in the ERP system at all, or were entered at a much later date. In practice, it was therefore impossible to perform the optimization and route planning task with the data from the ERP system.

A good alternative would have been to require these data from the transport firms that carried the goods to the supply base. This would have been rather a simple process, because only a few transport companies were involved and because all the necessary data, container type, weight, type of content are known when a truck is loaded. It could have been implemented by a simple Web interface. By requiring the firms to report loads at the time the trucks left for the supply base, the base would have ample time to plan the next shipment. However, such a system – even one as simple as this – would violate the ‘all in one’ philosophy. The optimization process was therefore put on ice until the necessary data could be obtained from the ERP system.

## Case 4: does a variant generator solve the problem?

Case 4 is an engineer-to-order company. The company makes one customized product that is reengineered for every order according to customer’s specification. The number of orders is small, about 50 a year, but with a high value of each order. The ability to produce exactly to the customers’ specification is the basis for the company’s existence in the marketplace, although they operate in a high-cost area.

Because of both an increase in the number of orders and the need of customization for each order, they encountered problems with their manual planning systems. The company was also unable to calculate a reliable price as well as setting a realistic due date for the order during the request for quote process. It was therefore decided that an ERP system was needed.

The process set up for finding the best ERP system was a textbook example. A committee was set down with representatives from various departments. The day-to-day efforts were organized by the head of the IT department, while the CEO of the firm chaired the committee. The CEO was also deeply engaged in the process in the role of ‘sponsor’ for the system. The committee started by defining and formalizing what they wanted to achieve from the ERP system. Based on this, a list of potential systems was set up, and the committee performed an indepth study of every system, including a visit to reference customers. Finally a contract was made with an ERP vendor who delivered a system that seemed to fulfil all of the company’s requirements. This vendor had competent personnel locally and several successful implementations in the area.

We were called in 6 months later when the installation process seemed to stall. The reason for this was not difficult to uncover. In the requirement specification for the ERP system, it was clearly stated that it must be possible to adjust the product description, mainly the Bill-of-Material, to each order. To fulfil this requirement, the vendor of the system wanted to use a variant generator that had been added to the last version of the system. While a traditional Bill-of-Material states which components go into a product, a variant generator will allow alternatives for these relationships. These alternatives may be controlled by a set of conditions that specify which alternative component is to be used in each case. By such a system, it is possible to use one Bill-of-Material for a large set of similar products.

The vendor had given demonstrations of how the variant generator could be applied, and had also given a reference to a local company that used the generator. Our company was therefore quite astonished when the vendor had problems implementing this part of the system.

Several methods for handling variant-oriented manufacturing are discussed in the literature (see e.g. van Veen, 1992; Olsen et al., 1997; Olsen & Sætre, 1998). Mechanisms for describing a diverse set of products in terms of variants are also included in many ERP systems. These systems are complex and it is difficult, both for the vendor’s sales representatives and for the customer, to have an overview of their possibilities and limitations. Our task was therefore to explain that a variant generator requires the concept of a generic product which is described with all possible variants ahead of order specification. An order must then include specification of a sufficient set of parameters or conditions to produce a specific product description from the generic description. For example, if colour is a variant parameter, an order specification must include a value for colour. The customer can only choose between predefined possibilities. However, in an engineer-to-order production, the customer is given much more freedom. Here the customer’s specification, of faster, higher, stronger, lighter, etc., will normally result in new engineering, redesign or readjustment of products, drawings, components, etc. The set of variants is unlimited, and not predetermined.

In the case company, these factors had not been understood, neither by the customer nor by the software vendor. Certainly, the generator worked well in the reference installation, but even if there were superficial similarities, the reference company was within the variant philosophy. Yes, both firms produced mechanical products, both were custom-oriented, both operated in the world market, but while our case company engineered to customer specifications, the other let customers specify the value of predetermined variables.

In the sad story of Case 4, the ERP vendor was able to convince the company that the smart thing to do was to adjust the business philosophy to the ERP system. The company and the ERP vendor spent years trying to accommodate the production modes to the software. In the end, the discrepancy between the system and the real world became so apparent that the ERP project was dropped.

## DISCUSSION

We have presented four cases. Each of these shows that an ERP implementation is not straightforward for the type of companies that we consider here. Three of the case companies have moved into niches in order to survive in a highly competitive world market, offering expensive but quality products that can be customized to a high degree. That is, their business idea is based on being different, not to run a standard shop. The other case (Case 3) is performing a very specialized function within a larger enterprise, and can in this respect be viewed as a niche company.

The question can be raised whether an ERP system, with a built-in reference model, is the correct solution for niche companies. Although the ERP system has potential to be adjusted for each implementation, the ‘black box’ nature of the system is a problem. As we have seen from some of the cases presented here, the customers do not always have a clear understanding of what they have bought, and how to apply the system in the most beneficial way.

Clearly, our case companies need more expertises, not only experts with a thorough knowledge of ERP systems and the basis for the algorithms and methods used within the system, but also knowledge of the company, its business ideas and future prospects. In some cases, expert knowledge of the company and the ERP system will make it possible to parameterize the system according to the requirements. In other cases, one may find that the ERP system does not fulfil the basic needs of the company. Alternative solutions should then be considered.

An interesting alternative that we have used with success in several cases is to let the company develop its own core IT systems. When we propose this possibility, we are fighting two myths that both have a very strong foothold in industry:

1 A company must be willing to adjust to a standard ERP system. The system incorporates ‘best practice’ solutions and it is therefore important to use the ERP for all applications. Only then will one get the full benefits of integration.

2 In-house development is extremely expensive and impossible to consider for small- or medium-sized companies.

We shall come back to these ‘myths’ later in our discussion of developing proprietary software.

## Adjusting to the ERP

In some settings, one should be willing to adjust internal processes to that of a standard system. For example, it is reasonable to be willing to replace own formats of forms and printouts whenever necessary. The cost of implementing proprietary formats would be high and the advantage gained diminutive as the formats provided by the system are probably as good as the formats already in place in the company. It may even be appropriate to change important processes within the company when this is a deliberate effort to move the company into a new technology. A good example could be the airline that simplifies their price and discount structure in order to make it easier to implement a user-friendly Web-based booking system.

Enterprise Resource Planning systems are developed for the mainstream company, or for a set of companies within an industry. For example, we find ERP systems tailored for the printing business, for the pharmaceutical industry, etc. If a company finds itself to be a typical member of one of these groups, it should probably be able to adjust to the ERP system, or the ERP system can be adjusted to the company through parameterization. However, companies may also end up by adjusting their business ideas to the reference model of the ERP system, for example, to reduce the level of customization in order to be able to use the system more efficiently. Here the overall aim of making the company more competitive is compromised by the effort to get the ERP system working. We see examples of this in all our four cases.

## Does the ‘all-in-one’ idea work?

Having data in many different systems, perhaps stored with many formats and accessible by different user interfaces is a nuisance. It is therefore natural that many executives go for the allin-one solution, i.e. installing a company-wide ERP system. However, this may solve only a part of the problem. The important question here is to find out why there were so many systems in the first place. A well-run organization does not develop or buy new systems if the systems already in place do the work. But the real world is complex. Each division or line will have to interface with their part of the world. For example, while the rig executive needs detailed specifications of a drill head, the division running the supply vessels only needs container type, weight, date of arrival, destination rig and perhaps a priority. The component may be the same, a drill head, but very different data and services are needed in order to procure and transport such a component.

The disadvantage of allowing proprietary systems is that these may not be integrated with other systems. In a large enterprise, the head office may not get the aggregated data they need from a proprietary divisional system, neither may other divisions be able to access the system.

Within a company, data may be hidden in different proprietary systems, and not be available to other systems. As a result, similar data may be stored in many places, making maintenance a huge problem. However, this is not a fundamental part of proprietary systems. Today we have the tools necessary to integrate singular systems. This can be done by letting the systems use the same databases, using the existing standards for database interconnectivity. $\mathsf { O r } ,$ in a large enterprise, one could implement Web services for each proprietary system, thus allowing access from the outside (Nghiem, 2002; Chatterjee et al., 2003).

Using our Case 3 as an example, we could allow the supply base to develop its own systems as long as these offered Web services to the rest of the organization. For example, the head office would need a service from which they could get aggregated data on all transports, and the rig operators would probably need an interface where they could obtain all data on the next shipment. Web services can be implemented either as manual routines, for example, accessible over a Web interface, or as automatic services, available to other application programmes. In the latter case, the request and response would be performed automatically, very similar to the way we perform procedure calls within a computer programme. Today we have many tools for implementing these solutions. By using these techniques we can offer local flexibility, allowing proprietary systems, and still retain many of the important benefits of an all-inone solution. That is, the Web services provide a common interface to all systems, while the systems themselves may be very different, and may offer their individual set of additional services locally.

## Retaining control

Developing a proprietary system is a strategic alternative to a general ERP system. That is, instead of trying to fit niche functionality into general business processes, a company can leverage its idiosyncrasies by developing their own core systems. This approach acknowledges that the existence of most niche companies is based on their knowledge of a small market segment, the customers, their needs and a set of services and products to fulfil these needs. Even in a time when outsourcing is a hot topic, no one would suggest that a company outsource their core functions. In some respects, this is what one does when using a standard ERP system (see Carr, 2003; 2004). These systems integrate all the information and all processes within the company – accounting, financial, customer relation data, supply chain data, production planning, and so on. They provide information to management as well as to the foreman. In this respect, the company is embodied in the ERP system. Thus, if the niche company wants to advocate its idiosyncrasy, it needs to develop its core functions into a system that reflects its own business ideas, practices and style. This is a natural development when IT systems move from automating simple functions to being knowledge systems, shifting from administrative to strategic importance. The IT system will now be a part of the company’s competitive advantage, not to be shared by others.

As an example, our Case 4 company, the engineer-to-order manufacturer, should consider a system that enables it to keep a high level of customization while performing internal processes from design through production to service in an efficient manner. Because of a relatively low number of orders, an IS might offer better advantages than a typical automation system. An IS would solve many of the problems that the company is encountering today – maintaining a high degree of flexibility. In such a setting, the system will keep track of information, while the decisions are made manually. While automation requires a high degree of formalization, an IS can be used with lower formalization levels, thus allowing more flexibility. For example, in order to use MRP techniques to calculate the need for components, a rigid product structure must be in place. If the idea is instead to assist the user, an IS could provide historical data such as specifications and drawings for earlier product variants, and assist the competent user in modifying these structures to the current specifications. A specific Bill-of-Material for a new order could then be made partly by specifying values of common variables and partly by a manual process.

Because no such system was found in the market, the company should have developed its own core functions instead of trying to accommodate the business to the ERP system. Such a development would force the company to go through all core function, defining terminology, data records, processes and functions, a very important task in itself. Further, one would obtain a system that could be developed along with the company, where every change in business strategies, internal processes or the environment could be replicated in the system. That is, the company would have complete control over all important business functions. Such a system would give a competitive advantage in the marketplace.

Rapid change to market demand is a must for most companies. With full control of the core functions, the IT system can change with the company. We saw an example in Case 2, where the company split orders to a supplier, first sending a generic order, then an order for classification. These orders were handled manually in our case company, shifting the administrative load to the supplier. Luckily, this supplier had control over its own IT system, and it was quite simple for them to modify the order handling and planning part to accommodate the split orders. That is, the supplier was able not only to uphold but also to strengthen the relationship with its important customer while maintaining efficiency. This functionality would have been very difficult to achieve in a standard ERP system, as such a change would have affected the core part of the system.

## In-house development

An ERP system is a huge software package. Even the customization, the parameter setting, of such a system is a serious undertaking. At the outset, it seems improbable that any business will succeed in designing their own system. However, a small company will only need a fraction of the functionality implemented in a standard ERP system. Further, there is no need to reinvent the wheel. All applications that can be adequately served by off-the-shelf products should be excluded from the in-house development. For most companies, this list will include office packages, CAD/CAM software, accounting and finance systems.

Are we back in the old days with a set of isolated systems? No, by relying on modern standards, such as the query language SQL for relational databases, methods for connecting to databases (such as ODBC), macro facilities within off-the-shelf products, export/import routines in office programmes and XML for structuring data, different systems within a company can share both functionality and data. If we need connectivity to the outside, this can be implemented by Web services, as discussed previously. In this way, we can realize the basic stratagem behind the all-in-one, making data available to all systems and users. Data are stored only once, but in this case in several interconnected databases.

We have developed systems based on this principle for one manufacturer, a foundry that cast and machine propeller blades. All applications that are proprietary to the business, the core functions, have been implemented using a standard development tool. We have used programming techniques that have much in common with the rapid application development methodology that is based on extensive use of prototyping (Beynon-Davies et al., 2000; Gittleman, 2004). Other applications common to the industry or general business functions are handled by off-the-shelf software. The in-house developed system acts as ‘glue’ between these packages. For example, order entry, planning and production control are a part of the core functions in this company. Whenever an order is completed, the in-house programme will store all data needed for sending an invoice to the customer directly into the database of the standard accounting system. Next time when this system is run, it will print an invoice, calculate valueadded tax, update the necessary accounts, etc. From the user’s point of view, there are two systems but only one database.

In principle, what we do is to categorize the application in different parts:

1 idiosyncratic functions (core part);

2 industry functions (pertaining to all businesses within this type of industry); and

3 general business functions (common to most businesses).

we also solve the need for computer systems for each part separately. For part (3) there will be a wide range of software packages offered. The company can choose whatever it wants, as long as the system supports open standards. For (2) one will have a limited number of products, and in some areas it may even be necessary to start development projects within industry organizations, dividing the costs between all participants. For the core functions (1) it is important that the company is in full control, that the system is developed according to its needs and that it can evolve along with the company. This part of the IT system must support the idiosyncrasy of the company, and give it a competitive edge. The integration between the idiosyncratic part and the standard system is presented in Figure 2.

Usually a company has to develop only a small fraction of the IT functions needed. For smalland medium-sized companies, there are also a range of development systems available that can be used to make this process effective. A standard approach is to utilize a programming language such as Java or Visual Basic and include open source components. An alternative is to use one of several ‘application generators’ that integrate user interface, a programming language and a database interface. These developing tools increase the effectiveness of a systems developer tremendously, compared with traditional programming. It is also important that they support prototyping, which is so important in the development of proprietary systems for one set of users. The simplicity of modifying forms, reports, database or programming code is also important in this setting. For example, often we find ourselves in a situation where we modify the system as it is used. This works fine as long as users and developers work on separate modules. Since core software models the business processes, they are changed on a regular basis. From our experience we see that we perform small changes perhaps as often as twice a month in this company, and major modifications and additions perhaps twice a year.

Gluing together different systems has some disadvantages for users. Even if the systems are connected behind the scenes, they will have different user interfaces. On the other hand, modern software packages have great commonality in their ‘look & feel’. There is also the advantage that one can choose the best solution within any area. If one at a later stage finds that another off-the-shelf package may do a better job, it is quite easy to replace a programme, as long as the new system supports the underlying standards. It is also important that the costs of buying and licensing standard off-the-shelf software are in most cases negligible compared with that of ERP systems.

Thus, one often ends up with a higher level of functionality than if everything came from the same ERP vendor, at a much lower cost. It is also interesting to note that the major part of the development effort often is in the early phases, that is, in the requirement specification. The reason for this is, of course, that designing core functions could just as well be defined as Business Process Reengineering, Total Quality Management, Supply Chain Management or any other process that aims at making the company more competitive. Here the effort is as much to clarify and develop good processes as that of actual system development. When these parts have come into place, our experience shows that the actual programming has been a straightforward phase.

When implementing an ERP system, one will to some extent get support from the reference models embodied in the system. If the needs of a company are closely related to the basic philosophy of the ERP system, this parameter setting may be a simple process. In all other cases, it may be as time-consuming as programming, especially if one has to work around the reference model. The system design and programming phase of the in-house alternative may not be directly compared with the ‘parameter’ setting phase of an ERP installation, but programming may also be a part of ERP implementation where it is necessary to develop customized functions that work along with the standard parts. Training is necessary in both cases, but from our own experience we have found that training is quite simple with the in-house alternative, as the system will be so closely modelled to the needs of the users that most functions will be intuitive.

The largest difference is the end result. After an ERP installation the company will have a system that is used by many others. If the company is similar to these, they will have the advantage that development costs are divided among many. If not, the disadvantage will be the difficulty of getting necessary functions implemented as a part of the standard system. Further, at best they will operate as well as their competitors. With in-house development they will have a system that can advance with the company, a system that is their own and that can be modified whenever necessary. This may give a competitive advantage.

In-house development requires IT competence on a high level. However, internal IT competencies are probably necessary for any company that relies on extensive IT usage, independent of the implementation strategy (Caldeira & Ward, 2002). A company will need employees or outside consultants that are experts in systems development, and also know the company, its products and markets. That would have been very difficult 20 years ago, but today most universities have established strong programmes in IT, on all levels. There are candidates available that, over time, have the potential to develop the systems that a niche company needs. This is a long-term effort, but with the ‘glue’ principle the proprietary systems can be built up in a modular manner, using one module at a time.

For the foundry mentioned previously, we started nearly 10 years ago by developing a planning system. At that time, the company had only a general idea of their future production plan, and it was difficult to set a realistic due date on orders. Scheduling was a complex process since capacity was based on the different combinations of orders that could be performed each day, not on simple time-resource calculations. The planning part has later been extended with a system that handles core administrative functions. In this process, we have also been able to involve both master and PhD students. For example, a PhD student has developed an algorithm to optimize the production planning system (Nonås & Olsen, 2005). This algorithm alone gives a 10–20% productivity gain, and is only possible to implement in a proprietary system.

For another part of this company, we have developed algorithms and systems that automate processes for production control. Some of this functionality has been implemented in order to solve an explicit problem, such as the planning system; other parts have been implemented more as an experiment or on a nice-to-have basis. Today we experience that many of the latter functions have given the company a clear competitive advantage, allowing them to handle extremely complex orders that are an important source of revenue.

## CONCLUSION

Based on four case studies, we have raised the question of whether an ERP system is the best solution for niche companies in all situations. These companies thrive on being special, in product line, in their ability to customize products, in their agility or in customer-relation management. A general ERP system will, as we show from all our four cases, easily come in conflict with these idiosyncrasies. The ‘black box’ nature of many ERP systems is also a problem, as business processes embedded in the system may not be visible for the user. We have observed that companies have neither the necessary overview of what such systems can offer, nor of the reference models embodied within the systems. In some cases, companies may not even have a clear metaview of their own business.

Today most companies experience a situation where they have to react to new demands, from customers, regulators or as a result of technological changes, at all times. That is, their situation is dynamic – not static. If the business processes are modelled in their IT systems, these must also be changed on a regular basis. Thus, they must have control over core software. For many companies, this can only be achieved with the use of proprietary systems. The alternative, travelling to international user conferences to advocate for a modification in a generic system, is not productive.

As an alternative to using an ERP system, we have discussed in-house development of core functions, using off-the-shelf products for other and more standard functions. This process also requires a good understanding of system goals, processes and data. However, one is, to a greater extent, forced to work through the specification when choosing the in-house approach. That is, the company can acquire a metaview of its business processes as the specification is developed, and there will be time to study alternative processes based on the possibility that IT offers.

By ‘gluing’ together different systems we can achieve a holistic IT solution. This is made possible by a set of formal and de facto standards that allows different programmes to share information. Further, developers today have a wide range of efficient high-level development tools available. Together with large libraries of premade components, this makes it possible for a company to develop its own IT systems with moderate costs.

Four cases are not enough for a scientific conclusion. However, that has not been the aim of this paper. We hope that we, based on the experience with these four companies, have conveyed that an ERP system may not always be the best solution for a niche company. Whether it is or not depends on the level of idiosyncrasy of the company. While some companies may use a standard ERP system or tune such a system to their needs, and while some may carry out their niche activities outside the IT system, others will find that only a proprietary IT system can fulfil their needs.

With proprietary development, the IT system can not only support the special business processes of the niche company, but also move along with the company into the future. The inhouse development effort can be used to align the IT systems with changes in processes on a dynamic basis. That is, every decision for a change can be implemented in the system immediately. Thus, one gets an IT system that supports the agility of a modern company. Further, IT can now be an input to the strategic decisions made in the company, where the freedom of having full control of core IT functions can be used to implement completely new ways of performing business processes. Of course, competitors may follow along and use the same approach. However, it may often be a good strategy to attack from a different position. Not all competitors have the ability to compete on another playing field, such as IT. The companies that build up competence in this area may therefore get an important strategic advantage.
