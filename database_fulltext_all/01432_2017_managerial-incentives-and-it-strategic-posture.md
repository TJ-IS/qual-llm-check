# Managerial Incentives and IT Strategic Posture

Ling Xue,<sup>a</sup> Gautam Ray,<sup>b</sup> Xia Zhao<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Computer Information Systems, J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, Georgia 30303; <sup>b</sup> Information and Decision Sciences, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, Minnesota 55455; <sup>c</sup> Department of Information Systems and Supply Chain Management, Bryan School of Business and Economics, University of North Carolina at Greensboro, Greensboro, North Carolina 27410 Contact: lxue5@gsu.edu (LX); rayxx153@umn.edu (GR); x\_zhao3@uncg.edu (XZ)

Abstract. This study examines how managerial incentives may drive firms to adopt a proactive strategic posture to implement more information technology (IT) than competitors. We consider both performance incentives that motivate managers to enhance firm returns and risk incentives that motivate managers to take risks. Our empirical analysis shows that while the proactiveness in IT strategic posture leads to both firm returns and firm risk, risk incentives rather than performance incentives essentially drive the proactiveness in IT strategic posture. These findings highlight the issue of managerial risk aversion and the important role of risk incentives in strategic IT decisions. Our study also shows that in diversified firms, risk incentives have a stronger marginal impact on the proactiveness in IT strategic posture in secondary business areas than in primary business areas. Performance incentives, however, may even generate a negative marginal impact on the proactiveness in IT strategic posture in secondary business areas. These results generate important implications for corporate owners regarding how to use various managerial incentives to motivate strategic IT decisions.

## 1. Introduction

Although information technology (IT) is a generalpurpose technology, firms often difer in their strategic posture in taking IT initiatives (Chan et al. 1997). In particular, while some firms adopt a proactive posture to take the lead in employing new IT initiatives, other firms may choose a reactive posture to follow existing IT practices (Lu and Ramamurthy 2010, Ravichandran and Liu 2011, Mithas et al. 2013). Prior information systems (IS) research contends that firms’ proactive strategic posture in IT can explain many observable IT behaviors and outcomes. For example, proactive firms are suggested to be able to better utilize IT to achieve organizational agility (Lu and Ramamurthy 2011), seize more organization-specific IT innovation opportunities (Swanson and Ramiller 2004), integrate IS planning with business planning (King and Teo 2000), dynamically adjust their digital business strategies (Mithas et al. 2013), and realize more significant performance improvement over time (Lu and Ramamurthy 2010).

A proactive strategic posture requires that top managers of the firm adopt a proactive stance in their strategic decision making (Covin and Slevin 1989). IS research also suggests that the individual incentives of top managers plays an important role in shaping a firm’s strategic IT decisions (e.g., Armstrong and

Sambamurthy 1999). However, how top managers are motivated to adopt a proactive IT strategic posture has been underinvestigated. When considering how internal organizational processes (Ravichandran and Liu 2011) and external environmental factors (Mithas et al. 2013) influence the proactiveness in a firm’s IT strategic posture, prior studies often take the top managers’ individual-level incentives as given. In a separate stream of IS research, studies have shown that managerial incentives can be ofered through compensation mechanisms to influence IT decisions and outcomes (Talmor and Wallace 1998, Anderson et al. 2000, Masli et al. 2014). Yet these studies were not specific about any IT strategic posture, and they only focused on one type of managerial incentive, that is, incentive for performance enhancement. Therefore, there is a need to study how individual-level managerial incentives, especially diferent types of incentives, may take efect to motivate a proactive IT strategic posture at the firm level.

We adopt the principal-agent perspective (Grossman and Hart 1983, Jensen and Murphy 1990) to examine how managerial incentives take efect in shaping a firm’s IT strategic posture. The principal-agent framework assumes that conflicts of interest exist between the principal (the corporate owner) and the agent (the top manager) and results in the agency problem. To align the strategic orientations of top managers with the interest of the firm, corporate owners need to provide managerial incentives through compensation. The literature has considered two types of managerial incentives (e.g., Core and Guay 2002, Coles et al. 2006, Low 2009): performance incentives and risk incentives. Performance incentives are compensation mechanisms designed to tie top managers’ wealth income with a firm’s performance and motivate managers to enhance the firm’s performance. Risk incentives are compensation mechanisms designed to motivate managers, especially risk-averse managers, to take business-related risks that otherwise they would be reluctant to take. We follow the literature to consider both types of incentives.

The consideration of both incentives helps advance the understanding of managerial incentives in the IT context, whereas prior IS studies have focused only on performance incentives. For example, performance incentives may not be efective in driving risky firm strategies as performance incentives may escalate the risk aversion of managers (May 1995). Risk incentives, however, can drive many risky firm strategies, such as reseach and development (R&D) expenditure, business focus, corporate leverage, resource exploration, corporate derivative holding, etc. (Cohen et al. 2000, Coles et al. 2006, Knopf et al. 2002, Rajgopal and Shevlin 2002). In our case, the consideration of both incentives is warranted as proactive IT strategic posture generates both the performance impact and the risk impact. First, the proactiveness in strategic posture entails gaining a competitive advantage and improving a firm’s performance by acting on new opportunities (Lumpkin and Dess 1996). Prior IS research suggests that adopting innovative IT is a key to establishing competitive advantages (e.g., Powell and Dent-Micallef 1997, McAfee and Brynjolfsson 2008) and improving firm performance (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Kobelsky et al. 2008, Mithas et al. 2012). In this regard, rewarding managers with performance incentives is a potential way to motivate them to adopt a proactive IT strategic posture. Second, in the IT context, proactiveness in strategic posture is often accompanied by risk taking (Ravichandran and Liu 2011). Proactive firms that adopt innovative technologies earlier and to a greater extent than others often have to bear more technological uncertainties and obtain less complementary IT knowledge from others (Ravichandran and Liu 2011) Top managers therefore need to take substantial risks when adopting a proactive IT strategic posture, which warrants the use of risk incentives.

We empirically examine the relationships between managerial incentives and the proactiveness in IT strategic posture using a large sample of U.S. firms. Our analysis illustrates the diferential impacts of the two types of managerial incentives. Although proactiveness in IT strategic posture contributes to both performance and risk, risk incentives are efective in driving proactiveness while performance incentives are not. This finding highlights the importance of using risk incentives to motivate proactiveness in IT strategic posture. In addition to examining the efects of managerial incentives on firm-level IT strategic posture, we also consider how these efects may difer across the diversified business areas of a firm. Such consideration is important because diversified business areas are often associated with diferent risk environments that influence the efects of managerial incentives. The existing literature, however, lacks a consensus on how strategic posture may vary across diversified business areas. For example, some studies suggest that diversification may hinder risk taking in firms’ secondary business areas because top managers may lack control over risky projects in these areas (Baysinger and Hoskisson 1989, Hill and Hoskisson 1987). However, other studies suggest that managers’ risk-taking posture in primary business areas may be driven by the pressure to improve performance and not by their actual risk orientations or incentives (Noy and Ellis 2003). In addition, other research argues that expansion into secondary business areas provides managers with more opportunities to allocate resources to support risk taking in these areas (Williamson 1975, Eisenmann 2002). Our study illustrates that although firms may adopt a more proactive IT strategic posture in their primary business areas, the marginal efect of risk incentives in fostering proactiveness in IT strategic posture is stronger in secondary business areas. Performance incentives, by contrast, may discourage a proactive IT strategic posture in secondary business areas. This study shows how the efects of the two types of managerial incentives on IT strategic posture may be diferent in various areas of a diversified firm.

This research contributes to multiple streams of literature. First, our study illustrates how the proactiveness in IT strategic posture may be driven by managerial incentives, in addition to other organizational and environmental determinants considered in the existing IS research (e.g., Ravichandran and Liu 2011, Mithas et al. 2013). It also highlights the salience of top managers’ personal orientations in shaping corporate IT strategies (Armstrong and Sambamurthy 1999, Jarvenpaa and Ives 1991). Second, this research extends the extant IS literature on managerial compensation (e.g., Anderson et al. 2000, Masli et al. 2014) by showing that managerial compensation actually drives proactiveness in IT strategic posture through risk incentives, rather than the well-studied performance incentives. The contribution is to highlight the issue of managerial risk aversion that has been underinvestigated in prior IS studies. This study also extends the IT risk research (e.g., Tanriverdi and Ruefli 2004, Dewan and Ren 2011) by proposing mechanisms that foster strategic orientation to take the risk with IT. Third, this study examines how the efects of performance incentives and risk incentives in driving proactive IT strategic posture may operate diferently in a diversified business environment. The contribution is to extend our existing understanding of the relationship between diversification and IT strategies.

## 2. Theory and Hypothesis Development 2.1. Proactiveness in IT Strategic Posture

In the strategy literature, the concept of strategic posture is used to refer to a firm’s overall strategic orientation in the competitive setting and it can be characterized using an entrepreneurial-conservative continuum (Karagozoglu and Brown 1988, Miles and Snow 1978). Starting with Miller (1983), a large body of entrepreneurship research characterizes entrepreneurial orientation using three dimensions: proactiveness, innovativeness, and risk taking (e.g., Covin and Slevin 1989, Schafer 1990, Wiklund and Shepherd 2003). Proactiveness refers to a posture of (1) anticipating and acting on future demands beyond the present scope of operations (Venkatraman 1989), and (2) acting as a leader to take aggressive initiatives ahead of competitors and establish unique advantages (Miller and Friesen 1978, Miller 1983). Innovativeness refers to a firm’s tendency to engage in creative ideas and development that result in new products, services, or technological processes (Lumpkin and Dess 1996). Risk taking refers to a tendency to make risky resource commitments that have a reasonable chance of costly failures (Miller and Friesen 1978). The three dimensions, although defined separately, can closely accompany each other in some strategic areas. The original conceptualization in Miller (1983) suggests that these three dimensions may sometimes be correlated strongly enough to jointly comprise a unidimensional strategic posture. For example, for firms that are proactive in relying on technological or product innovations to strategically step out of their comfort zones and distinguish themselves from competitors, their proactive posture is associated with strategic orientation for innovativeness and risk taking.

IT is a specific area in which the proactiveness dimension is strongly correlated with the innovativeness and risk-taking dimensions of strategic posture. Given the innovative nature of IT, firms that take a proactive posture in adopting new ITs cannot be those that lack the posture to pursue innovativeness. For example, when proactive firms tend to leverage IT to explore new market opportunities, they often need to create and introduce more innovative ITenabled services to seize the new market opportunities (Joshi and Das 2015). Also, proactive firms that take a lead in using IT to create and meet future needs must encounter technological uncertainties that are not faced by other competitors. In this case, proactive firms receive less complementary IT knowledge, solutions, and services from others to deal with the risks (Ravichandran and Liu 2011) and they thereby also need a strong orientation for risk taking. In the IT context, the association between proactiveness and the other two dimensions of entrepreneurial orientation helps explain the multifaceted impact of proactiveness. For example, the improved performance of proactive firms can be attributed to their associated innovativeness in establishing and leveraging IT-based technological advantages over other competitors. The high-risk trajectories of proactive firms can be attributed to their strategic orientation to take IT risks.

The consideration of the IT strategic posture helps to understand the process of making strategic IT decisions (Ravichandran and Liu 2011). Although prior IS studies have examined firms’ IT-related proactiveness in various contexts, such as technology adoption (Zaheer and Zaheer 1997), business-IT alignment (Chan et al. 1997), IT capability building (Lu and Ramamurthy 2011), IT innovation (Swanson and Ramiller 2004), and IT investment (Ravichandran and Liu 2011, Mithas et al. 2013), there is no formal theoretical definition of proactiveness in IT strategic posture. Extending the concepts in the strategy literature to the IT context, we define IT strategic posture as the firm’s overall strategic stance toward taking IT initiatives in the competitive setting. In this research, we conceptualize the proactiveness in IT strategic posture (referred to as PITSP hereafter) as the firm’s strategic orientation to (1) seek new opportunities of IT investment to expand IT infrastructure development beyond current operations so as to build unique competitive advantages; and (2) act as a leader, rather than as a follower, in investing in IT and developing IT infrastructure. Firms with PITSP typically make more aggressive IT investments than others and actively expand their IT infrastructure beyond the needs of current operations (Mithas et al. 2013, Joshi and Das 2015). Nonproactive firms, by contrast, often adopt reactive strategies such as lagging behind and imitating others in building IT (Lu and Ramamurthy 2010), or passive strategies of cutting back on IT investment (Mithas et al. 2013). Although firms can choose to be either proactive or reactive/passive in IT strategic posture, our research focuses on how to motivate a PITSP, as PITSP suggests that corporate decision makers agree more on the relevance and importance of IT in firm business. Also, in this research, we focus more specifically on the investment in IT infrastructure as IT infrastructure serves as the foundation on which other IT components operate (Weill and Vitale 2002). Most innovative and value-adding IT practices cannot be realized without the supporting IT infrastructure. Moreover, the expansion of other IT components is usually correlated with the underlying IT infrastructure (Gao and Hitt 2012).

## 2.2. Proactiveness in IT Strategic Posture, Firm Return, and Risk

From the principal-agent perspective, the incentive mechanisms that corporate owners design to motivate strategic actions from managers are based on a firm’s outcomes, such as return and risk (Mehran 1995). Therefore, to better understand how performance and risk incentives may influence IT strategic posture, it is worthwhile to first consider how IT strategic posture is linked to firm return and risk. The relationship between PITSP and firm return can be explained based on the resource-based view (RBV) (Barney 1991, Mata et al. 1995, Wade and Hulland 2004). First, PITSP emphasizes innovative IT development that extends beyond the firms’ routine business operations. Such IT development should help the firm enhance returns by exploiting idiosyncratic resources to leverage new market opportunities. Empirical evidence also suggests that with the operational needs of IT being controlled for, firms’ idiosyncratic excessive IT investments significantly enhance firm returns (Kobelsky et al. 2008). Second, PITSP emphasizes being a leader rather than a follower in investing in IT. Such a tendency helps establish IT-based competitive advantages (Clemons and Row 1991, McAfee and Brynjolfsson 2008, Mithas et al. 2013) that improve firm returns (Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Dewan and Ren 2011, Mithas et al. 2012). The tendency to take the lead in IT development also helps the firm leverage other complementary capabilities, e.g., operational eficiency, to realize strategic returns (Barua and Kriebel 1991). Moreover, some proactive IT initiatives (e.g., early adoptions of enterprise software) may signal to external investors about the long-term growth opportunities of the firm and result in favorable market reactions (Oh et al. 2006, Dewan and Ren 2007). Therefore, PITSP is expected to be associated with the improvement in firm returns. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1 (H1A). Proactiveness in IT strategic posture is positively associated with firm performance.

Following the finance literature and IT risk research (e.g., Dewan et al. 2007), we consider firm risk as the variation in performance (e.g., return). The relationship between PITSP and firm risk is mainly explained by the risky nature of IT and the risk-taking perspective in IS research (Ravichandran and Liu 2011). First, IT risk research (e.g., Dewan et al. 2007) suggests that IT in general is associated with more performance uncertainties than other types of assets. The proactive allocation of more investment resources to IT therefore exposes the firm to more risks. Second, while conservative firms can mitigate risk by imitating others or strategically lagging in IT investment (Mithas et al. 2013), proactive firms tend to be first movers to experiment with new IT development. Therefore, firms with PITSP have few benchmarks for their new IT initiatives and have to face more uncertain outcomes (Swanson and Ramiller 2004). Third, PITSP also manifests in a strong commitment to developing and maintaining in-house IT infrastructure (Ravichandran and Liu 2011). Firms with PITSP have to rely less on other market-based solutions (e.g., outsourcing) to mitigate or hedge technological and operational risks in IT. Fourth, industry norms and the actions of competitors provide observable benchmarks and successful cases for managers to justify their IT decisions (Haunschild and Miner 1997). Existing operational needs for IT can also help justify resource commitment to IT. However, when proactive firms deviate from industry norms and existing operations to engage in innovative IT development, external investors may encounter shortterm uncertainties in judging the value of excessive IT investment and development, which may lead to volatilities in stock performance and market returns (Bharadwaj et al. 2009). Therefore, in capital markets, proactive firms have to experience a growth trajectory with more return volatilities (i.e., risks). Accordingly, PITSP is expected to be associated with firm risk. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1 (H1B). Proactiveness in IT strategic posture is positively associated with firm risk.

## 2.3. Principal-Agent Model and Managerial Incentives

The principal-agent model suggests that conflicts of interest exist between shareholders (the principal) and managers (the agent) of the firm because managers, unlike shareholders who own the firm, may have less incentive to enhance firm value (Jensen and Meckling 1976). Also, shareholders and managers have diferent risk orientations. While shareholders are often risk neutral as they can diversify their investments, managers are usually risk averse because a large proportion of their undiversified personal wealth income comes from the firm and they also face employment risk (Grossman and Hart 1983, Jensen and Murphy 1990). To motivate managers to improve firm value and market return, shareholders often base a portion of managers’ compensation on certain observable signals that are indicative of their managerial productivity. Stock price, which is directly related to firm value, is the most commonly used signal. Therefore, compensation mechanisms that tie managerial income with the firm’s stock return, e.g., stock-based and option-based compensation, are typical managerial performance incentives used in executive compensation (Mehran 1995). In this study, we follow the finance and accounting literatures to conceptualize performance incentives as the sensitivity of managerial income to the change in stock price, i.e., delta (Core and Guay 2002, Coles et al. 2006, Low 2009). Compensation with a high delta level means that managers gain more when the firm’s market performance increases and therefore managers have a higher incentive to improve firm performance.

The use of performance incentives, however, further exposes managers to firm risk. As performance incentives link managers’ personal wealth income with firm’s market performance, the volatility of performance, or, the market risk, results in lower utilities for risk-averse managers because of their concave (in firm’s market performance) utility functions. Consequently, the use of performance incentives may drive risk-averse managers to forego high-risk initiatives with uncertain returns (Jensen and Meckling 1976, May 1995), e.g., R&D activities (Dechow and Sloan 1991). To motivate strategic risk taking, shareholders need to provide risk-averse managers with additional risk incentives. For example, option compensation, which provides managers convex income functions in stock return, is often used to deliver managerial risk incentives because it can counteract the efect of risk aversion (please see, e.g., Core and Guay 2002, Jensen and Meckling 1976, Smith and Watts 1992). In this study, we follow the finance and accounting literatures to conceptualize risk incentives as the sensitivity of managerial option value to stock return volatility, i.e., vega (Guay 1999, Cohen et al. 2000, Coles et al. 2006). Compensation with a higher vega level means that managers’ option values increase when the market risk increases (i.e., when the stock return becomes more volatile) and therefore managers receive higher incentive to take risks.

Prior IS research has only focused on performance incentives, not on risk incentives. For example, Talmor and Wallace (1998) examined how equity compensation in the computer industry boosts long-term firm performance. Anderson et al. (2000) showed that firm stock returns increase with incentive payment in executive compensation (i.e., option holdings). Option compensation, however, was conceptualized as performance incentives rather than risk incentives. Anderson et al. (2002) showed that IT CEOs’ exercisable stock options had a stronger performance-enhancing efect in a down market than in an up market, but their unexercisable options had a weaker performanceenhancing efect. Masli et al. (2014) found that firms use equity compensation to motivate IT spending and improve the performance-enhancing efect of IT. None of these studies, however, have taken into account the role of risk incentives.

## 2.4. Managerial Incentives and Proactiveness in IT Strategic Posture

As performance incentives tie managerial income with the firm’s performance, the provision of performance incentives should enable managers to benefit from the performance improvement caused by PITSP. Therefore, the use of more performance incentives, or, higher delta, should motivate managers to pursue PITSP. This logic is in line with other IS research suggesting that performance incentives, such as equity compensation, drive overall IT investment (Masli et al. 2014). However, it is worth noting that performance incentives may also generate a counterefect that discourages PITSP, and this counterefect has been underinvestigated in prior IS studies. By linking managerial income to firm performance, performance incentives also expose managers to more variance in firm performance (i.e., firm risk) and therefore may undermine risk-averse managers’ intention to adopt risky strategies (Garen 1994). Therefore, a positive association between performance incentives and PITSP is contingent on that the risk-exposing efect of performance incentives is not significant enough to ofset the motivating efect of performance incentives. Overall, we hypothesize a positive association between performance incentives and proactiveness in IT strategic posture.

## Hypothesis 2 (H2A). Performance incentives are positively associated with the proactiveness in IT strategic posture.

Managerial risk aversion may hinder PITSP if top managers become wary about deviating from industry norms and established business operations. For example, although top managers are likely to foresee the strategic value of IT because of their position and broader perspectives about the firm (Jarvenpaa and Ives 1991), high-risk IT development cannot always be justified by the traditional performance measures (e.g., return on investment) in the early stages of investment (Tallon et al. 2000, Kobelsky et al. 2008) and when top managers are under pressure to smooth short-term earnings (Gunny 2010). As a result, without suficient risk incentives, top managers may be reluctant to risk their personal financial and human capital to proactively bet on risky IT investments. Likewise, risk-averse managers are likely to follow industry norms of IT development, as they may believe that imitating the norms makes them less vulnerable to being singled out and subject to blame, if their IT decisions are proven to be suboptimal later (Mithas et al. 2013). Prior IS studies on institutional influence (e.g., Teo et al. 2003, Liang et al. 2007, Ravichandran et al. 2009) also suggest that imitating industry norms is a way to minimize risk and experimental cost in IT development. Therefore, without strong risk incentives, risk-averse managers may be reluctant to pursue PITSP and deviate from industry norms of IT development.

Managerial risk incentives (vega) enable top managers to gain financially from firm risk, and such financial gain may ofset the managerial disutility of risk and provide motivation for PITSP. The extant literature has documented that risk incentives are associated with a variety of corporate risk-taking strategies. For example, risk incentives have been found to lead to higher corporate leverage (Cohen et al. 2000), higher stock return volatility (Guay 1999), more R&D expenditure (Coles et al. 2006), more resource exploration efort (Rajgopal and Shevlin 2002), and less use of risk-hedging derivatives (Knopf et al. 2002). By the same token, we expect that risk incentives motivate top managers to pursue PITSP. In addition, since IT is often used to support and complement other risk strategies such as product innovation (Kleis et al. 2012, Xue et al. 2012), risk incentives are also expected to motivate a more proactive development of IT along with other firm risk strategies. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2 (H2B). Risk incentives are positively associated with the proactiveness in IT strategic posture.

## 2.5. Diversification, Managerial Risk Incentives, and Proactiveness in IT Strategic Posture

The relationship between IT and firm diversification has been an important research issue in the IS literature (e.g., Hitt 1999, Dewan et al. 1998, Dewan and Ren 2011). Most of the existing studies, however, focus on the efect of IT in reducing coordination costs and use this perspective to explain the relationship between IT and diversification (e.g., Hitt 1999, Dewan et al. 1998). Some recent studies also consider how diversification may help reduce IT risk and improve the return from IT investment (Dewan and Ren 2011). However, less attention has been paid to how a diversified corporate environment may influence the impact of managerial incentives on IT strategies. In this study, we examine how managerial incentives may influence the proactiveness in IT strategic posture diferently across a variety of business areas. Specifically, we compare between focal firms’ primary business areas and their secondary business areas. Primary business areas are industries in which firms’ headquarters operate, and secondary business areas are other industries that firms diversify into. We examine how the marginal efects of managerial incentives on PITSP are diferent between primary and secondary business areas.

Prior IS research has also recognized that diversification may mitigate the overall risk of IT (Dewan and Ren 2011). However, given such risk-mitigating efect, it is not clear how diversification may influence the impact of managerial incentives on PITSP. On one hand, diversification reduces the impact of individual business areas on the overall performance of the firm (Amihud and Lev 1981), mitigating top managers’ concern about risky outcomes, and making them more willing to take risk in new and diversified business areas (Eisenmann 2002). On the other hand, concerns about the lack of control in the diversified firms also discourage top managers from initiating risky projects, especially in the secondary business areas (Hill and Hoskisson 1987, Baysinger and Hoskisson 1989). Therefore, it is important to study how managerial incentives may afect the PITSP diferently across the primary and secondary business areas.

In general, firms’ risk strategies are associated with more outcome uncertainties in secondary business areas than in primary business areas. Diversification generates information-processing constraints that prevent top managers from acquiring deep knowledge about the less familiar secondary business areas (Dundas and Richardson 1982). The lack of knowledge results in higher risks in secondary business areas (Eisenmann 2002). Top managers also have less control over projects in the secondary areas (Baysinger and Hoskisson 1989, Hill and Hoskisson 1987, Hoskisson and Hitt 1988), which further reduces their confidence about risk strategies in these areas. Consequently, from the risk-avoidance perspective, risk-averse managers may reduce their risk-taking eforts in secondary business areas. As performance incentives escalate managers’ risk aversion, performance incentives may be less efective in secondary business areas, than in primary business areas, to motivate PITSP. In addition, secondary-business operations often contribute less to the overall firm performance than primarybusiness operations. When performance incentives tie managers’ wealth to the overall firm performance, managers are likely to prioritize the improvement in primary-business operations because doing so allows them to obtain higher marginal benefits from performance incentives. Likewise, when adopting PITSP to improve the overall firm performance (as a response to performance incentives), managers are also likely to prioritize PITSP that improves primary-business operations. We therefore expect that the marginal efect of performance incentives in motivating PITSP is weaker in secondary business areas than in primary business areas. We develop the following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 3 (H3A). Performance incentives contribute less to the proactiveness in IT strategic posture in firms’ secondary business areas than in their primary business areas.

Managerial risk incentives, however, allow top managers to gain from the variance in firm performance. When managers are driven by risk incentives to become more risk oriented, their risk taking is likely to increase more significantly in secondary business areas than in primary business areas. First, there is anecdotal evidence that in primary business areas, managers choose to increase risk taking because they are under pressure to improve performance, not because they become more risk-oriented (Noy and Ellis 2003). In other words, managerial risk taking in primary business areas is more influenced by performance pressure. By contrast, in the secondary business areas, managers have greater discretion and their risk taking is driven more by their own risk orientation (Noy and Ellis 2003). Risk incentives therefore should be more efective to drive risk-taking strategies in secondary areas. Second, in secondary business areas, firms have less established business practices and therefore risk-seeking managers can identify more opportunities for risk taking (Eisenmann 2002). Firms often have less legacy systems and standardized processes in secondary business areas and can have more room to use IT-based innovation and experiment with high-risk and high-return business models. The “internal market” between diversified units also enables firms to reallocate resources to fund risk-taking initiatives in secondary business areas (Williamson 1975). Therefore, when managers are provided with more risk incentives, their tendency to adopt a PITSP may increase more significantly in secondary business areas than in primary business areas. We thus expect that the marginal efect of risk incentives in driving PITSP is higher in secondary business areas, or, risk incentives contribute more to PITSP in secondary areas. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 3 (H3B). Risk incentives contribute more to the proactiveness in IT strategic posture in firms’ secondary business areas than in their primary business areas.

## 3. Data and Measurement 3.1. Data

In our study, the primary source of data on establishment-level IT implementation is Harte–Hank’s Computer Intelligence (CI) database, which has been used extensively in the IS literature as a reliable source of IT data (e.g., Chwelos et al. 2010, Dewan and Ren 2011, Kleis et al. 2012). We obtained IT implementation data on 625,371 unique establishments of U.S. firms over the period of 1999–2009, with a total of 2,915,763 establishment-year observations. This sample is used for deriving establishment-level measures of PITSP. Out of the entire sample of unique establishments, we were able to acquire the corresponding firmlevel information for 199,348 unique establishments, which formed a sample of 10,241 unique U.S. firms. We matched this sample with Compustat, Compustat Segment, and CRSP (Center for Research in Security Prices) databases to obtain firm-level accounting and stock market data.

We used the Execucomp database to obtain data on top management compensation. Execucomp provides data on salary, bonus, and total compensation for the top five executives (ranked annually by salary and bonus). Following prior studies (Aggarwal and Samwick 2006), we considered managerial compensation as the total compensation of all the top five executives. We also followed the literature to eliminate firms in the utility, finance, and insurance industries, as these firms are subject to high regulation and therefore incentive payments play a less important role (Coles et al. 2006, Smith and Watts 1992). The match with Execucomp reduced the size of our firm-level sample to 9,509 firm-year observations, with 1,492 unique firms covering 66,725 unique establishments. The observed firms in our sample cover about 10.7% of all observable establishments in the CI database. We use t-tests to compare between the establishment group covered by our firm-level sample and the entire establishment group in $\mathrm { C I } ,$ and find no significant diference between their average annual employee numbers and average annual revenues.

## 3.2. Variables

To measure PITSP, we used available data from the CI database to assess installed bases of three types of IT components: decentralized computing equipment (DCE), centralized computing equipment (CCE), and network communication equipment (NCE). Following the IT value literature (Chwelos et al. 2010, Kleis et al. 2012), we use the number of PCs and workstations as a proxy for DCE, the number of servers as a proxy for CCE, and the number of network nodes as a proxy for NCE. We first use the following model to assess the extent to which each establishment leads in IT development within its industry:

$$
\begin{array}{r} \frac {\Delta \mathrm{IT} _ {i , t}}{E _ {i , t - 1}} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \bigg (\frac {1}{E _ {i , t - 1}} \bigg) + \beta_ {1} \bigg (\frac {R _ {i , t - 1}}{E _ {i , t - 1}} \bigg) \\ + \beta_ {2} \bigg (\frac {\Delta R _ {i , t}}{E _ {i , t - 1}} \bigg) + \beta_ {3} \bigg (\frac {\Delta E _ {i , t}}{E _ {i , t - 1}} \bigg) + \varepsilon_ {i, t}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $E _ { i , t }$ is the number of employees of establishment i in year $t , \Delta E _ { i , t } = E _ { i , t } - \Vec { E _ { i , t - 1 } }$ is the increase in establishment employees. The term $R _ { i , t }$ is the revenue of establishment i in year $t ,$ and $\Delta R _ { i , t } = R _ { i , t } - R _ { i , t - 1 }$ is the increase in establishment revenue. $\Pi _ { i , t }$ is one of the three measures of IT development, i.e., $\Pi _ { i , t } =$ $\{ \mathrm { D C E } _ { i , t } , \mathrm { C C E } _ { i , t } , \mathrm { N C E } _ { i , t } \}$ , and $\Delta \mathrm { I T } _ { i , t } ^ { - \phantom { } } = \mathrm { I T } _ { i , t } - \mathrm { I T } _ { i , t - 1 } ^ { \phantom { } }$ is the increase in establishment IT development. Equation (1) is estimated for each (two-digit NAICS (North American Industry Classification System)) industryyear group of establishments. The logic is that for each industry-year group, Equation (1) characterizes the general industry-norm pattern of establishment-level IT increase (on a per capita basis, for the normalization purpose) alongside the size (measured by revenue) and operational growth (measured by increases in employees and revenue) of establishments.<sup>1</sup> When an establishment incurs more IT increases than the general pattern, it means that this establishment takes the lead to engage in more IT, consistent with the notion of proactiveness (Miller 1983). Moreover, the extra IT increases are not explained by the regular operational growth and industry norms of IT practices. Rather, they reflect the notion of proactiveness that excessive IT development is undertaken to seize new opportunities beyond the current operations (e.g., more business digitization or IT-enabled innovations) (Venkatraman 1989). Therefore, the regression residuals capture the degree of proactiveness, with a positive (negative) residual indicating being more (less) proactive in the establishment-level IT strategic posture. We use the residuals of Equation (1) as the measure of establishment-level PITSP. We use all 2,915,763 establishment-year observations to more accurately capture the business norms of diferent industries, although not all establishments are covered by the subsequent firm-level sample.

In the extant literature, both the survey approach (Ravichandran and Liu 2011, Lu and Ramamurthy 2011) and the secondary-data approach (Kobelsky et al. 2008, Mithas et al. 2013) have been used to assess the proactiveness in IT strategies. Our approach is consistent with research that has used regression on secondary data to assess the proactiveness in IT development (Kobelsky et al. 2008). This approach is reflective of contextual IT needs and the observed outcomes of proactiveness. In addition, our approach based on establishment-level data also allows us to better assess PITSP in diversified business environments.

The establishment-level assessment allows us to take into account cross-industry heterogeneity faced by large diversified firms. We aggregate the establishment-level PITSP measures into the firm level, weighted by revenue shares of establishments, to construct three firm-level PITSP measures in the areas of DCE, CCE, and NCE, and denote them as AIDCE, AICCE, and AINCE, respectively. We then regress the market return and risk of firms against AIDCE, AICCE, and AINCE, controlling for other contingency factors. Following prior research (e.g., Dewan and Ren 2011), the market return of the firm is measured using a one-year average monthly stock return, and the market risk is measured using a one-year volatility of monthly stock return. We focus on the market return and risk because managerial incentive compensation is usually tied to the firm’s market performance and risk. The control variables include commonly used variables in studying firm performance and risk (e.g., Guay 1999, Low 2009, McAlister et al. 2007), such as R&D, advertising expenditure (ADV), capital investment (CAPX), firm leverage (LVRG), firm size (SIZE), firm diversification (DIV), lagged firm return/risk, and market return/risk. We also use dummies to control for the industry- and year-fixed efects. The measurement details of these variables are in Table 1.

The measurement of managerial incentives merits careful attention. Although prior studies used specific compensation components (e.g., equity and option holding) to measure incentives, the drawbacks of such approaches are notable. Some components, such as options, are linked to both performance and risk. The value of in-the-money options changes with both stock price (performance) and stock return volatility (risk). The variance of option value caused by firm risk may still concern risk-averse managers, and such concern may discourage managerial risk taking (Lambert et al. 1991). Therefore, to better separate between performance and risk incentives, we follow the finance and accounting literatures to use vega as the measure of risk incentives (Guay 1999, Cohen et al. 2000, Coles et al. 2006). Vega refers to the sensitivity of the option value to stock return volatility, or, the market risk (Guay 1999). When managers are compensated with high-vega options, their option values increase when the return of the underlying stock becomes more volatile (or, the market risk of the firm increases). Managers can then essentially gain from firm risk. Such gain can counteract risk-averse managers’ concern about risk and motivate their risk taking. Therefore, vega is an appropriate measure of risk incentives.<sup>2</sup> We adopt the one-year approximation approach in Core and Guay (2002) to derive the vega and delta of top managers’ option portfolios.<sup>3</sup> The approach of Core and Guay (2002) relies on the Black and Scholes (1973) option valuation model, as modified by Merton (1973) to account for dividends, to assess the value of different types of options (e.g., newly granted and previously granted options, exercisable and unexercisable options). This approach has been used extensively in the literature (e.g., Coles et al. 2006, Low 2009). Vega is measured as the change in the manager’s overall option value for a 0.01 change in the annualized standard deviation of stock returns.

As performance incentives reflect the change in the manager’s overall compensation with the stock price (i.e., market performance), they are measured using delta, which is defined as the sum of option delta and share holdings of managers (Low 2009). Option delta is defined as the change in the manager’s overall option value for a 1% change in stock price.

The control variables that we use in testing H2A and H2B are all based on the existing literature, and the detailed definitions are in Table 1. Specifically, we control for managers’ cash compensation (Coles et al. 2006). CEO tenure and CEO turnover (whether the CEO changed) are used to control for the potential impact of the CEO’s personal background (Berger et al. 1997, Miller et al. 2002). We include market-tobook (MTB) ratio and sales growth (SG) to control for the impact of growth opportunities on investment policies (Coles et al. 2006). We include other expenditures that may afect IT, such as R&D, advertising, and capital investment (Dewan and Ren 2011). To control for firms’ cash available to finance new projects, we include a measure of surplus cash (SPLS) as developed in Richardson (2006). Firm diversification is included as it is a key factor influencing corporate risk taking. We include firm size and leverage to control for firm scale and financing characteristics. Industry dummies and year dummies are used to control for the industry fixed efect and the year fixed efect. We also use three firm-level IT intensity measures (DCEI, CCEI, and NCEI) to control for the general usage levels of the three types of IT components. Unlike the three PITSP measures, which are developed based on the increase of IT, these IT intensity measures capture the existing installed base of IT.

## 4. Empirical Models

We first examine the relationship between PITSP and firm return and risk. We follow the approach in prior IS studies $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Dewan et al. 2007) by regressing firm market return and risk against the three PITSP measures along with other control variables. We specify the following system of equations:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{Return} _ {i, t} = & \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \mathrm{AIDCE} _ {i, t - 1} + \alpha_ {2} \mathrm{AICCE} _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \alpha_ {3} \mathrm{AINCE} _ {i, t - 1} + \eta [ \mathrm{Controls} ] _ {i, t - 1} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}, \\ \mathrm{Risk} _ {i, t} = & \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \mathrm{AIDCE} _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} \mathrm{AICCE} _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {3} \mathrm{AINCE} _ {i, t - 1} + \rho [ \mathrm{Controls} ] _ {i, t - 1} + \sigma_ {i, t}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

(3)

We employ diferent estimation approaches to ensure robustness of the result and address the potential endogeneity issue (details in sections below). Clusterrobust standard errors are used in hypothesis testing to address the potential issues of heteroskedasticity and within-cluster error correlation. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) of all independent variables are below 10, suggesting no serious issue of multicollinearity.

We next examine how managerial incentives influence PITSP. As we consider three measures of PITSP, we specify a system of three equations

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{AIDCE} _ {i, t} = & \alpha_ {1 0} + \alpha_ {1 1} \mathrm{Delta} _ {i, t - 1} + \alpha_ {1 2} \mathrm{Vega} _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \lambda_ {1} [ \text {Other Controls} ] _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \theta_ {1} \mathrm{DCEI} _ {i, t - 1} + v _ {i, t}, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{AICCE} _ {i, t} = & \alpha_ {2 0} + \alpha_ {2 1} \text {Delta} _ {i, t - 1} + \alpha_ {2 2} \text {Vega} _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \lambda_ {2} [ \text {Other Controls} ] _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \theta_ {2} \text {CCEI} _ {i, t - 1} + \sigma_ {i, t}, \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{AINCE} _ {i, t} = & \alpha_ {3 0} + \alpha_ {3 1} \mathrm{Delta} _ {i, t - 1} + \alpha_ {3 2} \mathrm{Vega} _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \lambda_ {3} [ \text {Other Controls} ] _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \theta_ {3} \mathrm{NCEI} _ {i, t - 1} + \tau_ {i, t}, \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

where delta and vega are in their corresponding natural logarithm values. We use the lagged values of independent variables to mitigate endogeneity concerns. The use of lagged values of delta and vega reflects that, based on their sense about how their compensation incomes will change along with the firm performance and risk, managers can plan their future strategies to improve performance and take risk. The rationale is that delta and vega, as general compensation features, are assessed using historical data (e.g., the past movements of stock and option prices). The three IT intensity measures $\begin{array} { r l } {  { ( \mathrm { D C E I } _ { i , t - 1 } ^ { - } , \dot { \mathrm { C C E I } _ { i , t - 1 } } , } } \end{array}$ and $\mathrm { N C E I } _ { i , t - 1 } )$ are used to control for the existing usage levels of corresponding components. We estimate the system of equations using seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) to take into account the potential cross-equation correlation. A Breusch–Pagan test of independence rejects the assumption of cross-equation independence, suggesting the advantage of using SUR estimators over ordinary least squares (OLS) estimators. A robustness check using equation-by-equation OLS estimation, however, generates very consistent results. We have also conducted additional analyses to ensure that the model estimation is not influenced by potential endogeneity (as illustrated below). Again, clusterrobust standard errors are used in hypothesis testing. VIFs of independent variables do not indicate any serious issue of multicollinearity.

To examine the impact of managerial incentives on PITSP in diversified firms, we distinguish between the firm’s primary-industry (two-digit) segments and their secondary-industry segments. A firm’s primary industry is the two-digit industry of the firms’ headquarters, and its secondary industries are all of the other twodigit industries that the firm operates in. For each firm, instead of aggregating the three PITSP measures at the firm level, we aggregate them separately for all of the primary-industry establishments and for all of the secondary-industry establishments, weighted by corresponding revenue shares. Primary-industry establishments and secondary-industry establishments are identified using the establishment-level NAICS codes provided by the CI Technology database. For example, for decentralized computing equipment, instead of deriving a single measure of AIDCE for each firm, we constructed two measures: the primary-industry AIDCE (denoted as PAIDCE) and the secondaryindustry AIDCE (denoted as SAIDCE). We then use the same specification (model 4) to construct two separate equations, with PAIDCE and SAIDCE as the dependent variables, respectively. We estimated these two equations using seemingly unrelated regression to examine how managerial incentives may influence PAIDCE and SAIDCE diferently. The same approach is also used to examine the other two measures of PITSP. For this analysis we remove single-business firms and this reduces the sample size to 6,685 firmyear observations.

## 5.2. Managerial Incentives and Proactive IT Strategic Posture

Table 3 presents results on the impacts of managerial incentives. The coeficients of vega are positive and significant in all three equations, suggesting that the risk incentives provided in compensation foster PITSP.

Therefore, H2B is supported. However, the coeficients of delta are in general not significant in all three equations. This suggests that the performance incentives do not foster PITSP. Therefore, H2A is not supported. These results are essentially in line with the perspective of managerial risk aversion in the classic principalagent model. Higher delta means that managerial compensation is tied more closely to performance. As a result, performance variance (i.e., the firm risk) can lead to a higher fluctuation in managerial compensation, and managers are subject to the efect of firm risk to a greater extent. The disutility generated from the greater risk exposure may therefore ofset the gain from the performance, making performance incentives not efective in motivating risk-averse managers to pursue PITSP. The negative coeficients of cash compensation suggest that top managers compensated with more salary and bonuses are even less likely to pursue PITSP. A potential explanation is that top managers relying more on cash compensation are worried about the short-term efect of IT investments decreasing earnings and therefore choose to maximize their cash compensation by minimizing IT spending (Masli et al. 2014). This finding is also consistent with prior studies suggesting that top managers with higher cash compensation seek to avoid risk (Berger et al. 1997, Guay 1999).

## 5.4. Robustness Checks and Additional Analyses

Firm Return and Risk Models. To address the potential endogeneity of the three PITSP measures in the firm return and risk models (Equations (2) and (3)), we employ a three-stage least squares (3SLS) estimator for robustness check, treating the three PITSP measures as endogenous variables. We use an extra instrument, abnormal number of IT suppliers (ANITS), which is measured using the sum of all IT suppliers (e.g., hardware and software vendors, service providers, etc.) of the firm, adjusted by the corresponding industry mean. ANITS is correlated with the three PITSP measures, but has no direct association with firm return or risk. The 3SLS results are qualitatively consistent with the main SUR results. Wu–Hausman tests indicate no significant endogeneity bias for the three PITSP measures (with T statistics ranging from <sup>−</sup>0.553 to 1.501, p > 0.1), suggesting no advantage of using 3SLS over SUR.

It is also worth noting that certain features of residual-based measures (zero means, independent and identically distributed with normal distribution, and minimized sum of squared values) for PITSP may also introduce measurement errors. We therefore employ errors-in-variables (EIV) models to verify the impacts of PITSP on firm return and risk. The EIV models assume that the true AIDCE, AICCE, and AINCE are the sums of the corresponding observed measure (the residual-based measure in this case) and an unobserved error term (i.e., the measurement error). We estimate these models using the generalized method of moments (GMM) estimators with high-order moment instruments (Dagenais and Dagenais 1997), and the results are qualitatively consistent with those of the main analysis. We also employ a two-step GMM estimator (Erickson and Whited 2002) that deals better with the EIV models with both measurement-error variables (AIDCE, AICCE, and AINCE in our case) and no-measurement-error variables (other controls in our case). This estimation also yields qualitatively consistent results.

Managerial Incentives. We also conduct robustness checks on the SUR results in Table 3. We first conduct two checks, one with the three PITSP measures derived using employee-weighted aggregation (from the establishment level to the firm level), and one with the three PITSP measures derived using fourdigit industry-year regressions (at the establishment level). As shown in Online Appendices 2.1 and 2.2, the results are qualitatively consistent with those of the main analysis. We also conduct a 3SLS estimation of Equations (3)–(5), treating delta and vega as endogenous variables and employing institutional equity holding as an extra instrument (Hartzell and Starks 2003). The 3SLS estimation generates qualitatively consistent results and Wu–Hausman tests suggest no significant endogeneity bias for either delta (T statistics ranging from <sup>−</sup>0.257 to 1.048, p > 0.1) or vega (T statistics ranging from 0.063 to 1.112, p > 0.1). 3SLS is therefore not necessarily favored over the main SUR estimation.

Considering that CEOs may play more important roles than other senior executives in strategic decisions (Aggarwal and Samwick 2006, Coles et al. 2006), we also conduct an additional analysis by distinguishing between CEO compensation and the compensation of other top managers. We separately derive delta, vega, and cash compensation for CEOs and the other top four executives. We estimate a system of equations with similar specifications as Equations (4)–(6), but with the overall managerial compensation replaced by the separate compensation of CEOs and other members of top management. As shown in Online Appendix 2.3, none of the coeficients of CEO delta or management team delta is positive and significant, which further confirms that performance incentives do not drive PITSP. The coeficients of CEO vega are all positive and significant, suggesting that CEO risk incentives have significant explanatory power for PITSP. In the equations for AIDCE and AINCE, the coeficients of management team vega are positive and significant but not significantly diferent from those of CEO vega. These results imply that management team risk incentives are as important as CEO risk incentives in driving PITSP in DCE and NCE. However, in the equation for AICCE, the coeficient of management team vega is not significant. A potential explanation is that the CEO may play a more important role than other top managers in planning centralized computing equipment. Overall, the results suggest that CEO risk incentives may be more efective than management team risk incentives in driving PITSP, though management team incentives are also important.

Another plausible concern is that our PITSP measures mainly capture the development of hardware infrastructure. To the extent that other IT expenditures (e.g., system software and maintenance) are correlated with hardware infrastructure development (Gao and Hitt 2012), our PITSP measures are informative of firms’ overall PITSP. However, the implementation of application software may be less correlated with hardware infrastructure development. Therefore, we also conduct a robustness analysis using an alternative PITSP measure based on application software data. We obtained additional establishment-level data on enterprise software installation from the CI Technology database. Our data contain the establishment-level installed bases (i.e., the numbers of user accounts) of enterprise software in five functional areas: financial management, customer relationship management (CRM), supply chain management (SCM), human resources (HR) management, and the overall ERP system. We use the same industry-year regression as in Equation (1) to derive an establishment-level aggressive implementation of enterprise software, denoted as AIES, and aggregate this measure to the firm level. We then regress the firm-level AIES against managerial incentives and other controls as in Equations (4)–(6). Because of the limited availability of the enterprise software data, our sample contains 71,664 establishment-year observations and 2,011 firm-year observations over a period of 2005–2009. The results, as shown in Online Appendix 3, are consistent with the main analysis, i.e., firms’ proactiveness in adopting enterprise software is mainly driven by managerial risk incentives rather than performance incentives.

Diversification and Managerial Incentives. We rerun the SUR on firm return and risk with the overall PITSP measures replaced by separate PITSP measures in primary and secondary businesses. The results, as shown in Online Appendix 4, indicate that, in general, primary-industry PITSP and secondaryindustry PITSP both contribute to firm return, with primary-industry PITSP contributing more. In addition, primary-industry PITSP and secondary-industry PITSP both contribute to firm risk, and the diference between their impacts is not significant. The lower performance contribution of secondary-industry PITSP may help explain why performance incentives are even less likely to motivate PITSP in secondary business areas.

In the analysis in Section 5.2, the separation between primary and secondary business areas was based on two-digit NAICS codes and mainly reflected unrelated diversification. We also rerun the analysis by replacing the overall firm diversification variable with two variables, related diversification (RD) and unrelated diversification (UD), which are measured using entropy measures as in Palepu (1985). Conceptually, RD reflects the extent to which firm businesses are distributed evenly across related four-digit industries within the same corresponding two-digit industry groups, and UD reflects the extent to which firm businesses are distributed evenly across diferent two-digit industry groups (measurement details are in Online Appendix 5). Our sample split lets us compare between a firm’s primary business areas (with a specific two-digit code) and secondary business areas (with diferent two-digit codes from the primary code) at the aggregate level. RD and UD can provide more granularity to characterize firms in terms of how their businesses spread within primary business areas (e.g., through RD) and within secondary business areas (e.g., through both RD and UD).

We add interaction terms between managerial incentives and the two diversification variables. A model with delta <sup>×</sup> RD and delta <sup>×</sup> UD interactions indicates that these interactions have no significant efect. We then run a model with vega <sup>×</sup> RD and vega <sup>×</sup> UD interactions. As shown in Online Appendix 5, the vega <sup>×</sup> RD interaction has negative and significant efects in most equations, indicating that the efects of risk incentives on PITSP are weakened by related diversification. A plausible explanation is that since related diversification mitigates overall firm risk and the efects of risk strategies, managers are discouraged to pursue PITSP. By contrast, the vega <sup>×</sup> UD interaction has insignificant efects in the primary business areas, but has positive and significant efects in the equations for secondary-industry AIDCE and secondaryindustry AINCE. These results support the insight that managers’ own risk orientations (caused by risk incentives) are more likely to drive PITSP in noncore businesses (i.e., unrelated diversified areas).

## 6. Discussion

Despite the prevalence of arguments in the IS literature that IT strategies need top management support (Armstrong and Sambamurthy 1999, Chatterjee et al. 2002, Jarvenpaa and Ives 1991), the literature is not very specific about how top management support for high-risk IT strategies can be secured. This study adopts a principal-agent perspective to study how the top management can be motivated to take a proactive IT strategic posture for the firm. The central idea is that managerial compensation can serve as a mechanism to motivate proactiveness in IT strategic posture. The findings of the study generate theoretical implications that contribute to multiple streams of research. A summary of key contributions and implications of this study is provided in Online Appendix 6. First, the agency perspective adopted in this study explains how firms’ proactive posture in taking IT initiatives are dependent on individual-level incentives of managers, in addition to other organizational (e.g., Ravichandran and Liu 2011) and environmental factors (e.g., Mithas et al. 2013) examined in prior IS research. This perspective not only reinforces the importance of top management support in IT strategies (e.g., Armstrong and Sambamurthy 1999), but also moves a step further to suggest how to realize top management support through compensation mechanisms. By theorizing and testing the impact of managerial incentives on PITSP, this study also echoes the call for research on top management support in the context of IT strategies with proactive strategic orientation (Swanson and Ramiller 2004).

Second, the extant literature suggests performance incentives as an efective way to motivate IT investment (e.g., Masli et al. 2014). Our study extends this understanding by illustrating that the efect of performance incentives may be confounded by managerial risk aversion. Performance incentives may be inadequate to motivate a proactive IT strategic posture, in spite of the performance-enhancing efect of such strategic posture. The theoretical implication is that by increasing risk exposure, performance incentives make risk-averse managers sufer more from income volatility and such an efect is significant enough to discourage managers from pursuing PITSP. Our study further shows that risk incentives are necessary to motivate managers to pursue PITSP. In this way, our study contributes to the existing IS research (e.g., Anderson et al. 2000, Masli et al. 2014) by illustrating risk incentives as another available compensation mechanism to drive IT strategies.

Third, by theorizing and empirically illustrating the role of risk incentives in realizing PITSP, our study extends the IT risk research to the area of corporate governance. IT risk research has demonstrated the risky nature of IT (Dewan et al. 2007, Tanriverdi and Ruefli 2004). However, less is known about the implication of IT risk for the design of managerial incentives. The principal-agent perspective suggests that the main cause of agency problems is the inconsistent risk orientations of corporate owners and managers. Risky IT strategies, such as PITSP, may entail more agency problems and therefore merit careful attention in compensation design. The contribution of our study is to illustrate the usefulness and importance of incorporating IT risk in compensation design so as to address agency problems in strategic IT decisions.

Fourth, this study also sheds light on how managerial incentives take efect diferently to drive PITSP in diversified business environments. This investigation helps reconcile the contradictory views in the literature regarding how diversification influences managerial risk taking. On one hand, the existing theories suggest more risk taking in primary businesses because of their importance in driving performance (Hill and Hoskisson 1987). On the other hand, prior studies also suggest more opportunities for risk taking in secondary businesses (Eisenmann 2002). Our study reconciles these views by illustrating that managers’ risk taking in secondary businesses is more sensitive to their own risk incentives, although they may be forced to take more risks in primary businesses because of performance pressures. This finding not only is consistent with some anecdotal evidence (e.g., Noy and Ellis 2003), but also adds to the IS literature that considers the interplay between IT strategies and the diversified business environment (Hitt 1999, Dewan and Ren 2011).

For practice, the implications of this study center around the issue of compensation design from the perspective of corporate owners. For corporate boards that are keen on proactive IT strategies, the design of executive compensation needs to be geared more toward rewarding managers for strategic risk taking. Appropriate compensation design can allow corporate boards to actually utilize the risky nature of IT initiatives to motivate managers. Diversified firms can also leverage risk incentives to realize more proactive IT strategies in secondary businesses that they expand into.

This study has limitations that suggest directions for future research. First, in assessing PITSP, our measures mainly capture the hardware infrastructure. Although we also collected additional data on application software (i.e., enterprise software) to verify our insights, our measures do not have the granularity to assess the quality and human capital associated with IT initiatives. They are also not suficient to capture the proactiveness in many new areas of IT, such as outsourcing (Mithas et al. 2013) and adoption of emerging technologies (e.g., mobile devices). All of these issues deserve future research attention. Second, our study focuses on the market return and risk of the firm. The main reason is that managerial incentives are tied to market performance as market performance is more observable and measureable. However, the consideration of market risk may not fully reflect other risks faced by the firm, e.g., operational risk. Therefore, how PITSP is influenced by concerns about operational risk deserves further examination. Third, our sample mainly covers large firms that allow us to examine PITSP in more diversified environments. We have verified that no sample bias exists at the establishment level. However, the more complex corporate structures may still make large diversified firms systematically diferent from small single-business firms.

## 7. Conclusion

Adopting a principal-agent perspective, this study examines how performance incentives and risk incentives motivate the top management to take a proactive IT strategic posture for the firm. Our findings suggest that although adopting a proactive IT strategic posture enables firms to enhance performance, performance incentives do not sufice to motivate top management to adopt proactive IT strategic posture, possibly because of managerial risk aversion. By contrast, risk incentives that enable top management to benefit from risk taking can efectively motivate the adoption of proactive IT strategic posture, and this motivating efect is more prominent in firms’ secondary business areas than in their primary business areas.

The study provides some promising areas for future research. First, future research may examine proactive IT strategic posture in the areas with emerging technologies such as social media, mobile computing, and cloud computing. More investigation is required to study how managerial incentives drive managers to proactively adopt these technologies and realize their strategic value. Second, our study focuses on the impact of managerial incentives on strategic IT decisions. Future research may also consider how the use of IT may improve organizational processes and governance and thus contribute to the optimal design of managerial incentives. Third, in addition to diversification, future research may also consider how the relationship between managerial incentives and proactive IT strategies may be influenced by other organizational factors, such as board structure and demographic characteristics of top managers. Fourth, as our data does not identify IT shared by firms’ primary and secondary industries, future research using more refined data sources may explore the relationship between proactiveness in IT strategic posture and the implementation of shared IT.
